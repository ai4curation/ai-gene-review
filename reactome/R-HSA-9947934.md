# CASP4 binds exposed LPS on the bacterial surface
**Reactome ID:** [R-HSA-9947934](https://reactome.org/content/detail/R-HSA-9947934)
**Species:** Homo sapiens

## Summary

Caspase-4 (CASP4) binds intracellular bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) with high affinity via its N-terminal caspase activation and recruitment domain (CARD) (Shi J et al., 2014; An J et al., 2019, 2022; Wang K et al., 2020; Dilucca M & Petr Broz P 2023). LPS can be released from phagocytosed bacteria following rupture of the phagolysosomal membrane, allowing bacteria to escape into the host cytosol. Guanylate-binding proteins (GBP), specifically GBP1, GBP2, GBP3, and GBP4, are a family of interferon-inducible, dynamin-like GTPases, that localize to the surface of Gram-negative bacteria. There, they assemble into a supramolecular complex, known as GBP coat. This GBP complex disrupts bacterial membranes, exposing LPS and thereby facilitating the binding of CASP4 to the bacterial surface (Santos JC et al., 2020, Wandel MP et al., 2020; Dickinson MS et al., 2023). Alternatively, LPS can enter the host cell cytosol through endocytosis of LPS-containing outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), which are naturally secreted by Gram-negative bacteria (Wacker MA et al., 2017; Bitto NJ et al., 2018; reviewed by Barker JH & Weiss JP 2019; Page MJ et al., 2022).<p>Upon LPS binding, CASP4 oligomerizes and undergoes activation through the auto-proteolytic cleavage (Wang K et al., 2020; Chan AH et al., 2023; Dilucca M & Petr Broz P 2023). Activated CASP4 subsequently cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD), which is also a substrate of CASP1, CASP5, and Casp11, a murine ortholog of CASP4/CASP5 (Shi J et al., 2014, 2015; Kayagaki N et al., 2015; Zhao Y et al., 2018; Wang K et al., 2020). The released N-terminal fragment of GSDMD oligomerizes to form pores in the cell membrane, leading to pyroptosis, a lytic, pro-inflammatory form of cell death that eliminates infected cells and prevents pathogen spread (Liu X et al., 2016; Ding J et al., 2016; Sborgi L et al., 2016; Aglietti RA et al., 2016). In addition, CASP4 and CASP5 cleave pro-interleukin-18 (pro-IL-18) at aspartic acid D36 with high efficiency, producing the mature, active cytokine (Shi X et al., 2023; Exconde PM et al., 2023; Devant P et al., 2023; reviewed by Exconde PM, 2024). In contrast, cleavage of pro-IL-1β by CASP4 and CASP5 at D27 produces an inactive fragment that lacks receptor-stimulating activity (Exconde PM et al., 2023; reviewed by Exconde PM, 2024). An alternative CASP4-mediated cleavage at D116, the canonical pro-IL-1β activation site, has been observed but occurs with lower efficiency comparing to pro-IL-18 processing (Bibo-Verdugo B et al., 2020; Chan AH et al., 2023; Devant P et al., 2023).<p>Intracellular bacterial pathogens have evolved strategies to suppress host inflammatory responses. For example, Shigella flexneri secretes the type III secretion system (T3SS) effector OspC3, which catalyzes ADP-riboxanation of CASP4 at the active site arginine residue R314, thereby inhibiting its enzymatic activity and blocking CASP4-mediated pyroptosis (Li Z et al., 2021; Hou Y et al., 2023).
