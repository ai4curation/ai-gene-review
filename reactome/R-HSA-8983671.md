  17–18 base pairs (bp) in length (Sarkar SN et al., 1999; 2002, 2005; Hartmann R
  et al., 2003; Donovan J et al., 2013; Schwartz SL & Conn GL 2019; Schwartz SL et
  al., 2022). Unlike many other dsRNA sensors, OAS1 lacks a conventional RNA-binding
  domain (RBD). Instead, it recognizes its ligand through a positively charged surface
  that bridges the minor grooves of the dsRNA helix (Donovan J et al., 2013). Binding
  of dsRNA induces a conformational rearrangement in the N-terminal lobe of OAS1 that
  aligns the catalytic triad (D75, D77, and D148) to coordinate two Mg²⁺ ions, creating
  a competent active site for ATP binding and enzymatic function (Sarkar SN et al.,
  1999; 2002, 2005; Hartmann R et al., 2003; Donovan J et al., 2013; Lohöfener J et
  al., 2015; Schwartz SL et al., 2020). Activated OAS1 functions as a nucleotidyltransferase,
  that polymerizes ATP into 5′-triphosphorylated 2′-5′-linked oligoadenylates (2-5A)
  in the presence of Mg²⁺ ions, producing a mixture of molecules typically ranging
  from dimers to pentamers or longer (Sarkar SN et al., 1999; Lohöfener J et al.,
  2015; Poulsen JB et al., 2015; Di H et al., 2020; Stein RL 2024). 2-5A molecules
  bind and activate RNase L, which cleaves single stranded viral and cellular RNAs,
  leading to translation arrest and stimulation of RIG-I (DDX58)-mediated responses,
  which together contribute to an antiviral response (Donovan J et al., 2017).<p>Several
  isoforms of OAS1 with different C-terminal regions are generated by alternative
  splicing (Bonnevie-Nielsen V et al., 2005; Di H et al., 2020). Among them, the cytosolic
  p42 and membrane-associated p46 are expressed at significantly higher protein levels
  compared to other isoforms (p44, p48, p52) in mammalian cells (Li H et al., 2017;
  Soveg FW et al., 2021). The splice-site SNP rs10774671 determines which isoform
  predominates, with the A allele producing p42 and the G allele generating p46 (Li
  H et al., 2017; Di H et al., 2020). OAS1 p46 contains a C-terminal CAAX motif that
  undergoes prenylation for targeting to endomembranes, whereas the absence of the
  CAAX motif in OAS1 p42 isoform prevents membrane anchoring, restricting p42 primarily
  to the cytosol (Skrivergaard S et al., 2019; Soveg FW et al., 2021; Wickenhagen
  A et al., 2021). Both isoforms bind viral dsRNA (Sarkar SN et al., 1999; 2002, 2005;
  Hartmann R et al., 2003; Donovan J et al., 2013, reviewed by Schwartz SL & Conn
  GL 2019). However, the cytosolic localization of OAS1 p42 reduces its antiviral
  efficacy compared to p46 isoform, against positive-sense single-stranded RNA (+ssRNA)
  viruses such as flaviviruses, e.g., West Nile virus (WNV) and Dengue virus (DENV),
  coronaviruses including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2),
  and picornaviruses, e.g., coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3) that replicate within endomembrane-derived
  viral replication organelles (Soveg FW et al., 2021; Wickenhagen A et al., 2021).
  Consequently, AA genotype individuals expressing only p42 show increased susceptibility
  to severe SARS-CoV-2 infection (Wickenhagen A et al., 2021; Banday AR et al., 2022),
  WNV infection (Lim JK et al., 2009) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection (El Awady
  MK et al., 2011), while G allele carriers, who express p46, have lower risk of severe
  COVID-19 (Soveg FW et al., 2021; Banday AR et al., 2022; Huffman JE et al., 2022).<p>While
  many viruses attempt to evade the innate immune system by sequestering their genomes
  and replication intermediates within specialized organelles or complexes (Stancheva
  VG & Sanyal S 2024), dsRNA becomes accessible to cytosolic OAS1 via several mechanisms
  (Son KN et al., 2015; Chen YG & Hur S 2022). For example, OAS1 may detect replicative
  dsRNA intermediates that leak or protrude to the cell cytoplasm from viral replication
  organelles of (+)ssRNA viruses such as SARS-CoV-2 (Wolff G et al., 2020; Huang Y
  et al., 2024). For viruses that replicate in the nucleus, such as the negative-sense
  RNA orthomyxoviruses, e.g., influenza A virus (IAV), dsRNA replication intermediates
  or aberrant transcripts can be exported to the cytoplasm. This leakage is normally
  restricted by viral proteins, such as NS1 protein of IAV (Krug RM 2015; Nakano M
  et al., 2025), but unshielded viral RNA species (Nakano M et al., 2025) or host
  transposable element-derived dsRNAs induced by infection (Lork M et al., 2025) can
  reach the cytosol for OAS1 recognition.<p>In addition to its enzymatic role, OAS1
  has been reported to bind AU-rich elements in host mRNAs, including IRF1 and IFNβ
  mRNAs, exerting distinct RNA-regulatory effects depending on the target transcript
  (Harioudh MK, Perez J, So L et al., 2024; Harioudh MK, Perez J, Chong Z et al.,
  2024). OAS1 binds the AU-rich element in the 3′ UTR of IFNβ mRNA and prolongs its
  half-life, thereby sustains interferon production to restrict viral replication
  via IFNAR signalling (Harioudh MK, Perez J, Chong Z et al., 2024). In the context
  of bacterial infection, OAS1 also binds IRF1 mRNA through a predicted AU-rich motif
  in its 3′ UTR (Harioudh MK, Perez J, So L et al., 2024). However, OAS1 binding to
  IRF1 mRNA does not affect its turnover or decay, but instead enhances IRF1 mRNA
  translation by localizing it to endomembranes, leading to increased expression of
  antibacterial effectors such as GBP1 (Harioudh MK, Perez J, So L et al., 2024).
  Overall, OAS1 activity integrates dsRNA sensing with 2–5A synthesis, which leads
  to RNase L activation, and potential an RNase-L-independent RNA-regulatory functions
  to mount an effective response against microbial pathogens.<br><br><br>

# OAS1 binds viral dsRNA intermediates produced by (+)ssRNA virus
**Reactome ID:** [R-HSA-8983671](https://reactome.org/content/detail/R-HSA-8983671)
**Species:** Homo sapiens

## Summary

In the cytosol, human 2′-5′-oligoadenylate synthetase 1 (OAS1) acts as a sensor of viral infection by detecting double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) of approximately 17–18 base pairs (bp) in length (Sarkar SN et al., 1999; 2002, 2005; Hartmann R et al., 2003; Donovan J et al., 2013; Schwartz SL & Conn GL 2019; Schwartz SL et al., 2022). Unlike many other dsRNA sensors, OAS1 lacks a conventional RNA-binding domain (RBD). Instead, it recognizes its ligand through a positively charged surface that bridges the minor grooves of the dsRNA helix (Donovan J et al., 2013). Binding of dsRNA induces a conformational rearrangement in the N-terminal lobe of OAS1 that aligns the catalytic triad (D75, D77, and D148) to coordinate two Mg²⁺ ions, creating a competent active site for ATP binding and enzymatic function (Sarkar SN et al., 1999; 2002, 2005; Hartmann R et al., 2003; Donovan J et al., 2013; Lohöfener J et al., 2015; Schwartz SL et al., 2020). Activated OAS1 functions as a nucleotidyltransferase, that polymerizes ATP into 5′-triphosphorylated 2′-5′-linked oligoadenylates (2-5A) in the presence of Mg²⁺ ions, producing a mixture of molecules typically ranging from dimers to pentamers or longer (Sarkar SN et al., 1999; Lohöfener J et al., 2015; Poulsen JB et al., 2015; Di H et al., 2020; Stein RL 2024). 2-5A molecules bind and activate RNase L, which cleaves single stranded viral and cellular RNAs, leading to translation arrest and stimulation of RIG-I (DDX58)-mediated responses, which together contribute to an antiviral response (Donovan J et al., 2017).<p>Several isoforms of OAS1 with different C-terminal regions are generated by alternative splicing (Bonnevie-Nielsen V et al., 2005; Di H et al., 2020). Among them, the cytosolic p42 and membrane-associated p46 are expressed at significantly higher protein levels compared to other isoforms (p44, p48, p52) in mammalian cells (Li H et al., 2017; Soveg FW et al., 2021). The splice-site SNP rs10774671 determines which isoform predominates, with the A allele producing p42 and the G allele generating p46 (Li H et al., 2017; Di H et al., 2020). OAS1 p46 contains a C-terminal CAAX motif that undergoes prenylation for targeting to endomembranes, whereas the absence of the CAAX motif in OAS1 p42 isoform prevents membrane anchoring, restricting p42 primarily to the cytosol (Skrivergaard S et al., 2019; Soveg FW et al., 2021; Wickenhagen A et al., 2021). Both isoforms bind viral dsRNA (Sarkar SN et al., 1999; 2002, 2005; Hartmann R et al., 2003; Donovan J et al., 2013, reviewed by Schwartz SL & Conn GL 2019). However, the cytosolic localization of OAS1 p42 reduces its antiviral efficacy compared to p46 isoform, against positive-sense single-stranded RNA (+ssRNA) viruses such as flaviviruses, e.g., West Nile virus (WNV) and Dengue virus (DENV), coronaviruses including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), and picornaviruses, e.g., coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3) that replicate within endomembrane-derived viral replication organelles (Soveg FW et al., 2021; Wickenhagen A et al., 2021). Consequently, AA genotype individuals expressing only p42 show increased susceptibility to severe SARS-CoV-2 infection (Wickenhagen A et al., 2021; Banday AR et al., 2022), WNV infection (Lim JK et al., 2009) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection (El Awady MK et al., 2011), while G allele carriers, who express p46, have lower risk of severe COVID-19 (Soveg FW et al., 2021; Banday AR et al., 2022; Huffman JE et al., 2022).<p>While many viruses attempt to evade the innate immune system by sequestering their genomes and replication intermediates within specialized organelles or complexes (Stancheva VG & Sanyal S 2024), dsRNA becomes accessible to cytosolic OAS1 via several mechanisms (Son KN et al., 2015; Chen YG & Hur S 2022). For example, OAS1 may detect replicative dsRNA intermediates that leak or protrude to the cell cytoplasm from viral replication organelles of (+)ssRNA viruses such as SARS-CoV-2 (Wolff G et al., 2020; Huang Y et al., 2024). For viruses that replicate in the nucleus, such as the negative-sense RNA orthomyxoviruses, e.g., influenza A virus (IAV), dsRNA replication intermediates or aberrant transcripts can be exported to the cytoplasm. This leakage is normally restricted by viral proteins, such as NS1 protein of IAV (Krug RM 2015; Nakano M et al., 2025), but unshielded viral RNA species (Nakano M et al., 2025) or host transposable element-derived dsRNAs induced by infection (Lork M et al., 2025) can reach the cytosol for OAS1 recognition.<p>In addition to its enzymatic role, OAS1 has been reported to bind AU-rich elements in host mRNAs, including IRF1 and IFNβ mRNAs, exerting distinct RNA-regulatory effects depending on the target transcript (Harioudh MK, Perez J, So L et al., 2024; Harioudh MK, Perez J, Chong Z et al., 2024). OAS1 binds the AU-rich element in the 3′ UTR of IFNβ mRNA and prolongs its half-life, thereby sustains interferon production to restrict viral replication via IFNAR signalling (Harioudh MK, Perez J, Chong Z et al., 2024). In the context of bacterial infection, OAS1 also binds IRF1 mRNA through a predicted AU-rich motif in its 3′ UTR (Harioudh MK, Perez J, So L et al., 2024). However, OAS1 binding to IRF1 mRNA does not affect its turnover or decay, but instead enhances IRF1 mRNA translation by localizing it to endomembranes, leading to increased expression of antibacterial effectors such as GBP1 (Harioudh MK, Perez J, So L et al., 2024).
