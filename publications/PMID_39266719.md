## Full Text

Abstract

E3 ubiquitin ligases are very important for regulating antiviral immunity during viral infection. Here, we discovered that Ankyrin repeat and SOCS box-containing protein 3 (ASB3), an E3 ligase, are upregulated in the presence of RNA viruses, particularly influenza A virus (IAV). Notably, overexpression of ASB3 inhibits type I IFN (IFN-I) responses induced by Sendai virus (SeV) and IAV, and ablation of ASB3 restores SeV and H9N2 infection-mediated transcription of IFN-β and its downstream interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs). Interestingly, animals lacking ASB3 presented decreased susceptibility to H9N2 and H1N1 infections. Mechanistically, ASB3 interacts with MAVS and directly mediates K48-linked polyubiquitination and degradation of MAVS at K297, thereby inhibiting the phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 and downregulating downstream antiviral signaling. These findings establish ASB3 as a critical negative regulator that controls the activation of antiviral signaling and describe a novel function of ASB3 that has not been previously reported.

Subject terms: Infectious diseases, Signal transduction

Introduction

The innate immune system serves as the host’s first line of defense against viral infections, with type I interferons (IFN-Is) considered critical components of antiviral innate immunity [ 1 ]. Viral nucleic acids are recognized by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which activate downstream signaling cascades. RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs), such as retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)-like receptors and melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 (MDA5), are primarily responsible for recognizing viral RNA [ 2 ]. In contrast, viral DNA is sensed by cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) [ 3 ]. Mitochondrial antiviral signaling proteins (MAVS) or stimulators of interferon genes (STING) are activated as adapter molecules and initiate signaling, which drives the recruitment of downstream TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1)/I-kappa B kinase ε (IKKε) [ 4 ]. This cascade then causes nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) to be phosphorylated, facilitating their translocation into the nucleus and causing the synthesis of IFN-I [ 5 , 6 ].

Influenza A virus (IAV) is a major zoonotic pathogen that poses a considerable challenge to influenza management and containment because of its broad host tropism and frequent genetic segment mutation and reassortment [ 7 ]. IAV is a negative-sense RNA virus belonging to the Orthomyxoviridae family. Numerous studies have demonstrated that IAV infection is capable of limiting the host’s innate immune response [ 8 ], and infection in mice is exacerbated by the H5 subtype of highly pathogenic avian influenza virus with the I283M/K526R mutation in PB2 [ 9 ]. Our previous study also revealed that PB1-mediated degradation of MAVS promoted H7N9 virus infection [ 10 ]. In fact, throughout infection, the virus and host interact dynamically, and the host can control the IFN-I response in a variety of ways.

A growing body of research suggests that hosts use several cellular proteins to shield MAVS and trigger effective antiviral immune responses. For example, TRIM31 facilitates MAVS aggregation and activation through Lys63-linked polyubiquitination [ 11 ], and UBL7 enhances antiviral innate immunity by promoting Lys27-linked polyubiquitination of MAVS [ 12 ]. However, not all proteins trigger defence mechanisms to protect the host during viral infection. According to recent studies, NOG1 targets IRF3 binding to the promoter, inhibiting type I IFN production [ 13 ], and SIRT2 disrupts the formation of the G3BP1-cGAS complex by deacetylating G3BP1, inhibiting cGAS activity and activating downstream signaling pathways [ 14 ]. Therefore, understanding how host proteins regulate immune responses is essential for effective viral clearance.

Protein ubiquitination modifications intricately govern the regulatory dynamics within the RLR signaling pathway. Ubiquitination as a 76 amino acid small-molecule protein requires three levels of enzymatic reactions to link the lysine on the substrate, including the E1 enzyme for activation, the E2 enzyme for delivery, and the E3 enzyme for ligation [ 15 ]. Ankyrin repeat and SOCS box-containing protein 3 (ASB3) are members of the ASB family. Unlike other E3 enzymes, the ASB protein requires binding to three auxiliary proteins (Rbx2, Cullin 5, and Elongin B/C) to form an E3 enzyme complex that catalyzes ubiquitination [ 16 ]. A previous study reported that the E3 enzyme ASB1, which belongs to the same ASB family, effectively suppresses TAB2-linked K48 polyubiquitination, consequently facilitating the initiation of the NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades [ 17 ]. Conversely, ASB8 actively promotes the degradation of TBK1/IKKε K48-linked polyubiquitination and downregulates IFN-I production [ 18 ]. The discussion surrounding ASB3 focuses on how it contributes to the ubiquitin‒proteasome-dependent destruction of the tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNF-R2) [ 19 ]. The E3 enzymes known to modulate the signaling of RLRs belong primarily to the TRIM and RNF families [ 20 ]. However, no investigations concerning the modulation of the RLR signaling pathway by ASB3 have been reported.

Our study revealed that during IAV infection, the E3 ligase ASB3 functions as a negative regulator of RLR-mediated innate antiviral immunity. The overexpression of ASB3 led to reduced expression levels of IFN-I, whereas ASB3 deficiency activated TBK1 and IRF3 phosphorylation to trigger IFN-I production, thereby inhibiting the replication of IAV. Mechanistically, ASB3 specifically interacts with MAVS and further blocks the IFN-I response by catalyzing the K48-linked polyubiquitination of MAVS at Lys297 and subsequent degradation via the proteasome pathway. According to our study, the ASB3 protein has a new function in the defense of the host immune system against the influenza virus.

Materials and Methods

Mice

ASB3 -/- mice were generated via PiggyBac transposon-based targeting technology and were kindly provided by Prof. Dongqin Yang (Shanghai Fudan University). The mice used in this study were housed under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions and had an FVB genetic background. An “E-value” analysis based on the “resource equation” method was used to estimate the sample size in the animal experiments. ASB3 +/+ , ASB3 +/- , and ASB3 -/- mice were identified via PCR amplification and DNA sequencing with the primers 5′-CTGAGATGTCCTAAATGCACAGCG-3′, 5′-CCATGACCAAACCCAATTTACACAC-3′, and 5′-TTGTTTTTTTTCCCCCCTAGACAGG-3′, respectively.

Cells, viruses, and plasmids

Human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T), Madin-Darbey Canine Kidne cells (MDCK) and human lung epithelial cells (A549) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) or DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 2 mM glutamine with penicillin (100 U/ml)/streptomycin (100 mg/ml). BMDMs and primary kidney epithelial cells were dissociated from the bone marrow and kidneys of WT or ASB3 -/- mice. The isolated BMDMs were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium, counted, and cultivated in growth medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, 2 mM glutamine, penicillin (100 U/ml)/streptomycin (100 mg/ml), 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 20 ng/ml recombinant M-CSF for 5 d [ 21 ]. MAVS -/- cells were stored in our laboratory. All the cells were cultured and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO 2 . A/chicken/Hong Kong/G9/1997 (H9N2) and A/Puerto Rico/8/34 (H1N1, PR8) were kindly provided by Prof. Wentao Yang (Jilin Agricultural University, China). Sendai virus (SeV), recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus expressing green fluorescent protein (VSV-GFP), and herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) were stored in our laboratory [ 22 ]. The plasmids for Flag-tagged RIG-I-N, MAVS, TRAF3, cGAS, STING, TBK1, IRF3, IRF3-5D, TRIF, ubiquitin (Flag-K48), HA-tagged MDA5-N, Myc-tagged ubiquitin (Myc-WT, Myc-K48, and Myc-K63), MAVS mutants (WT, K136R, K297R, and K362R), IFN-β-Luc, ISRE-Luc, and pRL-TK (internal control luciferase reporter plasmids) used in the study were described previously [ 10 ]. Plasmids for HA-tagged ASB3, ASB3 (ΔANK), and ASB3 (ΔSOCX) were kindly provided by Prof. Dongqin Yang (Fudan University, Shanghai, China).

Viral infections

The cells were grown to approximately 70% confluence and infected with H9N2, H1N1, SeV, VSV-GFP, or HSV-1. The cells were exposed to either virus dilutions supplemented with or without TPCK trypsin, incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO 2 for 2 h, and the supernatants were then removed. The cell monolayers were rinsed with PBS to remove unattached virus particles and then incubated in a virus maintenance medium (2% FBS) at 37 °C and 5% CO 2 for the designated time. For in vivo infection, 3-week-old ASB3 +/+ and ASB3 -/- mice were randomly divided into healthy and infected groups and observed for 1 week. Four-week-old female ASB3 +/+ and ASB3 -/- mice were intranasally inoculated with H9N2 (1 × 10 6 EID 50 /mouse) or H1N1 (1 × 10 5 EID 50 /mouse). The investigators were not blinded to the group allocation. The animals were observed daily for mortality, and their body weights were measured up to 14 d post-infection. On d 3 and 5, five mice from each group infected with H9N2, H1N1, or mock-infected with PBS were euthanized, and lung tissues were collected from each mouse for virus titration, immunoblotting, histochemical staining, and qPCR.

Antibodies and reagents

The following antibodies were used in this study: HRP-conjugated anti-HA (12013819001) and Myc (11814150001) antibodies (Roche); phosphorylated IκBα (AF5851), IκBα (AG2737), and HRP-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (A0216); HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (A0208) antibodies (Beyotime); FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (K1201); Cy5-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (K1212) secondary antibodies (APExBIO); anti-HA (66006-2-Ig), anti-DYKDDDDK (20543-1-AP), anti-β-actin (66009-1-Ig), and anti-TRAF3 (18099-1-AP) antibodies (Proteintech); anti-TRAF3 (18099-1-AP) antibodies (E8Z7M), anti-ubiquitin (K48) (8081) and HRP-conjugated mouse anti-rabbit IgG (Conformation Specific) (5127) antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology); and anti-ASB) and anti-ASB- The following agents were used in this study: 3-methyladenine (M9281), MG132 (M7449), DMSO (D2650), NH 4 Cl (09718), anti-Myc agarose affinity beads (A7470) and protein A/G agarose affinity beads (P6486/E3403) (Sigma); mouse recombinant M-CSF (315-02) (PeproTech); poly(I:C) (tlrl-picwlv) (InvivoGen); DAPI (C1005); ZVAD (caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK, C1202); NP-40 (ST366); and HEPES solution (C0215) (Beyotime). The TnT® T7/SP6 Quick Coupled Transcription/Translation System (L1170/L2080) (Promega) was used.

RNA-seq analysis

Lung tissues collected from ASB3 +/+ and ASB3 -/- mice infected with H9N2 or H1N1 were used for RNA-seq analysis. The whole sequencing process was carried out by Suzhou GENEWIZ Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). One microgram of total RNA was used for subsequent library preparation. Poly(A) mRNA isolation was performed via oligo(dT) beads. mRNA fragmentation was performed using divalent cations and high temperatures. Priming was performed via random primers. First-strand cDNA and second-strand cDNA were synthesized. The purified double-stranded cDNA was then treated to repair both ends, and dA-tailing was added in one reaction, followed by T-A ligation to add adaptors to both ends. Size selection of adaptor-ligated DNA was then performed via DNA clean beads. Each sample was then amplified via PCR via the P5 and P7 primers, and the PCR products were validated. Libraries with different indices were then multiplexed and loaded on an Illumina HiSeq/Illumina NovaSeq/MGI2000 instrument for sequencing via a 2 × 150 paired-end (PE) configuration according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA sequence of the lung tissues has been deposited on the Sequence Read Archive website with the BioProject accession number PRJNA1138650 (Temporary Submission ID: SUB14408937).

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and immunoblotting analysis

HEK293T cells at 80–90% confluency were washed with PBS three times and collected into tubes using a cell scraper. The same mass of lung tissue was washed three times with precooled PBS and mechanically homogenized with RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher) containing 1% PMSF. All samples were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, and 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with a Halt protease inhibitor cocktail and kept on ice for 30 min. After 30 min of incubation, the lysates were spun down at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 20 min. All supernatants were collected, and protein was quantified via a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime). The supernatants were pretreated with 30 µl of anti-Flag agarose affinity gels or protein A/G at 4 °C for 2 h. The indicated primary antibody or IgG control (mouse IgG, Beyotime) was then added to the pretreated lysates and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The IP complexes and whole-cell lysates were subjected to protein transfer via the conventional Western blotting method, and protein development was detected via the antibodies mentioned above. The immunoblots were observed via an Amey Imager 600RGB and quantified via ImageJ.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

Plasmids encoding Flag-tagged RIG-I-N, TRIF, MAVS, cGAS, STING, TBK1, and IRF3-5D or HA-tagged ASB3 were cotransfected with IFN-β-Luc, ISRE-Luc, and pRL-TK into HEK293T cells for 24 h via Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) reagent. Cell samples were collected at the indicated times and lysed or assayed via the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Gene (DLR™) Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, the relevant reporter gene activity was assayed with firefly luciferase and Renilla luciferase reagents.

Quantitative real-time RT‒PCR (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the transfected or infected cells via TRIzol (Thermo Fisher). Two micrograms of RNA were used to synthesize first-strand cDNA from Moloney mouse leukemia virus (M-MLV) (Promega). The amplification of specific PCR products was detected via SYBR Premix Ex TaqTM II (Vazyme). Real-time PCR was conducted via the Applied Biosystems 7500 real-time PCR System. The results are displayed as relative expression values normalized to that of GAPDH. Primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table .

Histology

The mice were euthanized with CO 2 from compressed gas cylinders. The lungs of ASB3 +/+ and ASB3 -/- mice infected or not infected with H9N2 (1 × 10 6 EID 50 /mouse) or H1N1 (1 × 10 5 EID 50 /mouse) on d 3 or d 5 were dissected ( n = 3), fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution overnight, embedded in paraffin, sectioned (thickness, 3 μm), stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) solution, and then examined by microscopy (Leica) for histological changes.

Confocal microscopy

HEK293T or A549 cells were collected at the indicated times. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized and blocked with 0.5% Triton X-100 and 5% skim milk for 1 h. The cells were then stained with specific primary antibodies and blotted with fluorescence-coupled secondary antibodies. The cell nuclei were labeled with DAPI. The stained cells were observed and photographed with a Zeiss microscope (LSM 710).

Virus titration

Viral titers of virus-containing culture supernatants were determined by end-point titration. For tissue infection samples, each sample was serially diluted 10-fold, and for cell infection samples, each sample was serially diluted 2-fold. The diluted samples were inoculated into MDCK cells. The supernatants were collected 48 h after inoculation and assayed for chicken erythrocyte agglutination ability as an indicator of a virus. Replicated infectious virus titers are reported as log10 TCID 50 /ml or log2 TCID 50 /ml and were calculated from three replicates via Reed–Muench’s method.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means ± SDs unless otherwise indicated. All normally distributed samples were analyzed via GraphPad Prism 8 software. The data were analyzed via unpaired two-tailed Student’s t -test or two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s test to correct for multiple comparisons. Probability (p) values of < 0.05 were considered significant: * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001, and **** P < 0.0001; n.s., not significant.

Results

ASB3 expression may depend on IAV infection

To confirm the key role of ASB3 in viral infection, A549 cells were infected with Sendai virus (SeV) or herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) or stimulated with poly(I:C) (an analog of viral dsRNA) and 2’3’-cGAMP (a ligand of STING) for 0, 6, 12, or 18 h, and the results revealed that the protein levels of ASB3 were significantly elevated in A549 cells infected with SeV or stimulated with poly(I:C) but not with HSV-1 and 2’3’-cGAMP compared with those in untreated cells (Fig. 1A, B ). Given the phenomenon of ASB3 upregulation in RNA virus infection, we next addressed the role of ASB3 in response to IAV, a negative-sense RNA virus with a segmented genome. Here, we choose two representative strains for infection: A/chicken/Hong Kong/G9/1997 (H9N2) and A/Puerto Rico/8/34 (H1N1, PR8). Similarly, ASB3 remained highly expressed in A549 cells infected with H9N2 or H1N1 (Fig. 1C, D ). Moreover, we observed ASB3 localization in infected cells via confocal microscopy. The fluorescence signals of the IAV NP and ASB3 proteins were significantly increased after H9N2 or PR8 infection (Fig. 1E, F ). Our findings indicate that ASB3 could be induced by IAV infection.

Fig. 1. ASB3 expression induced by IAV infection.

ASB3 negatively regulates viral RNA-triggered signaling

We noted the elevated expression of ASB3 during IAV infection, suggesting that ASB3 might perform a pivotal function in the host’s innate defence against viral infections (Fig. 1 ). To confirm this, HEK293T cells were transfected with IFN-β-Luc and ISRE-Luc along with HA-ASB3 expression plasmids and infected with SeV or stimulated with poly(I:C). The results revealed that the overexpression of ASB3 inhibited SeV- and poly(I:C)-induced (but not cGAS- and STING-induced) IFN-β and ISRE promoter activity in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2A ). Like its ability to suppress RIG-I-mediated reporter gene activity, ASB3 may also be involved in inhibiting TLR-dependent antiviral responses to some extent (Fig. 2B ). Immunoblot analyses also revealed that ASB3 promoted SeV replication (Supplementary Fig. A and B ). To further investigate the influence of the ASB3 protein on the host antiviral response, we assessed the gene transcription levels of IFN-β and its downstream IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) or the phosphorylation levels of signaling molecules in SeV- or IAV-infected cells. We found that the overexpression of ASB3 significantly inhibited the gene transcription levels of IFNB1, ISG15, IFIT2, and OASL (Fig. 2C ). Moreover, ASB3 inhibited not only the mRNA expression levels of IFNB1, CXCL10, IL-6, and TNF-α but also the phosphorylation levels of IRF3, IκBα, and p65 induced by H9N2 and H1N1 infection, which indicated further impairment of the antiviral response (Fig. 2D–G ). In addition, infection with the recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus expressing green fluorescent protein (VSV-GFP) was significantly promoted in HEK293T cells transfected with increasing amounts of exogenous ASB3 plasmids (Fig. 2H ). Indeed, the attenuation of the innate immune response mediated by ASB3 coincided with an increase in IAV replication (Fig. 2I, J ). These results indicate that ASB3 is an important negative regulator of the viral RNA-mediated antiviral innate immune response.

Fig. 2. ASB3 negatively regulates type I IFN production.

ASB3 deficiency enhances cellular antiviral responses

To determine whether endogenous ASB3 is equally capable of negatively regulating the IFN-I response, we performed RNA sequencing analysis and found that ASB3 deficiency adapts to IAV infection through the differential expression of some ISGs in murine lungs infected with H9N2 and H1N1 (Fig. 3A, B ). To further validate the results above, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and primary kidney epithelial cells isolated from ASB3 +/+ mice and ASB3 -/- mice were infected with SeV or H9N2 for 12 h. As expected, ASB3 deficiency significantly promoted SeV-induced gene transcription of Ifnb1, Isg56, Cxcl10, Ccl5, IL-6, and TNFα in BMDMs (Supplementary Fig. C ). Compared with primary kidney epithelial cells from ASB3 +/+ mice, primary kidney epithelial cells from ASB3 -/- mice infected with SeV presented higher gene transcription levels of Ifnb1, Isg15, Isg56, Cxcl10, Mx1, and Oasl1 (Supplementary Fig. D ). Similarly, we also detected increased gene transcript levels of Ifnb1, Isg15, Isg56, Cxcl10, Ccl5, and Mx1 in ASB3-deficient primary kidney epithelial cells and BMDMs infected with H9N2 (Fig. 3C, E ). Importantly, ASB3 deficiency increased the phosphorylation of IRF3, IκBα, and p65 induced by H9N2 infection (Fig. 3D, F ). Collectively, these results suggest that ASB3 deletion enhances IAV-induced IFN-I responses.

Fig. 3. ASB3 deficiency enhances the type I IFN response.

ASB3 specifically interacts with MAVS

To identify the regulatory role of ASB3 in the IAV-triggered RLR-IFN-β signaling axis, we transfected the RIG-I-N, MDA5-N, MAVS, or IRF3-5D plasmids together with the IFN-β or ISRE promoter in the presence or absence of ASB3. We observed that the overexpression of ASB3 inhibited RIG-I-N-, MDA5-N-, and MAVS-induced IFN-β or ISRE promoter activity in a dose-dependent manner but not TBK1- or IRF3-5D-induced IFN-β or ISRE promoter activity (Fig. 4A, B ). Since inhibition occurs upstream of TBK1, we speculated that ASB3 is strongly associated with MAVS. Further coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments revealed that ASB3 interacted specifically with MAVS but not with TRAF3 (Fig. 4C, D ). Confocal microscopy confirmed that ASB3 and MAVS (but not TRAF3) colocalized in the cytoplasm (Fig. 4E ). cGAS and STING serve as critical receptors and adaptor proteins in the immune response against DNA viruses. Interestingly, our findings indicate that ASB3 does not interact with either cGAS or STING (Fig. 4F ). In vitro transcription-translation assays also revealed that ASB3 directly interacted with MAVS (Fig. 4G ). Additionally, semi-endogenous and endogenous IP experiments demonstrated an increased interaction between ASB3 and MAVS during infection with the H9N2 or H1N1 virus (Fig. 4H, I ). Domain mapping experiments indicated that ASB3 interacted with MAVS through its ANK domain (Fig. 4J ). In summary, these findings indicate that MAVS represents a specific target of ASB3, further highlighting the distinct role of ASB3 in anti-RNA viral mechanisms.

Fig. 4. ASB3 specifically interacts with MAVS.

ASB3 mediates the degradation of MAVS via K48-linked polyubiquitination

Although prior studies have not revealed that ASB3 directly regulates MAVS protein stability (Fig. 4A, C ), we next sought to determine whether ASB3 affects MAVS expression. We cotransfected HA-tagged ASB3 with Flag-tagged MAVS, TBK1, or IRF3 and performed immunoblot analysis. We observed that ASB3 inhibited the protein expression of MAVS but not that of TBK1 or IRF3 (Fig. 5A ). Moreover, increasing amounts of ASB3 markedly decreased the protein level of endogenous MAVS (Fig. 5B ). To further investigate the mechanisms responsible for the degradation of MAVS by ASB3, we treated HEK293T cells with inhibitors of various protein degradation pathways. We found that ASB3-mediated MAVS degradation could be mostly restored by treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132. However, the autophagy inhibitors 3-methylamine (3-MA), the lysosome inhibitor ammonium chloride (NH 4 Cl), and the caspase inhibitor ZVAD were not effective (Fig. 5C ). Protein ubiquitination is a key step in the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway. Different immunoblot analyses of K48-linked or K63-linked ubiquitin revealed that ASB3 induced the polyubiquitination of MAVS via K48-mediated linkages but not K63-mediated linkages (Fig. 5D ). The overexpression of ASB3 markedly promoted the K48-linked polyubiquitination of MAVS with or without MG132 treatment (Fig. 5E–G ). To confirm these results, we used ASB3 truncations in subsequent experiments. We observed a significant reduction in MAVS polyubiquitination when the SOCX and ANK domains of ASB3 were deleted (Fig. 5H, I ). Moreover, overexpression of these truncations of ASB3 suggested that ANK and SOCX do not strongly suppress the activation of the IFN-β luciferase reporter and IFNB1 triggered by MAVS, SeV, H9N2, and H1N1. (Fig. 5J, K ). Overall, these data suggest that ASB3 promotes K48-linked polyubiquitination and degradation of MAVS via the proteasome pathway.

Fig. 5. ASB3 potentiates K48-linked polyubiquitination and degradation of MAVS.

The Lys297 residue of MAVS is critical for ASB3-mediated degradation

To further understand the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying the function of ASB3 in the degradation of MAVS, we mutated a series of lysine (K) residues in MAVS and examined their effects on ASB3-mediated MAVS degradation. We found that only the mutation of lysine 297 to arginine (K297R) abolished the ASB3-mediated degradation of MAVS (Fig. 6A ). Next, we supplemented various MAVS mutants (K136R, K297R, and K362R) into MAVS -/- HEK293T cells for further validation. The results from the reporter assays revealed that among the three loci in close proximity, MAVS K297R restored ASB3-mediated degradation, which again induced activation of the IFN-β promoter (Fig. 6B ). Similarly, the level of MAVS K297R-mediated phosphorylation of IRF3 and IκBα was not decreased (Fig. 6C ). Infection experiments revealed that transient overexpression of ASB3 promoted H9N2 or H1N1 replication in MAVS-WT (but not K297R) MAVS -/- HEK293T cells (Fig. 6D, E ). To further analyze the ubiquitination of MAVS at the K297 residue, MAVS-WT, MAVS-K136R, or MAVS-K362R were strongly ubiquitinated when Flag-tagged K48 ubiquitin was expressed or not expressed, whereas the ubiquitination of MAVS-K297R was reduced (Fig. 6F–H ). These data indicate that the Lys297 residue of MAVS is critical for ASB3-mediated degradation during H9N2 or H1N1 infection.

Fig. 6. ASB3-mediated MAVS degradation at residue K297.

ASB3 deficiency protects against H9N2 and H1N1 infection in mice

To examine the role of ASB3 in influenza virus infection in vivo, ASB3 +/+ and ASB3 -/- mice were infected with H9N2 and H1N1. Compared with ASB3 +/+ mice, ASB3 -/- mice presented a lower rate of body weight loss and a greater survival rate, suggesting that ASB3 deficiency is more resistant to H9N2 and H1N1 infection (Fig. 7A, B ) (Fig. 7D, E ). Furthermore, H9N2 or H1N1 infection induced more severe histopathological changes in the lungs of the ASB3 +/+ mice (Fig. 7C, F ). We also observed increased inflammatory cell infiltration in the lungs of ASB3 +/+ mice, particularly at 5 d of infection (Fig. 7G–I ). We also noted a significant reduction in H9N2 and H1N1 titers in the lungs of ASB3 -/- mice infected with H9N2 or H1N1 on d 5 (Fig. 7J, K ). These results demonstrated that ASB3 deficiency significantly restricts the virulence of H9N2 and H1N1 in mice and mitigates the pathological injury caused by viral infection.

Fig. 7. Loss of ASB3 enhances in vivo antiviral immunity.

ASB3 negatively regulates in vivo antiviral responses by degrading MAVS

To further clarify the role of ASB3 in inhibiting IFN-I production in vivo, we performed phosphorylation analyses on lung tissues from ASB3 +/+ and ASB3 -/- mice infected with H9N2 or H1N1. We found that ASB3 deficiency increased the phosphorylation levels of TBK1, IRF3, and IκBα in the lungs. Moreover, ASB3 exacerbated the degradation of MAVS during infection. In contrast, the MAVS expression level in the lungs of ASB3 -/- mice returned to normal on d 5. Notably, lower expression levels of NP were detected in ASB3-deficient lung tissues (Fig. 8A, B ). The same results were observed via confocal laser microscopy imaging (Fig. 8C ). qPCR analysis demonstrated that ASB3 deficiency also increased the gene transcription levels of IFN-β1 and ISG15 in the lungs (Fig. 8D, E ). These data indicate that ASB3 deletion effectively enhances the IFN-I response during influenza virus infection. To further corroborate the association between ASB3 and MAVS during in vivo infection, we conducted Co-IP and ubiquitination analyses. The results revealed that ASB3 strongly interacts with MAVS after infection with H9N2 or H1N1 (Fig. 8F ). Consistent with these results, ASB3 enhanced the K48-linked polyubiquitination of MAVS in the lungs of mice infected for 5 d, whereas ASB3 deficiency weakened this phenomenon (Fig. 8G ). Our findings suggest that ASB3-promoted degradation of MAVS inhibits the antiviral immune response induced by H9N2 and H1N1 infection.

Fig. 8. ASB3 deficiency eliminates MAVS degradation in the lungs.

Discussion

Following an extended period of coevolution and competition, hosts have evolved a number of potent defence mechanisms against viral infections. However, an increasing number of host suppressors that target the negative regulatory functions of the antiviral immune system are being identified. Although some of the proteins encoded by these viruses also function to restrict IRF3 phosphorylation [ 23 ], our primary interest in this scenario was the host response in the presence of ASB3. In the present study, we demonstrated that ASB3 inhibits antiviral innate immunity by showing that the overexpression of ASB3 impairs SeV- and IAV-induced activation of IFN-I and that ASB3-deficient mice are more resistant to H9N2 and H1N1 infection. Moreover, during H9N2 and H1N1 infection, ASB3 loss increases MAVS expression levels and IRF3 phosphorylation. Interestingly, ASB3 catalyzes the K48-linked polyubiquitination of lysine residues in MAVS, which reduces IFN-I signaling (Fig. 9 ).

Fig. 9. A working model of ASB3 inhibition of antiviral immunity.

Viral nucleic acids are sensed by RIG-I and cGAS, which then trigger the IFN-β signaling cascade and the production of hundreds of ISGs. Our study revealed that ASB3 expression may be increased in response to RNA virus infection (Fig. 1A ). Overexpression of ASB3 inhibited SeV- and poly (I:C)-induced IFN-β production (Fig. 2A ), whereas ASB3 deletion increased IFN-I (Fig. 3 ). ASB3, a member of the ASB family, is abundant in liver tissues [ 24 ]. Our study revealed that ASB3 expression is low in the A549 cell line but high in mouse lung tissue (Supplementary Fig. E ). Numerous malignancies have been linked to the ASB family of proteins, and various tumor types exhibit abnormal expression of ASB3 [ 25 ]. According to one study, ASB3 prevents CRC metastasis by lowering the protein expression of Vimentin [ 26 ]. Notably, recent research has revealed that Vimentin proteins act as negative regulators of antiviral immunity during RNA and DNA viral infections [ 27 ]. In contrast to these findings, our study reveals a novel biological function of ASB3.

Although we observed that ASB3 similarly inhibited TRIF-mediated IFN-β reporter gene activity, the inhibitory effect of ASB3 on SeV-, Poly(I:C)-, RIG-I-N-, MAVS-mediated IFN-β, and ISRE reporter gene activity was more potent than that of other strains (Fig. 2A ). Another study demonstrated that, compared with the knockdown of TLR3 alone, the knockdown of RIG-I alone or both RIG-I and TLR3 further downregulated PR8 infection-induced IRF3 phosphorylation [ 28 ]. Thus, our study suggests that ASB3 may be involved in TLR3-dependent IFN-I responses during IAV infection, but we are focusing more on its pronounced role in RIG-I signaling.

The C-terminal structural domain of MAVS allows it to bind to the outer mitochondrial membrane, where it is located. As a crucial adapter protein for RLR signaling, it plays a pivotal role in mediating signaling pathways [ 29 ]. Upon viral infection, MAVS needs to maintain a delicate balance between activation and inactivation to ensure that the host antiviral immune response is of appropriate intensity. In our study, we confirmed that ASB3 interacts specifically with MAVS (Fig. 4C–H ). Crucially, we also discovered that ASB3 is inactive in relation to TRAF3, cGAS, and STING (Fig. 4D ), which explains the unique response of ASB3 to RNA virus infection. Similarly, another study revealed that by interacting with MAVS, the E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF115 efficiently counteracts excessive antiviral responses [ 30 ]. The overreaction of host proteins is mostly dependent on the strategy of escaping the immunization of viral proteins. Previous studies have shown that the host protein phosphatase PPM1G dephosphorylates MAVS, thereby inhibiting MAVS activation [ 31 ]. Reports are suggesting that MAVS is dispensable for IAV infection in vivo [ 32 ], and we believe that the role of MAVS is more complex. On the one hand, it may trigger a compensatory phenomenon that mitigates the attenuation of the natural immune response caused by MAVS deficiency. On the other hand, as a key mitochondrial protein, MAVS influences the interaction between glucose metabolism and RLR signaling [ 33 ].

Furthermore, we showed that MAVS is degraded by ASB3 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5A ) via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. (Fig. 5B ). As the major route for removing damaged proteins, the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) is essential for preserving intracellular homeostasis. Several emerging classes of host inhibitory factors that target the functions of MAVS have been identified. For example, GP73 promotes MAVS degradation through a proteasome-dependent pathway [ 34 ]. Recent studies have shown that the E3 ligase Smurf1 enhances proteasome-mediated MAVS degradation [ 35 ]. However, TRIM25-mediated degradation of MAVS requires the release of signaling complexes into the cytoplasm, allowing IRF3 to be phosphorylated by TBK1 [ 36 ]. Currently, more reports on TIRM25 favor the activation of RIG-I [ 37 – 39 ]. Spatially, TRIM25-mediated activation of RIG-I occurs before RIG-I leaves TRIM25 to interact with MAVS on mitochondria [ 40 ]. Mechanistically, the main function of TRIM25 is to activate the IFN-I signaling pathway via K63 polyubiquitination of RIG-I [ 41 – 43 ]. Indeed, additional studies on MAVS degradation support the negative regulation of the IFN-I signaling pathway. For example, the E3 ligase Smurf1-mediated degradation of MAVS negatively regulates RIG-I-dependent immune signaling [ 44 ]. Other E3 ubiquitin ligases, such as AIP4, MARCH5, and STUB1, also mediate the degradation of the adaptor protein MAVS, thereby favoring viral replication and attenuating the antiviral immune response. [ 45 – 47 ]. In this study, we have similarly revealed the turnover of MAVS by ASB3, which is consistent with that of the aforementioned MAVS-targeting ubiquitination E3 ligases.

Ubiquitination is a major process influencing the posttranslational modification (PTM) of proteins. We confirmed that ASB3-triggered K48-linked polyubiquitination of MAVS inhibited the activation of IFN-I signaling (Fig. 6C–F ). Mapping revealed that ASB3 subsequently mediated the K48-linked polyubiquitination of MAVS at Lys297. (Fig. 7E–G ). Lys48 (K48)-linked and Lys63 (K63)-linked polyubiquitination are well recognized as the two most common types of polyubiquitination in ubiquitin-mediated protein modification. Certain E3 enzymes are responsible for conferring substrate specificity. Previous research has shown that the E3 ligase MARCH3 can facilitate K48-linked polyubiquitination at IL-3Rα K377, which has the effect of suppressing inflammatory responses that are generated by IL-3 [ 48 ]. Furthermore, it has been reported that the E3 ligase TRIP catalyzes K48-linked polyubiquitination repair of TBK1 at Lys372, promoting autophagy-lysosomal degradation of TBK1 [ 49 ]. According to a recent study, RIG-I-mediated MAVS aggregation is initiated by Riplet, a novel RIG-I-independent MAVS E3 ligase, through the catalysis of unanchored K63-linked polyubiquitin chains [ 50 ]. Naturally, other types of ubiquitination modifications with immunomodulatory functions have been reported. Huang et al. reported that PKR is polyubiquitinated in a K33-linked manner by the E3 ligase HECTD3, with PKR Lys68 serving as the main ubiquitination site for the HECTD3-modified innate response to RNA virus infection [ 51 ].

Importantly, the susceptibility of ASB3 -/- mice to influenza A virus was reduced (Fig. 7 ). We previously reported that ASB3 deletion does not activate or suppress innate immune signaling in the absence of viral infection. However, under certain pathological circumstances, ASB3 is overactivated to exert its immunosuppressive function [ 52 ]. We also observed reduced H9N2 and H1N1 titers in the lungs of ASB3 -/- mice (Fig. 7C, F ), which we attributed to an IFN-I antiviral immune response limited by ASB3-mediated impairment of MAVS function (Fig. 8 ). E3 enzymes can also target viral proteins to achieve limitations or aid in viral reproduction, as multiple investigations have demonstrated. Yuan et al. reported that the E3 enzyme RNF5-mediated K63-linked polyubiquitination at Lys15 of the M protein promoted SARS-CoV-2 replication [ 53 ], and Liu et al. reported that MARCH8 inhibited IAV release by redirecting the viral M2 protein from the plasma membrane to the lysosome for degradation [ 54 ]. More research is needed to determine whether ASB3 aids in the production or release of viral proteins.

In summary, we first proposed that the E3 ligase ASB3, whose expression is upregulated during influenza A virus infection, negatively regulates the innate immune response mediated by RLRs. Our study identified a mechanism by which ASB3 controls antiviral defence. The knowledge gained from these investigations will help develop ASB3 as a possible target for medications that combat influenza viruses.
