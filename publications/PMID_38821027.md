In this review, we explore the regulation of septal peptidoglycan (sPG)
synthesis in bacterial cell division, a critical process for cell viability and
proper morphology. Recent single-molecule imaging studies have revealed the
processive movement of the FtsW:bPBP synthase complex along the septum, shedding
light on the spatiotemporal dynamics of sPG synthases and their regulators. In
diderm bacteria (E. coli and C. crescentus), the movement occurs at two distinct
speeds, reflecting active synthesis or inactivity driven by FtsZ-treadmilling.
In monoderm bacteria (B. subtilis, S. pneumoniae, and S. aureus), however, these
enzymes exhibit only the active sPG-track-coupled processive movement. By
comparing the dynamics of sPG synthases in these organisms and that of class-A
penicillin-binding proteins in vivo and in vitro, we propose a unifying model
for septal cell wall synthesis regulation across species, highlighting the roles
of the sPG- and Z-tracks in orchestrating a robust bacterial cell wall
constriction process.

Bacterial Cell Division

In all walled bacteria, successful cell division relies on robust cell wall constriction. An ensemble of cytoplasmic, membrane, and periplasmic proteins carry out coordinated septal cell wall peptidoglycan (sPG) synthesis and hydrolysis to divide properly without lethal lesions in the cell envelope. The cell wall constriction process results in cell pole morphogenesis and, in many bacterial species, constitutes a major mechanism to determine cell shape through the spatiotemporal control of the cell division apparatus [1].

At the onset of cell division, the bacterial tubulin homolog and GTPase, FtsZ, along with its most conserved membrane anchor and polymerization regulator, FtsA, gather on the inner membrane at the future division site, or septum [2]. There, concentrated FtsZ-FtsA filaments assemble into discontinuous, treadmilling polymers, giving rise to a ring-like structure, termed the Z-ring [3–5] (Fig. 1A), as revealed by various imaging modalities [6,7]. Following and concurrently with the assembly of the Z-ring, a repertoire of cell wall proteins is recruited, leading to new septal peptidoglycan synthesis [8,9]. The essential lipid II (PG precursor substrate) flippase, MurJ [10], translocates lipid II from the cytoplasmic face of the inner membrane to the periplasmic face. Monofunctional SEDS (Shape Elongation Division Sporulation) proteins such as FtsW or bifunctional class A Penicillin Binding Proteins (aPBPs) [11] polymerize lipid II into peptidoglycan strands via glycosyltransferase (GT) reactions. Monofunctional class B PBPs (bPBPs) such as bPBP3 (FtsI) in E. coli or aPBPs crosslink the stem peptides of nascent glycan chains to the pre-existing cell wall via transpeptidation (TP). Biochemical studies show that SEDS proteins complex with bPBPs to carry out GT and TP reactions, while aPBPs can carry out both functions [11]. FtsW:bPBP complexes are regulated by FtsQBL and FtsN in diderm bacteria (Gram-negative) or the homologous complex DivIB/DivIC/FtsL in monoderm bacteria (Gram-positive) [12,13].

Genetics and bulk biochemistry have revealed the identities and functions of many key players of cell division. However, how these proteins and complexes are regulated in cells, a multifaceted and dynamically changing environment, remains elusive. Single-molecule fluorescence imaging studies in live bacterial cells are just starting to provide a wealth of information on the spatiotemporal regulations of cell wall synthesis, linking protein dynamics to activities. The first revelation came when FtsZ treadmilling was discovered [3,5,14] (Fig. 1B, C, left). Treadmilling occurs by continuous polymerization at the leading (+) end and depolymerization at the depleting (−) end of FtsZ filaments, driven by FtsZ’s GTPase activity. As such, an FtsZ polymer visualized by ensemble imaging appears to move directionally at ~30 nm/s, whereas single FtsZ molecules in the polymer are stationary with average lifetimes between 8–14 sec [15–18]. Today, FtsZ-treadmilling is found in all bacteria species examined to date and is generally accepted for its role in properly assembling and positioning the divisome apparatus. At the same time, sPG synthesis is considered the key driver of cell division [19–21].

The next revelation is the observation that FtsW:bPBP complexes move processively in a directed fashion along the septum at various speeds from 8 nm/s to 30 nm/s on average [3,5,16,22,23] (Fig. 1B, C, right). In diderm bacteria like E. coli and C. crescentus, the populations of FtsW:bPBP complexes that move relatively slowly in the range of 8 – 15 nm/s are active in sPG synthesis, and their speed indicates their sPG polymerization rate [22,24], while the populations that move relatively fast in the range of 20–30 nm/s are inactive and driven by FtsZ’s treadmilling dynamics. These two modes of movement of sPG synthases were termed “two-tracks”, a Z-track and an sPG-track, where the fast movement driven by Z-track’s treadmilling is mutually exclusive with the slow movement driven by autonomous sPG synthesis activity, and these enzymes and proteins can dynamically transition between the two tracks. In monoderm bacteria such as B. subtilis, S. pneumoniae, and S. aureus, however, only the slow-moving, sPG synthesis-driven populations of FtsW:bPBP complexes were observed, and they are mostly independent of FtsZ’s treadmilling dynamics [16,23,25]. Thus, it appears as a general scene that cell wall enzymes and “things that go” [26] need to “stay on track” to fulfill their functions in cell division and morphogenesis.

In this review, we delve into how single molecule tracking (SMT) studies have revealed the regulatory dynamics of cell division proteins and septal peptidoglycan synthesis, which shed light on in vivo sPG synthesis rates and processivity, protein-protein interactions, and metabolic inputs important for this essential cellular process to take shape. We also summarize efforts to reconstitute divisome dynamics in vitro. Importantly, we compare how different model organisms integrate these features to carry out the dynamic activities of peptidoglycan synthesis, cell division, and cell shape/pole morphogenesis.

Core Divisome Dynamics in Diderms (Gram-negative)Escherichia coliIn E. coli, single-molecule tracking has revealed that the sPG polymerase FtsW and TPase FtsI move directionally in two populations (Fig. 2A). The first population is driven by FtsZ’s treadmilling dynamics, where these proteins move fast at about 30 nm/s on average, matching the speed of FtsZ and FtsA ensemble filament bundles [5,27] (Fig. 2A, left). Mathematical modeling combined with tracking of FtsI single molecule dynamics in vertically oriented cells suggests sPG synthase complexes continually track the depleting end of the FtsZ filament through a Brownian ratcheting mechanism to follow the direction of the treadmilling Z-track [28]. These fast-moving FtsW and FtsI molecules are not engaged in sPG synthesis, as altering sPG synthesis activity does not change the speed of this population. The second population of FtsI and FtsW move relatively slower, around an average speed of 8 nm/s (Fig. 2A, right). These two motion modes constitute the “two-track” model because their speeds indicate their presence on the Z-track (movement driven by FtsZ-treadmilling) or the sPG-track (movement driven by sPG synthesis). In addition to the two tracks, a third stationary state exists, primarily reflecting a complex not engaged in synthesis, either poised for synthesis initiation or stuck in the middle of FtsA-Z polymers (Fig. 2A, middle). For cells grown in minimal M9 media, the fast, slow, and stationary populations are roughly ~ 40:20:40 in fractions (Fig. 2F). As FtsW and FtsI move in nearly identical manners on both tracks under various conditions, they most likely form an FtsWI sPG synthase complex and act as one unit together.What drives the processive movement of FtsWI on the sPG track? FtsI’s TPase activity appears to be the primary driver; FtsW’s GTase activity is necessary but insufficient. Specifically inhibiting FtsW’s GTase activity reduced, but not completely abolished, the presence of the slow-moving population of FtsW (22% fast, 16% slow, 62% stationary), while specifically inhibiting FtsI’s TPase activity using Aztreonam abolished all slow-moving FtsW molecules) [24] (9% fast, 2% slow, 89% stationary, Fig. 2F). These observations suggest that sPG glycan polymerization is insufficient to power the processive movement of FtsW, as FtsW is still active when FtsI is inhibited [12]. They also suggest an intriguing possibility that FtsI may be able to crosslink PG strands provided by aPBP GTases activities into the existing cell wall when FtsW is inactivated. Note that so far, SMT has not revealed a processively moving population of septal aPBPs in model rod-shaped bacteria, indicating that aPBPs are not in the same sPG synthase complex with FtsW:bPBP in these species under the conditions tested. Consistent with these suggestions, the slow-moving population of FtsW is completely abolished under the condition that all septal GTase activities from aPBPs, FtsW, and a monofunctional GTase MGT, are removed (~19% fast, ~ 0% slow, ~ 81% stationary), or PG precursors are depleted (~18% fast, ~2% slow, ~80% stationary) by inhibiting MurA, the enzyme catalyzing the first committed step of PG synthesis, using Fosfomycin (Fig. 2F).Most importantly, the slow-moving FtsWI population and its associated moving speed also respond to enhanced sPG synthesis activity. Superfission variants that are known to promote cell division or bypass the requirement of essential components, such as FtsN, increased the percentage of the slow-moving population of FtsWI, suggesting that active complex formation and its stability play a role in maintaining FtsWI’s enzymatic activities in cells (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, tracking of superfission mutant background under a variety of growth conditions, from slow growth in minimal media to rapid growth conditions with overexpression of UppS that increases lipid II levels, results in not only diminished fast-moving population of FtsW (~5% fast, ~51% slow, ~44% stationary), but also correspondingly increased speeds of the slow-moving population of FtsW (from 8 nm/s to 13 nm/s) and cell wall constriction rates (Fig. 2G). Thus, the moving speeds of active FtsWI are sensors for the robust metabolic flux that governs cellular growth and cell wall synthesis rates.Besides FtsWI, FtsB, a highly conserved scaffold protein and an essential component of the FtsQLB subcomplex in the divisome, also exhibited two moving populations on both Z- and sPG-tracks. The two populations can be similarly modulated by sPG synthesis activity. FtsB shifts to the slow (active) population in the superfission mutant background (FtsIR167S) and to the fast (inactive) population when lipid II levels are limited [29]. These observations suggest that FtsQLB may be in the same sPG synthase complex with FtsWI and transition on both tracks, consistent with a previous biochemical study in which the five proteins can be co-purified as one complex from P. aeruginosa [12,30]. Importantly, as FtsQLB are in the same inactive and active complex with FtsWI on both tracks, they likely function as a scaffold to support a stable conformation of FtsI for its activation on the sPG track, as confirmed by recent Cryo-EM and structural modeling studies [29,31–33].How are FtsWI activated on the sPG track? SMT of FtsN, the last protein recruited to the division site, revealed only one slow-moving population and a stationary population (~45% slow and ~ 55% stationary) [34]. Most importantly, under the same superfission and rapid growth condition with the overexpression of UppS, FtsN molecules increased their moving speed from ~ 9 nm/s to ~ 13 nm/s, similar to what was observed on FtsW [34]. These observations suggest that the binding of FtsWIQLB to FtsN activates FtsWI and that FtsN remains in the moving complex to maintain its processivity on the sPG track. This suggestion is consistent with the long-regarded role of FtsN as the septum constriction activator [35], but the underlying molecular mechanism is unknown. A recent work using structure prediction and molecular dynamics simulation showed that the essential domain of FtsN, a largely disordered peptide of ~ 50 aa long, acts like a shoelace to tie FtsI and FtsL together at the hub region, which leads to the shift of FtsI’s anchor domain away from blocking the catalytic pore of FtsW [29]. In C. crescentus, FzlA acts upstream of FtsI, where FtsW and FtsB are the last proteins to localize at the midcell before constriction [37]. While the speed of FtsZ’s treadmilling is challenging to determine precisely in C. crescentus cells due to their small cell sizes, the lifetime of single, stationary FtsZ molecules was at approximately ~12 s, in line with that of FtsZ from other species [16,17] and hence suggesting that FtsZ’s treadmilling speed in C. crescentus is consistent with different bacterial species (~30 nm/s). Consistent with these suggestions, the slow-moving population of FtsW is completely abolished under the condition that all septal GTase activities from aPBPs, FtsW, and a monofunctional GTase MGT, are removed (~19% fast, ~ 0% slow, ~ 81% stationary), or PG precursors are depleted (~18% fast, ~2% slow, ~80% stationary) by inhibiting MurA, the enzyme catalyzing the first committed step of PG synthesis, using Fosfomycin (Fig. 2F).

Most importantly, the slow-moving FtsWI population and its associated moving speed also respond to enhanced sPG synthesis activity. Superfission variants that are known to promote cell division or bypass the requirement of essential components, such as FtsN, increased the percentage of the slow-moving population of FtsWI, suggesting that active complex formation and its stability play a role in maintaining FtsWI’s enzymatic activities in cells (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, tracking of superfission mutant background under a variety of growth conditions, from slow growth in minimal media to rapid growth conditions with overexpression of UppS that increases lipid II levels, results in not only diminished fast-moving population of FtsW (~5% fast, ~51% slow, ~44% stationary), but also correspondingly increased speeds of the slow-moving population of FtsW (from 8 nm/s to 13 nm/s) and cell wall constriction rates (Fig. 2G). Thus, the moving speeds of active FtsWI are sensors for the robust metabolic flux that governs cellular growth and cell wall synthesis rates.

Besides FtsWI, FtsB, a highly conserved scaffold protein and an essential component of the FtsQLB subcomplex in the divisome, also exhibited two moving populations on both Z- and sPG-tracks. The two populations can be similarly modulated by sPG synthesis activity. FtsB shifts to the slow (active) population in the superfission mutant background (FtsIR167S) and to the fast (inactive) population when lipid II levels are limited [29]. These observations suggest that FtsQLB may be in the same sPG synthase complex with FtsWI and transition on both tracks, consistent with a previous biochemical study in which the five proteins can be co-purified as one complex from P. aeruginosa [12,30]. Importantly, as FtsQLB are in the same inactive and active complex with FtsWI on both tracks, they likely function as a scaffold to support a stable conformation of FtsI for its activation on the sPG track, as confirmed by recent Cryo-EM and structural modeling studies [29,31–33].

How are FtsWI activated on the sPG track? SMT of FtsN, the last protein recruited to the division site, revealed only one slow-moving population and a stationary population (~45% slow and ~ 55% stationary) [34]. Most importantly, under the same superfission and rapid growth condition with the overexpression of UppS, FtsN molecules increased their moving speed from ~ 9 nm/s to ~ 13 nm/s, similar to what was observed on FtsW [34]. These observations suggest that the binding of FtsWIQLB to FtsN activates FtsWI and that FtsN remains in the moving complex to maintain its processivity on the sPG track. This suggestion is consistent with the long-regarded role of FtsN as the septum constriction activator [35], but the underlying molecular mechanism is unknown. A recent work using structure prediction and molecular dynamics simulation showed that the essential domain of FtsN, a largely disordered peptide of ~ 50 aa long, acts like a shoelace to tie FtsI and FtsL together at the hub region, which leads to the shift of FtsI’s anchor domain away from blocking the catalytic pore of FtsW [29]. This conformational change may allow nascent sPG strands to emerge from the pore to reach up to be crosslinked by FtsI’s TPase domain, hence constituting an activation mechanism of FtsWI by FtsN.

These observations demonstrate that the processive movement of FtsW:bPBP1 and DivIB complex is intimately linked to the cell division and sPG synthesis process, whereas FtsZ treadmilling plays a less critical role. In other words, the growth rate and cell division rate are proportional to the speeds of the complex but not FtsZ treadmilling.

Class A aPBPsWork in B. subtilis and E. coli has shown that class A aPBP molecules actively synthesizing PG stay stationary but do not move processively as FtsW:bPBP proteins (Fig. 3A). This conclusion was supported by the observations that lower doses of aPBP induction led to more stationary molecules. In contrast, overexpression of aPBP led to more diffusive molecules, likely indicating the saturation of available synthesizing sites [41,42]. In E. coli, the stationary population can be increased by inhibiting lipid II synthesis using D-cycloserine, which presumably increases lesion sites in the cell wall for aPBP1b to bind and repair [42]. Why active aPBPs remain stationary is unclear, but in E. coli, their membrane lipoprotein activators LpoA/B may help “docking” cognate aPBP1a/b into stationary complexes [42,43]. These outer membrane-anchored activator molecules can only reach inner membrane-anchored aPBP molecules through cell wall lesions to activate them. Hence, if an aPBP molecule moves away, it will disengage with its activator and become inactivated. However, in E. coli, the GTase-constitutively active variant aPBP1bE313D still remained stationary even though it does not require LpoB anymore. Furthermore, B. subtilis does not have outer membrane-anchored activators, suggesting other factors may be at play. One potential feature regulating spatiotemporal dynamics of aPBPs are the intrinsically disordered protein region (IDR) at the C-terminus of monoderm aPBPs, which may extend through holes in the cell wall [44]. Additionally, FtsN has been reported to interact with and regulate aPBP1b [31,45]. FtsN has a large stationary populations [34], which could potentially contribute to the observed stationary behavior of aPBP1b through their binding.A final twist to the behavior of aPBPs came from work in S. pneumoniae, where a subset (21%) of aPBP1a molecules at the midcell moved processively, with the remaining molecules staying stationary (Fig. 3B). Mutating the TPase activity of aPBP1a abolished the processive movement [46]. What dictates processive motions of FtsW:SEDS complexes versus mostly stationary motions of aPBPs remains to be determined. Differences between species may begin to shed light on the requirements for processive motions of these complexes -- be it something intrinsic to protein structural dynamics and coordination between domains or interactions with existing PG structures that allows for “threading” of the cell wall—these questions remain challenging to address. Divisome Dynamics in vitro:Single-molecule imaging in vitro using reconstituted assembly of the E. coli division apparatus components has been instrumental in discovering the treadmilling behavior of FtsZ. Using supported lipid bilayers (SLB) as a two-dimensional surface, FtsAZ filaments from E. coli were shown to treadmill [14]. Interestingly, while FtsW, FtsI, FtsB, and a truncated FtsN variant move directionally and processively by end-tracking treadmilling FtsZ polymers in cells through the Brownian-ratchet mechanism [24,28,29], these proteins in the reconstituted system followed treadmilling FtsZ polymers by a diffusion-and-capture mechanism, and no directional motion at the single-molecule level was observed [48]. Diffusing molecules in the lipid bilayer are captured by FtsAZ filaments and diffuse away when the depolymerizing end of the FtsAZ filament approaches. As such, these proteins follow the directional movement of treadmilling FtsAZ filaments in ensemble imaging without persistent end-tracking, as observed in vivo. The lack of directional movement of these proteins in vitro could be due to their fast diffusion speeds in the SLB because the assay can only use the cytoplasmic tails anchored by His-tags on the SLB. It may also indicate that additional cellular inputs are required to relay FtsAZ’s treadmilling to sPG synthase. For example, FtsA has been shown to interact with FtsW to activate its GTase synthesis activity [49]. Therefore, it is tempting to speculate FtsW as a candidate linking treadmilling filaments and processive movement of FtsI and FtsB on the Z-track in the SLB system. While SLB experiments have not revealed a link between FtsAZ treadmilling and processive movements of the FtsWIQLB complex, studying a gain-of-function FtsA mutant, FtsA*, has shown an increased propensity to recruit the cytoplasmic tails of FtsN [50]. This observation aligns well with the superfission phenotype of FtsA* as increased FtsN levels at the septum would activate FtsWIQLB at higher levels [29]. However, this mechanism of FtsA* does not fully uncover how such a mutation will help to partially bypass the loss of FtsN in cells, which requires further examination [51]. Until recently, in vitro PG synthesis reactions were limited to bulk biochemical studies, which often involved the utilization of lipid II and proteins in micelles. Biochemical reconstitution of PG synthesis on supported lipid bilayers has allowed for assessing PG synthesis in more biologically relevant systems [52]. Tracking E. coli aPBP1b molecules in SLB in the presence of membrane-embedded lipid II resulted in slower-moving molecules with a decreased diffusion coefficient than those without lipid II (Fig. 3C). The absence of processive movement of aPBP1b in vivo and in vitro again raises the question of why its processivity in synthesizing glycan strands does not power the processive motions, as shown for FtsWIQLB. Nonetheless, the slower diffusion dynamics of aPBP1b in the presence of its substrate lipid II is consistent with the in vivo stationary nature of active aPBP1b molecules. The processive motion would likely require the presence of a PG template by which the synthase can move processively.

In this theme, cell division involves two steps. The first step is the assembly of a divisome, which often requires FtsZ’s treadmilling, and the second step requires sPG synthesis to enable cell wall constriction and FtsZ is no longer necessary [53] (Fig. 4A). These two steps are reflected in the two-track model observed in E. coli and C. crescentus, where the core sPG synthesis complex FtsWIQLB can dynamically transition between the Z-track and the sPG track. As cell division naturally transitions from the first, FtsZ-dependent step to the second, FtsZ-independent but sPG synthesis-dependent step, the synthesis complex may also naturally transition from the Z-track to the sPG track more frequently. A recent theoretical study coupled with a reanalysis of previous SMT data suggests that it is likely to be the case [54]. The Brownian ratchet model predicts that a cell wall protein can persistently track the depolymerizing end of a treadmilling FtsZ filament and hence exhibit the Z track-driven processive motion only when its binding potential to the FtsAZ filament and its diffusion speed on the membrane are within a certain range [28]. It is possible that these parameters of cell wall enzymes of different species are drastically different. Another possibility is that the abundantly available sPG synthesis sites in monoderm bacteria may keep all available enzymes occupied without releasing them to the Z-track, or that these enzyme complexes may exist in a primed, active state once it arrives midcell and does not need activators such as FtsN and/or FzlA as that in diderm bacteria, which is regulated by FtsZ’s treadmilling. These possibilities are also consistent with the notion that in Gram-negative bacteria, a treadmilling Z-track is required for a better, more controlled distribution of cell wall synthases along the septum, as these bacteria have a limited cell-wall layer (Fig. 4B). In S. pneumoniae and S. aureus, nascent FtsZ filaments are often observed to treadmill at the quarter cell positions and orthogonal to the constricting septum respectively, in addition to matured treadmilling FtsZ-ring at the constricting, midcell septum position [16,55] (Fig. 4C). It is possible that some cell wall enzymes follow these nascent, non-midcell FtsZ filaments prior to commencing on midcell septum synthesis as was observed in decondensed Z-rings in B. subtilis [17]. A recent theoretical study suggests that treadmilling FtsZ filaments could act as a conveyer to nucleate the assembly of mature sPG synthase complex [54]. As such, under hypothetical conditions that sPG synthase complex is not completely assembled, or that the binding of sPG enzymes to synthesis sites, not just the availability of lipid II substrate or enzyme activities, is diminished, we may observe the re-emergence of the Z-treadmilling dependent population. One example is that recent SMT experiments of FtsN variants without the denuded glycan binding SPOR domain demonstrated the re-appearance of the fast-moving population in E. coli [34].

Future PerspectivesWhile we have learned a great deal about how septal needs to take place in a controlled manner concurrently or sequentially to separate the two daughter cell wall synthases need to “stay on track” to carry out their essential functions in building the perfect septum, we are still only seeing one side of this process. To ensure successful cell wall constriction, cell wall hydrolysis cells. In C. crescentus, E. coli, and S. pneumoniae, the synthesis and cleavage of the septal cell wall are largely concurrent, leading to the concerted inward growth of septum and simultaneous separation of two daughter cells [39,56–58] (Fig. 4B, C). In B. subtilis and S. aureus, however, the cleavage of the old septal cell wall lags behind the new septal cell wall synthesis and leads to the formation of a thick septum before the separation of the two daughter cells [59,60] (Fig. 4B, C). What marks the cleavage sites and causes these differences? What are the dynamics of cell wall hydrolases such as lytic transglycosylases (Lts), glycosidases, and amidases involved in division? Do they also move processively and together with the synthase complex and hence forming a “third track”, or do they behave like randomly diffusing and occasionally stationary aPBPs? Different motions may signal the mechanisms of these enzymes’ working and how they are coordinated with synthases in time and space to avoid cell wall lesions during constriction. Some recent Cryo-ET and super-resolution imaging studies have started to shed light on these questions, and it would be exciting to see how future SMT studies could help shape the picture of bacterial cell wall constriction [39,56,61].
