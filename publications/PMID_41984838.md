Drought limits crop productivity, and effective mitigation requires a
mechanistic understanding of how abscisic acid (ABA) perception translates
hormone levels into physiological responses. In seed plants, ABA is sensed by
PYR/PYL/RCAR (PYR/PYL) receptors, which inhibit 2C protein phosphatases (PP2Cs),
thereby releasing Snf1-related protein kinases and driving stomatal closure and
stress-responsive transcription. Yet how receptor architecture evolved to tune
ABA dependence and dynamic range remains unclear. Here, we combine structural
biology, biochemistry, evolutionary analysis, and in planta assays across algal,
bryophyte, and angiosperm receptors to uncover a minimal molecular code that
governs ABA sensitivity and oligomeric state. We identify a five-residue
signature: Three leucines in the ligand pocket stabilize the gate in a closed
conformation, conferring ligand-independent PP2C inhibition (ancestral trait),
while two interface residues toggle dimerization (Leu/Lys) versus monomerization
(Cys/Ser), thereby setting ABA affinity. Structure-guided swaps reciprocally
convert behaviors: Introducing the three leucines plus interface substitutions
renders the ABA-dependent dimeric Citrus sinensis CsPYL1 into a monomer-like
with ABA-independent activity, whereas the converse changes in monomeric
Marchantia polymorpha MpPYL1 enforce dimerization and lower ABA affinity. In
planta, reporter assays and mutant analyses reveal complementary operating
ranges: Monomeric, high-affinity receptors decode low ABA under mild stress,
while dimeric, reduced-affinity receptors sustain signaling at high ABA during
acute drought, expanding the system's overall dynamic range and robustness.
These results resolve the apparent paradox of low-affinity dimers as an
evolutionary innovation rather than a loss of sensitivity, link receptor
architecture to ABA-mediated response, and provide actionable design principles
for engineering ABA signaling to enhance crop drought resilience.

## Full Text

Desiccation tolerance is vital for modern crops in the face of climate change and intensifying drought. Global warming and climate change intensify the effects of typical seasonal weather patterns and atmospheric phenomena that limit freshwater availability, posing a significant threat to crop production. Plant transpiration through stomata is the primary source of water loss during gas exchange for photosynthesis (1). Under drought conditions, the phytohormone ABA regulates stomatal movements, controlling plant transpiration and root water uptake (2–4). Thus, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying ABA signaling is essential for developing strategies to enhance crop resilience to drought.

Under nonstress conditions, Snf1-related protein kinases (SnRK2) are kept inactive through stable interactions with a subfamily of type 2C protein phosphatases (PP2Cs) (5). During drought, increased ABA levels are detected by the PYR/PYL/RCAR (PYR/PYL) family of receptors and the PP2Cs to form PYR/PYL:ABA:PP2C ternary complexes (6, 7). This interaction disrupts the SnRK2–PP2C complex, leading to SnRK2 activation via B2/B3 type MAPKKKs (8–12). Once activated, SnRK2 phosphorylates anion channels, causing guard cell turgor loss and stomatal closure (13–15).

In Arabidopsis, the PYR/PYL proteins constitute a multigene family, yet whether they fulfill fully redundant or specialized functions remains unclear (8). While sequence-based classification places them into three clades, biochemical analysis reveals two distinct receptor classes: dimeric and monomeric (16). The intrinsic affinities of PYR/PYL proteins for ABA are influenced by the oligomeric state of their apo forms. Dimeric receptors exhibit lower ABA binding affinities because they have to overcome an energetic cost associated with dissociating into monomers to form a one-to-one receptor–PP2C complex (7, 16). This feature ensures a dynamic range for ABA perception extending from the low nM to the low μM ABA concentrations, consistent with the physiological levels reported in plant cells (17).

The study of PYR/PYL ABA receptors and their downstream signaling partners across plant evolution illustrates how plants progressively refined drought-response mechanisms to cope with terrestrial environments (18). The ability of ABA to regulate stomatal closure through SLAC-type anion channels emerged in seed plants coinciding with the recruitment of SnRK2 kinases into the core signaling module. Earlier lineages, such as bryophytes and lycophytes, lack ABA-responsive SLAC activation and rely instead on transcriptional and metabolic adjustments to osmotic stress (15). This physiological transition was preceded and enabled by molecular diversification within the ABA receptor family. Monomeric receptors, exemplified by Marchantia polymorpha MpPYL1, represent an ancestral configuration that can repress PP2Cs even in the absence of ABA, supporting basal phosphatase regulation in early land plants (19). In divergent lineages such as mosses, a reduction in ABA-independent receptor activity and, consequently, an increase in the dynamic range of ABA perception are observed (18). The later evolution of dimeric receptors in seed plants did not merely reduce ABA affinity but instead introduced an additional regulatory layer that expanded the system’s dynamic range (15, 16).

This evolutionary coupling of receptor biochemistry with signaling output exemplifies how modular rewiring of ancient pathways generated the sophisticated drought-responsive networks that characterize modern seed plants. Hence, since ABA signaling function diversified across plant evolution, connecting molecular receptor features to physiological control is essential for rational strategies to improve drought resilience. For instance, the coexistence of two receptor classes in seed plants implies complementary sensing strategies, yet the molecular basis for this diversification and its mapping onto physiological outcomes remain unresolved. Specifically, a mechanistic explanation is required that links specific structural residues to changes in oligomeric state, ABA affinity, and downstream stress responses.

Here we integrate structural, biochemical, evolutionary, and in planta analyses across algal, bryophyte, and angiosperm receptors to identify a five-residue molecular signature that governs gate stabilization, oligomerization, and ABA dependence in PYR/PYLs. Structure-guided swaps convert monomeric and dimeric behaviors, revealing how two residues at the interface set oligomeric state and affinity, while three Leu residues in the pocket enforce ligand-independent activity. These mechanisms explain the coexistence of monomeric, high-affinity sensors and dimeric, reduced-affinity switches in seed plants, establishing two overlapping ABA-sensing ranges that underlie robust stomatal and transcriptional responses. This framework provides actionable design principles for engineering ABA signaling in crops.

From MpPYL1 of Marchantia polymorpha, a model for the earlier stages of land plant colonization, to the emergence of monomeric receptors with the ability to bind and be activated by ABA, and ultimately to dimeric receptors characterized by reduced ABA affinity.Complementary Roles of Monomeric and Dimeric Receptors in Decoding ABA Signaling.The evolution of ABA receptors from ABA-independent ancestral proteins presents an apparent paradox. In the apo form of CsPYL1, dimerization is primarily stabilized by intermolecular interactions involving the C-terminal α-helix and the gate loop in its open conformation, as well as the Lys-loop, which contains the conserved Lys88 that interacts with ABA in the bound state. Upon ABA binding and subsequent gate closure, the buried surface area at this interface is reduced, a change likely linked to dimer dissociation and phosphatase binding (6). Remarkably, despite the distinct oligomeric nature of CsPYL1 and MpPYL1, the residues involved in oligomerization and phosphatase binding are highly conserved, except Leu195 and Lys199 in CsPYL1, which correspond to Cys194 and Ser198 in MpPYL1 (Fig. 3A). A detailed analysis of the interfaces revealed the structural impact of these substitutions. The replacement of Leu195 with Cys194 induces a shift of His89 inward toward the receptor core, likely to avoid a steric clash with the polar thiol group of Cys194 (Fig. 3 B and C). This rearrangement reorients the His89 carbonyl, flipping it from facing the ABA-binding pocket to pointing outward toward the molecular surface. Additionally, the substitution of Lys199 with the smaller Ser198 side chain facilitates this structural reorganization within the receptor.

Together, these conformational changes would lead to a substantial reduction in the interfacial area required for dimer formation, thereby disfavoring dimerization. Remarkably, His89 is a Proline in class I monomeric receptors and has been shown to adopt an equivalent carbonyl conformation and to determine the oligomeric state of the receptors (Fig. 3D) (16).

To assess the impact of the Leu195 to Cys194 and Lys199 to Ser198 substitutions on the biochemical properties of CsPYL1, we generated the corresponding CsPYL1 mutants. We analyzed their oligomeric state using size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), measured their ability to inhibit HAB1 phosphatase activity in the presence of ABA, and determined their crystal structures in complex with HAB1 and ABA.

The CsPYL1 Leu195Cys mutant displayed two distinct SEC peaks corresponding to dimeric and monomeric forms, indicating partial disruption of dimerization (Fig. 3E). Indeed, we obtained a poorly diffracting crystal form of the apo CsPYL1 Leu195Cys mutant at 3.5 Å resolution, which was sufficient to reveal a dimer conformation distinct from that observed in the wild-type apo CsPYL1 structure (6) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). This variant showed increased ABA-mediated phosphatase inhibitory activity, with an IC50 for ABA of 130 nM (Fig. 3F).

Interestingly, the CsPYL1 Lys199Ser mutant retained its dimeric character at the concentration used in the SEC experiments (40 μM), but exhibited a significantly enhanced phosphatase inhibitory activity, with an IC50 for ABA of 63 nM (Fig. 3 E and F), comparable to the value for the monomeric MpPYL1 and those reported for monomeric receptors (24). To determine whether CsPYL1 forms a dimer while CsPYL1 Lys199Ser variant dissociates at the protein concentrations employed for the phosphatase inhibition assays, we designed a fluorescence-based assay to monitor changes in the intrinsic fluorescence of the sole tryptophan residue in CsPYL1 across a serial dilution of the protein, both in the absence and presence of a saturating ABA concentration (200 μM). As observed for AtPYL1 and AtPYR1 (16), our data indicate that CsPYL1 predominantly exists as a dimer in its apo form, as evidenced by the minimal changes in fluorescence across the range of protein concentrations tested. In contrast, in the presence of saturating ABA, a clear concentration-dependent change in fluorescence was observed, consistent with a transition from the dimeric to the monomeric state at lower protein concentrations (Fig. 3G).

Remarkably, CsPYL1-K199S exhibited a pronounced change in fluorescence at concentrations below 4 μM, regardless of ABA presence, suggesting a higher dissociation constant than that of wild-type CsPYL1. This behavior is consistent with the monomer-like properties of the CsPYL1-K199S mutant (Fig. 3H).

The crystallographic analysis revealed that in the CsPYL1 Lys199Ser:ABA:HAB1 complex, the main chain and side chain of His89 adopt a conformation identical to that observed in wild-type MpPYL1 (Fig. 3 I and C). On the other hand, the electron density map at His89 for the CsPYL1 Leu195Cys:ABA:HAB1 complex reveals that the side chain does not undergo the conformational change observed for MpPYL1 (Fig. 3J). Altogether, biochemical and structural data demonstrate that while the Leu195 to Cys194 substitution contributes to dimer dissociation, the Lys199 to Ser198 substitution is necessary and sufficient to confer enhanced ABA-binding properties characteristic of MpPYL1 and other monomeric receptors to CsPYL1.

DiscussionThe ability of plants to perceive the hormone abscisic acid arises from the evolutionary refinement of an ancient, ligand-independent signaling module that added the ABA dependency during land colonization to cope with changing environmental conditions (19). This perception further evolved to lose its ligand-independent activity, therefore increasing the dynamic range of ABA perception and ensuring a finely tuned machinery for stress response, completely dependent on ligand binding in dimeric receptors (18). While this supports a “linear” model and aligns with the conserved role of ABA in dehydration protection and receptor diversification in seed plants, increasing evidence points to the coexistence of a mosaic evolutionary history. For instance, the fact that ABA-dependent activation of SLAC anion channels, central to stomatal closure in seed plants, is coincident with the emergence of these dimeric receptors (15), suggests that such increased dynamical range have emerged in a divergent manner that has functional consequences in plant physiology (Fig. 6). In this respect, ABA sensitivity depends on downstream signaling architecture, including PP2C–SnRK2 coupling and SLAC/SLAH effector integration (13, 14), which did not evolve alongside ABA perception and differ across lineages. Lycophytes and ferns, despite occupying an intermediate evolutionary position between bryophytes and seed plants, show little evidence for SLAC kinase sensitivity (15), highlighting a patchy distribution that is difficult to reconcile with a simple linear progression of ABA signaling. These dose-dependent effects include ABA-mediated PYR/PYL receptor control of seed development and stomatal movements, as well as transcriptional regulation of multiple ABA signaling components, such as PYR/PYL receptors and downstream targets including ABR, HAI1, and MYB60 (3, 8), highlighting how changing ABA levels fine-tune developmental and stress responses. Beyond their evolutionary significance, these insights provide the basis for engineering tailored ABA receptors with customized signaling properties, offering a promising strategy to understand the specific function of different PYR/PYL isoforms and to improve plant resilience in the context of climate change.

The identification of ABA receptors with constitutive activity has been used to study receptor-specific functionality through a gain-of-function approach (46). In this context, the generation of different PYR/PYL engineered receptors harboring the three Leu residues that confer ABA-independent activity (Fig. 2) represents a promising tool. Notably, CsPYL1-Ind exhibits weak affinity for ABA, suggesting that its activity could be further tuned through the use of alternative, ecofriendly synthetic ligands (23). Such ligands could potentiate receptor activation under severe or prolonged stress conditions, thereby extending the regulatory capacity of the ABA signaling pathway beyond its natural limitations.

Our mutational work further reveals approaches for tailoring receptor sensitivity. Substitutions that enhance ABA affinity by promoting dissociation of dimeric receptors (L195C and K199S) could be used to generate crops that respond more readily to endogenous ABA. This would enable faster stomatal closure and ABA-dependent transcriptional activation at lower hormone levels, thereby improving drought tolerance. Conversely, the properties of the engineered MpPYL1Cs receptor demonstrate that dimerization enforces strict ABA dependency, a property that may be exploited to design receptors acting as “emergency switches.” Such dimeric receptors could be deployed to promote complete stomatal closure only under high ABA concentrations, providing plants with a controlled mechanism to prevent excessive water loss during acute stress episodes.

The residues governing dimerization interfaces and ABA dependence are highly conserved across different receptor families, including rice (47) and wheat (22), suggesting that homologous receptors in major crops operate via similar mechanisms. Consequently, the structural principles described here are expected to have analogous functional consequences in crop PYLs, supporting the extrapolation of our findings to agriculturally relevant species. Indeed, previous in planta expression studies of ZcPYL8 and MpPYL1 in ABA-deficient Arabidopsis backgrounds demonstrated that intrinsic receptor properties defined in vitro translate into distinct physiological signaling outputs, even in the absence of endogenous ABA. Together with our genetic and biochemical analyses, these findings support the idea that engineered monomeric- and dimeric-like receptors will confer predictable and tunable ABA signaling behaviors when expressed in planta.

Collectively, these strategies underscore the biotechnological potential of modulating ABA receptor activity. Once the amino acid residues that govern receptor dimerization and ligand affinity are identified, we can rationally design tailored receptors with customized signaling properties. Such engineered receptors could be deployed to optimize stress resilience in plants, either by broadening the range of ABA responses or by enabling activation through tailored, environmentally sustainable ligands.

Real-time qPCR was performed in a QuantStudio 3 system (Applied Biosystems) using PyroTaq EvaGreen qPCR Master Mix 5X (Cultek), which includes EvaGreen dye and carboxy-X-rhodamine (ROX) as a passive reference dye. The primer pairs used targeted the ABA-responsive genes RAB18 and RD29B, and ACTIN-8 as the housekeeping gene (54). Relative gene expression was quantified using the comparative Ct method (2-ΔΔCt), with expression levels normalized to ACTIN-8.
