MuSK is a receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) that plays essential roles in the
formation and maintenance of the neuromuscular junction. Distinct from most
members of RTK family, MuSK activation requires not only its cognate ligand
agrin but also its coreceptors LRP4. However, how agrin and LRP4 coactivate MuSK
remains unclear. Here, we report the cryo-EM structure of the extracellular
ternary complex of agrin/LRP4/MuSK in a stoichiometry of 1:1:1. This structure
reveals that arc-shaped LRP4 simultaneously recruits both agrin and MuSK to its
central cavity, thereby promoting a direct interaction between agrin and MuSK.
Our cryo-EM analyses therefore uncover the assembly mechanism of agrin/LRP4/MuSK
signaling complex and reveal how MuSK receptor is activated by concurrent
binding of agrin and LRP4.

## Full Text

The activation of muscle-specific receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK), known as MuSK, leads to clustering of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (AChRs) on the postsynaptic muscle cell surface, a critical step in the formation and maintenance of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) (1–6). The ability of MuSK to function depends on its cognate ligand agrin (3). However, unlike other RTKs, MuSK does not bind directly to agrin; instead, it requires a coreceptor LRP4 for agrin binding and its activation (7, 8). It has been proposed that, upon formation of 2:2:2 agrin/LRP4/MuSK signaling complex, MuSK undergoes autophosphorylation and initiates a complex signaling pathway by recruiting a variety of downstream factors, such as Dok7 (9). The C-terminal region of Dok7 can be phosphorylated by activated MuSK and then serves as a scaffold to recruit additional downstream targets, i.e., rapsyn, which would lead to AChR clustering (10, 11). Interestingly, it has also been suggested that Dok7 may act as a cytoplasmic “ligand” for MuSK, which may stimulate MuSK dimerization and activation, independent of agrin and LRP4 (12).

Structurally, the MuSK receptor is composed of three consecutive Ig-like domains (Ig1 to Ig3) followed by a cysteine-rich domain (CRD) in the extracellular region, a single transmembrane helix (TM), and an intracellular kinase (6, 13, 14) (Fig. 1A). Agrin, the ligand of MuSK, is secreted as a very large heparan-sulfate proteoglycan containing nine follistatin-like (FS) domains, two laminin EGF–like (LE) domains, and one SEA domain in the N-terminal region as well as four EGF-like (EGF) domains and three laminin globular (LG1 to LG3) domains in the C-terminal region (Fig. 1A). Like MuSK, LPR4 is also a single-TM receptor. The extracellular region of LRP4 contains eight LDLa repeats, six EGF-like domains, and four β-propeller domains (β1 to β4) (Fig. 1A). Previous studies have reported the crystal structures of Ig1–Ig2 and CR domains of MuSK as well as the complex between the LG3 domain of agrin and first β-propeller domain of LRP4 (15–17). The crystal structure of agrin-LG3 and LPR4-β1 complex shows that a long loop of agrin-LG3, named neuron-specific z8 loop, contacts the central cavity of LPR4-β1 (17). Such interaction is critical for agrin-induced MuSK activation. In addition, mutagenesis studies have suggested that the Ig1 domain of MuSK is important for LRP4 binding (18). However, the structure of signaling complex composed of agrin, LRP4, and MuSK remains unknown. Solving such a structure may explain why both agrin and LRP4 are required for MuSK activation.

Here, we determined the cryo-EM structure of 1:1:1 agrin/LRP4/MuSK complex at overall 3.8 Å resolution. Our structure shows that the first three β-propeller domains of LRP4 assume a rigid arc-shaped conformation, and both agrin and MuSK are located in the central cavity of this LRP4 “arc.” In such a structural configuration, the arc-shaped LRP4 acts as a “clamp” that holds agrin and MuSK closely and thereby significantly increases their binding affinity. Thus, our structure reveals why LRP4 is essential for MuSK signaling and provides insights into the assembly mechanism of MuSK signaling complex.

Putative Mechanism Underlying Agrin/LRP4-Induced MuSK Activation.

Despite our cryo-EM structure providing key insight into the assembly mechanism of agrin/LRP4/MuSK complex, this model of 1:1:1 complex cannot explain how agrin together with LRP4 induces oligomerization and activation of MuSK. It is plausible that the two semicircle-shaped 1:1:1 agrin/LRP4/MuSK complexes could further pack into a 2:2:2 agrin/LRP4/MuSK complex through undefined LRP4/LRP4 and/or agrin/LRP4 interactions, leading to cross-autophosphorylation of MuSK and subsequent activation of the downstream signaling cascades. The formation of higher-order complex has been demonstrated to be an important feature of many RTK signaling mechanisms (22, 23). The MuSK in the higher-order oligomeric state might be able to recruit multiple copies of Dok7 and rapsyn, thereby facilitating the formation of AChR clusters. The active MuSK signaling complexes did not exist in our cryo-EM dataset, suggesting that the dimeric or higher-ordered MuSK signaling complex is less stable than monomeric complex and they tend to fall apart on the cryo-EM grid. Therefore, using the more stable full-length MuSK for structural studies would be the key for capturing the MuSK in the fully functional state.

The proteins were further purified by SEC using a Superose 6 Increase 3.2/300 or Superose 6 Increase 10/300 columns equilibrated with 25 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, and 2 mM CaCl2. To reconstitute the agrin–MuSK–LRP4 complex, the purified LRP4-ECD, MuSK-ECD, and agrin-ECD were mixed with a molar ratio of 1:1.5:1.5. The mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 2 h, before being purified by SEC using Superose 6 Increase 3.2/300 column. The peak fractions for the agrin–LRP4–MuSK complex were collected and concentrated to 0.56 mg/mL for cryo-EM studies.

Image Processing.The cryo-EM refinement statists is summarized in SI Appendix, Table S1. A total of 3,560 movie frames of agrin–LRP4–MuSK micrographs were motion-corrected and binned two-fold, resulting in a pixel size of 0.83 Å, and dose-weighted using MotionCor2 (27). The CTF parameters were estimated using Gctf (28). RELION3 was used for the following processing (29). Particles were first roughly picked by using the Laplacian-of-Gaussian blob method, and then subjected to 2D classification. Class averages representing projections of the agrin–LRP4–MuSK sample in different orientations were used as templates for reference-based particle picking. Extracted particles were binned three times and subjected to 2D classification. Particles from the classes with fine structural feature were selected for 3D classification using an initial model generated from a subset of the particles in RELION. Particles from one of the resulting 3D classes showing good secondary structural features were selected and reextracted into the original pixel size of 0.83 Å. Subsequently, we performed finer 3D classification by using local search in combination with small angular sampling, resulting in two different classes. In one class, agrin, LPR4, and MuSK were all resolved, while in another class, only agrin and LRP4 were resolved. The cryo-EM map for these two distinct classes was resolved at 3.8 Å and 3.7 Å resolution, respectively.

Model Building and Refinement.Model building of agrin–LRP4–MuSK complex was initiated by rigid-body docking of individual domains from the crystal structures of LG3 of agrin, the β1 of LRP4, and the Ig1 of MuSK (15, 17). The models of Ig2 and Ig3 of MuSK cannot perfectly fit into the cryo-EM density, confirming that the MuSK Ig1 domain is involved in the binding to agrin and LRP4, which is consistent with previous mutagenesis results (18). The models of β2 and β3 of LRP4 were first generated by AlphaFold2 (30). Manual building was carried out using the program Coot (31). The model was refined by using the real-space refinement module in the Phenix package (V1.17) (32). Restraints on secondary structure, backbone Ramachandran angles, and residue sidechain rotamers were used during the refinement to improve the geometry of the model. MolProbity 4.5 as a part of the Phenix validation tools was used for model validation (SI Appendix, Table S1). Figures were generated in Chimera 1.17 (33).

Upon reaching 90% confluence, C2C12 cells were grown in culture medium containing DMEM supplemented with horse serum (2% v/v) to induce myotube differentiation. On day 5 of differentiation, myotubes were treated with either wild-type or mutant agrin (1 nM) for 24 h. The myotubes were then fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde/0.1 M PB solution for 30 min, washed with PBS, and then incubated in Texas-Red-conjugated α-bungarotoxin (α-bgt) (2 nM, Molecular Probes) for 30 min. The myotubes were then washed with PBS and coverslip-mounted in Fluoro-Gel (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and imaged under a fluorescence microscope. For each condition, 20 images were randomly captured using a Hamamatsu ORCA-285 camera. A cluster with a length ≥4 μm was defined as a patch of AChRs. The numbers of AChR clusters on myotubes were normalized to the length of myotubes. Eighty myotubes were measured for each condition using NIH Image J software, and data collected from four independent experiments were used for statistical analyses.
