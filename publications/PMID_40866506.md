Pathogenesis-related protein 1 (PR-1) is a critical component of plant defense
mechanisms, exhibiting significant antifungal activity. This study focused on
the PR-1 protein from Zea mays and five other plant species (Triticum aestivum,
Hordeum vulgare, Oryza sativa, Avena sativa, and Secale cereale) to explore
their physicochemical, structural, and functional characteristics. The PR-1 gene
from Z. mays was isolated and expressed in Escherichia coli BL21-DE3 cells,
enabling structural characterization. Recombinant expression in E. coli enabled
structural characterization and functional studies of the PR-1 protein, laying a
foundation for future experiments. Sequence analysis revealed high similarity
(65-90%) among PR-1 proteins across species, although physicochemical properties
varied based on plant origin. Structural analysis showed a conserved beta-barrel
structure surrounded by alpha-helices in all PR-1 proteins. In addition to in
silico and structural characterization, antifungal bioassays were performed
using the purified recombinant PR-1 protein against Fusarium oxysporum, a major
fungal phytopathogen. These assays demonstrated notable antifungal activity,
supporting the predicted defense role of PR-1 and laying experimental groundwork
for its potential use in developing fungi-resistant transgenic crops.

These pathogens can cause infections that greatly impair plant health and survival. Such infections disrupt various physiological and metabolic processes within plants, leading to substantial annual losses in crop yield and quality1,2. The harmful effects of these infection on agricultural productivity emphasize the importance of developing effective disease control measures and resilient crop varieties. This is crucial for ensuring sustainable food production and stability in agriculture over the long term. In Pakistan, fungal diseases has the most severe yield losses7. Resistance breeding and pesticides are the two most commonly used methods to combat these plant diseases. However, the extensive use of pesticides leads to severe environmental problems8. The most effective alternative is genetically engineered plants with enhanced characteristics, such as increased resistance to pathogens9.

Plants uses various defense mechanisms, such as the production of antibiotic compounds known as phytoalexins and the reinforcement of cell walls to create a formidable physical barrier against infectious agents10,11. Moreover, plants exhibit a dynamic response to stress induced by different microbes and chemical compounds12, including salicylic acid13 and ethylene, mimicking the effects of pathogenic infections. Pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins play an important role in plant defenses. They are triggered when plants encounter pathogens or compounds that simulate pathogenic effects, contributing to resistance and protection14. Among these, PR-1 proteins are recognized as key markers of systemic acquired resistance (SAR) and have been shown to exhibit antifungal and antimicrobial activities15. PR-1 proteins may interfere with pathogen sterol metabolism, disrupting membrane integrity and growth, thus playing a direct role in plant immunity. These defense proteins are induced under stress conditions, such as pathogen attack, and are critical for protecting plants against a wide range of biotic stresses16,17. Based on their structural variabilities and biological activities, 17 different families have been characterized by classifying PR proteins (PR1-PR17)15,18. Among these proteins are lytic enzymes such as β-1, 3-glucanases (belonging to the PR-2 family) and chitinases (belonging to the PR-3, PR-4, PR-8, and PR-11 families). These enzymes are thought to help defend the plant by breaking down the cell walls of invading fungi, thereby inhibiting their growth and spread20.

PR-1 proteins are recognized as excellent markers for SAR (systemic acquired resistance) due to the correlation found between systemic resistance and PR proteins16,21. PR-1 protein is proposed to protect the plant from environmental stresses, is involved in the antifungal activity, and possess resistance to viral invasion22. However, the exact biological function and the biochemical pathways involved are not well known up to date. Since its discovery in 1970s, PR-1 proteins are identified in a wide variety of plant species23. PR-1 proteins have been characterized in Arabidopsis24, maize25, tobacco26, wheat27, rice28, tomato29, barley30 and pepper31. Maize is one of the most consumed cereal crops worldwide and is an important staple food for millions of people. Fungal attacks on crop cause a massive damage and reduce yield and quality of the crop32. However, the presence of PR-1 protein provide protection against many fungi types. Among the monocotyledonous plants, PR-1 protein gene was first discovered in Z. mays33. While the PR-1 sequence and its role in plant defense have been studied for over three decades, this study presents the first successful recombinant expression of a Zea mays PR-1 protein in a prokaryotic system, enabling detailed structural characterization. Our study focuses on a specific PR-1 homolog (GenBank ID: NM_001159109.2), selected based on its high expression levels in response to pathogen stress and its homology with other well-characterized PR-1 proteins in plants.

In addition to cloning and expression, we conducted a comprehensive in silico analysis of PR-1 proteins across multiple plant species, highlighting sequence diversity, physicochemical properties, and predicted structural features. Recognizing the presence of multiple PR-1 paralogs in the maize genome, we acknowledge the need for further studies on allelic variation across genotypes to explore functional diversity.

Importantly, to support our bioinformatic findings and gain preliminary functional insights, we performed antifungal bioassays of the maize PR-1 protein against Fusarium oxysporum, a major phytopathogen responsible for wilt diseases in various crops. Which provided experimental support for our in silico structural predictions and functional characterization. These assays revealed notable antifungal activity, suggesting a conserved defense role of the protein. This represents a foundational step toward experimental validation and supports the potential application of PR-1 proteins in plant protection strategies.

The observed antifungal activity suggests a conserved defense role of PR-1 proteins and represents a foundational step toward experimental validation. These findings support the potential application of maize PR-1 in crop protection strategies, while also opening avenues for further exploration of its molecular mechanisms and role in plant-pathogen interactions.

The antifungal activity of purified recombinant Zea mays PR-1 protein was assessed against Fusarium oxysporum using a disk diffusion assay, adapted from the method described by Kumar et al.72. The assay was performed using 100 × 15 mm sterile plastic Petri plates containing 35 mL of potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium. Fungal inoculum of F. oxysporum was prepared by allowing mycelial expansion on the PDA plates, after which sterile paper discs were carefully placed on the surface of the fungal lawn. Four different treatments were tested: (1) 40 µg of purified recombinant PR-1 protein, (2) 30 µg of purified recombinant PR-1 protein, (3) 20 µg of purified recombinant PR-1 protein, and (4) a negative control containing only the protein elution buffer. Each concentration was loaded onto individual sterile discs and placed equidistantly on the fungal plate. The plates were then incubated at 28°C under controlled conditions, and fungal growth was monitored until clear crescent-shaped zones of inhibition appeared around the discs containing recombinant protein. The size and clarity of the inhibition zones were used to assess the antifungal activity. The absence of inhibition around the buffer-only control disc confirmed that the antifungal effect was due to the recombinant PR-1 protein and not the buffer components. This assay demonstrated the potential of the PR-1 protein to inhibit the growth of F. oxysporum, suggesting its functional role in plant defense.

Antifungal activity assay of recombinant ZmPR-1 protein
The antifungal activity of purified recombinant Zea mays PR-1 protein was assessed against Fusarium oxysporum using a disk diffusion assay, adapted from the method described by Kumar et al.72. The assay was performed using 100 × 15 mm sterile plastic Petri plates containing 35 mL of potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium. Fungal inoculum of F. oxysporum was prepared by allowing mycelial expansion on the PDA plates, after which sterile paper discs were carefully placed on the surface of the fungal lawn. Four different treatments were tested: (1) 40 µg of purified recombinant PR-1 protein, (2) 30 µg of purified recombinant PR-1 protein, (3) 20 µg of purified recombinant PR-1 protein, and (4) a negative control containing only the protein elution buffer. Each concentration was loaded onto individual sterile discs and placed equidistantly on the fungal plate. The plates were then incubated at 28°C under controlled conditions, and fungal growth was monitored until clear crescent-shaped zones of inhibition appeared around the discs containing recombinant protein. The size and clarity of the inhibition zones were used to assess the antifungal activity. The absence of inhibition around the buffer-only control disc confirmed that the antifungal effect was due to the recombinant PR-1 protein and not the buffer components. This assay demonstrated the potential of the PR-1 protein to inhibit the growth of F. oxysporum, suggesting its functional role in plant defense.

Discussion
PR proteins are a group of antifungal and antibacterial proteins with diverse functions and structures. Systemic acquired resistance (SAR) is a plant defense mechanism that induces the expression of PR proteins and provides protection against a variety of plant pathogens50. The expression of PR-1 proteins is considered a promising marker for SAR, but their precise biological mechanisms remain elusive. In our study, the PR-1 gene from Zea mays was cloned, characterized, and expressed in E. coli51. PR proteins are found to be either acidic or basic in nature. The PR-1 protein from Zea mays is acidic, similar to several PR-1 proteins reported in Oryza sativa. Among 12 PR-1 proteins in Oryza sativa, seven are acidic28,52 and of the 24 PR-1 proteins detected in Glycine max, 15 are acidic in nature 53. In contrast, a greater number of PR-1 proteins detected in Solanum lycopersicum in response to various biotic and abiotic stresses are basic29. This pH-dependent diversity may reflect an adaptive evolution of PR-1 proteins in different plant species in response to specific environmental or pathogenic stresses. In our study, the PR-1 protein from Zea mays, with a molecular weight of approximately 17 kDa, was successfully expressed in E. coli BL21-DE3 cells after induction with 1 mM IPTG, and the results were analyzed using SDS-PAGE. For comparison, a previous study, reported the expression of the NPR-1 protein from Nicotiana benthamiana with a molecular weight of 28 kDa, induced with 1.5 mM IPTG, and analyzed through SDS-PAGE54. The Zea mays PR-1 gene demonstrated 95% sequence similarity to the genomic clone gene (Sequence ID: AC116033.3). Sequence homology comparisons suggest evolutionary conservation in the PR-1 family, which is crucial for maintaining its structural integrity and function under stress conditions.The PR-1 proteins are highly conserved, characterized by cysteine-rich structures, α-helices, and β-sheets 23. Similar structural orientations were observed in Zea mays PR-1, highlighting the functional importance of cysteine residues in forming disulfide bonds, crucial for maintaining protein stability. While the cysteine content plays a structural role, additional studies are required to establish its direct contribution to antimicrobial activity23. Similarly, a study on NPR-1 concluded the presence of the four α- helices and two β-sheets and six cysteine residues54. The physicochemical properties such as pI, extinction coefficient (EC), and instability index provide valuable insights into the functional attributes of PR-1 proteins. The observed variations in EC among the PR-1 proteins reflect differences in aromatic amino acid content, influencing UV absorption and light interaction capabilities. All PR-1 proteins analyzed were stable except for the Oryza sativa sequence A0A0D3QSW1, which had an instability index of 47.05. This finding aligns with the hypothesis that stability variations in PR-1 proteins may impact their functional efficacy under stress conditions.Like other PR proteins, expression of PR-1 proteins is induced by a number of biotic and abiotic stresses. (Black pepper) PR-1 protein (PnPR-1) has shown resistance to oomycete pathogen P. capsica in Panniyur-1 plants55. The PR-1 gene isolated from Triticum turgidum ssp. durum (TdPR1.2) was induced for 48 hours using salicylic acid. This induction demonstrated a significant antimicrobial effect against eight different bacterial species, as well as the fungal species Septoria tritici. This finding highlights the potential of TdPR1.2 in enhancing disease resistance in crops by providing broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity56. In addition, the study reported that through a GST-Pull down assay the PR-1 protein has the ability of bind to Calmodulins (CaMs) in CaM/Ca2+ complex. Moreover, the antimicrobial effect of TdPR1.2 was stimulated by CaM/Ca2+ complex in the presence of magnesium ions (Mn2+)57. The wheat pathogenesis-related protein (TdPR1.2) has been shown to enhance tolerance to both abiotic and biotic stresses in transgenic Arabidopsis plants58. This improvement in stress resistance highlights the potential application of TdPR1.2 in developing crops with better resilience to environmental challenges and pathogen attacks58. Soybean PR-1 (GmPR-1) genes are exclusively expressed under multiple stress conditions53. ConclusionThis study provides the first comprehensive structural and functional characterization of the pathogenesis-related protein 1 (PR-1) from Zea mays, alongside comparative analysis with PR-1 proteins from other key cereals, including wheat, barley, rice, oats, and rye. Despite high sequence similarity across species, variations were observed in physicochemical attributes such as hydrophobicity, influenced by species-specific differences. Structural modeling revealed a conserved architecture comprising a central β-barrel flanked by α-helices, suggesting evolutionary conservation in plant immune response mechanisms. Importantly, the study combined in silico characterization with experimental validation, confirming the antifungal role of ZmPR-1. The antifungal assays provided strong biological evidence supporting the predicted defensive function, thus bridging the gap between computational prediction and practical application. These findings significantly strengthen the understanding of PR-1-mediated defense and highlight its potential utility in developing disease-resistant crop varieties through molecular breeding and genetic engineering. This work lays a strong foundation for future exploration of PR-1 proteins as eco-friendly alternatives to chemical fungicides in sustainable agriculture.
