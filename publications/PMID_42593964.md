Lipid droplets (LDs) are dynamic organelles central to cellular energy
homeostasis and stress adaptation. Maintaining LD balance is critical for cell
function, yet the mechanisms governing selective LD degradation remain unclear.
Here, we reveal a pathway promoting efficient vacuolar sequestering and
potential degradation of a Pdr16-marked LD subpopulation, thereby linking LD
metabolism to organelle communication. During nutrient limitation, the Rab
GTPase Ypt7 and its guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF), Mon1-Ccz1,
specifically localize to Pdr16-LDs. This targeting relies on a conserved
amphipathic helix within Ccz1. Importantly, Ypt7 activation recruits the SNX-BAR
retromer complex to the vacuole-LD interface at sites of invagination. There,
retromer potentially interacts with its cargo receptor, Vps10, to promote
positive membrane curvature that drives the late stage of vacuolar invagination
required for LD internalization and degradation. These findings suggest a link
between LD turnover, organelle interactions, and cellular adaptation to
metabolic stress, providing new insight into lipid homeostasis.

Introduction

Lipids are fundamental to life, serving as building blocks for cell membranes, a concentrated form of energy storage, and critical signaling molecules. Maintaining lipid homeostasis is, therefore, important for cellular function and adaptation to fluctuating environmental conditions.1 Lipid droplets (LDs) are dynamic organelles present in nearly all eukaryotic cells and serve as the primary storage site for neutral lipids—including triacylglycerol (TAG) and sterol esters (SEs)—within a hydrophobic core surrounded by a phospholipid monolayer.2,3,4,5 LDs are remarkably plastic, constantly undergoing formation, growth, and breakdown to meet cellular demands.3,6 The consumption of LDs is mediated through two major pathways: lipolysis and lipophagy.7 Lipolysis involves the enzymatic degradation of stored triglycerides resulting in the release of fatty acids and glycerol.8 Alternatively, lipophagy is the lysosome-mediated degradation of LDs that proceeds through macrolipophagy, where LDs are sequestered within autophagosomes before lysosomal fusion, and microlipophagy, which involves direct lysosomal engulfment of LDs.9,10,11

LDs are not a homogenous population and exhibit functional diversity, driven by distinct surface protein compositions. This heterogeneity allows cells to tailor LD metabolism to specific needs.4,12,13,14 Recent findings have identified Pdr16 as a marker for a specific LD subpopulation localized in proximity to nuclear-vacuole junctions (NVJs), contact sites between the nuclear endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and the vacuole. Two additional proteins, Ldo16 and Ldo45, are residents of the Pdr16-LDs, and they cooperate with seipin to regulate LD composition.15,16 Notably, the Ldo16-45 complex tethers Vac8, forming vacuole-LD contact sites (vCLIPs) that is essential for macrolipophagy.17,18

Rab proteins act as molecular switches, cycling between inactive GDP-bound and active GTP-bound states, and are activated by guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs).19,20,21,22,23,24,25,26,27 These activated Rabs then recruit effector proteins, which mediate diverse cellular processes including vesicle formation, transport, and fusion. Rab7, a key regulator of endosomal and autophagosomal trafficking, is activated by the Mon1-Ccz1 complex. This activation is crucial for the proper function of these organelles, particularly in the fusion of multivesicular bodies/late endosomes and autophagosomes with lysosomes.27,28,29,30,31,32 Interestingly, Mon1-Ccz1 targeting to autophagosomes requires an amphipathic helix (AH) in Ccz1 and Atg8, while targeting to endosomes relies on negative charges of phospholipids and Rab5-GTP.27,33,34,35 Rab7 recruits multiple effectors, including the highly conserved retromer complex, which mediates the sorting of specific cargoes like Vps10 from endosomes to Golgi. These cargoes are concentrated into distinct domains of the endosomal membrane, where tubules form to facilitate protein transport to the Golgi.36,37,38,39,40 Importantly, Rab7 has also been shown to play a key role during LD breakdown.41,42,43 However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this process have remained unclear.

Here, we found that Ypt7, a Rab7 homolog, and its GEF, the Mon1-Ccz1 complex, specifically target Pdr16-positive LDs during stationary phase. Deletion of MON1, CCZ1, or YPT7 results in a strong accumulation of these Pdr16-LDs. Importantly, targeting of the Mon1-Ccz1 complex to LDs depends on the same AH within Ccz1 that is also required for its binding to autophagosomes. A point mutation in this AH specifically abolishes Ccz1 localization to LDs, leading to Pdr16-LD accumulation in yeast cells, without disrupting its function in the endocytic and autophagic pathways. Upon activation by Mon1-Ccz1, Ypt7 recruits the retromer complex to the vacuole-LD interface, particularly at tips of vacuolar invaginations. Retromer mutants accumulate Pdr16-labeled LDs together with elevated cellular levels of triglycerides, ergosterol esters, and diacylglycerol (DAG). They also display a strong increase in the number of invaginated vesicles attached to the inner vacuolar membrane, suggesting a role for retromer at the late stage for vacuole invagination, potentially by promoting positive membrane curvature to facilitate LD internalization and subsequent degradation. Vps10, a retromer cargo receptor, targets Pdr16-LDs specifically during stationary phase and is also required for Pdr16-LD entry into the vacuole, indicating that it potentially functions in concert with retromer.

ResultsMon1-Ccz1 and Ypt7 localize to Pdr16-labeled LDs during stationary phaseLDs undergo substantial remodeling during the stationary phase of cell growth, which involves changes in their distribution, composition, and interactions with other cellular components. These alterations are critical for maintaining cellular lipid homeostasis and adapting to nutrient-limiting conditions.3,44 Previous work has shown that Mon1-Ccz1 and Ypt7 localize to endosomes under nutrient-rich conditions and to autophagosomes during nitrogen starvation28,33,35,45 (Figure 1A). Interestingly, during nitrogen starvation, we observed very few Ccz1 signals on round structures identified as LDs, indicating limited LD targeting under these conditions. We, therefore, asked which conditions promote robust recruitment of Mon1-Ccz1 and Ypt7 to LDs and compared their subcellular distributions in both log and stationary phases. Specifically, we assessed the localization of GFP-tagged Ccz1 and Ypt7 relative to Erg6, a functionally tagged LD marker carrying a mKate fluorophore. Under nutrient-rich conditions, Ccz1 displayed a punctate pattern consistent with endosomal localization,28,33,47 with minimal co-localization with Erg6-marked LDs (Figures 1B and 1D). Similarly, Ypt7 localized to the vacuolar membrane with a single punctum that did not co-localize with LDs (Figure 1C). However, during stationary phase, Erg6-marked LDs turned into larger ring-like formations. Notably, Ccz1 also formed ring-like structures and significantly co-localized with Erg6 (Figure 1B), as did Ypt7. Interestingly, some LDs were observed inside the vacuole, surrounded by Ypt7 (Figures 1C and 1D). However, we observed that only approximately 60% of Erg6-marked LDs were positive for Ccz1. Considering the existence of distinct LD subpopulations within a single cell, each differing in protein and lipid composition and recruitment capacity, we hypothesized that Mon1-Ccz1 and Ypt7 might target a specific LD subpopulation. Pdr16 marks a specific LD subpopulation localized near NVJs during stationary phase; Ldo45 was previously identified as a modulator of Pdr16 localization in a yeast screen, as was Ccz1.15 We, therefore, investigated whether Mon1-Ccz1 and Ypt7 are specifically recruited to Pdr16-LDs during stationary phase. To address this, we first analyzed the co-localization of GFP-tagged Ccz1 or Ypt7 with mKate-tagged Pdr16 or with the Pdr16-LD-resident proteins Ldo16/45 (LDO) in the exponentially growing and stationary phase cells. Pdr16 puncta were found to be enriched on only a fraction of cellular LDs.15,16 During stationary phase, Pdr16 and LDO exhibited strong co-localization with Ccz1 (over 80%) and remained significantly co-localized with Ypt7, though to a lesser extent than with Ccz1 (Figures 1E–1G; Figures S1A and S1B), whereas no co-localization was observed during log phase (Figures 1E–1G). To confirm whether this recruitment is specific for Pdr16-positive LDs, we compared GFP-Ccz1 or GFP-Ypt7 co-localization with Pdr16-mKate versus total LDs labeled using the LD-specific probe FAS.46 As expected, GFP-Ccz1 and GFP-Ypt7 co-localized strongly with Pdr16-LDs but not with the FAS-positive LD pool that lacks Pdr16 (Figures 1H and 1I). Interestingly, during nitrogen starvation, we observed very few Ccz1 signals on round structures identified as LDs, indicating limited LD targeting under these conditions. We, therefore, asked which conditions promote robust recruitment of Mon1-Ccz1 and Ypt7 to LDs and compared their subcellular distributions in both log and stationary phases. Specifically, we assessed the localization of GFP-tagged Ccz1 and Ypt7 relative to Erg6, a functionally tagged LD marker carrying a mKate fluorophore. Under nutrient-rich conditions, Ccz1 displayed a punctate pattern consistent with endosomal localization,28,33,47 with minimal co-localization with Erg6-marked LDs (Figures 1B and 1D). Similarly, Ypt7 localized to the vacuolar membrane with a single punctum that did not co-localize with LDs (Figure 1C). However, during stationary phase, Erg6-marked LDs turned into larger ring-like formations. Notably, Ccz1 also formed ring-like structures and significantly co-localized with Erg6 (Figure 1B), as did Ypt7. Interestingly, some LDs were observed inside the vacuole, surrounded by Ypt7 (Figures 1C and 1D). However, we observed that only approximately 60% of Erg6-marked LDs were positive for Ccz1. Considering the existence of distinct LD subpopulations within a single cell, each differing in protein and lipid composition and recruitment capacity, we hypothesized that Mon1-Ccz1 and Ypt7 might target a specific LD subpopulation. A conserved AH in Ccz1 facilitates the recruitment of Mon1-Ccz1 to LDsTo determine the function of Ypt7 on LDs, we next investigated how Mon1-Ccz1 targets LDs. Mon1-Ccz1 is recruited to endosomes by Rab5 proteins and to autophagosomes through interaction with Atg8 (LC3 in mammals).33,34 We, therefore, first investigated whether the endocytic or autophagy pathway, specifically Rab5 or Atg8, is required for Mon1-Ccz1 targeting to LDs during stationary phase. To test this, we analyzed the co-localization of GFP-Ccz1 with Erg6-mKate in wild-type and various mutant cells during both log and stationary phases. Seipin is a protein involved in LD formation, and its absence disrupts normal LD growth and maturation, resulting in the formation of larger, aberrant LDs.48,49,50,51,52 Notably, in cells lacking Sei1, the yeast seipin homolog, Ccz1 formed elongated structures in stationary phase, rather than the ring-like structures overlapping and surrounding LDs in wild-type cells. However, these elongated Ccz1 structures still co-localized with Erg6-marked LDs (Figures S2A–S2C). Mon1-Ccz1 targets PI3P-positive endosomal membranes via interaction with Rab5 proteins.53 Consistent with previous studies, loss of Mon1, Vps21/Rab5, or the CORVET subunit Vps8 (a Rab5 effector protein) strongly re-localized Ccz1 to the cytosol under growing conditions28,45 (Figure S2A). In contrast, Ccz1 remained punctate, likely representing endosomes, in atg1Δ or atg8Δ cells with impaired autophagy. Interestingly, in these mutants, Ccz1 was still present on LDs during stationary phase, similar to wild-type cells (Figures S2A and S2B). These data suggest that Mon1-Ccz1 localization to LDs during stationary phase does not require the endocytic or autophagy pathways or its known interacting partners, Rab5 proteins and Atg8; however, normal LD biogenesis is essential for its distribution on LDs.Proteins that target LDs from the cytoplasm often contain AHs. These AHs, characterized by opposing hydrophilic and hydrophobic faces, enable proteins to bind to the LD surface by inserting their hydrophobic residues into the monolayer—a mechanism important for various cellular processes involving lipid storage and metabolism.54 Interestingly, a conserved C-terminal AH was identified in Ccz1, which is required for Mon1-Ccz1 function in autophagy but not endosomal maturation35 (Figure 2A). We asked whether Mon1-Ccz1 targeting to LDs depends on this AH. To examine this, we first tested whether the AH alone is sufficient to target Mon1-Ccz1 to LDs. We compared the localization of a GFP-tagged Ccz1 fragment (amino acids 270–403) containing the AH35 with that of full-length Ccz1, relative to Erg6-mKate, under both growing and stationary phase conditions. Intriguingly, the Ccz1 fragment strongly co-localized with LDs, even in log phase, whereas no co-localization was observed with full-length Ccz1 under this condition. Furthermore, this co-localization was significantly higher with the Ccz1 fragment than with full-length Ccz1 during stationary phase (Figures 2B and 2C). These data strongly indicate that the AH in Ccz1 is sufficient to target Mon1-Ccz1 to LDs. To further investigate the direct interaction between the Ccz1 AH and lipid surfaces, we generated a GFP-Ccz1 construct with an inverted AH sequence. This inversion was designed to preserve the overall physicochemical properties of the AH, including its amphipathic character (Figure S2D), and, therefore, minimize disruption of its membrane adsorption properties.55 Notably, this inverted construct still localized to Erg6-marked LDs, although with reduced co-localization compared with wild-type Ccz1 (Figures S2E and S2F). This finding supports our model in which the Ccz1 AH contributes to the localization of Mon1-Ccz1 to LDs through direct interaction with the lipid surface. To further assess the function of this AH, we generated a point mutation in the AH (Ile341Ala), which introduced a less hydrophobic residue into the hydrophobic face to avoid complete disruption of the structure that would impair autophagy (Figure 2D). Strikingly, in the ccz1Ile341Ala mutant, no co-localization of GFP-Ccz1 with Erg6-marked LDs or Pdr16-positive LDs was observed during stationary phase (Figures 2E–2G). These data demonstrate that the specific amphipathic character of the AH in Ccz1 facilitates its targeting to LDs. Furthermore, Pdr16-LDs accumulated significantly in the ccz1Ile341Ala mutant, while the number of total Erg6-labeled LDs increased only slightly, though significantly (Figures 2E–2H). A conserved AH in Ccz1 facilitates the recruitment of Mon1-Ccz1 to LDs

To determine the function of Ypt7 on LDs, we next investigated how Mon1-Ccz1 targets LDs. Mon1-Ccz1 is recruited to endosomes by Rab5 proteins and to autophagosomes through interaction with Atg8 (LC3 in mammals).33,34 We, therefore, first investigated whether the endocytic or autophagy pathway, specifically Rab5 or Atg8, is required for Mon1-Ccz1 targeting to LDs during stationary phase. To test this, we analyzed the co-localization of GFP-Ccz1 with Erg6-mKate in wild-type and various mutant cells during both log and stationary phases. Seipin is a protein involved in LD formation, and its absence disrupts normal LD growth and maturation, resulting in the formation of larger, aberrant LDs.48,49,50,51,52 Notably, in cells lacking Sei1, the yeast seipin homolog, Ccz1 formed elongated structures in stationary phase, rather than the ring-like structures overlapping and surrounding LDs in wild-type cells. However, these elongated Ccz1 structures still co-localized with Erg6-marked LDs (Figures S2A–S2C). Mon1-Ccz1 targets PI3P-positive endosomal membranes via interaction with Rab5 proteins.53 Consistent with previous studies, loss of Mon1, Vps21/Rab5, or the CORVET subunit Vps8 (a Rab5 effector protein) strongly re-localized Ccz1 to the cytosol under growing conditions28,45 (Figure S2A). In contrast, Ccz1 remained punctate, likely representing endosomes, in atg1Δ or atg8Δ cells with impaired autophagy. Interestingly, in these mutants, Ccz1 was still present on LDs during stationary phase, similar to wild-type cells (Figures S2A and S2B). These data suggest that Mon1-Ccz1 localization to LDs during stationary phase does not require the endocytic or autophagy pathways or its known interacting partners, Rab5 proteins and Atg8; however, normal LD biogenesis is essential for its distribution on LDs.

Proteins that target LDs from the cytoplasm often contain AHs. These AHs, characterized by opposing hydrophilic and hydrophobic faces, enable proteins to bind to the LD surface by inserting their hydrophobic residues into the monolayer—a mechanism important for various cellular processes involving lipid storage and metabolism.54 Interestingly, a conserved C-terminal AH was identified in Ccz1, which is required for Mon1-Ccz1 function in autophagy but not endosomal maturation35 (Figure 2A). We asked whether Mon1-Ccz1 targeting to LDs depends on this AH. To examine this, we first tested whether the AH alone is sufficient to target Mon1-Ccz1 to LDs. We compared the localization of a GFP-tagged Ccz1 fragment (amino acids 270–403) containing the AH35 with that of full-length Ccz1, relative to Erg6-mKate, under both growing and stationary phase conditions. Intriguingly, the Ccz1 fragment strongly co-localized with LDs, even in log phase, whereas no co-localization was observed with full-length Ccz1 under this condition. Furthermore, this co-localization was significantly higher with the Ccz1 fragment than with full-length Ccz1 during stationary phase (Figures 2B and 2C). These data strongly indicate that the AH in Ccz1 is sufficient to target Mon1-Ccz1 to LDs. To further investigate the direct interaction between the Ccz1 AH and lipid surfaces, we generated a GFP-Ccz1 construct with an inverted AH sequence. This inversion was designed to preserve the overall physicochemical properties of the AH, including its amphipathic character (Figure S2D), and, therefore, minimize disruption of its membrane adsorption properties.55 Notably, this inverted construct still localized to Erg6-marked LDs, although with reduced co-localization compared with wild-type Ccz1 (Figures S2E and S2F). This finding supports our model in which the Ccz1 AH contributes to the localization of Mon1-Ccz1 to LDs through direct interaction with the lipid surface. These data demonstrate that the specific amphipathic character of the AH in Ccz1 facilitates its targeting to LDs. Furthermore, Pdr16-LDs accumulated significantly in the ccz1Ile341Ala mutant, while the number of total Erg6-labeled LDs increased only slightly, though significantly (Figures 2E–2H). DiscussionLDs are central to cellular energy homeostasis, and their dynamic regulation—including formation, growth, and turnover—is essential for adaptation to fluctuating environmental conditions. While LD degradation by lipolysis and autophagy (called macrolipophagy) has been well studied, the direct degradation of LDs by vacuoles through invagination, termed microlipophagy, remains unclear. Here, we identify a pathway through which LDs are targeted for potential degradation via vacuole invagination during nutrient limitation. Specifically, we found that the Rab GTPase Ypt7, activated by its GEF complex Mon1-Ccz1, localizes to a subpopulation of LDs marked by the protein Pdr16 at the vacuolar surface. Deletion of MON1, CCZ1, or YPT7 resultes in the accumulation of these Pdr16-positive LDs. Further investigation revealed that an AH in Ccz1 facilitates its recruitment to LDs in both yeast and mammalian cells. Crucially, we discovered that the SNX-BAR retromer complex functions downstream of Ypt7 at the LD-vacuole interface, potentially promoting LD invagination. Retromer and its cargo Vps10 co-localize with Pdr16 specifically under nutrient-limited conditions, and retromer co-localizes with Ypt7 at the tips of invaginated vacuoles. Loss of either retromer or Vps10 resultes in Pdr16-LD accumulation. In addition, disruption of retromer function causes massive accumulation of invaginated vesicles that remain attached to the vacuole membrane, accompanied by a significant accumulation of TAG, ergosterol esters, and DAG during stationary phase. These findings support a model in which Mon1-Ccz1-mediated activation of Ypt7 on Pdr16-LDs at the vacuolar surface recruits SNX-BAR retromer to the LD-vacuole interface. There, retromer, likely together with its cargo Vps10, facilitates LD internalization by promoting positive membrane curvature during late stage of vacuole invagination for potential subsequent LD degradation.Our data indicate that an AH within the C-terminal domain of Ccz1 contributes to its localization to LDs in both yeast and mammalian cells. In yeast cells, a GFP-tagged Ccz1 fragment (amino acids 270–403) containing the AH strongly co-localizes with LDs during both log and stationary phases, whereas full-length Ccz1 targets LDs only during stationary phase. This suggests a regulatory mechanism by which Ccz1 is excluded from LDs during log phase. The specificity of this AH for LD targeting is striking as the same AH is required for Mon1-Ccz1 targeting to autophagosomes,27,47 yet a subtle reduction in AH hydrophobicity specifically abolishes LD targeting without affecting autophagic function. This differential targeting likely reflects differences in the lipid composition and membrane packing of LDs and autophagosomes. Alternatively, Mon1-Ccz1 may interact with a specific partner on LDs, a possibility we have not yet excluded.Notably, the ccz1Ile341Ala mutation causes the accumulation of Pdr16-positive LDs in yeast cells, and the corresponding human CCZ1Leu287Ala mutation similarly accumulates LDs in COS7 cells. However, it remains unknown whether these accumulated LDs in COS7 cells are equivalent to yeast Pdr16-LDs.Furthermore, we identify the SNX-BAR retromer complex as a crucial effector at the LD-vacuole interface. Retromer localizes to Pdr16-LDs at the periphery of NVJ and co-localizes with LDO during stationary phase. Consistent with a potential role in LD degradation, loss of retromer results in a significant increase in TAG, ergosterol esters, and DAG during stationary phase. It is important to note that the increase in TAG levels during the stationary phase occurs even in wild-type cells, indicating a simultaneous increase in lipid synthesis. Thus, steady-state TAG levels alone are not a straightforward indicator of degradation activity. The more telling comparison is between wild-type and retromer-mutant cells; the latter show a significant accumulation of TAG and a unique build-up of DAG and ergosterol esters during the stationary phase. This suggests that these neutral lipids, which might be degraded via lipophagy in wild-type cells, are not effectively processed in the mutant. The accumulation of ergosterol esters, in particular, underscores a disruption in normal lipid degradation pathways, as these esters are well-documented substrates of lipophagy. Their stable levels in wild-type cells, contrasted with their accumulation in mutant cells, indicate a blockade in LD degradation in the absence of retromer. While steady-state lipidomics provides valuable insights, it does not differentiate between lipid synthesis and degradation rates. Future studies using pulse-chase techniques could offer detailed insights into these dynamics. Additionally, the retromer-mutant phenotype is marked by an accumulation of LDs and invaginated vesicles connected to the vacuolar membrane. This observation is corroborated by our cryo-EM study in the ccz1Ile341Ala mutant, where LDs accumulated and showed partial engulfment by the vacuolar membrane, implying an obstruction in the invagination phase. Taken together, these findings emphasize that the retromer complex operates at a late stage of vacuolar invagination to facilitate subsequent LD degradation. This underscores its broader role in maintaining lipid homeostasis. Interestingly, we observed that retromer co-localizes with Ypt7 at the tips of invaginated vacuoles, suggesting a specific role at these membrane regions. Retromer is well known for mediating cargo recycling and for generating membrane curvature through its SNX-BAR subunits Vps5 and Vps17.36,72 We, therefore, propose that, together with its cargo, retromer creates positive curvature at the tips of the invaginated vacuole “hugging” LDs. LDs are central to cellular energy homeostasis, and their dynamic regulation—including formation, growth, and turnover—is essential for adaptation to fluctuating environmental conditions. While LD degradation by lipolysis and autophagy (called macrolipophagy) has been well studied, the direct degradation of LDs by vacuoles through invagination, termed microlipophagy, remains unclear. Here, we identify a pathway through which LDs are targeted for potential degradation via vacuole invagination during nutrient limitation. Specifically, we found that the Rab GTPase Ypt7, activated by its GEF complex Mon1-Ccz1, localizes to a subpopulation of LDs marked by the protein Pdr16 at the vacuolar surface. Deletion of MON1, CCZ1, or YPT7 resultes in the accumulation of these Pdr16-positive LDs. Further investigation revealed that an AH in Ccz1 facilitates its recruitment to LDs in both yeast and mammalian cells. Crucially, we discovered that the SNX-BAR retromer complex functions downstream of Ypt7 at the LD-vacuole interface, potentially promoting LD invagination. Retromer and its cargo Vps10 co-localize with Pdr16 specifically under nutrient-limited conditions, and retromer co-localizes with Ypt7 at the tips of invaginated vacuoles. Loss of either retromer or Vps10 resultes in Pdr16-LD accumulation. In addition, disruption of retromer function causes massive accumulation of invaginated vesicles that remain attached to the vacuole membrane, accompanied by a significant accumulation of TAG, ergosterol esters, and DAG during stationary phase. These findings support a model in which Mon1-Ccz1-mediated activation of Ypt7 on Pdr16-LDs at the vacuolar surface recruits SNX-BAR retromer to the LD-vacuole interface. There, retromer, likely together with its cargo Vps10, facilitates LD internalization by promoting positive membrane curvature during late stage of vacuole invagination for potential subsequent LD degradation.

Our data indicate that an AH within the C-terminal domain of Ccz1 contributes to its localization to LDs in both yeast and mammalian cells. In yeast cells, a GFP-tagged Ccz1 fragment (amino acids 270–403) containing the AH strongly co-localizes with LDs during both log and stationary phases, whereas full-length Ccz1 targets LDs only during stationary phase. This suggests a regulatory mechanism by which Ccz1 is excluded from LDs during log phase. The specificity of this AH for LD targeting is striking as the same AH is required for Mon1-Ccz1 targeting to autophagosomes,27,47 yet a subtle reduction in AH hydrophobicity specifically abolishes LD targeting without affecting autophagic function. This differential targeting likely reflects differences in the lipid composition and membrane packing of LDs and autophagosomes. Alternatively, Mon1-Ccz1 may interact with a specific partner on LDs, a possibility we have not yet excluded.

Notably, the ccz1Ile341Ala mutation causes the accumulation of Pdr16-positive LDs in yeast cells, and the corresponding human CCZ1Leu287Ala mutation similarly accumulates LDs in COS7 cells. However, it remains unknown whether these accumulated LDs in COS7 cells are equivalent to yeast Pdr16-LDs.

Furthermore, we identify the SNX-BAR retromer complex as a crucial effector at the LD-vacuole interface. Retromer localizes to Pdr16-LDs at the periphery of NVJ and co-localizes with LDO during stationary phase. Consistent with a potential role in LD degradation, loss of retromer results in a significant increase in TAG, ergosterol esters, and DAG during stationary phase. It is important to note that the increase in TAG levels during the stationary phase occurs even in wild-type cells, indicating a simultaneous increase in lipid synthesis. Thus, steady-state TAG levels alone are not a straightforward indicator of degradation activity. The more telling comparison is between wild-type and retromer-mutant cells; the latter show a significant accumulation of TAG and a unique build-up of DAG and ergosterol esters during the stationary phase. This suggests that these neutral lipids, which might be degraded via lipophagy in wild-type cells, are not effectively processed in the mutant. The accumulation of ergosterol esters, in particular, underscores a disruption in normal lipid degradation pathways, as these esters are well-documented substrates of lipophagy. Their stable levels in wild-type cells, contrasted with their accumulation in mutant cells, indicate a blockade in LD degradation in the absence of retromer. While steady-state lipidomics provides valuable insights, it does not differentiate between lipid synthesis and degradation rates. Future studies using pulse-chase techniques could offer detailed insights into these dynamics. Additionally, the retromer-mutant phenotype is marked by an accumulation of LDs and invaginated vesicles connected to the vacuolar membrane. This observation is corroborated by our cryo-EM study in the ccz1Ile341Ala mutant, where LDs accumulated and showed partial engulfment by the vacuolar membrane, implying an obstruction in the invagination phase. Taken together, these findings emphasize that the retromer complex operates at a late stage of vacuolar invagination to facilitate subsequent LD degradation. This underscores its broader role in maintaining lipid homeostasis. Interestingly, we observed that retromer co-localizes with Ypt7 at the tips of invaginated vacuoles, suggesting a specific role at these membrane regions. Retromer is well known for mediating cargo recycling and for generating membrane curvature through its SNX-BAR subunits Vps5 and Vps17.36,72 We, therefore, propose that, together with its cargo, retromer creates positive curvature at the tips of the invaginated vacuole “hugging” LDs. This curvature drives the formation of tubular extensions that facilitate the final step of vacuolar invagination and enable LD internalization. Supporting this model, we detected the canonical retromer cargo Vps10 at the LD-vacuole interface. Loss of Vps10 caused accumulation of Pdr16-positive LDs adjacent to the vacuole. Vps10 is a sorting receptor that shuttles cargo between the Golgi and endosomes, and its retrieval from the vacuole depends on both retromer and Ypt7.79 We, therefore, suggest that Vps10, together with retromer-generated SNX-BAR tubules, removes an as-yet-unknown vacuolar cargo and provides the membrane curvature necessary for the late stage of invagination, thereby permitting degradation of Pdr16-associated LDs. We note, however, that the buildup of Pdr16-LDs in vps10Δ cells might reflect secondary consequences of other Vps10 functions; so, indirect effects cannot be ruled out at this stage.

Our results extend recent reports that vCLIPs, composed of LDO and Vac8, are essential for microlipophagy.17,18 Deletion of LDO or VAC8 causes mis-localization of Ccz1, Ypt7, and the retromer complex to Pdr16-positive LDs during stationary phase, whereas the ccz1Ile341Ala mutant and loss of retromer do not disrupt vCLIP positioning. These observations suggest that the Ypt7-retromer complex may function downstream of vCLIP in the LD-vacuole degradation pathway. Although the precise interplay between vCLIPs and the Ypt7-retromer axis remains to be defined, it is likely that the two systems cooperate to coordinate LD targeting and degradation. In this model, the Ypt7-retromer complex could act at vCLIPs to sharpen LD positioning and promote membrane invagination required for LD clearance.

Future studies will focus on elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms by which Ypt7 and retromer promote LD invagination into the vacuole, identifying the specific retromer and Vps10 interaction partners involved, and determining how these proteins are regulated at membrane contact sites. Understanding these processes will provide valuable insights into the complex interplay between LD metabolism, organelle communication, and cellular adaptation to stress.

Lipid extraction and quantitative LC-MS/MS analysisYeast lipids were extracted with chloroform:methanol as described previously (2:1, v/v).84 Briefly, two OD600 units of yeast cells were harvested and resuspended in 0.5 mL MS-grade methanol. Cell lysis was achieved via bead-beating, and the resulting lysates were transferred to glass tubes. Chloroform and citric acid were used to achieve phase separation. The bottom organic phase containing lipids was collected and dried using a vacuum concentrator system (Beijing JM Tech). The dried lipid extracts were reconstituted in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of methanol and dichloromethane supplemented with 5 mM ammonium acetate. Quantitative analysis was performed using Lipidomix standards (Avanti Polar Lipids) as internal spike-in controls. Chromatographic separation was carried out on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 130 Å, 2.1 mm × 50 mm; Waters) coupled to a QTRAP 6500+ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX). The mobile phases consisted of Buffer A (methanol/acetonitrile/water, 1:1:1, v/v/v, with 5 mM ammonium acetate) and Buffer B (100% isopropanol with 5 mM ammonium acetate). The flow rate was set at 0.15 mL/min with the following gradient program: 0–1 min, 20% B; 1–3 min, 20%–60% B; 3–13 min, 60%–98% B; 13.0–13.1 min, 98%–20% B; and 13.1–16 min, equilibration at 20% B. Data acquisition was conducted in positive ionization mode using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions specific for diacylglycerols (DAG), triacylglycerols (TAG), and ergosterol esters. Lipid species were identified by comparing retention times with authentic standards, and quantification was performed based on peak areas using the software OS v3.4 (AB SCIEX).
