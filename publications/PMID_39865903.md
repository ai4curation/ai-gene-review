# Heterozygous variants in AP4S1 are not associated with a neurological phenotype.

Biallelic loss-of-function variants in AP4S1 cause childhood-onset hereditary
spastic paraplegia. A recent report suggested that heterozygous AP4S1 variants
lead to a syndrome of lower limb spasticity and dysregulation of sphincter
function. We critically evaluate this claim against clinical observations in 28
heterozygous carriers of the same AP4S1 variant (NM_007077.3: c.289C>T,
p.Arg97Ter). In these 14 males and 14 females (mean age: 37.6 ± 4.9 years [SD],
range: 30-50 years), we ascertain no increased prevalence of neurological
manifestations. Alternative causes should be considered when evaluating patients
with heterozygous AP4S1 variants and neurological symptoms, as misattribution of
pathogenicity can impact clinical care and genetic counseling.

Introduction

Hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP) encompasses a diverse group of inherited neurological disorders characterized by progressive spasticity and weakness of the lower limbs. To date, more than 90 genetic loci have been implicated in HSP, exhibiting varying inheritance patterns and a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations. The genetic landscape of HSP continues to expand, with ongoing discoveries providing insight in its complex pathogenesis.

Spastic paraplegia type 52 (SPG52) is an autosomal‐recessive form of childhood‐onset complex HSP caused by biallelic (homozygous or compound‐heterozygous) loss‐of‐function variants in the AP4S1 gene.

,
2
,
3
 The AP4S1 gene encodes the σ1 subunit of the adaptor protein complex 4 (AP‐4), which plays a crucial role in protein trafficking within neurons.
4
 Clinical features of SPG52 include early‐onset progressive lower limb spasticity, global developmental delay, epilepsy, and developmental brain malformations, reflecting the essential role of AP‐4 in neuronal function and development.

In a recent report, Popescu described two members of the same family (son and mother) affected by spastic paraparesis, urinary and bowel dysfunction and a developmental coordination disorder, both heterozygous carriers of a previously reported likely pathogenic nonsense mutation in AP4S1 (NM_007077.3: c.289C>T, p.Arg97Ter).
5
 The author suggested that heterozygous variants in AP4S1 may be associated with an HSP phenotypes, implying a possible autosomal‐dominant inheritance pattern. Such claims have significant implications for genetic counseling, diagnosis and clinical management. Determining the pathogenicity of heterozygous variants is thus crucial to avoid misclassification and ensure accurate patient care.

ResultsThe 28 heterozygous carriers of the c.289C>T (p.Arg97Ter) variant in AP4S1, all parents of individuals with SPG52, were asymptomatic and showed no significant neurological impairment. This includes 14 female and 14 male individuals, with a mean age of 37.6 ± 4.9 years (SD) at the time of evaluation, spanning a range of 30–50 years. The cohort included individuals of diverse ethnic background (16 White [4 Spanish, 4 Romanian, 2 Turkish, 2 Polish, 2 Ashkenazi‐Jewish, and 2 Sephardic‐Jewish], 6 Asian [4 Punjabi‐Indian, 2 Japanese], and 6 mixed ethnic/racial background).Detailed neurological history and examinations revealed normal muscle tone and strength, with no signs of spasticity, hyperreflexia, ankle clonus, Babinski sign, or coordination deficits. Clinical history indicated no urinary or fecal incontinence, and no history of developmental delays or intellectual disability was identified.The c.289C>T (p.Arg97Ter) variant did not co‐segregate with neurological phenotypes within families. Only individuals with biallelic (homozygous or compound‐heterozygous) variants were clinically affected, while heterozygous carriers remained asymptomatic. This lack of co‐segregation indicates that the variant does not act in a dominant manner to cause HSP, supporting an autosomal‐recessive inheritance model for SPG52.

Only individuals with biallelic (homozygous or compound‐heterozygous) variants were clinically affected, while heterozygous carriers remained asymptomatic. This lack of co‐segregation indicates that the variant does not act in a dominant manner to cause HSP, supporting an autosomal‐recessive inheritance model for SPG52.

DiscussionThe absence of neurological symptoms in heterozygous carriers of the AP4S1 c.289C>T (p.Arg97Ter) variant, coupled with the lack of co‐segregation of the variant with the disease phenotype, suggests that haploinsufficiency of AP4S1
is insufficient to cause HSP. Only individuals with biallelic loss‐of‐function variants exhibit clinical manifestations of SPG52, reinforcing the autosomal‐recessive inheritance pattern.The gnomAD 4.1.0 (non‐UKB) database reports a maximum allele frequency of 3.122e‐4 in individuals of East Asian ancestry for above variant in AP4S1, with an allele count of 186/1,613,462. This relatively high occurrence in the general population strongly suggests that when occurring in a heterozygous state, this variant is not pathogenic. Remarkably, gnomAD includes multiple loss‐of‐function variants in AP4 genes (AP4S1, AP4M1, AP4B1, and AP4E1), many with allele counts >10. This relatively frequent presence implies that heterozygous loss‐of‐function variants are generally not pathogenic. Furthermore, the broad ancestral distributions in our cohort make it unlikely that a single hypomorphic allele or haplotype, specific to one ancestral population, is co‐segregating with the c.289C>T (p.Arg97Ter) variant to modulate disease risk. is insufficient to cause HSP. Only individuals with biallelic loss‐of‐function variants exhibit clinical manifestations of SPG52, reinforcing the autosomal‐recessive inheritance pattern.

The gnomAD 4.1.0 (non‐UKB) database reports a maximum allele frequency of 3.122e‐4 in individuals of East Asian ancestry for above variant in AP4S1, with an allele count of 186/1,613,462. This relatively high occurrence in the general population strongly suggests that when occurring in a heterozygous state, this variant is not pathogenic. Remarkably, gnomAD includes multiple loss‐of‐function variants in AP4 genes (AP4S1, AP4M1, AP4B1, and AP4E1), many with allele counts >10. This relatively frequent presence implies that heterozygous loss‐of‐function variants are generally not pathogenic. Additionally, the AstraZeneca PheWAS database (UKB 500k v2) shows no gene‐ or variant‐level associations between the AP4 subunit genes and phenotypes categorized under the nervous system in the ICD‐10.

Even when considering the possibility of an ancestry‐linked hypomorphic allele in trans with the p.Arg97Ter variant, the lack of any clinical phenotype in our heterozygous cohort and the absence of known hypomorphic alleles in AP4S1 that segregate with disease in a population‐specific manner do not support this scenario. Furthermore, the broad ancestral distributions in our cohort make it unlikely that a single hypomorphic allele or haplotype, specific to one ancestral population, is co‐segregating with the c.289C>T (p.Arg97Ter) variant to modulate disease risk. This diverse ancestral distribution, coupled with consistent asymptomatic presentations across all groups, further supports our conclusion that heterozygous AP4S1 loss‐of‐function variants do not confer a neurological phenotype.

Further supporting these clinical observations, in vitro assays have previously demonstrated that haploinsufficiency of AP4S1 in cells does not lead to any functional deficiency in the AP‐4 complex.

Misattributing pathogenicity to heterozygous variants in known recessive genes like AP4S1 can lead to several adverse outcomes, including inappropriate changes to care management and mental well‐being associated with the receipt of a genetic diagnosis.
10

We therefore strongly believe further investigations are required before suggesting that heterozygous variants in AP4S1 are associated with a neurological phenotype. The report by Popescu provides an insufficient basis for this hypothesis. Further research in large prospective natural history studies is needed to conclusively determine the pathogenicity of heterozygous AP4S1 variants.
