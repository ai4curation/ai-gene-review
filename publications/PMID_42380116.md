The yeast Swi-Snf co-activator was the first chromatin remodelling complex
discovered which can disrupt promoter nucleosomes to enable transcription. The
Tup1-Cyc8 complex was the first global co-repressor identified which can
position nucleosomes across promoters to block transcription. However, the
global extent of their antagonistic regulation of gene transcription in yeast is
unknown. We have identified the genes repressed by Tup1-Cyc8 and activated by
Swi-Snf in glucose-grown cells which include inactive and actively transcribed
genes. We show that Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 are present at both active and
inactive gene promoters, where SNF2 is required for full Tup1-Cyc8 occupancy.
The influence of Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 upon nucleosome positioning is specific
to each co-regulated gene and extends over the available upstream intergenic
region. We propose Tup1-Cyc8 and Swi-Snf are constitutively present at
co-regulated gene promoters where their interplay influences the entire
intergenic upstream chromatin environment of target genes and transcription.

Introduction

The DNA of eukaryotic cells exists as a nucleoprotein complex called chromatin. The fundamental subunit of chromatin is the nucleosome which comprises ~147 bp of DNA wrapped around an octamer of two each of the four core histone proteins; H2A, H2B, H3 and H41. This structure can form higher order structures which packages the DNA within the nucleus and is considered refractory to processes including DNA replication, repair, recombination and gene transcription2–4. However, chromatin is dynamic and can be altered to allow or deny access to the underlying DNA when required.

This alteration, or remodelling, of chromatin structure can be achieved by ATP-dependent chromatin remodelling complexes, which use the energy from ATP hydrolysis to either slide, disrupt, or completely remove nucleosomes to enable access to the DNA5–7. The first ATP-dependent chromatin remodelling complex discovered was the yeast Swi-Snf complex, which is generally considered to be a co-activator of gene transcription via its remodelling of nucleosomes at promoters to enable transcription initiation8–11. In yeast, Swi-Snf is required for the activation of 10% of the ~6000 protein-encoding genes in glucose-grown cells, and numerous other genes in response to changes in the cells’ environment12–15.

The 1.1 MDa Swi-Snf complex is made up of 12 sub-units. The Snf2p subunit is the ATPase-containing, catalytic heart of the complex, which provides the energy to drive the nucleosome remodelling6,16–18. The Swi1p and Swi3p subunits form the backbone of the complex, whilst Snf6p is required for association of Snf2p with this backbone19,20. The Snf5p subunit is involved in recruitment of Swi-Snf to target genes in conjunction with gene–specific transcriptional activators19. Snf2p also contains a bromodomain which can promote occupancy of the complex at acetylated chromatin regions commonly found at active gene promoters thereby influencing recruitment and reinforcing association of Swi-Snf at target genes21,22. Together, the different subunits form distinct modules which ensure correct Swi-Snf recruitment, stability, and activity occur at target genes to elicit transcription23.

Conversely, the yeast Tup1-Cyc8 complex was one of the first global co-repressors of transcription discovered24,25. Tup1-Cyc8 represses 5-10% of yeast genes, including genes involved in sugar metabolism, DNA damage, flocculation, mating type selection, meiosis, and those that respond to oxygen depletion26–29. This 1 MDa complex is composed of 1 subunit of Cyc8p and 4 Tup1p proteins and is recruited to target genes by sequence-specific DNA binding proteins30,31. The Tup1p C-terminus contains seven WD40 motifs which mediates interactions with other proteins, whilst the N-terminus is required for Tup1p multimerisation and interaction with Cyc8p32,33. Cyc8p contains 10 tetratricopeptide (TPR) repeats, on which differing combinations of association with Tup1p can determine repression of specific genes34,35. The generally accepted model for Tup1-Cyc8 activity proposes that Cyc8p mediates recruitment to specific genes, whilst Tup1p elicits repression of transcription25,36. However, evidence suggesting that Tup1p and Cyc8p can function independently from each other indicates that the model is more complex28.

Tup1-Cyc8 repression has been proposed to occur via multiple mechanisms, including recruitment of histone deacetylases (HDACs), establishment of ordered nucleosomal arrays over promoters, and direct inhibition of RNA polymerase II (Pol II)37–41. However, these mechanisms of action are not necessarily mutually exclusive, and there is evidence of some genes being subject to multiple mechanisms of repression by Tup1-Cyc842. It has also been proposed that Tup1-Cyc8 acts by passively blocking activating factors to potentially unify the mechanisms described above43.

Both of these complexes are evolutionary conserved from yeast to humans and, when mutated in human cells, diseases and developmental disorders can occur24,44. For example, mutations in human Swi-Snf complex subunits have been found in 20% of all cancers, whilst mutations in human transducin-like enhancers of split (TLE) proteins, which are ancestors of Tup1p, are also associated with various cancers45–49.

The yeast Tup1-Cyc8 and Swi-Snf complexes, therefore, represent archetypal global regulators of transcription responsible for switching genes on and off in yeast and human cells. However, aside from a few gene-specific studies in yeast, the full extent of how these two complexes antagonistically regulate gene transcription is unknown13,50,51.

We have identified 207 genes subject to Swi-Snf activation and Tup1-Cyc8 repression in glucose-grown yeast. These include genes which were repressed in wt, and genes which were active in wt at levels determined by the net positive and negative influence of Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8, respectively. ChIP analysis revealed that Snf2p and Tup1-Cyc8 were present at the same regions at both active and inactive co-regulated gene promoters in wt, where SNF2 was required for full Tup1p and Cyc8p occupancy. We show that Snf2p and Cyc8p can interact with each other and demonstrate that the nucleosome remodelling activities by Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 are specific to each target gene and extend over the available upstream intergenic region. Furthermore, we show that co-regulated genes located nearest to telomeres, and which have the longest gene-free upstream intergenic regions, are subject to the most robust regulation by Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8. These data suggest Tup1-Cyc8 and Swi-Snf occupancy and activity are intimately intertwined, whereby they influence the available upstream intergenic chromatin environment of target genes and determine the transcriptional outcome.

Antagonistic chromatin remodelling events by Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 are gene-specific

The lack of a clear remodelling signature by Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 at the co-regulated gene promoters could reflect distinct nucleosome positioning and remodelling events occurring at these genes which obscure any consensus signature when averaged. To determine the extent of co-regulated gene repression by CYC8, we examined the levels of their de-repression in snf2 cyc8 compared to snf2 (Fig. 8a-d). This should reveal upregulation due to the sole loss of CYC8, independent of any influence from SNF2. Consistent with published data, the co-regulated genes located nearest to telomeres were de-repressed to the greatest extent, suggesting that these genes were the most strongly repressed (Fig. 8a, b)40. Analysis of repression by CYC8 of the co-regulated genes when separated into the FLO1- and SED1-type cohorts revealed that, on average, there was no difference in the levels of de-repression (Fig. 8c), suggesting both types of gene were subject to the same extent of CYC8-dependent repression.

When the SED1- and FLO1 type genes were separated into those located within 50 kb or out with 50 kb of a telomere (Fig. 8d), the SED1- and FLO1-type genes located within 50 kb of the telomere were both equally de-repressed, and to the greatest extent, compared to the FLO1- and SED1-type genes located out with 50 kb of a telomere. Of the genes located furthest away from the telomere, the SED1-type genes were de-repressed to the greatest extent compared to the FLO1 type genes located at this location (Fig. 8d).

Thus, on average, all antagonistically regulated genes located nearest telomeres were subject to an equally strong repression by CYC8 whereas, of the weaker repressed genes located further than 50 kb from a telomere, the SED1 type genes were subject to greatest repression by CYC8 (Fig. 8e).

To assess the extent of the positive influence by SNF2 upon co-regulated gene transcription we compared the fold changes of the genes in snf2 cyc8 versus cyc8 (Fig. 8f - i). By comparing the drop in transcription of the co-regulated genes in the double mutant compared to cyc8, this should reveal the extent of the SNF2-dependent positive influence upon gene transcription in the absence of CYC8.

Firstly, this analysis revealed that the average fold decrease in transcription in snf2 cyc8 versus cyc8 was greatest for co-regulated genes located within 50 kb of a telomere, suggesting co-regulated genes located nearest to the telomere were subject to the strongest activation by Swi-Snf (Fig. 8f, g). When the co-regulated genes were separated into the FLO1- and SED1-type genes, the FLO1-type genes showed the greatest drop in transcription in snf2 cyc8 versus cyc8 suggesting that, overall, the FLO1-type genes were subject to the greatest activation by Swi-Snf (Fig. 8h).

Finally, when the SED1- and FLO1-type genes were separated into those located within or out with 50 kb of a telomere, there was no difference in the levels of down regulation of the FLO1- and SED1-type genes in snf2 cyc8 versus cyc8 when either gene type was located nearest the telomere (Fig. 8i, see genes within 50 kb). However, of the genes located out with 50 kb from the telomere, the FLO1-type genes were down-regulated to the greatest extent compared to the SED1-type genes located at this telomere distant location (Fig. 8i, see genes outside 50 kb). Thus, Swi-Snf exerts the greatest positive influence upon all co-regulated genes located nearest to the telomere, and the FLO1-type genes located furthest away from the telomere (Fig. 8j).

Together, these data suggest that Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 co-regulated genes located nearest to telomeres which, on average, have the longest gene-free upstream regions, are subject to the strongest activation by Swi-Snf and the greatest repression by Tup1-Cyc8.

We have identified 207 genes subject to activation by Swi-Snf and repression by Tup1-Cyc8 in glucose-grown yeast. These antagonistically regulated genes include active and repressed genes such as SED1 and FLO1 respectively13. Considering that many of the antagonistically regulated genes repressed by Tup1-Cyc8 in wt are stress response genes, these genes will presumably be activated in a Swi-Snf-dependent manner in response to signals which are absent in the conditions used here64.

Our initial analysis suggested that the FLO1-type genes were subject to a mutually exclusive regulatory mechanism whereby Swi-Snf activates transcription following its recruitment in the absence of Tup1-Cyc8, whilst the SED1-type genes were regulated by the concerted positive and negative influence upon transcription by Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8. In support of this interpretation, Tup1p, Cyc8p, and Snf2p were all detected by ChIP-exo at the actively transcribed SED1-type gene promoters in wt, whereas only Tup1p and Cyc8p were found at the inactive FLO1-type gene promoters in wt54. However, gene-specific ChIP analysis found a small but significant peak of Snf2p at the repressed FLO1 and FLO9 genes in wt, whilst further probing of the ChIP-exo data revealed other Swi-Snf subunits could also be detected at these inactive genes. Together, these data suggest that Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 complexes are constitutively present at antagonistically regulated genes whether active or inactive.

The absence of a signal for Snf2p in the ChIP-exo data at the FLO1 type genes in wt compared to the signal for Snf2p detected via gene-specific ChIP could reflect our use of an antibody against the Snf2p N-terminus and a snf2 deletion mutant control. These data also highlight that the Swi-Snf subunits have differing amenities to detection by ChIP, with Snf2p being particularly difficult to detect. This raises the question as to whether the weak signal detected for Snf2p at the inactive FLO1 and FLO9 genes in wt is because there is a low level of Swi-Snf at these inactive gene promoters or is due to epitope masking of high Snf2p occupancy at these genes65. For example, Snf2p could be masked by the other Swi-Snf complex subunits, or by the repressive chromatin architecture at the inactive FLO1 type gene promoters. In support of this latter situation, Tup1p and Cyc8p are undetectable by ChIP at the well characterised SUC2 gene when it is inactive due to Tup1-Cyc8 dependent glucose repression28,54. The Tup1-Cyc8 complex could also be masking Snf2p detection, with the increased Snf2p signal after Cyc8p depletion via anchor away being the result of its unmasking in the absence of Cyc8p. This would be consistent with the mechanism for Tup1-Cyc8 function where it has been proposed to mask activation domains of transcription factors to inhibit transcription43. Alternatively, it could be that the association and residence time of Swi-Snf at these inactive co-regulated genes is transient, leading to the low detection signal66,67.

Tup1-Cyc8 does not bind DNA directly at target genes, but is recruited via gene specific transcription factors which interact with Cyc8p to which Tup1p is bound24,36. This model predicts that in the absence of Cyc8p, Tup1p should not be present at target genes. However, our analysis confirmed published data showing that Tup1p can occupy genes in the absence of Cyc8p28. Furthermore, we showed that SNF2 was required for full Tup1p (and Cyc8p) occupancy at co-regulated genes in the presence or absence of Cyc8p. These data raise the possibility that Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 are located together at the promoters of active and inactive antagonistically regulated genes and that Swi-Snf is directly required for full Tup1-Cyc8 occupancy at these co-regulated genes.

To test this hypothesis, sequential ChIP analysis could be used to confirm Tup1-Cyc8 and Swi-Snf co-occupancy at the co-regulated gene promoters68. Secondly, although the Co-IP and Turbo-ID support Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 as being in close proximity within the cell, it does not prove the two complexes directly interact. Indeed, the role of SNF2 influencing Tup1-Cyc8 occupancy could be indirect via transcription of other genes involved in the recruitment or stability of Tup1-Cyc8 at co-regulated genes. Furthermore, the decreases in cellular Tup1p and Cyc8p levels detected in snf2 mutants could result in an altered protein stoichiometry at target genes which is enough to displace, and possibly re-distribute, Tup1p and Cyc8p.

Regardless, potential constitutive co-occupancy of genes by both Tup1-Cyc8 and Swi-Snf would enable dynamic fine-tuning of on-going transcription and allow rapid switching on and off of target genes. This would be relevant to the numerous stress-response genes within the antagonistically regulated gene cohort, enabling the cell to rapidly respond to changes in the environment. The interplay between Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 shown here, when coupled with evidence of Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 also having respective repressive and activator functions, blurs the strict definitions of Tup1-Cyc8 being a co-repressor and Swi-Snf being a co-activator28,69–73. Long-range chromatin remodelling by Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 has been shown to occur over a 5 kb upstream region during changes in gene transcription at the FLO1 and SUC2 genes13,51. Tup1-Cyc8 and Isw2p have been shown to position nucleosomes over the promoter and gene coding region of the repressed RNR3 gene, which Swi-Snf disrupts following RNR3 induction14,79. Long-range remodelling by Swi-Snf and Isw1p has also been observed at the HIS3 gene, where induction of transcription was accompanied by the disruption of nucleosomes over the entire gene sequence and flanking regions60,61. The data here furthers these findings and suggests that the ability of Tup1-Cyc8 and Swi-Snf to influence chromatin over extensive regions could be a general behaviour of these complexes.

A caveat of this study is that we cannot ensure that all far upstream remodelling events attributed to Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 are relevant to the co-regulated genes. Indeed, our study will reveal chromatin remodelling events attributable to other factors, in addition to remodelling effects relevant to the promoter or distal areas of the upstream flanking genes. Furthermore, we show evidence of transcription occurring within the gene-free upstream regions in the cyc8 and cyc8 snf2 mutants that could contribute to the remodelling observed. Further studies will be needed to tease apart how the remodelling events reported here are relevant to each gene and if the intergenic transcription is a cause or a consequence of any of the long-range remodelling observed3,59,77,80.

On average, the co-regulated genes found nearest telomeres had the longest gene-free upstream regions and were subject to the strongest regulation by Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8. Relocating such genes to alternative chromosomal locations and measuring transcription would address if this distinct regulation is due to a position effect or is due to their promoter architecture. Answers to questions such as (i) how complexes located within 500 bp of gene TSS can bring about these more distant remodelling events and, (ii) how does the extent of remodelled chromatin allow stronger regulation, could lie in (i) looping of chromatin to bring into contact remodelers and chromatin at distant sites, and (ii) the acetylation status of the upstream gene regions influencing gene transcription40,53,81,82. Finally, the intergenic transcription observed could also contribute to the downstream gene regulation83–85.

In summary, we propose that the intimate interplay between Swi-Snf and Tup1-Cyc8 at active and inactive co-regulated gene promoters influences the available upstream chromatin environment and regulates target gene transcription.

MNase-seq digital indirect-end label (dIEL) processing and renderingIn addition to containing sequence read pairs derived from distinct MNase-protected chromatin species such as nucleosomes, the MNase-seq data also provides direct information on the accessibility of DNA regions such as nucleosome linkers and nucleosome free regions. Before the advent of next-generation sequencing, partial MNase digests of chromatin would have been separated according to size by gel electrophoresis and the accessibility of DNA regions assayed by visualising band intensity after Southern blotting with indirect-end label probes110. To recapitulate this process, all MNase-seq single R1 reads (without selection according to corresponding paired read insert size) were aligned to the R64 SacCer3 reference genome using bowtie-1.3.1 (command line flags -n 0 -k 1 --best --trim3 14 –sam), sorted into chromosome order using samtools-1.9, and then calculated the frequency distribution of read 5’ ends (MNase cleavage sites) binned every 10 bp across the genome and smoothed to a 3 bin moving average, using the script SAM2dIEL_sgr_full.plx. This allows rendering (Fig. 6d–f) in the Integrated Genome Browser using Transparent B/W Heat Map mode (Y Axis Scale Min = -40; Max =-20) in which MNase cleavage sites appear as dark “bands” of varying intensity creating a digital indirect-end-label facsimile – hence “dIEL”.

Mass spectrometry analysisSamples were analyzed by multidimensional protein identification technology (MudPIT) essentially as described112. In brief, samples were loaded offline onto three- phase MudPIT columns and peptides were separated using an Agilent 1260 Infinity quaternary HPLC pump with a combination of three buffer systems: Buffer A (5% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid), Buffer B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid), and Buffer C (5% acetonitrile, 500 mM ammonium acetate, 0.1% formic acid). Eluted peptides were analyzed on an Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) equipped with an electrospray ionisation source and operated in positive ion mode. Full MS scans were acquired using the Orbitrap mass analyzer over an m/z range of 400-1600. The 15 most intense precursor ions were selected for MS/MS analysis, using a dynamic exclusion time of 90 seconds. MS/MS spectra were acquired in the ion trap using collision-induced dissociation (CID) with a normalised collision energy of 35%.The resulting RAW data files were converted into MS2 peak list files using the in-house software RawDistiller (version 1.0)113. These peak list files were then searched using ProLuCID (version 1.3.3)114, with mass tolerances set to 10 ppm for precursor ions and 500 ppm for fragment ions. Only fully tryptic peptides were considered, with up to 3 missed cleavages allowed. The sequence database used for searching contained 5951 S. cerevisiae protein sequences downloaded from the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (2022-01-12 release), 426 common contaminants, and 6377 shuffled versions of all sequences for estimating false discovery rates. Database searches accounted for differential mass shifts of +15.9949 Da on methionine (oxidation) and +226.0776 Da on lysine (biotinylation), as well as a static mass shift of +57.0215 Da on cysteine (carbamidomethylation).Following database searches, the resulting.sqt files were analyzed using DTASelect (version 1.9)115 in conjunction with the in-house software Swallow113 to control false discovery rates (FDRs), which were maintained below 5% at both the peptide and protein levels. The mean peptide FDR levels were 0.26% (Swi3-TurboID samples) and 0.50% (Prp45-TurboID samples). The mean protein level FDR levels were 1.07% (Swi3-TurboID samples) and 1.65% (Prp45-TurboID samples). Relative protein abundance was quantified using a spectral counting–based approach. Replicate analyses were compared to controls using Contrast (version 1.9)115, alongside the in-house tool Sandmartin113. The resulting Contrast.txt files were processed using NSAF7x64113 to generate distributed normalised spectral abundance factor (dNSAF) values for proteins. The NSAF7x64 filter requires a minimum peptide length of 7. A minimum of either 2 distinct peptides or at least 1 peptide redundantly identified were required for protein identification. The statistical tool QPROT116 was then used to identify proteins significantly enriched in experimental samples relative to controls (log2FC > 1, Z-score > 1.645). The entire in-house software suite (Kite) used for the MudPIT mass spectrometry analysis is available in Zenodo113 (https://zenodo.org/records/5914885) and on GitHub (https://github.com/tzw-wen/kite/tree/master/kitelinux).
