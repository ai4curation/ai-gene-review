Angiopoietin-like 3 (ANGPTL3) inhibits lipases that hydrolyze triglycerides
(TGs) in TG-rich lipoproteins (TRLs). We evaluated TRL-TGs, TRL particle
(apolipoprotein B), palmitate, and glucose kinetics during a mixed-meal test
that included intravenous and oral tracer administrations in people with
extremely rare compound heterozygous ANGTPL3 loss-of-function mutations
(ANGPTL3-/- group, n = 3) and matched control participants (n = 7). Multi-organ
(liver, muscle, and adipose tissue) insulin sensitivity was evaluated with a
two-step hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp procedure and glucose and palmitate
tracer infusions. We find that plasma TG and TRL particle concentrations are
more than 10-fold lower in the ANGPTL3-/- than in the control group due to both
markedly reduced liver-derived TRL particle and TG secretion rates combined with
increased plasma clearance of both liver- and gut-derived TRLs. Palmitate and
glucose kinetics during the meal test are not different between the groups. We
conclude that the biological function of ANGPTL3 reaches beyond inhibiting
intravascular lipase activity.

Introduction

Triglyceride (TG)-rich lipoproteins (TRLs), which consist of large, gut-derived chylomicrons (defined as lipoprotein particles with a Svedberg flotation coefficient [Sf] > 400) and smaller, liver-derived very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) (i.e., particles with a Sf 20–400),1 are directly involved in the development of atherosclerosis.2,3 During TRL particle assembly, TGs are loaded onto a single apolipoprotein B (B-48 from the gut and B-100 from the liver and the gut) molecule that serves as a lipid carrier. Intravascular delipidation of TRL particles results in gradually smaller TG-poorer chylomicron remnants and small, TG-poorer VLDL and eventually even smaller, denser intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL) (Sf 12–20) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) (Sf < 12).1 In addition, there is direct production of small, VLDL-sized, gut-derived lipoprotein particles.4

Angiopoietin-like 3 (ANGPTL3) is a liver-derived circulating protein that inhibits lipoprotein lipase and endothelial lipase,5,6,7 which catalyze the hydrolysis of TGs in TRLs.8,9 People who carry rare (prevalence <0.5%) mutations that cause ANGPTL3 deficiency have low concentrations of TRL in plasma and a reduced risk of coronary atherosclerosis.10,11 Although accelerated plasma lipid clearance is likely the major cause of low circulating TRL in people with ANGPTL3 deficiency, results from studies conducted in mice and in cell culture systems, as well as in people who were treated with pharmacological inhibitors of ANGPTL3, suggest that ANGPTL3 also blocks hepatic VLDL assembly and secretion.12,13,14 Furthermore, based on data obtained in genetically modified mice and in mice treated with anti-ANGPTL3 antibodies, it has been proposed that ANGPTL3 increases (presumably caused by insulin resistance) adipose tissue-derived fatty acid supply to the liver for TG synthesis and secretion.12,15,16 The metabolic consequences of ANGPTL3 deficiency on lipid kinetics and multi-organ (liver/endogenous glucose production, muscle/glucose disposal, and adipose tissue/fatty acid release) insulin sensitivity in people are unknown.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effect of complete biological ANGPTL3 deficiency (through compound heterozygous loss-of-function mutations in ANGTPL3) on the metabolic response (TRL, palmitate, and glucose kinetics) to mixed-meal ingestion, assessed by using a mixed-meal test in conjunction with a complex multiple stable isotopically labeled tracer (intravenous glucose, palmitate, glycerol, and leucine; oral tripalmitin) protocol (Figure 1A). In addition, we evaluated (1) the 24-h metabolic profile (plasma glucose, insulin, and lipid) and (2) multi-organ insulin sensitivity (i.e., the inhibitory effect of insulin on endogenous/hepatic glucose production and fatty acid appearance in plasma and the stimulatory effect of insulin on plasma glucose disposal), assessed by using a two-stage (low- and high-dose insulin) hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp procedure (HECP) in conjunction with intravenous glucose and palmitate tracer infusions (Figure 1B).Figure 1Outline of the study protocols(A) Mixed-meal test with tracers administered intravenously and orally, as part of the meal that served as the first meal during the 24-h metabolic profiling protocol.(B) Two-stage hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp procedure in conjunction with tracer infusions. Both the meal test and the clamp procedure were initiated in the morning after participants fasted overnight. Abbreviation: BSA, body surface area.

DiscussionThe regulation of plasma lipid concentrations is complex and involves multiple tissues/organ systems and enzymes that are involved in their synthesis, assembly, and secretion into the bloodstream, intravascular modification, and removal from circulation. The critical involvement of ANGPTL3 in regulating plasma lipid metabolism was discovered in a mouse model with striking hypolipidemia that was attributed to the lack of an inhibitory effect of ANGPTL3 on intravascular TG lipases.17 ANGPTL3 inhibition has since garnered considerable attention as a therapeutic target for hyperlipidemia,17,18 but the full biological function of ANGPTL3 is still unclear. We leveraged our access to a small group of people with complete lifelong biological ANGPTL3 deficiency that provides an opportunity to examine the effect of ANGPTL3 on metabolic function. We used a multi-tracer mixed-meal protocol in conjunction with a two-step HECP that included tracer infusions to provide a comprehensive evaluation of postprandial TRL-TGs, TRL particle, fatty acid, glucose kinetics, and multi-organ insulin sensitivity. This study extends the work of other investigators who evaluated lipid concentrations during an oral high-fat challenge and noted a markedly reduced postprandial increase in TRL in people with complete lifelong biological ANGPTL3 deficiency.19,20 The results from our detailed kinetic analyses demonstrate that more than 10-fold lower postprandial TRL-TG and TRL particle concentrations in people with ANGPTL3 deficiency are caused by both much faster intravascular clearance of TRL-TG and TRL particles and a lower secretion rate of liver- but not gut-derived TRL particles and TRL-TGs. Insulin sensitivity and free fatty acid and glucose kinetics are not affected by ANGPLT3 deficiency. These data demonstrate that the biological function of ANGPTL3 in people reaches beyond its well-established inhibitory effect on intravascular lipase activity and provide new insights into our understanding of the regulation of lipoprotein metabolism in people.The observation that complete ANGPTL3 deficiency in people associated with markedly enhanced clearance of TRL-TG and TRL particles is not surprising given the results from studies in vitro and in genetically modified animal models.5 In fact, increased clearance of TRL is the presumed mechanism for the low plasma lipids associated with ANGPTL3 deficiency.5 However, to our knowledge, the effect of complete lifelong ANGPTL3 deficiency on TRL kinetics has so far not been evaluated in people. We found that the TRL-TG plasma clearance rate was about 4-fold higher in the ANGPTL3−/− group compared with the control group, but this difference in plasma clearance was insufficient to account for the much more pronounced (more than 10-fold) difference in plasma TRL-TG concentration. We found that liver- but not gut-derived TRL-TG secretion rate was also significantly lower in the ANGPTL3−/− group. Additionally, liver- but not gut-derived TRL particle (apoB) secretion rate was lower in the ANGPTL3−/− group. The regulation of plasma lipid concentrations is complex and involves multiple tissues/organ systems and enzymes that are involved in their synthesis, assembly, and secretion into the bloodstream, intravascular modification, and removal from circulation. The critical involvement of ANGPTL3 in regulating plasma lipid metabolism was discovered in a mouse model with striking hypolipidemia that was attributed to the lack of an inhibitory effect of ANGPTL3 on intravascular TG lipases.17 ANGPTL3 inhibition has since garnered considerable attention as a therapeutic target for hyperlipidemia,17,18 but the full biological function of ANGPTL3 is still unclear. We leveraged our access to a small group of people with complete lifelong biological ANGPTL3 deficiency that provides an opportunity to examine the effect of ANGPTL3 on metabolic function. We used a multi-tracer mixed-meal protocol in conjunction with a two-step HECP that included tracer infusions to provide a comprehensive evaluation of postprandial TRL-TGs, TRL particle, fatty acid, glucose kinetics, and multi-organ insulin sensitivity. This study extends the work of other investigators who evaluated lipid concentrations during an oral high-fat challenge and noted a markedly reduced postprandial increase in TRL in people with complete lifelong biological ANGPTL3 deficiency.19,20 The results from our detailed kinetic analyses demonstrate that more than 10-fold lower postprandial TRL-TG and TRL particle concentrations in people with ANGPTL3 deficiency are caused by both much faster intravascular clearance of TRL-TG and TRL particles and a lower secretion rate of liver- but not gut-derived TRL particles and TRL-TGs. Insulin sensitivity and free fatty acid and glucose kinetics are not affected by ANGPLT3 deficiency. These data demonstrate that the biological function of ANGPTL3 in people reaches beyond its well-established inhibitory effect on intravascular lipase activity and provide new insights into our understanding of the regulation of lipoprotein metabolism in people.

The observation that complete ANGPTL3 deficiency in people associated with markedly enhanced clearance of TRL-TG and TRL particles is not surprising given the results from studies in vitro and in genetically modified animal models.5 In fact, increased clearance of TRL is the presumed mechanism for the low plasma lipids associated with ANGPTL3 deficiency.5 However, to our knowledge, the effect of complete lifelong ANGPTL3 deficiency on TRL kinetics has so far not been evaluated in people. We found that the TRL-TG plasma clearance rate was about 4-fold higher in the ANGPTL3−/− group compared with the control group, but this difference in plasma clearance was insufficient to account for the much more pronounced (more than 10-fold) difference in plasma TRL-TG concentration. We found that liver- but not gut-derived TRL-TG secretion rate was also significantly lower in the ANGPTL3−/− group. Additionally, liver- but not gut-derived TRL particle (apoB) secretion rate was lower in the ANGPTL3−/− group. These data suggest that ANGPTL3 has profound effects on TRL metabolism that are not limited to the intravascular removal process but also target hepatic TRL secretion.

The mechanisms responsible for the lower secretion rate of liver-derived TRL particles and TGs in the participants with ANGPTL3 deficiency are not known. The available data from preclinical models (mice, HepG2 cells), including our own, suggest that increased presecretory apoB degradation, rather than decreased apoB synthesis, reduces VLDL particle secretion in ANGPTL3 deficiency.12,13 The mechanisms could also involve increased intrahepatic degradation of newly formed or plasma-derived lipids due to higher intracellular hepatic lipase activity. On the other hand, our data demonstrate a smaller contribution of plasma palmitate to TRL-TG palmitate in the ANGPTL3−/− group, presumably because of differences in intracellular fatty acid handling between the ANGPTL3−/− and control groups. Together, these findings suggest that intrahepatic fatty acid handling, rather than systemic fatty acid availability for TG synthesis, regulates hepatic TG secretion in people with ANGPTL3 deficiency. Channeling hepatic fatty acids toward pathways other than TG synthesis in people with ANGPTL3 deficiency could, at least in part, underlie the enhanced degradation of newly synthesized apoB in the liver, which occurs when TG supply is limited.26

The prevalence of diabetes/suspected diabetes in the ANGPTL3−/− group in our study is quite remarkable considering that diabetes is a major risk factor for hyperlipidemia,27 and vice versa, hypertriglyceridemia has been implicated in causing insulin resistance and T2D.28 The meal test results from our study demonstrate that the participants with diabetes or suspected diabetes had markedly impaired beta-cell function (insulin secretion rate in relation to plasma glucose).

A major strength of our study is the detailed metabolic kinetic analysis that included an assessment of TRL-TG and TRL particle kinetics as well as an assessment of postprandial fatty acid and glucose kinetics and an assessment of multi-organ insulin sensitivity by using a HECP. Moreover, we evaluated the effect of lifelong complete ANGPTL3 deficiency.

In conclusion, our comprehensive metabolic kinetic analyses applied in people with complete, biological ANGPTL3 deficiency and matched control participants provide unique insights into the biological function of ANGPTL3. The data from our study provide strong evidence that ANGPTL3 is involved in regulating intravascular clearance of TRL (chylomicrons and VLDL). Moreover, our data suggest that ANGPTL3 deficiency reduces hepatic TRL secretion, and this effect is unrelated to alterations in insulin action or circulating free fatty acids.

A second catheter was inserted into a hand vein for serial sampling of arterialized blood by using the heated hand technique.30 Immediately thereafter, an infusion of [6,6-2H2] glucose (priming dose: 22 μmol/kg, infusion rate: 0.22 μmol/kg/min) dissolved in normal saline solution was initiated and maintained until the end of the HECP. Two hours later, an infusion of [U-13C16] palmitate (infusion rate: 6 nmol/kg FFM/min), bound to human albumin was initiated. At 210 min after starting the glucose tracer infusion, the two-step HECP was initiated. During the first 150 minutes, insulin was infused at 7 mU/m2 of body-surface area (BSA) per min (initiated with stepped priming doses of 28 and 14 mU/m2 BSA/min for 5 min each). During the subsequent 240 minutes, insulin was infused 50 mU/m2 BSA/min (initiated with stepped priming doses of 200 and 100 mU/m2 BSA/min for 5 min each). The infusion rates of [6,6-2H2] glucose and [U-13C16] palmitate were decreased by 50% of basal values during stage 1 (low-dose insulin) and further reduced by 50% during stage 2 (high-dose insulin) of the HECP because of the expected decreases in endogenous glucose production and adipose tissue lipolytic rate.31,32 Dextrose (20% w/v), enriched to 2.5% with [6,6-2H2] glucose (to minimize changes in plasma glucose isotopic enrichment) was infused to maintain plasma glucose at 100 mg/dL. Blood samples were collected before starting the tracer infusion, and every 10 min during the last 30 minutes of the end of the basal period and stages 1 and 2 of the HECP to determine plasma metabolite and hormone concentrations, and glucose and palmitate kinetics.
