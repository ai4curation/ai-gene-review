Ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) contain various modifications that play critical roles in
ribosome assembly and function. Here, we discovered two stereoselective
methylations of the rRNA backbone in the peptidyl-transferase center (PTC) of
the 50S subunit of Escherichia coli cultured under anaerobic conditions.
Methylation occurs at carbon 5'(S) of ribose moieties of dihydrouridine at
position 2449 (D5Sm2449) and 2'-O-metylcytidine at position 2498 (Cm5Sm2498). We
identified the rlmX gene, encoding a cobalamin-dependent radical
S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) methyltransferase responsible for these methylations.
Intriguingly, D5Sm2449, Cm5Sm2498, and 5-hydroxycytidine (ho5C2501) in the PTC
were elevated under anaerobic growth conditions. A double knockout strain
lacking rlmX and rlhA (responsible for ho5C2501) impaired anaerobic growth.
Biochemical studies showed that these rRNA modifications stimulate protein
synthesis. The cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the ribosome
indicated that these hypoxia-induced modifications stabilize the P-site and the
PTC. These findings demonstrate that ribosomes are activated by hypoxia-induced
modifications to enhance translational capability and thereby survival, under
anaerobic conditions.

SUMMARY

Ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) contain various modifications that play critical roles in ribosome assembly and function. Here, we discovered two stereoselective methylations of the rRNA backbone in the peptidyl-transferase
center (PTC) of the 50S subunit of Escherichia coli cultured under anaerobic conditions. Methylation occurs at
carbon 5′ (S) of ribose moieties of dihydrouridine at position 2449 (D5Sm2449) and 2′ -O-metylcytidine at position
2498 (Cm5Sm2498). We identified the rlmX gene, encoding a cobalamin-dependent radical S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) methyltransferase responsible for these methylations. Intriguingly, D5Sm2449, Cm5Sm2498, and
5-hydroxycytidine (ho5C2501) in the PTC were elevated under anaerobic growth conditions. A double knockout
strain lacking rlmX and rlhA (responsible for ho5C2501) impaired anaerobic growth. Biochemical studies
showed that these rRNA modifications stimulate protein synthesis. The cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM)
structure of the ribosome indicated that these hypoxia-induced modifications stabilize the P-site and the
PTC. These findings demonstrate that ribosomes are activated by hypoxia-induced modifications to enhance
translational capability and thereby survival, under anaerobic conditions.

INTRODUCTION
RNA molecules contain a wide variety of modifications that are
introduced enzymatically after transcription. To date, ∼150 types
of RNA modifications have been identified in various RNA molecules from all domains of life.1 RNA modifications play crucial
roles in modulating RNA stability and function.2,3 The concept of
‘‘epitranscriptomics’’ highlights the importance of RNA modifications as a regulatory element in gene expression.4–7
Ribosomes are highly structured ribonucleoprotein complexes that translate genetic information in messenger
(m)RNAs to produce corresponding proteins. Functional regions of ribosomes, in particular the peptidyl-transferase center (PTC) and the decoding center (DC), are mainly composed
of ribosomal (r)RNAs, indicating that rRNAs play major roles

78

in fundamental processes in translation.8–15 rRNAs contain a
number of post-transcriptional modifications that are primarily
base and ribose methylations, and pseudouridines (Ψ).16,17
rRNA modifications are clustered in functionally important
regions of ribosomal subunits, including the PTC, the DC, binding sites of transfer (t)RNAs and mRNAs, and the interfaces of
subunits. rRNA modifications perform critical roles in ribosome
assembly and translational fidelity.17–19
Translational regulation is a vital process controlling gene
expression. Ribosomes are highly heterogeneous and contribute
to translational regulation. Specialized ribosomes comprise
specific protein components and/or variations and modifications
of rRNA.20–24 Even in bacteria, subpopulations of ribosomes
harbor different proteins and/or rRNAs to modulate translation
in response to environmental conditions.25,26

In Escherichia coli, 17 types of rRNA modifications have been
identified at 36 positions in rRNAs.18,27,28 These include 10 Ψs,
24 methyl modifications, one dihydrouridine, and one 5-hydroxycytidine.27,28 The frequencies of some of these rRNA
modifications are altered in response to changes in cellular
conditions, facilitating fine-tuning of ribosome assembly and
function.29,30Curiously, 13 of 25 rRNA modifications occur in
the PTC in 23S rRNA. Although the functional roles of the majority of rRNA modifications in the PTC remain elusive, some are
involved in ribosome assembly and accurate translation.27,31,32
While the deletion of each individual modification has only a
minor effect on bacterial growth, the combined loss of these
rRNA modifications impairs ribosome assembly and translational activity, leading to severe growth defects.33,34 Furthermore, the frequencies of certain rRNA modifications change
in response to cellular conditions, thereby fine-tuning ribosome
assembly and function.29,30 Together, these observations
reveal the unexpected plasticity and interdependence of
rRNA modifications within the PTC.33–35
Substoichiometric 5-hydroxycytidine, present at position
2501 (ho5C2501),36,37 is generated by RlhA,38 a hydroxylase
that uses prephenate as an oxygen donor.39,40 We observed a
growth phase-dependent alteration in ho5C2501 frequency
and its regulation by environmental iron concentration.38 This
observation prompted us to speculate that rRNA modifications
in the PTC might be regulated by various environmental stresses
and stimuli.
In this study, we first compared the status of rRNA modifications in E. coli cultured under aerobic and anaerobic conditions,
and found that the levels of ho5C2501 and two specific methylations of dihydrouridine at position 2449 (D2449) and Cm2498
were drastically increased under anaerobic conditions. We
determined the chemical structures of the hypoxia-specific
methylations of D2449 and Cm2498 using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. The stereoselective methylation
occurs at carbon 5′ (S) of the ribose moieties of both D2449

Article
and Cm2498. We named these 5′ (S)-methyl dihydrouridine
(D5Sm) 2449 and 5′ (S)-2′ -O-dimethylcytidine (Cm5Sm) 2498.
This study identifies an RNA modification involving ribose
methylation outside the 2′ position, representing an unprecedented example of RNA backbone modification. We identified
the rRNA large subunit methyltransferase X (rlmX) gene,
encoding a cobalamin (Cbl)-dependent radical S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) (RS) methyltransferase (MTase) responsible for
the 5′ (S)-methylation of both D5Sm2449 and Cm5Sm2498.
D5Sm2449, Cm5Sm2498, and ho5C2501 in the PTC were specifically increased under hypoxic growth conditions. A double
knockout (KO) strain lacking rlmX and rlhA (responsible for
ho5C2501) exhibited slower anaerobic growth. We also found
that these hypoxia-induced rRNA modifications promoted peptide bond formation and protein synthesis in vitro. The molecular
basis of PTC stabilization by these rRNA modifications was
explored by cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) analysis of the
E. coli 70S ribosome. These findings demonstrate that ribosomal
function is dynamically regulated by hypoxia-induced rRNA
modifications to enhance translational capability and contribute
to survival in anaerobic conditions.

methylations

nucleosides, and detected base-related ions (BH2+) of dihydrouracil and cytosine from the respective nucleosides (Figure 1D), indicating that additional methyl groups are attached to their ribose
portions. We also analyzed [U + Me] nucleoside from the ΔyhiN

[U + Me] nucleoside was co-injected with authentic 2′ -O-methyluridine (Um) nucleoside. These eluted separately at distinct peaks
(Figure 1E), demonstrating that [U + Me] is not Um.

large quantity of [U + Me] nucleoside (34 μg) from 50 g of E. coli

ΔyhiN cells grown under anaerobic conditions (see also STAR

(Figure S1C). These results strongly suggest that the methyl group
is bound to carbon C5′ (Figure 1F). Nuclear Overhauser effect
spectroscopy (NOESY) also confirmed this structure (Figure S1D).
Next, we determined the stereochemistry of the methyl group
by referring to previous studies46,47 (Table S1). Based on the
chemical shift of the H3′ peak and the J-value of H3′ -H4′ and
H4′ -H5′ interactions, the stereochemistry of the methyl group
was determined to be in the S conformation. Thus, we renamed
[U + Me] and [D + Me] as 5′ (S)-methyluridine (U5Sm) and 5′ (S)methyldihydrouridine (D5Sm), respectively.
To examine the effect of 5′ (S)-methylation on ribose conformation, we calculated the proportion of the C2′ -endo form of U5Sm
based on J-coupling values of ribose protons from two-dimensional COSY analysis (Table S1).46 The analysis revealed
that ∼50% of U5Sm adopts a C2′ -endo conformation, indicating
an equilibrium with C3′ -endo in roughly equal proportions. This
observation was further confirmed through NOESY analysis
(Figure S1D), in which comparable cross-peak intensities were
detected for both H2′ -H6 and H3′ -H6 interactions, supporting
the near-equal presence of C2′ - and C3′ -endo forms in U5Sm.
By contrast, it is well established that D primarily favors a
C2′ -endo conformation,48 while 2′ -O-methylation shows a preference for C3′ -endo.49 Thus, the ribose conformations of D5Sm
and Cm5Sm are likely significantly influenced by these factors.

Identification and characterization of RlmX responsible
for hypoxia-induced rRNA methylations
Next, we searched for an E. coli gene responsible for the hypoxia-induced methylations of D5Sm2449 and Cm5Sm2498. By
screening 15 uncharacterized MTases and 6 known RNA
MTases in E. coli (Table S2) under anaerobic conditions, we
found that D5Sm2449 and Cm5Sm2498 were converted to
D2449 and Cm2498, respectively, in strain ΔygiQ (Figures 2A
and S4). Both methylations were restored by ectopic expression
of a plasmid-encoded ygiQ gene, indicating that ygiQ is essential
for D5Sm2449 and Cm5Sm2498 formation in E. coli (Figures 2A
and S4). Hereafter, we refer to ygiQ as rlmX.
RlmX homologs are widely distributed in bacteria and
some archaeal species (Figure S3A). RlmX is characterized
as a Cbl-dependent radical SAM enzyme, which is composed
of a Cbl-binding domain, an RS domain, and a DUF3362
domain (Figure 2B). By comparing the AlphaFold2 structure
of RlmX with crystal structures of Cbl-dependent RS MTase,50
we identified Cbl-binding motifs, SAM-binding motifs, and a
[4Fe-4S] cluster-binding site in RlmX (Figures 2B, S5A–S5F,
and S6A–S6D). In the sequence alignment of bacterial RlmX
homologs (Figure S5A), we found an RlmX-specific Cys cluster (C453, C458, and C464) in the RS domain (Figures 2B
and S5A–S5F).
To examine whether these conserved residues are required
for RlmX function, the E. coli ΔrlmX strain was complemented
with plasmid-encoded rlmX carrying a series of mutations under anaerobic conditions (Figures 2B and 2C). The modification frequency of D5Sm2449 was measured for each strain by
RNA-MS (Figure 2C). In the Cbl-binding domain (Figures S5C
and S6A), the GG motif is critically required for the interaction
with the dimethylbenzimidazole (DMB) moiety of Cbl.51 G160N
completely lost methylation ability (Figure 2C), highlighting the
importance of this motif. In the RS domain (Figures 2B, S5E,
and S6C), we mutated each of the Cys residues (C386,
C390, and C393) in the [4Fe-4S] cluster, which resulted in
the complete loss of RlmX activity (Figure 2C). We also
mutated each of the Cys residues in the RlmX-specific Cys
cluster (C453, C458, and C464) (Figures S5B and S5F), which
resides on the surface of RlmX and is thought to contribute to
RNA recognition through zinc binding.52 Interestingly, all three

Article
Cys residues were essential for the methylation (Figure 2C).
Regarding SAM recognition, the side chain of E525 directly interacts with the ribose of SAM (Figure S5E). E525A mutation
impaired the activity (Figure 2C), demonstrating the importance of this interaction. These results demonstrate that
conserved residues and motifs in RlmX for Cbl-binding,
SAM-binding, the [4Fe-4S] cluster, and zinc binding are
required for hypoxia-induced 5′ (S)-methylations. Detailed descriptions for all mutants examined in this study are provided
in the supplemental information.
Based on mutational analysis of rlmX, we next examined the
availability of ferric iron and Cbl for the hypoxia-induced rRNA
methylations. Upon chelating Fe2+ ions by the addition of 2,
2′ -dipyridyl (DIP) to the Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (Figure 2D),
the frequency of D5Sm2449 decreased markedly, while D2449

Two 5′ (S)-methylations by RlmX and ho5C formation by RlhA

DISCUSSION
In this work, we identified previously undiscovered rRNA modifications D5Sm and Cm5Sm in E. coli cells cultured anaerobically.
These modifications remained undiscovered because ribosomes had been prepared from an aerobic culture of bacteria.
Our findings suggest the presence of additional RNA modifications that may be specifically induced by environmental factors
and cellular stress conditions. To date, ∼150 distinct RNA modifications have been identified.1 Only 2′ -O-methylations63 and
2′ -phosphorylation41 are ribose modifications, while other modifications occur at the nucleobases. Thus, 5′ (S)-methylation represents the unprecedented ribose modification occurring other
than the 2′ position. Additionally, we detected small amounts
of precursor forms U5Sm and C5Sm. This discovery introduces
the concept of 5′ (S)-methylation in the sugar-phosphate backbone, expanding our understanding of the diversity and functionality of RNA modification.

Article
Given that 5′ (S)-methylation acts as a rotational barrier to
the C4′ –C5′ bond, this modification likely stabilizes the rRNA
structure by restricting ribose conformational flexibility. It also
provides local hydrophobicity to the RNA backbone, which
could strengthen interdomain interactions within the PTC. In
addition, the cryo-EM results suggested that these modifications
strengthen interdomain interactions within the PTC.
RS enzymes form a large and diverse superfamily of proteins
that use Fe-S clusters as cofactors.64 These enzymes catalyze a
wide array of reactions, including methylation, methylthiolation,
carboxymethylation, tricyclic purine formation, and deazaguanine
synthesis. RS enzymes play essential roles in the biosynthesis
of antibiotics,53,65,66 herbicides,67 and molybdopterins,68 and
are involved in the modification of fatty acids,69 RNAs,70–75 and
proteins.76 Additionally, RS enzymes participate in various physiological functions, and loss-of-function mutations in RS enzymes
are linked to several human diseases.77,78
Within this superfamily, RS MTases utilize Fe-S clusters to reductively cleave SAM, generating the highly reactive 5′ -deoxyadenosyl (5′ -dAdo) radical. This radical abstracts a hydrogen
atom from a normally inert carbon on the substrate, enabling
the transfer of a methyl group from the donor.79,80 While RS
MTases are conserved across all three domains of life, many
of their specific substrates and functions remain unknown. In
many cases, Cbl serves as a cofactor, mediating methyl transfer
from a second SAM molecule to the substrate.79,81
In vivo complementation and in vitro reconstitution studies
revealed that RlmX catalyzes 5′ (S)-methylation through a
mechanism similar to other Cbl-dependent RS MTases53,82
(Figure 4D). In this proposed mechanism, the [4Fe-4S] cluster
cleaves SAM to generate a 5′ -dAdo radical, which abstracts
the 5′ (S)-H from D2449 or Cm2498. Following this, a methyl
group is transferred from Me-Cbl (III) to form either D5Sm2449
or Cm5Sm2498. After the reaction, Cbl (II) is reduced to Cbl
(I) by an electron donor and then receives a methyl group from
a second SAM molecule to regenerate Me-Cbl (III). In our
in vitro studies, we used MV to reduce Cbl (II). In cellular conditions, however, electron donors such as flavodoxin, flavodoxin
reductase, and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate
reduced form (NADPH) may serve this function, as observed in
other Cbl-dependent RS MTases.53,82

The residues that constitute the PTC are highly conserved
across bacterial species.86 These residues form the intricate
structure of the PTC through various interactions, including
base pairing and stacking, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions. Even a single point mutation in these residues
can disrupt PTC function, resulting in decreased PTase activity and a slow-growth phenotype.62,87 Our findings demonstrate that the three hypoxia-induced modifications enhance
PTase activity and boost ribosome function in protein synthesis. Previous studies have established that the growth rate of
E. coli correlates closely with ribosome abundance,88,89 and
that ribosome biogenesis imposes a substantial energetic
burden; consequently, ribosome numbers are reduced under
anaerobic conditions.90 Thus, reinforcing ribosome function
through these modifications to increase translational efficiency is a rational strategy. From an application perspective,
bacterial ribosomes prepared from anaerobic culture might be
useful for efficient protein production in in vitro translation
systems.
Furthermore, these modifications may not only generally increase PTase activity, but also fine-tune PTase activity in an
amino acid-specific manner. In other words, the modifications
might optimize translation elongation speed under anaerobic
environments, thereby contributing to mRNA stability and proteostasis. To test this hypothesis, it is necessary to perform
detailed biochemical measurements of PTase activity for

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS
Bacterial strains and cultivation
A series of E. coli genomic-deletion strains (OCL/R-series) derived from MG1655 sp. (MG1655 rpsL polA12 Zih::Tn10) were kindly
provided by Dr Jun-ichi Kato.91 Strains ME5008 (MG1655sp ΔrhsB-uspA::kanr) and ME5078 (MG1655sp ΔrhsB-yiaE::kanr) lack regions containing yhiN. Single-deletion strains with kanamycin resistance markers (Keio collection)103 and their parental strain
BW25113 were obtained from the Genetic Stock Research Center, National Institute of Genetics, Japan. We disrupted rlmX in
BW25113 using the chloramphenicol acetyltransferase cassette (Cmr) by one-step gene inactivation104 using the DNA oligos
listed in Table S4 to yield strain ΔrlmX::Cmr. To construct the double knockout ΔyhiN/ΔrlmX (Knr, Cmr) or ΔrlhA/ΔrlmX (Knr, Cmr)
strain, ΔrlmX::Cmr was incorporated into the ΔyhiN::Kmr or ΔrlhA::Kmr strain by P1 transduction. The Kmr cassette was removed
from the knockout strains using pcp20,104 then Δrna::Kmr was introduced into these knockout strains by P1 transduction to prevent
degradation of ribosomes during purification. Single-colony isolation was repeated twice for all strains constructed in this study.
Genotyping of knockout strains was conducted by colony PCR.
E. coli cells were cultured in LB medium or M9 medium at 37◦ C. For aerobic growth, E. coli cells were cultured in flasks with
vigorous shaking (135 rpm). For sub-anaerobic growth, E. coli cells were cultured in Erlenmeyer flasks with mild shaking (80 rpm).
For anaerobic growth, E. coli cells were cultured in Erlenmeyer flask with mild shaking (80 rpm) in an anaerobic chamber (oxygen
concentration < 0.5%). Unless otherwise stated, E. coli cells were cultured under aerobic conditions.
For growth analysis in liquid medium, overnight full-growth preculture was harvested and resuspended in fresh LB medium. Resuspended cells were diluted (OD600 1×10-2), then 10 μl of diluted cultures were mixed with 190μl of fresh LB medium in each well of
96 well microplate with round bottom (Iwaki). Then, the optical density at 600 nm of each well was measured every 15 min for 24 h with
continuous shaking at 37◦ C in air or an anaerobic chamber, using an EPOCH 2 Microplate Reader (BioTek).
For growth analysis on agar plates, the Kmr cassette of KO strains was removed as described above. Then, overnight full-growth
precultures of WT and KO strains (no resistance against antibiotics) were harvested and resuspended in autoclaved water twice.
Then, resuspended cells were serially diluted (OD600 from 10-1 to 10-8), spotted onto agar plates and cultivated at 37◦ C in air or
an anaerobic chamber.

Mass spectrometry of rRNA modifications
Mass spectrometry of rRNA modifications was performed essentially as previously described.30 The 53-mer-RNA segment (C2424–
A2476) and 50-mer-RNA segment (A2478–C2527) of 23S rRNA were extracted from E. coli total RNAs using synthetic DNA oligos
listed in Table S4.
To measure the frequencies of each rRNA modification, these rRNA segments (1 pmol) were digested with 10 units of RNase T1
(#EN0541, ThermoFisher Scientific) in 20 mM NH4OAc (pH 5.3) at 37◦ C for 60 min. The digest was mixed with an equal volume of
0.1 M triethylamine acetate and subjected to capillary LC-nano ESI-MS on an LTQ Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific)
equipped with a DiNa splitless nanoflow high-performance LC (HPLC) system (KYA Technologies) as previously
described.41,42,106,107 Modification frequencies were calculated as the ratio of the extracted ion chromatogram (XIC) peak intensity
of modified and unmodified fragments.
To analyze the chemical structures of the modified nucleosides, the isolated rRNA segments (20 pmol) were digested into nucleosides as described previously.30 Nucleosides were dried in vacuo, dissolved in 90% acetonitrile (10% water), and subjected to hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography using a cHILIC column or reversed-phase chromatography using an ODS column
coupled with ESI-MS using a Q Exactive hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped
with an ESI source and an Ultimate 3000 LC system (Dionex).108
Purification of ribosomes
Crude ribosomes were isolated from E. coli cells cultured under aerobic or anaerobic conditions as previously described.30 They
were then suspended in RBS buffer A [10 mM Mg(OAc)₂, 100 mM NH₄Cl, 20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.6), and 6 mM β-Me] and layered
onto a sucrose gradient (10–50%, w/v) in RBS buffer A. Ultracentrifugation was performed in a SW-28 rotor (Beckman) at 25,000 rpm
for 15 h at 4◦ C. The sucrose gradient was fractionated using a Piston Gradient Fractionator (BioComp Instruments), and the optical
density at 260 nm of each fraction was measured with an EPOCH 2 Microplate Reader (BioTek). Fractions containing tight-coupled
70S ribosomes were pooled and recovered by ultracentrifugation in a 70 Ti rotor (Beckman) at 40,000 rpm for 15 h at 4◦ C in RBS
buffer A. The pellet was resuspended in RBS buffer A and subjected to a second ultracentrifugation step in a 70 Ti rotor (Beckman)
at 45,000 rpm for 3 h at 4◦ C. The final pellet was resuspended in RBS buffer A and stored at -80◦ C.
To prepare 50S subunits, tight-coupled 70S ribosomes were resuspended in RBS buffer C [1 mM Mg(OAc)2, 60 mM NH4Cl, 60 mM
KCl, 20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.6) and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)], layered on top of a sucrose gradient (10–50%, w/v) in RBS buffer C,
and ultracentrifuged in a SW-28 Rotor (Beckman) at 25,000 rpm for 15 h at 4◦ C. The fractions corresponding to 50S subunits were
pooled and recovered by ultracentrifugation in a 70 Ti rotor (Beckman) at 45,000 rpm for 24 h at 4◦ C in RBS buffer A. The pellet was
resuspended in RBS buffer A and stored at -80◦ C. 30S subunits were purified from crude ribosomes derived from aerobically-grown
WT cells, similar to the purification procedure for 50S subunit.

Purification of U5Sm2449 nucleosides
To purify large amounts of rRNAs, 40 L of the E. coli Δrna/ΔyhiN (Knr) strain was cultivated under sub-anaerobic conditions until
OD600 reached 0.2. The cells were then chilled quickly and harvested by centrifugation to obtain 50 g cell pellets, followed by extraction of 1.2 g of crude ribosomes as described above. Crude ribosomes were resuspended in 300 mL lysis buffer B [50 mM NaOAc (pH
5.2), 5 mM Mg(OAc)2, 15 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (pH 8.0), 0.5% sodium lauryl sulfate (SDS)], mixed with an equal
volume of acidic phenol (pH 5.3), stirred vigorously for 30 min at room temperature, then heated at 60◦ C for 10 min, followed by supplementation of 3 mL of 10% SDS. The mixture was further stirred for 2 h at room temperature, then centrifuged at 3,630 × g for
30 min at 4◦ C. After the upper aqueous layer was collected, the lower layer was mixed with 300 mL lysis buffer B, stirred for 1 h
at room temperature, and centrifuged at 3,630 × g for 30 min at 4◦ C. The upper layer was again collected. The aqueous layers
were combined, mixed with a 0.5 volume of chloroform, and stirred for 30 min at room temperature, followed by centrifugation at
3,630 × g for 30 min. The upper layer was collected and mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol, then chilled at -30◦ C for overnight. The rRNA pellet was harvested by centrifugation at 3,630 × g for 30 min at 4◦ C, then resuspended in 120 mL of deionized water.
Total rRNA was further purified by ethanol precipitation, then resuspended in 70 mL of deionized water. The total yield of rRNA was
660 mg (∼400 nmol).
Purification of the 53-mer-RNA rRNA segments (C2424–A2476) was essentially conducted as described above but with several
modifications. Total rRNAs were incubated with 4 μmol of biotinylated DNA oligo (Table S4) in 50 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.6) and
100 mM KCl, then incubated at 90◦ C for 5 min and cooled to 30◦ C (-1◦ C/min). Uncovered regions of rRNAs were then digested
with 80,000 units of RNase T1 (#EN0541, ThermoFisher Scientific) and 80 μg of RNase A (#AM2270, ThermoFisher Scientific) for
60 min on ice. The RNA mixture was subjected to phenol/chloroform (pH 7.9) extraction and precipitated with isopropanol. The
biotinylated heteroduplex and the free DNA oligos were captured by Streptavidin Sepharose Beads (Cytiva) using a peristaltic
pump system. The heteroduplex and the DNA oligos were extracted from the beads using TRIzol LS reagent (Thermo Fisher
Scientific) and precipitated with isopropanol. The RNA extract was digested by 14,400 units of RNase T1 (#EN0541,
ThermoFisher Scientific) in 20 mM NH4OAc (pH5.3) and incubated at 37◦ C for 1 h, then heated at 70◦ C for 10 min. Dissociated
DNA oligos were removed using Streptavidin Sepharose Beads (GE Healthcare) and a peristaltic pump system. The extracted
RNAs were digested into nucleosides by incubation at 37◦ C for 1.5 h in a 7 mL solution with 12.6 units of nuclease P1
(#145-08221, Wako Pure Chemical Industries), followed by addition of 350 μL of 1 M ammonium bicarbonate (pH 8.2) and 12.6 units
of phosphodiesterase I (#LS003926, Worthington), and incubated at 37◦ C for 1.5 h. Next, 12.6 units of bacterial alkaline phosphatase
(BAP) from E. coli C75 (Wako Pure Chemical Industries) was added and incubated at 37◦ C for 1.5 h. The total nucleoside mixture
was 5.0 mg.
An HP1100 liquid chromatography system (Agilent Technologies) was used for isolating U5Sm nucleosides. The nucleoside mixture
was purified using an Inertsil ODS-3 column (5 μm particle size, 10 × 250mm) with a guard column. The mobile phase consisted
of 5 mM NH4OAc (pH 5.3; solvent A) and 60 % acetonitrile (ACN; solvent B), and the flow rate was 1 mL/min. The gradient was

These procedures were repeated three times to purify U5Sm nucleosides. The total yield of U5Sm nucleoside was 34 μg.

Reconstitution of holo-RlmX
Reconstitution of holo-RlmX was carried out as described previously53,111 with several modifications. All manipulations were performed under strictly anaerobic conditions (<2 ppm O₂) in an anaerobic glove box maintained in an atmosphere of 95% N₂ and
5% H₂, with a palladium catalyst. All solutions, buffers, and recombinant apo-RlmX used for reconstitution were deoxygenated by
bubbling for at least 3 h. The apo-RlmX solution (17.3 μM in 300 μL) was mixed with 4.8 μL of 100 mM DTT (final concentration
1.57 mM) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature, followed by addition of a 10-fold molar excess of FeCl₃ and further incubation for another 10 min at room temperature. A 10-fold molar excess of Na₂S was then added to the mixture, which was incubated
for 30 min at room temperature, followed by addition of a 5-fold molar excess of Me-Cbl or CN-Cbl and a further incubation for 2.5 h at
20◦ C. The resulting iron sulfide precipitate was removed by filtration. The mixture was desalted using a Sephadex NAP-5 column
(Cytiva) to remove excess irons and Cbl. The reconstituted holo-RlmX was concentrated by ultrafiltration. The iron and Cbl content
in holo-RlmX was quantified using ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy and an iron quantification kit (BioChain).
In vitro reconstitution of 5′ (S)-methylations by RlmX
The deproteinized rRNA substrates were extracted from the 50S subunit isolated from the Δrna/ΔrlmX (kanr) strain using TRIzol LS
reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacture’s procedure. For preparation of unmodified 23S rRNA and its RNA
segments, DNA templates harboring T7 promoter and rRNA sequence were PCR-amplified from pRB103 plasmid (encoding rrnB
operon, zeocin resistance marker, and pSC101 ori)92 using KOD-plus-Neo (Toyobo). A series of primers used for this experiment
are listed in Table S4. Then, in vitro transcription using T7 RNA polymerase was performed as described previously.105 In vitro reconstitution of 5′ (S)-methylations of rRNAs by RlmX was performed at 37◦ C for 1 h under strictly anaerobic conditions in a globe box. The
standard reaction conditions contained 50 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.6), 5 mM MgCl2, 50 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM NADPH, 1 mM MV,
1 mM SAM, 0.1 mM Me-Cbl or CN-Cbl, 0.4 μM rRNA substrates, and 0.4 μM recombinant holo-RlmX. Following the reaction, rRNAs
were extracted from the mixture using TRIzol LS reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacture’s procedure, and
5′ (S)-methylations at the target sites were analyzed by LC-MS as described above. S-(5′ -adenosyl)-L-methionine-d3 disulfate salt
(Medical Isotopes, Inc) was used for isotope labeling experiments.
In vitro translation by anaerobic ribosomes
In vitro translation was performed using a PURExpress ΔRibosome Kit (New England Biolabs) following the manufacturer’s instructions with some modifications. The reaction mixture (25 μL), containing isolated ribosomes (23.7 pmol), a plasmid encoding E. coli
dihydrofolate reductase (250 ng), and 1.2 μL of [35S] methionine (NEG009A; PerkinElmer; 370 MBq/ml, 37 TBq/mmol), was incubated
at 37◦ C. At each reaction time point, a 5 μL aliquot of the reaction mixture was collected and chilled on ice to stop the reaction. At the
endpoint, a 5 μL aliquot of the reaction mixture was taken at 30 min. Each sample was mixed with an equal volume of loading solution
and subjected to SDS-PAGE. The gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB), dried, and exposed to an imaging plate
overnight. Radiolabeled bands were visualized using an FLA-7000 instrument (FujiFilm) and quantified with MultiGauge software
(FujiFilm).

0.45μm NC; Cytiva) and nylon membranes (Amersham Hybond N+, RPN303B; Cytiva) were rinsed with reaction buffer [50 mM

Peptidyl transferase assay and initiation complex formation in vitro
All experiments described below were carried out in polymix buffer [20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.6), 95 mM KCl, 5 mM NH4Cl,
0.5 mM CaCl2, 8 mM putrescine, 1 mM spermidine, and 1 mM dithioerythritol (DET)] using an RQF-3 Quench-flow Apparatus
(KinTek Corp, Austin, TX, USA). The rate of initiation complex formation was measured as previously described114 with some modifications. The 30S initiation complex (30SIC) was prepared by incubation of WT 30S subunits (1.0 μM) with 2.5 μM of A36 mRNA
(2.5 μM, CAA UUA AGG AGG UAU ACU AUG UUC GUA GCG AGC UAA; Microsynth), [35S] fMet-tRNAiMet (2 μM, 6000 dpm/pmol),
and initiation factors IF1, 2, and 3 (each in a 2-fold excess over ribosomes) at 37◦ C for 15 min. The 50S subunits (0.5 μM) from a
series of E. coli strains were preincubated with 10 mM puromycin at 37◦ C for 15 min. The rate of [35S] fMet-puromycin formation,
indicating complete initiation, was determined by the rapid mixing of 30SIC (1 μM) with 50S (0.5 μM)-puromycin (10 mM) mixture
at 37◦ C.
The rate of peptide bond formation between peptidyl tRNA and puromycin was measured as previously described115 with some
modification. To prepare the 70S ribosomes carrying fMet-Phe-tRNAPhe at the P-site, EF-Tu-GTP-[3H] Phe-tRNAPhe ternary complex
(TC) was formed by incubating [3H] Phe-tRNAPhe (10 μM) with EF-Tu (20 μM) and 1 mM GTP for 15 min at 37◦ C. Next, TC was mixed
with 50S subunit (0.5 μM) for 5min at 37◦ C. 70S ribosomes carrying fMet-Phe-tRNAPhe at the P-site were prepared by mixing equal
volumes of 30SIC (1 μM) and 50S (0.5 μM)-TC mixture and a catalytic amount of EF-G at 37◦ C for 5 min. The rate of peptide bond
formation was measured upon rapid mixing of equal volumes (18 μL) of 70S complex and 10 mM puromycin.
Reactions were quenched with 25% formic acid, neutralized by addition of 500 μL of 1.5M Tris-HCl (pH 8.8) and extracted with
1 mL of ethyl acetate. Radioactivity in the ethyl acetate layer was counted and time courses were fitted to single-exponential kinetics
according to the formula: FfMet-Puro = F0*(1-exp[-kobs * t]), where FfMet-Puro denotes the fraction of the or fMet-Phe-puromycin formed,
F0 is the maximal amount of puromycin-reactive 70S complexes, and kobs is the apparent first-order rate constant.
