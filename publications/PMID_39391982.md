Plants have evolved mechanisms to anticipate and adjust their growth and
development in response to environmental changes. Understanding the key
regulators of plant performance is crucial to mitigate the negative influence of
global climate change on crop production. EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3) is one such
regulator playing a critical role in the circadian clock and
thermomorphogenesis. In Arabidopsis thaliana, ELF3 contains a prion-like domain
(PrLD) that acts as a thermosensor, facilitating liquid-liquid phase separation
at high ambient temperatures. To assess the conservation of this function across
the plant kingdom, we traced the evolutionary emergence of ELF3, with a focus on
the presence of PrLDs. We found that the PrLD, primarily influenced by the
length of polyglutamine (polyQ) repeats, is most prominent in Brassicales.
Analyzing 319 natural A. thaliana accessions, we confirmed the previously
described wide range of polyQ length variation in AtELF3, but found it to be
only weakly associated with geographic origin, climate conditions, and classic
temperature-responsive phenotypes. Interestingly, similar polyQ length variation
was not observed in several other investigated Bassicaceae species. Based on
these findings, available prediction tools and limited experimental evidence, we
conclude that the emergence of PrLD, and particularly polyQ length variation, is
unlikely to be a key driver of environmental adaptation. Instead, it likely adds
an additional layer to ELF3's role in thermomorphogenesis in A. thaliana, with
its relevance in other species yet to be confirmed.

Introduction

Plants, like other organisms, encounter both predictable and unpredictable environmental changes. While regular light/dark and warm/cool cycles can be anticipated by the plants’ internal circadian clock, climate change may disturb this and other systems. Consequently, evolutionary adaptation to such systemic disturbances necessitates an organism's ability to acclimate during growth and development to changing environments. Therefore, the capacity for acclimation is likely an important trait on which natural selection acts, enabling a species to adapt. Understanding the key players involved in these processes will help to increase the fitness in crops and mitigate the negative influence of climate change.

As plants increasingly encounter predictable environmental changes, circadian anticipation is a fundamental attribute contributing to plant performance. The plant circadian clock is composed of multiple interconnected transcriptional–translational feedback loops (Huang and Nusinow 2016; Nohales and Kay 2016). These loops can be classified in a time-of-day dependent manner based on the phase of involved clock components. The morning loop includes CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED 1 (CCA1) and LATE ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL (LHY), which positively regulate the expression of PSEUDO-RESPONSE REGULATOR 9 (PRR9) in the morning and PRR7 in the afternoon (Farré et al. 2005; Nakamichi et al. 2010), while repressing two additional afternoon-phased genes, PRR5 and GIGANTEA (GI) (Lu et al. 2012; Kamioka et al. 2016). PRR9, PRR7, and PRR5 subsequently repress the expression of CCA1 and LHY, allowing the induction of evening-phased genes (Nakamichi et al. 2010; Adams et al. 2015). At dusk, the accumulation of TIMING OF CAB EXPRESSION 1 (TOC1) suppresses GI, which in turn triggers TOC1 activation (Kim et al. 2007). Additionally, three evening-phased proteins—EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3), EARLY FLOWERING 4 (ELF4), and LUX ARRYTHMO (LUX)—accumulate and form a protein complex known as the evening complex (EC) (Hsu et al. 2013). The EC directly represses the transcription of PRR9, PRR7, and GI, leading to the accumulation of CCA1 and LHY before dawn (Nusinow et al. 2011; Herrero et al. 2012; Ezer et al. 2017). Through this endogenous network, external cues, known as Zeitgeber, can be used as timing input to generate precise internal biological rhythms. However, not all circadian clock components can act as Zeitnehmers, which are capable of receiving timing information from Zeitgebers. Recent studies have identified ELF3 and GI as essential Zeitnehmers for clock entrainment to photoperiod signals (Anwer et al. 2020), while ELF3 alone can function as a temperature Zeitnehmer, sensing warm/cool cycles (Zhu et al. 2022).

While the circadian clock equips plants to manage daily environmental fluctuations, they still face challenges from climate change, such as rising ambient temperatures. Plants can rapidly acclimate to elevated temperatures through various morphological and developmental adjustments, collectively known as thermomorphogenesis (Delker et al. 2014; Quint et al. 2016). In Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings, these adjustments include elongated hypocotyls and upward leaf bending, which are known to enhance cooling capacity in controlled environments (van Zanten et al. 2009; Crawford et al. 2012). PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PIF4) is a central regulator of thermomorphogenesis signaling. It accumulates at warm temperatures and activates auxin biosynthesis genes, which promote cell elongation in petioles and hypocotyls, leaf hyponasty, and flowering (Franklin et al. 2011; Kumar et al. 2012; Park et al. 2019). In this thermomorphogenesis pathway, PIF4's function is regulated by temperature sensing systems and the circadian clock. The photoreceptor phytochrome B (phyB) was the first plant temperature sensor to be identified (Jung et al. 2016; Legris et al. 2016). Warm temperatures accelerate the dark/thermal reversion of phyB from its active Pfr form to its inactive Pr form (Delker et al. 2017). The active Pfr form of phyB mediates PIF4 degradation by stabilizing ELF3 (Nieto et al. 2015). ELF3 contains a prion-like domain (PrLD) that functions as a thermosensor, enabling liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) of ELF3 from its dilute phase into liquid droplets (dense phase) at high temperatures (Jung et al. 2020; Hutin et al. 2023). This dense phase aggregation of ELF3 coordinates with its restricted mobilization to the nucleus (Ronald et al. 2021, 2022), potentially relieving its direct interaction with PIF4 (Nieto et al. 2015) and the transcriptional repression of PIF4 by the EC (Box et al. 2015; Raschke et al. 2015). Given its multiple roles in temperature sensing, circadian clock, and thermomorphogenesis, ELF3 has been identified as a key plasticity gene crucial for plant acclimation (Blackman 2017; Laitinen and Nikoloski 2019).

Expanding our knowledge generated from A. thaliana to crops is essential for achieving crop-level adaptations and ensuring yield stability under global climate change (Challinor et al. 2014). Natural variation or loss-of-function in ELF3 generally impacts circadian clock regulated photoperiodic flowering in various crop species, including rice (Matsubara et al. 2012; Saito et al. 2012; Andrade et al. 2022), barley (Faure et al. 2012; Zakhrabekova et al. 2012; Zahn et al. 2023), wheat (Alvarez et al. 2016, 2023; Mizuno et al. 2023; Wittern et al. 2023), soybean (Lu et al. 2017; Bu et al. 2021; Fang et al. 2021), and chickpea (Ridge et al. 2017). ELF3 variants facilitate cultivation of crops under altered photoperiods, which is crucial for crop domestication and spatial distribution. Beyond its role in the circadian clock, ELF3 is also involved in barley morphological and developmental acclimations to high temperatures (Ford et al. 2016; Ejaz and von Korff 2017; Zhu et al. 2023), suggesting conserved functions in temperature responsiveness. However, unlike A. thaliana, the monocot grass Brachypodium distachyon lacks a temperature-responsive PrLD in ELF3 (Jung et al. 2020). Consequently, the conserved functions of ELF3 with respect to temperature sensing remain uncertain, especially in monocots.

In A. thaliana, the temperature sensing PrLD of ELF3 exhibits natural variation in the length of a polyglutamine (polyQ) stretch, due to expanded cytosine-adenine-adenine (CAA) repeats (Undurraga et al. 2012). Similar to ELF3's aggregation in response to high temperatures (Jung et al. 2020), polyQ-extended proteins in humans also aggregate in degenerated neurons, leading to polyQ diseases (Fan et al. 2014). This parallel suggests that the length of polyQ stretches determines the thermosensing function of ELF3-PrLD in A. thaliana. Despite the known variation in ELF3-polyQ length, the potential effects and evolutionary significance of this variation remain largely unexplored, even in the model A. thaliana. In this study, we aim to elucidate the evolutionary trajectory of ELF3 by systematically tracing its emergence across the plant kingdom, with a particular focus on the PrLD. Using 319 A. thaliana accessions and 89 accessions from four additional Brassicaceae species, we examined the correlation between ELF3-polyQ variation and factors such as geographic origins, local environments, and temperature-responsive phenotypes. The absence of reliable correlations between phenotype and polyQ length, coupled with the concentrated presence of ELF3-PrLD within the Brassicales order, suggests that this variation is unlikely to represent a widespread evolutionary adaptation to diverse environments.

Arabidopsis thaliana ELF3 contains a PrLD which is required for LLPS in response to temperature changes (Jung et al. 2020; Hutin et al. 2023). Research on mammalian and particularly yeast prions has led to the development of various prediction tools for PrLDs, based on two different models. The compositional model identifies a large number of weak interactions distributed along the PrLD, characterized by a biased amino acid composition (Toombs et al. 2010, 2012; Gil-Garcia et al. 2021). In contrast, the soft-amyloid model emphasizes the presence of stretches with mild amyloid potential, embedded within an intrinsically disordered protein region (IDPR) (Sabate et al. 2015; Gil-Garcia et al. 2021). Given that IDPRs are known drivers of biological LLPS (Uversky 2019), we first analyzed the sequence composition of the identified ELF3/EEC sequences from 274 plant genomes using localCIDER (Holehouse et al. 2017). All ELF3/EEC sequences were classified as weak polyampholytes and polyelectrolytes, characterized by a fraction of charged residues and a net charge per residue below 0.25, which are hallmarks of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) (supplementary fig. S2a, Supplementary Material online). To determine whether the PrLD is conserved in these ELF3/EEC homologues, we then conducted a Prion-Like Amino Acid Composition (PLAAC) analysis on all sequences, obtaining PrD scores and Log-likelihood ratios (LLR) that indicate the likelihood of prion subsequences (Alberti et al. 2009; Lancaster et al. 2014). Unlike the PrD score, the LLR does not impose a hard cutoff. For example, the PrLD of A. thaliana ELF3 had an identical PrD score and LLR of 31.53, encompassing two subsequence regions (Fig. 1; supplementary figs. S1 and S3, Supplementary Material online). When considering the hard cutoff using the PrD score, the PrLD prediction identified ELF3 sequences predominantly from core eudicots (Fig. 1; supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online). Additionally, ELF3 homologues in the bryophytes Physcomitrium patens and Sphagnum fallax, as well as the monocot Sorghum bicolor, were predicted to have a PrLD with a relatively low but positive PrD score. However, with exception of S. bicolor and consistent with a previous finding in B. distachyon (Jung et al. 2020), monocots generally lack such a domain in their ELF3. High PrD scores were found almost exclusively in Brassicales ELF3, with several species exhibiting even higher scores than A. thaliana (Capsella grandiflora: 59.89, Arabidopsis lyrata: 58.27, Capsella rubella: 57.52, Alyssum linifolium: 49.96, Arabidopsis halleri: 46.36, Descurainia sophioides: 45.54, Brassica rapa: 32.62, and Isatis tinctoria: 32.09), suggesting potentially conserved temperature sensing functions of PrLDs across Brassicales. Moreover, although there are four highly conserved sequence stretches between ELF3 and EEC, the third conserved region (III) was located within the gap of the predicted PrLD (Fig. 2; supplementary fig. S3, Supplementary Material online), and the polyQ stretch diverged between Brassicales ELF3 and all other sequences (Fig. 2). Consequently, none of the sequences in the EEC clade were predicted to have a PrLD (Fig. 1).To complement the composition-based PrLD prediction of PLAAC, we also analyzed the ELF3/EEC sequences using PrionW (Zambrano et al. 2015). PrionW utilizes a combination of compositional and soft-amyloid model for PrLDs prediction. Using the default settings for Q + N richness of 25% and a pWALTZ cutoff of 73, the initial analysis identified only three hits: ELF3 of I. tinctoria (Brassicales), Populus deltoides and Populus trichocarpa (both Malpighiales). To include A. thaliana ELF3, which has an experimentally proven PrLD function mediating LLPS (Jung et al. 2020; Hutin et al. 2023), we relaxed the PrionW analysis parameters to a Q + N richness of 18% and a pWALTZ cutoff of 68. This adjustment resulted in a set of 54 sequences with predicted PrLD, which included 19 Brassicales sequences. Fourteen of these overlapped with the PLAAC set of 52 sequences, including 33 Brassicales sequences (supplementary fig. S2b, Supplementary Material online). Unlike the PLAAC results, PrionW hits were also identified in the EEC clade (supplementary fig. S2c, Supplementary Material online). Arabidopsis thaliana ELF3 contains a PrLD which is required for LLPS in response to temperature changes (Jung et al. 2020; Hutin et al. 2023). Research on mammalian and particularly yeast prions has led to the development of various prediction tools for PrLDs, based on two different models. The compositional model identifies a large number of weak interactions distributed along the PrLD, characterized by a biased amino acid composition (Toombs et al. 2010, 2012; Gil-Garcia et al. 2021). In contrast, the soft-amyloid model emphasizes the presence of stretches with mild amyloid potential, embedded within an intrinsically disordered protein region (IDPR) (Sabate et al. 2015; Gil-Garcia et al. 2021). Given that IDPRs are known drivers of biological LLPS (Uversky 2019), we first analyzed the sequence composition of the identified ELF3/EEC sequences from 274 plant genomes using localCIDER (Holehouse et al. 2017). All ELF3/EEC sequences were classified as weak polyampholytes and polyelectrolytes, characterized by a fraction of charged residues and a net charge per residue below 0.25, which are hallmarks of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) (supplementary fig. S2a, Supplementary Material online). To determine whether the PrLD is conserved in these ELF3/EEC homologues, we then conducted a Prion-Like Amino Acid Composition (PLAAC) analysis on all sequences, obtaining PrD scores and Log-likelihood ratios (LLR) that indicate the likelihood of prion subsequences (Alberti et al. 2009; Lancaster et al. 2014). Unlike the PrD score, the LLR does not impose a hard cutoff. For example, the PrLD of A. thaliana ELF3 had an identical PrD score and LLR of 31.53, encompassing two subsequence regions (Fig. 1; supplementary figs. S1 and S3, Supplementary Material online). When considering the hard cutoff using the PrD score, the PrLD prediction identified ELF3 sequences predominantly from core eudicots (Fig. 1; supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online). Additionally, ELF3 homologues in the bryophytes Physcomitrium patens and Sphagnum fallax, as well as the monocot Sorghum bicolor, were predicted to have a PrLD with a relatively low but positive PrD score. However, with exception of S. bicolor and consistent with a previous finding in B. distachyon (Jung et al. 2020), monocots generally lack such a domain in their ELF3. Interestingly, EEC homologues emerged later and are restricted to eudicots (Table 1), suggesting that a duplication event of ELF3 in the last common ancestor of the eudicots gave rise to EEC in this lineage.

To explore the evolution and divergence of ELF3 and EEC in greater detail, we identified the protein homologues of ELF3 and EEC from 274 plant genomes (supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online) and reconstructed their phylogenetic relationships. Sequences from similar angiosperm groups (e.g. basal angiosperms, monocots, eudicots, and core eudicots) generally clustered together in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1; supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online). As anticipated, ELF3 and EEC formed two distinct clades, with EEC being confined to core eudicots. In orders such as Buxales, Trochodendrales, Proteales, and Ranunculales, which are eudicots but not core eudicots, only ELF3 homologues were detected, clustering with ELF3 from basal angiosperms and monocots (supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online). Interestingly, this clade is more closely related to the EEC clade than to the ELF3 clade from core eudicots. To identify sequence features that distinguish ELF3 and EEC, we selected 32 species (8 Poales and 24 core eudicots that have both ELF3 and EEC homologues, including 7 Brassicales species) for sequence alignment. As previously reported (Liu et al. 2001), four highly conserved regions (I–IV) were detected within ELF3 and EEC in these species (Fig. 2). Poales (monocots) ELF3 contained a unique region (VII) and shared a conserved region (VI) with core eudicots EEC in the amino-terminal region, potentially differentiating them from the core eudicot ELF3s. Additionally, both EEC and ELF3 from Brassicales exhibited several unique features (regions V, VI, VIII, IX, and X) compared with other core eudicots (Fig. 2). This observation aligns with the extended branch lengths of Brassicales ELF3 and the next closely related core eudicots in the phylogenetic tree (supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online). These findings indicate that the Brassicales ELF3 sequences have only recently diverged and remain closely related to one another.

Arabidopsis thaliana ELF3 contains a PrLD which is required for LLPS in response to temperature changes (Jung et al. 2020; Hutin et al. 2023). Research on mammalian and particularly yeast prions has led to the development of various prediction tools for PrLDs, based on two different models. The compositional model identifies a large number of weak interactions distributed along the PrLD, characterized by a biased amino acid composition (Toombs et al. 2010, 2012; Gil-Garcia et al. 2021). In contrast, the soft-amyloid model emphasizes the presence of stretches with mild amyloid potential, embedded within an intrinsically disordered protein region (IDPR) (Sabate et al. 2015; Gil-Garcia et al. 2021). Given that IDPRs are known drivers of biological LLPS (Uversky 2019), we first analyzed the sequence composition of the identified ELF3/EEC sequences from 274 plant genomes using localCIDER (Holehouse et al. 2017). All ELF3/EEC sequences were classified as weak polyampholytes and polyelectrolytes, characterized by a fraction of charged residues and a net charge per residue below 0.25, which are hallmarks of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) (supplementary fig. S2a, Supplementary Material online). To determine whether the PrLD is conserved in these ELF3/EEC homologues, we then conducted a Prion-Like Amino Acid Composition (PLAAC) analysis on all sequences, obtaining PrD scores and Log-likelihood ratios (LLR) that indicate the likelihood of prion subsequences (Alberti et al. 2009; Lancaster et al. 2014). Unlike the PrD score, the LLR does not impose a hard cutoff. For example, the PrLD of A. thaliana ELF3 had an identical PrD score and LLR of 31.53, encompassing two subsequence regions (Fig. 1; supplementary figs. S1 and S3, Supplementary Material online). When considering the hard cutoff using the PrD score, the PrLD prediction identified ELF3 sequences predominantly from core eudicots (Fig. 1; supplementary fig. S1, Supplementary Material online). Additionally, ELF3 homologues in the bryophytes Physcomitrium patens and Sphagnum fallax, as well as the monocot Sorghum bicolor, were predicted to have a PrLD with a relatively low but positive PrD score. However, with exception of S. bicolor and consistent with a previous finding in B. distachyon (Jung et al. 2020), monocots generally lack such a domain in their ELF3. High PrD scores were found almost exclusively in Brassicales ELF3, with several species exhibiting even higher scores than A. thaliana (Capsella grandiflora: 59.89, Arabidopsis lyrata: 58.27, Capsella rubella: 57.52, Alyssum linifolium: 49.96, Arabidopsis halleri: 46.36, Descurainia sophioides: 45.54, Brassica rapa: 32.62, and Isatis tinctoria: 32.09), suggesting potentially conserved temperature sensing functions of PrLDs across Brassicales. Moreover, although there are four highly conserved sequence stretches between ELF3 and EEC, the third conserved region (III) was located within the gap of the predicted PrLD (Fig. 2; supplementary fig. S3, Supplementary Material online), and the polyQ stretch diverged between Brassicales ELF3 and all other sequences (Fig. 2). Consequently, none of the sequences in the EEC clade were predicted to have a PrLD (Fig. 1).

To complement the composition-based PrLD prediction of PLAAC, we also analyzed the ELF3/EEC sequences using PrionW (Zambrano et al. 2015). PrionW utilizes a combination of compositional and soft-amyloid model for PrLDs prediction. Using the default settings for Q + N richness of 25% and a pWALTZ cutoff of 73, the initial analysis identified only three hits: ELF3 of I. tinctoria (Brassicales), Populus deltoides and Populus trichocarpa (both Malpighiales). To include A. thaliana ELF3, which has an experimentally proven PrLD function mediating LLPS (Jung et al. 2020; Hutin et al. 2023), we relaxed the PrionW analysis parameters to a Q + N richness of 18% and a pWALTZ cutoff of 68. This adjustment resulted in a set of 54 sequences with predicted PrLD, which included 19 Brassicales sequences. Fourteen of these overlapped with the PLAAC set of 52 sequences, including 33 Brassicales sequences (supplementary fig. S2b, Supplementary Material online). Unlike the PLAAC results, PrionW hits were also identified in the EEC clade (supplementary fig. S2c, Supplementary Material online). These combined data, using different PrLD prediction models, reveal isolated cases of PrLD emergence in selected species, with an expansion of this domain primarily restricted to ELF3 homologues across Brassicales.

Based on previously published findings on ELF3-polyQ function and variation (Undurraga et al. 2012; Jung et al. 2020), it has been suggested that such variation enables an evolutionary adaptation to diverse latitudes and/or climates (Wilkinson and Strader 2020; Xu et al. 2021). To test this hypothesis, we first mapped all accessions according to their ELF3-polyQ length and geographic origins (coordinates), with a focus on European regions where most accessions were collected. We did not observe specific distribution patterns of ELF3-polyQ length, as accessions collected from nearby locations often varied in polyQ length (Fig. 5a). For instance, two accessions with 26Q from Spain (ID: 9584, supplementary table S3, Supplementary Material online) and Central Europe (ID: 7520) were intermixed with accessions with relatively short polyQ stretches in their ELF3. However, when considering all accessions, we observed a weak negative correlation between polyQ length and latitude (Fig. 5b), as well as a weak positive correlation between polyQ length and elevation (Fig. 5c). This can be attributed to accessions with long ELF3-polyQ stretches in non-European regions (supplementary fig. S6, Supplementary Material online). For example, all four accessions from Azerbaijan had 22 to 23Q (ID: 9069, 9070, 9089, and 9091), one accession with the longest polyQ was from the Indian Ladakh plateau (29Q, ID: 8424), and another with 27Q was from Japan (ID: 7207) (supplementary table S3, Supplementary Material online; supplementary fig. S6, Supplementary Material online). Nonetheless, since accessions with long polyQ stretches are also found in European regions with relatively high latitude and low altitude, the overall association between polyQ variation and geographic data remains inconclusive.

However, it remains unclear whether and how the PrLD is conserved in ELF3 orthologs across the plant kingdom. In this study, which involved genome scans of species across all major branches of the plant tree of life, we found that the PrLD, primarily characterized by polyQ length, is most prominently present and expanded in Brassicales. Consequently, the molecular functions of ELF3 in temperature-responsive aggregation are unlikely to be conserved in species outside of the Brassicales. Although experimental evidence is somewhat limited, the ELF3 homologue in the monocot B. distachyon has been shown to lack the ability to phase separate (Jung et al. 2020). Materials and MethodsPlant Materials and Growth ConditionsNatural accessions of A. thaliana obtained from Nottingham Arabidopsis Stock Centre (NASC) are listed in supplementary table S3, Supplementary Material online. For screening of 253 accessions, seeds were surface–sterilized by washing with 70% ethanol for 3 min, and with 4% NaClO (with 0.3% TritonX) for 8 min using an orbital shaker. Seeds were then rinsed with sterile water three times for 10 min each and stratified in sterile water for 3 d at 4 °C in darkness. Sterilized seeds were allowed to germinate on solid A. thaliana solution nutrient medium with 1% (w/v) sucrose (Lincoln et al. 1990). Seedlings were grown on vertically oriented plates in long days (LDs, 16 h light: 8 h dark) with 90 μmol m−2s−1 photosynthetically active radiation using white fluorescent lamps (­T5 4000 K). Seedlings were grown at constant 20 °C for 4 d, and were either shifted to 28 °C or kept at 20 °C for an additional 4 d. Seedlings were imaged and the length of the hypocotyl was measured using RootDetection 0.1.2 (http://www.labutils.de/rd.html). The experiments were performed separately in nine sequential batches and Col-0 (Accession ID: 6909, supplementary table S3, Supplementary Material online) was included in each batch (n = 6 to 32). To compare the data obtained among different batches, the hypocotyl length of each accession was calculated by normalizing the absolute value to the median hypocotyl length of Col-0 at 20 °C for each batch.
