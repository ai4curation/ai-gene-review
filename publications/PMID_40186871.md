The obligate intracellular pathogen Chlamydia trachomatis replicates in a 
specialized membrane-bound compartment where it repositions host organelles 
during infection to acquire nutrients and evade host surveillance. We describe a 
bacterial effector, Dre1, that binds specifically to dynactin associated with 
host microtubule organizing centers without globally impeding dynactin function. 
Dre1 is required to reposition the centrosome, mitotic spindle, Golgi apparatus, 
and primary cilia around the inclusion and contributes to pathogen fitness in 
cell-based and mouse models of infection. We utilized Dre1 to affinity purify 
the megadalton dynactin protein complex and determined the first cryoelectron 
microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of human dynactin. Our results suggest that Dre1 
binds to the pointed end of dynactin and uncovers the first bacterial effector 
that modulates dynactin function. Our work highlights how a pathogen employs a 
single effector to evoke targeted, large-scale changes in host cell organization 
that facilitate pathogen growth without inhibiting host viability.

INTRODUCTIONChlamydia trachomatis is an obligate bacterial pathogen that causes sexually transmitted infections and blinding trachoma and is also associated with cervical and ovarian cancers.1,2 The World Health Organization estimates that over 131 million new C. trachomatis infections occur annually, and there is currently no effective vaccine.3,4 Following internalization by non-phagocytic host cells, C. trachomatis resides within a membrane bound protected niche (the inclusion) that it must actively modify to scavenge essential nutrients and evade host cell surveillance.5
C. trachomatis selectively repositions host structures, including actin, microtubules (MTs), centrosomes, the Golgi apparatus (GA), the endoplasmic reticulum, and lipid droplets around the inclusion during infection to physically stabilize the inclusion and to acquire essential host-derived metabolites. A detailed understanding of how bacterial and host proteins facilitate this host cell reorganization remains elusive.5–7 A family of chlamydial secreted effectors known as inclusion membrane proteins (Incs), which are unique to the order Chlamydiales, have increasingly been recognized as modulators of host cell structures. Their host targets and mechanisms of action are incompletely understood.5,7 Incs are translocated by the type III secretion system into the inclusion membrane. Their N and C termini extend into the host cytosol, strategically positioning the Incs at the host-pathogen interface.8–10 We previously employed an affinity purification-mass spectrometry (AP-MS)-based strategy to systematically identify host binding partners of transfected Incs in order to determine the roles of Incs. This analysis identified an interaction between the host dynactin complex and the C. trachomatis Inc CT192/CTL0444 (hereafter referred to as Dre1 for Dynactin recruiting effector 1).11Dynactin facilitates intracellular trafficking by linking the cellular motor dynein to specific cargo and enhancing dynein processivity along MTs.12–14 In addition, dynactin directly binds MTs and functions to anchor and organize MTs at MT organizing centers (MTOCs) arrayed at the centrosome, the mitotic spindle, the GA, and the primary cilia.14–17 The developing C. trachomatis inclusion traffics along MTs in a dynein-dependent manner to the host centrosome,18 a non-membrane bound organelle that provides a template specifying the position of host organelles.19 The inclusion associates tightly with the centrosome throughout the C. trachomatis replicative cycle, leading to relocation of the centrosome during inclusion expansion at late stages of growth.19–22
C. trachomatis infection also leads to GA fragmentation and recruitment of GA mini-stacks to the inclusion.23–26In this work, we use cell biology, genetic, and structural approaches to demonstrate that Dre1 interacts specifically with subpopulations of dynactin associated with both centrosomal and non-centrosomal MTOCs, including those found at the centrosome, the mitotic spindle, the GA, and the primary cilia, to reposition these organelles to the inclusion, facilitating C. trachomatis intracellular growth. We utilized Dre1 as an affinity reagent to purify dynactin, a megadalton multiprotein host complex,27 from human cells for structural studies. We performed cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) on the affinity-purified Dre1-dynactin complex to present the first structure of human dynactin. Our structural data support a model where Dre1 binds the pointed-end subcomplex of dynactin. By interacting with specific populations of the multifunctional host dynactin complex, Dre1 selectively restructures the host cell interior to facilitate C. trachomatis growth without globally inhibiting other host cellular functions. Our work provides the first example of a bacterial effector that binds to dynactin and underscores unappreciated nuances of cellular functions of dynactin.

RESULTSDynactin interacts with Dre1 during C. trachomatis infectionOur previous AP-MS screen, referred to here as the transfection interactome, predicted high-confidence interactions between transfected C. trachomatis serovar D Dre1 (CT192) in HEK293T cells and all 11 subunits of the host dynactin complex11 (Figures 1A and 1B). Given that C. trachomatis genetic tools are most well developed in serovar LGV434/L27 (hereafter referred to as L2) and that Dre1 is highly conserved between serovars D (CT192) and L2 (CTL0444; 96% identity in the amino acid sequence; Figure S1), we performed all subsequent studies with L2. We confirmed that Dre1 from L2 interacts with the dynactin complex in the context of infection by performing AP-MS on lysates from HeLa cells infected with L2 transformed with a plasmid expressing Dre1 fused to a FLAG tag (L2+pDre1FLAG) (Figures 1A and 1B). Expression of Dre1 in the eluates was confirmed by immunoblotting with an anti-FLAG antibody and by silver stain (Figure S2A). Lysates of HeLa cells infected with L2 expressing vector only served as a control (Figure S2A).Protein-protein interactions were scored using the SAINT (significance analysis of interactome) algorithm,29 with a range of scores between 0 and 1 (Table S1). Nine of the 11 dynactin subunits exhibited high SAINT scores with a Bayesian false discovery rate of <0.17, indicating a high confidence and specific interaction (Figure 1B). p150glued (DCTN1), a dynactin subunit that scored highly in the transfection interactome, scored less high in the Dre1 infection interactome. However, we confirmed by immunofluorescent microscopy that the endogenous dynactin subunit p150glued was recruited to inclusions in cells infected with L2 overexpressing Dre1FLAG (L2+pDre1FLAG; Figure S2B). In parallel, we confirmed the specificity of the interaction between Dre1 and dynactin during infection by infecting HeLa cells with L2 expressing an unrelated Inc, IncG (L2+pIncGFLAG) and performing FLAG AP. Endogenous p27 (DCTN6) and Arp1a co-purified with Dre1 but not with IncG (Figure S2C). We conclude that the dynactin complex interacts reproducibly and specifically with Dre1 during C. trachomatis infection as well as during transfection. Notably, under the conditions used to affinity purify Dre1 in either the transfection or infection interactomes, Dre1 did not co-purify with dynein or any of the known adaptors that regulate dynein or dynactin activity (Table S1). Furthermore, we demonstrate that, unlike dynactin, whose recruitment to the inclusion during infection with L2+pDre1FLAG is pronounced (as assessed by GFP-Arp1a and by p150glued; Figures 1D and S2B), dynein recruitment to the inclusion appears to be more variable (Figure S2E).Dre1 is required for the recruitment of dynactin to the inclusion during infectionGiven the demonstrated interaction between Dre1 and dynactin, we tested the prediction that recruitment of dynactin to the inclusion membrane during infection should depend on expression of Dre1. For these studies, we utilized a chemically mutagenized strain of L2 that contains a single-nucleotide mutation in dre1 that introduces a stop codon at amino acid 2030 (Figure 1C; Table S2). Since the dynactin binding activity of Dre1 is C-terminal to the site of the stop codon (Figures 2A and 2B), L2Δdre1 is likely to phenocopy a loss-of-function mutant, even in the unlikely event that the predicted Dre1 20 amino acid peptide is stable. Hereafter, we refer to this mutant as L2Δdre1 and the complemented mutant, which has been transformed with a plasmid constitutively expressing Dre1, as L2Δdre1+pDre1FLAG. For these experiments, L2 and the uncomplemented L2Δdre1 strain were transformed with the control plasmid so that they were isogenic with L2Δdre1+pDre1FLAG. In cells transfected with a GFP-tagged dynactin subunit (GFP-Arp1a) and then infected with L2 or L2Δdre1, GFP-Arp1a was recruited to the inclusion membrane 24 h post infection (hpi) in HeLa cells infected with L2 but not with L2Δdre1 (Figure 1D). Furthermore, recruitment of GFP-Arp1a was restored in HeLa cells infected with the complemented strain, L2Δdre1+pDre1FLAG (Figure 1D). There was no defect in inclusion trafficking to the MTOC (Figure 1D) or in the number (Figure 1E) or size (Figure 1F) of inclusions in HeLa cells infected with L2 compared to L2Δdre1. We conclude that Dre1 is necessary for dynactin recruitment to the inclusion but that it is neither absolutely required for inclusion trafficking along MTs nor required for early steps of infection. These results suggest that Dre1 may modulate a specific function of dynactin distinct from its role in mediating cargo trafficking.The predicted structured subregions in the Dre1 C terminus are necessary for binding to dynactinWe defined the region(s) in Dre1 necessary and sufficient to interact with dynactin by transfecting HEK293T cells with truncations of streptavidin II-tagged Dre1 (Dre1Strep) followed by AP and immunoblotting. All variants encoding the Dre1 C-terminal cytoplasmic domain (Dre1181–231) co-affinity purified with endogenous p150glued (Figures 2A and 2B). Since Dre1Strep spanning residues 181–231 was poorly expressed (Figure 2B), we were unable to definitively assess whether this fragment is sufficient to bind to dynactin. We tentatively assign residues 181–231 as the dynactin binding domain (DBD; Figure 2A), although it is possible that additional residues upstream of amino acid 181 also contribute to dynactin binding.To further refine the DBD, we performed multiple sequence alignments of the Dre1 DBD from all known Chlamydia serovars encoding Dre1. This analysis identified two highly conserved regions in the Dre1 C terminus, named here CR1 and CR2, which are the only predicted structured regions in the DBD (Figure 2C). To test whether the CR1 and/or CR2 domains of Dre1 DBD are important for dynactin binding, we designed mutations predicted to disrupt Dre1-dynactin interactions. These Dre1 variants were tested in co-transfection assays for binding to multiple dynactin subunits (p150glued, p50-dynamitin [DCTN2], Arp1b) as well as to dynein and to a non-dynactin interacting partner of Dre1 identified in both the transfection11 and infection interactomes (four-and-a-half LIM domains protein 2 [FHL2]; Table S1; Figure 2D). Substitutions at 4 amino acids in CR1 (E193R, L194P, F195E, and L197W) or 2 amino acids in CR2 (E202P and V204P) disrupted dynactin and dynein binding (Figure 2D). A single substitution in CR1, in which phenylalanine 195 was changed to proline or to alanine (F195P or F195A) and which is predicted to disrupt the strand fold, decreased binding of endogenous dynactin subunits and dynein to the transfected Dre1 variants, with the proline substitution exhibiting a more severe defect (Figure 2D). In contrast, a conservative replacement of phenylalanine with tyrosine (F195Y) did not affect Dre1 binding to dynactin (Figure 2D). These data support a model where the Dre1:dynactin interaction depends on the secondary structure in that region rather than specific interactions with the F195 side chain. FLH2 co-purified with all Dre1 variants (Figure 2D), suggesting that the Dre1 structure was not significantly altered in the various mutants and that the structural requirements for Dre1 binding are specific to the Dre1:dynactin interaction.Dre1 can be used as an affinity agent to purify endogenous dynactin for structural studiesGiven that the entire megadalton multiprotein dynactin complex dynactin complex co-affinity purified with Dre1Strep11 (Figure 1B), we reasoned that we could employ transfected Dre1 as an affinity agent to efficiently purify the dynactin complex from human cells to determine the structure of Dre1-dynactin by cryo-EM. We overexpressed the Strep-tagged C terminus of Dre1, which includes the DBD (Dre182–231), in suspension-grown Expi293F cells and performed Strep AP to isolate the dynactin complex (Figure 3A). Cryo-EM analysis of this complex yielded two distinct reconstructions at ~3.5 Å global resolution (with resolution ranging from 3 to 7 Å) (Figures 3B and S3). The key difference between the two classes was the presence and absence of the shoulder region of dynactin, which has been observed previously in cryo-EM structures of porcine and bovine dynactin.27,33 The first class, composed of ~38% of the particles, included the shoulder subunits (p50, p150glued, and p24), while the second class, consisting of ~60% of particles, lacked the shoulder, likely indicating partial dissociation during purification (Figures 3B and 3C), as reported previously.27 Both classes closely matched the previously published porcine and bovine dynactin structures.27,33,34 After modeling the human dynactin subunits into the reconstructions, both maps revealed an extra density at the dynactin pointed end (p25/p27/p62) (Figure 3D) that was not observed previously in any reported dynactin complex structures (Figure S4). Although the resolution within the pointed end was not high enough to discern the protein sequence, precluding unambiguous protein assignment, these results suggest that Dre1 binds to the pointed-end complex of dynactin.The Dre1 DBD is sufficient for targeting to the centrosomal MTOCAs dynactin is localized to many distinct structures in cells where it organizes MTs, facilitates vesicle trafficking, and positions organelles, there may be functionally distinct subpopulations of dynactin.35–38 Since C. trachomatis inclusions associate with centrosomes and the MTOC,19,21,39 we were particularly interested in the population of dynactin localized at the centrosome that functions to anchor and organize MT arrays during interphase and mitosis.37,38,40 Live-cell microscopy of HeLa cells showed that the transiently expressed C-terminal fragment of Dre1 fused to super-folder GFP (sfGFP; Dre184–231-sfGFP) localizes specifically at the MTOC and at the centrosome but not along MTs (Figures 4A and 4B) despite the fact that dynactin localizes to both structures (Figure S5). Dre1 localization requires the DBD, as Dre184–180-sfGFP fails to localize to the MTOC or centrosome (Figures 4A and 4B). Furthermore, Dre1 localization does not depend on an intact MT network, as Dre1 and dynactin remain localized at the centrosome in HeLa cells that have been cold treated with nocodazole to disrupt MTs (Figure S5). We conclude that Dre1 specifically interacts with dynactin at centrosomes, possibly by targeting dynactin subpopulations that localize stably at the centrosome and that are organizing MTs nucleated at organelles38,41–44 rather than with the cellular pools of dynactin that are actively involved in MT transport.Dre1 modulates centrosome positioning during interphase and mitosisDuring C. trachomatis infection, centrosomes are recruited away from their typical juxtanuclear position and instead maintain a tight association with the inclusion.20 This process is dependent on de novo bacterial protein synthesis, suggesting that a C. trachomatis effector is involved.19 We therefore tested whether Dre1 is necessary for C. trachomatis-mediated recruitment of centrosomes to the inclusion. We performed immunofluorescence microscopy in HeLa cells infected for 36 h with L2, L2Δdre1, or L2Δdre1+pDre1FLAG and measured the distance between the centrosomes and the nearest nuclear face in three dimensions (Figures 5A and 5B). In uninfected cells, the average distance between the centrosome and the nucleus was 0.76 μm. That distance increased to 7.34 or 8.66 μm in cells infected with L2 or L2Δdre1+pDre1FLAG, respectively, and was diminished in cells infected with L2Δdre1 (2.99 μm). Thus, Dre1 contributes to centrosome repositioning during infection.Cells infected with C. trachomatis often have supernumerary centrosomes that are not efficiently clustered.20,45 We compared centrosome copy number and clustering in HeLa cells infected with Dre1-producing strains (L2 and L2Δdre1+pDre1FLAG) or a Dre1-deficient strain (L2Δdre1) for 36 h (Figures S6A and S6B). We observed that, although Dre1 does not contribute to altered centrosome copy number during infection (Figure S6B), Dre1 is required to inhibit clustering of supernumerary centrosomes (Figure 5C), a mechanism used by cells to ensure chromosome segregation in the presence of excess centrosomes.46 These findings are consistent with published results showing that, while dynactin plays a role in centrosome localization and function, dysregulation of dynactin does not result in centrosome copy number defects.47As Dre1 is required to override the host cell backup strategies that would otherwise allow supernumerary centrosomes to cluster during interphase, we next assessed the effect of Dre1 on host centrosome localization during mitosis. Typically, aberrant mitotic spindles form when centrosome clustering is inhibited.48 To determine whether the inhibition of centrosome clustering by Dre1 during infection can lead to the formation of aberrant spindles, we used a brief cold shock to synchronize HeLa cells49 and then infected the synchronized cells with L2, L2Δdre1, and L2Δdre1+pDre1FLAG for 24 h. Uninfected mitotic cells nearly always formed bipolar spindles, while 47.4% and 52% of mitotic L2-infected or L2Δdre1+pDre1-infected cells exhibited aberrant spindles, respectively (Figures 5D and 5E). In contrast, very few aberrant spindles were observed in cells infected with L2Δdre1. Thus, Dre1 contributes to the formation of aberrant spindles in mitotic cells during infection, likely as a downstream effect of Dre1-dependent inhibition of centrosome clustering. Our data support a model where Dre1 dictates centrosome position during interphase and early mitosis.As dynactin localizes to MT minus ends that congregate at spindle poles during mitosis,50 we determined whether the inclusion localizes to the host spindle poles in a Dre1-dependent manner (Figure 5D). While L2 inclusions were localized at the spindle poles, L2Δdre1 inclusions were displaced from the spindle pole and were often found displaced from the metaphase plate as well. Association of the inclusion at the spindle pole was restored in the complemented mutant. Interestingly, Dre1-directed localization of the inclusion at the spindle pole often led to displacement of host chromosomes from the metaphase plate (Figure 5D).Host mitotic failure is often seen during C. trachomatis infection and is believed to be a mechanism by which C. trachomatis prevents loss of host resources to a daughter cell.51,52 Thus, Dre1-mediated association with dynactin at the spindle pole might position the inclusion to physically inhibit mitosis and/or cytokinesis, leading to multinucleated host cells. Multinucleation was observed in 1.6% of uninfected HeLa cells (Figure S6C), while 22.3% of L2 infected cells were multinucleated by 48 hpi (Figure S6D). In contrast, only 8.3% of cells infected with L2Δdre1 were multinucleated, while L2Δdre1+p−Δre1FLAG-infected cells exhibited multinucleation rates similar to L2-infected cells (18.8%). Our results demonstrate that Dre1 plays a role in C. trachomatis-induced multinucleation, likely through its effects on centrosome position and spindle architecture.Dre1 repositions other dynactin-associated MTOCsWe next tested whether other organelles whose localization is specified by the centrosome are also repositioned during infection. The primary cilium is an MT-based sensory organelle involved in the regulation of many cellular processes that originates from the centrosome.53 Dynactin has been shown to play a role in anchoring cytoplasmic MTs to the base of the primary cilium.54 We infected A2EN cells, a human endocervical epithelial cell line that readily forms primary cilia in the presence of serum,55 with L2, L2Δdre1, or L2Δdre1+pDre1FLAG for 24 h and then fixed and stained for Arl13b (a protein that is highly enriched on the ciliary membrane). In cells infected with Dre1-producing bacteria (L2 or L2Δdre1+pDre1FLAG), one end of the cilium localizes at the inclusion membrane in ~80% of infected cells. In contrast, in cells infected with L2Δdre1, the cilium is only anchored at the inclusion membrane in ~15% of cells (Figures 6A and 6B). Together, these results demonstrate that Dre1 repositions the primary cilium during infection.The GA also functions as an MTOC, and dynein/dynactin regulates its structure and perinuclear localization.42,43 Dynactin binds MTs anchored at the GA as well as to βIII spectrin on GA membranes through its Arp1 subunit.43,56 In C. trachomatis-infected cells, the GA fragments into mini-stacks that are recruited around the inclusion.24 To test whether Dre1 mediates recruitment of the GA to the inclusion, HeLa cells were infected for 24 h with L2, L2Δdre1, and L2Δdre1+pDre1; fixed; and stained with anti-GM130 (a cis-GA marker) (Figure 6C). 52.8% of the inclusion surface area was within 1 μm of the GA in L2-infected cells, whereas only 23.3% of the inclusion surface area in L2Δdre1-infected cells was within 1 μm of the GA (Figure S6E). GA recruitment was restored to wild-type WT levels in the complemented strain (55.5%; Figure 6D).Dre1 modulates efficiency of inclusion fusionWhen multiple bacteria infect a single cell, they are each internalized within separate inclusions, which, upon trafficking to the MTOC, undergo homotypic fusion.57 Inclusion fusion contributes to C. trachomatis fitness in both cell-based models and in human infections.58–61 Additionally, inclusion fusion may serve as a mechanism for genetic exchange between bacteria62 and/or as a strategy to avoid competing for the same resources in the host cell. Inclusion fusion absolutely requires IncA, a soluble NSF attachment protein receptor (SNARE) domain-containing inclusion-localized effector.63,64 We tested whether Dre1 mediates inclusion fusion and whether centrosomal dynactin contributes to the process of inclusion fusion. Infections were performed at a high MOI (MOI of 5), and the numbers of inclusions per cell were compared in Dre1-expressing strains compared to the L2Δdre1 mutant. In cells infected with L2Δdre1, we observed a significant delay in inclusion fusion compared to L2, which was restored in cells infected with the complemented strain (L2Δdre1+pDre1) (Figure 7A). Of note, the requirement of Dre1 for inclusion fusion was not absolute. By 48 hpi, inclusion fusion in L2Δdre1 was similar to that in Dre1-expressing strains (Figure 7A). Importantly, loss of Dre1 does not affect IncA expression or localization (Figure 1D). Furthermore, L2-infected cells containing multiple inclusions exhibited Dre1-dependent GFP-Arp1a localization at the boundary membranes between unfused inclusions at 24 hpi (Figure 7B, white arrows) and remained visible in inclusions appearing to be undergoing fusion, as defined by non-continuous boundary membranes at the same time point (Figure 7B, yellow arrows). Our finding that dynactin (based on Arp1a staining) is present at both pre-fusion inclusions and in inclusions undergoing fusion suggests that Dre1 contributes to the efficiency of inclusion fusion, likely by localizing pre-fusion inclusions at the centrosomal MTOC through an interaction with centrosome-associated dynactin. Indeed, we observed that the rare L2Δdre1 inclusions that remained unfused at 36 hpi were located on opposite sides of the nucleus from one another (Figure S7), suggesting that the interaction between Dre1 and dynactin at centrosomes contributes to the close apposition of pre-fusion inclusions.To further test this hypothesis, we performed 1:1 co-infection for 36 h using GFP-expressing L2 (L2+pGFP), which should interact with centrosome-associated dynactin, and L2Δdre1 (which was not fluorescent), which should not interact with centrosome-associated dynactin. In cases where there were two WT (L2+pGFP) inclusions within the same cell, they were always found immediately adjacent to one another. In contrast, in cells containing one L2GFP inclusion and one L2Δdre1 inclusion or two L2Δdre1 inclusions, 90% of inclusions were found on opposite sides of the nucleus (Figures S7A and S7B). As loss of Dre1 expression during infection contributes to defects in repositioning host organelles around the inclusion, we tested the contribution of Dre1 to virulence in a cell-based assay and in a murine model of upper genital tract infection. Dre1 repositions other dynactin-associated MTOCs

We next tested whether other organelles whose localization is specified by the centrosome are also repositioned during infection. The primary cilium is an MT-based sensory organelle involved in the regulation of many cellular processes that originates from the centrosome.53 Dynactin has been shown to play a role in anchoring cytoplasmic MTs to the base of the primary cilium.54 We infected A2EN cells, a human endocervical epithelial cell line that readily forms primary cilia in the presence of serum,55 with L2, L2Δdre1, or L2Δdre1+pDre1FLAG for 24 h and then fixed and stained for Arl13b (a protein that is highly enriched on the ciliary membrane). In cells infected with Dre1-producing bacteria (L2 or L2Δdre1+pDre1FLAG), one end of the cilium localizes at the inclusion membrane in ~80% of infected cells. In contrast, in cells infected with L2Δdre1, the cilium is only anchored at the inclusion membrane in ~15% of cells (Figures 6A and 6B). Dre1 modulates efficiency of inclusion fusionWhen multiple bacteria infect a single cell, they are each internalized within separate inclusions, which, upon trafficking to the MTOC, undergo homotypic fusion.57 Inclusion fusion contributes to C. trachomatis fitness in both cell-based models and in human infections.58–61 Additionally, inclusion fusion may serve as a mechanism for genetic exchange between bacteria62 and/or as a strategy to avoid competing for the same resources in the host cell. Inclusion fusion absolutely requires IncA, a soluble NSF attachment protein receptor (SNARE) domain-containing inclusion-localized effector.63,64 We tested whether Dre1 mediates inclusion fusion and whether centrosomal dynactin contributes to the process of inclusion fusion. Infections were performed at a high MOI (MOI of 5), and the numbers of inclusions per cell were compared in Dre1-expressing strains compared to the L2Δdre1 mutant. In cells infected with L2Δdre1, we observed a significant delay in inclusion fusion compared to L2, which was restored in cells infected with the complemented strain (L2Δdre1+pDre1) (Figure 7A). Of note, the requirement of Dre1 for inclusion fusion was not absolute. By 48 hpi, inclusion fusion in L2Δdre1 was similar to that in Dre1-expressing strains (Figure 7A). Importantly, loss of Dre1 does not affect IncA expression or localization (Figure 1D). Furthermore, L2-infected cells containing multiple inclusions exhibited Dre1-dependent GFP-Arp1a localization at the boundary membranes between unfused inclusions at 24 hpi (Figure 7B, white arrows) and remained visible in inclusions appearing to be undergoing fusion, as defined by non-continuous boundary membranes at the same time point (Figure 7B, yellow arrows). Our finding that dynactin (based on Arp1a staining) is present at both pre-fusion inclusions and in inclusions undergoing fusion suggests that Dre1 contributes to the efficiency of inclusion fusion, likely by localizing pre-fusion inclusions at the centrosomal MTOC through an interaction with centrosome-associated dynactin. Indeed, we observed that the rare L2Δdre1 inclusions that remained unfused at 36 hpi were located on opposite sides of the nucleus from one another (Figure S7), suggesting that the interaction between Dre1 and dynactin at centrosomes contributes to the close apposition of pre-fusion inclusions.To further test this hypothesis, we performed 1:1 co-infection for 36 h using GFP-expressing L2 (L2+pGFP), which should interact with centrosome-associated dynactin, and L2Δdre1 (which was not fluorescent), which should not interact with centrosome-associated dynactin. In cases where there were two WT (L2+pGFP) inclusions within the same cell, they were always found immediately adjacent to one another. In contrast, in cells containing one L2GFP inclusion and one L2Δdre1 inclusion or two L2Δdre1 inclusions, 90% of inclusions were found on opposite sides of the nucleus (Figures S7A and S7B). These results suggest that the relative defect in L2Δdre1 inclusion fusion is at least in part a consequence of the failure of inclusions to be brought near each other by their simultaneous juxtaposition at the primary MTOC, an event that is likely mediated by the interaction between Dre1 and dynactin.

As loss of Dre1 expression during infection contributes to defects in repositioning host organelles around the inclusion, we tested the contribution of Dre1 to virulence in a cell-based assay and in a murine model of upper genital tract infection. First, we quantified whether Dre1 is required for C. trachomatis infectious progeny production in HeLa cells or in A2EN cells, which are both cell lines derived from cervical cells. We further demonstrate that the Dre1 is critical to the intracellular survival and pathogenesis of C. trachomatis. Our findings illuminate a new sophisticated strategy that is employed by an intracellular pathogen to selectively remodel the host cell while maintaining its viability.

Our work also illustrates how a bacterial effector can be used to purify a multiprotein complex. By exploiting Dre1 as an affinity reagent, we purified dynactin from human cell culture and solved the first cryo-EM structure of human dynactin. The structure is consistent with previously visualized porcine and bovine dynactin, demonstrating a remarkable degree of conservation. Importantly, our workflow—purification from small-scale mammalian cell culture using Dre1 as an affinity reagent—allows for structural studies of dynactin with mutated components and will open the door to a better mechanistic understanding of dynactin biology. Such studies were previously inaccessible, as dynactin required purification from natural sources, such as bovine or porcine brains.

Although the Dre1 region of our cryo-EM structure was not of high enough resolution to resolve the specific contacts of Dre1 with the individual subunits of the pointed end (p25, p27, and p62) of dynactin, this extra density is present only in our cryo-EM map as compared to dozens of existing cryo-EM maps of dynactin.27,34,66–68 These findings suggest that Dre1 interacts with the pointed end of dynactin. Using structural prediction and mutational analysis, we identified two highly conserved regions in the Dre1 C terminus that are specifically required for binding to dynactin. Interestingly, the pointed end is known to be involved in binding to dynactin adaptors.69,70 Adaptors activate dynein-dependent transport and couple dynactin to cargo and to dynein. Although several pointed-end adaptor binding complexes have been visualized, in all of those structures, the adaptors interact on the opposite face of the pointed end compared to Dre1 in our structure.27,66–68 Under our experimental conditions, no known dynactin or dynein-associated adaptor proteins co-purified with the Dre1-dynactin complex.11 This finding suggests the intriguing possibility that Dre1 binding to the pointed end disrupts the interaction of adaptors with dynactin or that Dre1 interacts with a subset of dynactin complexes lacking adaptors. How this relates to the preferential association of Dre1 with dynactin at MTOCs will be a fertile area for future investigation.

This study underscores the nuances of dynactin and dynein regulation and supports the emerging notion that there are subcellular pools of dynactin with distinct functions. Like many pathogens, C. trachomatis utilizes dynein to facilitate intracellular transport to the host MTOC.19 Surprisingly, Dre1 is not absolutely required for this initial step of infection, as we observed no defect in inclusion transport to the MTOC 24 hpi. Together with the data showing that Dre1 colocalizes specifically with dynactin at the MTOC and not on MTs, these data support the hypothesis that there are distinct dynactin subpopulations that perform different functions and that Dre1 may distinguish between these various pools. Dre1 is one of the few known pathogen effectors that specifically target dynactin rather than dynein.71,72 Given that Dre1 does not interact with any known adaptor proteins that enhance dynein processivity, we speculate that the primary role of the Dre1:dynactin interaction might be to target the MT-organizing functions of dynactin rather than targeting actively trafficking dynactin/dynein complexes.35,73 Understanding how Dre1 selectively targets this dynactin subpopulation is likely to provide important insight into the subspecialization of dynactin.

Our work adds to the growing evidence that C. trachomatis modulation of host cell division is central to its pathogenesis, as it employs multiple effectors that cause abnormalities in centrosome number and location, mitotic spindle defects, multinucleation, aneuploidy, and cytokinesis.20,22,51,52,74–77 Our results suggest that C. trachomatis Dre1 overrides the host’s centrosome positioning pathways, leading to abnormal spindle formation. Recently published studies reveal that C. trachomatis-induced centrosome amplification utilizes the secreted effector CteG, which interacts through its C terminus with centrin-2.74 Other effectors, including IncM, CT223/inclusion protein acting on MTs (IPAM), CT288, and chlamydial protease-like activity factor (CPAF) have also been implicated in C. trachomatis modulation of centrosome dynamics.7,75,77,78 Despite the reduced genome of C. trachomatis, the evolution and maintenance of multiple effectors to perturb these processes speaks to the importance of this strategy for this obligate intracellular pathogen. We favor the notion that C. trachomatis interfaces with the centrosome to regulate host cell morphology and block cytokinesis, giving the inclusion greater access to host cell nutrients and preventing the loss of resources to a daughter cell upon host division.

The ability of Dre1 to induce centrosome and mitotic abnormalities could contribute to the role of C. trachomatis as a co-factor, along with human papilloma virus (HPV), in the development of cervical cancer.2 Recent work has revealed that HPV and C. trachomatis infection induce centrosome overduplication in an additive manner.45 As centrosome de-clustering agents have been identified as promising targeted cancer therapeutics,46,79 Dre1 may be an attractive candidate for future studies.

C. trachomatis appears to modulate the primary cilium in multiple ways. It was recently reported that C. trachomatis induces disassembly of primary cilia.80 Our work adds an additional level of complexity in that we were able to observe repositioning of one end of primary cilia to the inclusion membrane. Whether recruitment of primary cilia to the inclusion precedes primary cilia disassembly remains to be determined.

In summary, we describe the first bacterial effector to directly bind to dynactin to modulate its function. Our work highlights how a single pathogen effector can facilitate multiple changes in host cell architecture by distinguishing and co-opting functionally unique subpopulations of a ubiquitous host protein complex. Future work elucidating the structural details of how Dre1 targets functionally distinct subsets of dynactin will likely enhance our understanding of the intricate mechanisms by which this essential human complex is regulated in healthy and diseased states.

Sample preparation and mass spectrometryEluates were digested with trypsin for LC-MS/MS analysis. Samples were denatured and reduced in 2M urea, 2 mM DTT, 10 mM NH4HCO3 for 30 min at 60°C, then alkylated with 2 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature for 45 min. Trypsin (Promega) was added at a 1:100 enzyme: substrate ratio and digested at 37°C overnight. Following digestion, samples were then concentrated using C18 ZipTips (Millipore Sigma) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Desalted samples were evaporated to dryness and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid for MS analysis. Digested peptide mixtures were analyzed by LC-MS/MS on an Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an Easy-nLC 1200 HPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were directly injected onto an analytical column (360 μm O.D. × 75 μm I.D.) with an integrated emitter (New Objective) that was packed with 25 cm of ReproSil Pur C18 AQ 1.9 μm particles (Dr. Maisch). The HPLC system delivered a gradient from 4% to 30% ACN in 0.1% formic acid over 43 min, followed by an increase to 80% ACN over 5 min, and lastly a hold at 80% ACN for 20 min. Peptides were introduced into the mass spectrometer by electrospray ionization in positive mode (1980V) with a transfer tube at 300°C. MS1 scans were performed with orbitrap detection in profile mode at a resolution of 120K, a scan range of 400–1600 m/z, a maximum in injection time of 100 ms, and AGC target of 200K ions, 1 microscan, an S-Lens RV of 60. Peptides with peptide isotopic distribution patterns (MIPS = on) of charge state 2–7 were selected for data-dependent MS2 fragmentation, with a dynamic exclusion time of 20s, a single selection being allowed, a +/− 10 ppm mass tolerance, and a minimum signal of 5K. Peptides selected for MS2 were fragmented by beam-type collisional activation (HCD), with a 1.6 m/z isolation window, a first mass of 100 m/z, a collision energy of 30, detection in the ion trap at a rapid scan rate in centroid mode, a 35 ms maximum injection time, and AGC target of 10K, inject ions for all available parallelizable time was activated.

Image processing and 3D reconstructionThe raw movies were corrected for both motion and radiation damage using MotionCor2.92 The corrected sums were subsequently imported into CryoSPARC294 for further processing. Micrograph contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated with the patch CTF job in CryoSPARC2. Micrographs were curated based on CTF fit resolution and ice thickness. Particles were initially manually picked, extracted, and 2D-classified. The initial classification yielded dynactin classes with and without shoulder. Particles were then template picked with 2D class averages low-pass filtered (to 20 Å). The resulting picks were extracted with 2× Fourier cropping and subjected to iterative rounds of ab initio and heterogeneous refinements. Once reasonable reconstructions were obtained (as examined by the Fourier shell correlation (FSC) curve shapes), the unbinned particles were re-extracted and run through subsequent rounds of heterogeneous and non-uniform refinements to achieve reconstructions with the highest resolution. The dynactin reconstructions from CryoSPARC2 included total 188,380 particles of which ~40% of the particles were with shoulder and ~60% particles did not show shoulder subunit. To mitigate streaking artifacts caused by the orientation bias of dynactin particles in the CryoSPARC reconstructions, the final particle stack was imported to cisTEM95 for further processing. Through iterative rounds of 3D classification and local refinement, the final reconstructions were obtained with 45,091 particles for the dynactin class with shoulder and 50,376 particles lacking the shoulder subunit with the overall resolution of 3.5 Å by gold standard FSC (GS-FSC) cut-off of 0.143 (masked). Local and directional resolutions for each map were evaluated using local resolution estimation and 3DFSC jobs in CryoSPARC v4. For both the reconstructions, the local resolution of the Arp1 filament ranged from 3 to 5 Å and the rest of the dynactin complex displayed local resolutions ranging from 3.5 to 7 Å. We combined the two sets of particles (with and without the dynactin shoulder) to test if higher particle count improved the resolution of specifically the pointed end. Further, we performed 3D classification without image alignment in cisTEM, using masks focused on different regions of dynactin (the pointed end, the barbed end and the shoulder) to improve resolution in the respective regions. Specifically, for the pointed end we tried masks of three sizes around the pointed end for iterative rounds of local refinement. These steps did not significantly improve the map quality from that presented here.

For the shoulder subunits DCTN1/p150-glued, DCTN2/p50 and DCTN3/p22 homology models were made in SwissModel97 using corresponding chains from PDB ID: 6ZNL as template. The helix bundle of DCTN1/p150-glued was modeled by fitting poly-alanine chains into the map. The initial models were subsequently fitted into the respective cryo-EM maps using the FastRelax protocol in Rosetta 3.0 in torsion space. The entire complex models underwent further manual examination and refinement in Coot99 and ISOLDE.100 The quality of the models was assessed using MolProbity, as part of the Phenix 1.2101 validation tools. The model building and refinement is summarized in Table S3.
