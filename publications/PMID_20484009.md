Abstract

Eukaryotic initiation factor 2B (eIF2B) is a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) whose activity is both tightly regulated and rate-controlling with regard to global rates of protein synthesis. Skeletal muscle eIF2B activity and expression of its catalytic ε-subunit (eIF2Bε) have been implicated as potential contributors to the altered rates of protein synthesis in a number of physiological conditions and experimental models. The objective of this study was to directly examine the effects of exogenously expressed eIF2Bε in vivo on GEF activity and protein synthetic rates in rat skeletal muscle. A plasmid encoding FLAG-eIF2Bε was transfected into the tibialis anterior (TA) of one leg, while the contralateral TA received a control plasmid. Ectopic expression of eIF2Bε resulted in increased GEF activity in TA homogenates of healthy rats, demonstrating that the expressed protein was catalytically active. In an effort to restore a deficit in eIF2B activity, we utilized an established model of chronic sepsis in which skeletal muscle eIF2B activity is known to be impaired. Ectopic expression of eIF2Bε in the TA rescued the sepsis-induced deficit in GEF activity and muscle protein synthesis. The results demonstrate that modulation of eIF2Bε expression may be sufficient to correct deficits in skeletal muscle protein synthesis associated with sepsis and other muscle-wasting conditions.

Keywords: mRNA translation, in vivo electroporation, muscle wasting

skeletal muscle exhibits a unique plasticity with regard to its capacity to increase or decrease in size (i.e., hypertrophy or atrophy) in response to a number of nutritional, environmental, and mechanical stimuli. The loss of skeletal muscle mass that occurs in a number of pathophysiological conditions can lead to adverse effects in functional strength and mobility and, in many conditions, is associated with increased morbidity and mortality (e.g., sarcopenia, sepsis, and cancer cachexia) (for reviews see Refs. 2 , 8 , 24 ). In contrast, repetitive application of a mechanical load, such as resistance exercise, leads to a transient shift in the balance between protein synthesis and degradation in favor of net protein synthesis, and thus the cumulative effect of repetitive bouts of resistance exercise is an increase in muscle size ( 4 , 49 , 50 ). To design appropriate therapeutic interventions to ameliorate skeletal muscle atrophy and maintain or increase muscle mass, the mechanisms that regulate skeletal muscle size must be thoroughly understood.

In essence, skeletal muscle hypertrophy requires increased rates of protein synthesis relative to rates of protein degradation, such that a net increase in size occurs due to protein accretion ( 4 , 18 , 49 , 50 ). The process of protein synthesis (i.e., translation of mRNA into protein) consists of three major steps: initiation, elongation, and termination. The initiation phase of translation is particularly well regulated and is generally the rate-controlling step of protein synthesis (for a recent review on translation initiation see Ref. 25 ). Eukaryotic initiation factor (eIF) 2B (eIF2B) is a critical component of the first and rate-controlling step of translation initiation. The eIF2B holoenzyme functions as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for another initiation factor, eIF2. The heterotrimeric G protein eIF2 must be in a GTP-bound state to bind initiator methionyl-tRNA to form a ternary complex that subsequently associates with the 40S ribosomal subunit. Once the GTP bound to eIF2 is hydrolyzed to GDP (a step catalyzed by eIF5), it is released from the ribosome, and a GEF is necessary to recycle the now inactive eIF2·GDP complex to its active GTP-bound form to replenish available ternary complex for subsequent rounds of initiation ( 22 , 48 ).

A unique characteristic of the eIF2B GEF is that it exists as a heteropentameric complex consisting of (in order of increasing molecular mass) the α-, β-, γ-, δ-, and ε-subunits. It has been established that the largest (ε) subunit possesses the catalytic GEF activity and can function in the absence of the other four subunits, albeit at a lower efficiency than the holoenzyme ( 1 , 9 , 19 ). Alterations in eIF2B activity and/or abundance are frequently associated with concomitant changes in skeletal muscle protein synthetic rate. For example, acute resistance exercise, which elicits increased rates of protein synthesis in skeletal muscle, has been associated with increased eIF2B activity ( 11 , 35 ) and eIF2Bε protein abundance ( 15 , 37 , 38 ). In contrast, severe diabetes is associated with reduced eIF2B activity ( 28 , 32 ), and the resistance exercise-induced increases in eIF2B activity and protein synthesis are abrogated in severe diabetes ( 35 ). The activity and content of eIF2B are reduced during aging in several rat tissues ( 33 ), and the increase in eIF2Bε protein abundance observed with functional overload-associated hypertrophy is diminished in older rats, where muscle growth in response to loading is also attenuated ( 23 ).

In addition to aging and diabetes, reduced eIF2B activity has also been implicated in mediating the deleterious effects of sepsis on skeletal muscle. Sepsis is characterized by dramatic reductions in protein synthesis with enhanced protein degradation, resulting in a net catabolic condition and the loss of skeletal muscle mass ( 42 ). The deficit in protein synthesis occurs in muscles that comprise predominantly fast-twitch fibers (e.g., psoas and gastrocnemius), but not in soleus or cardiac muscle ( 40 , 55 ). In rats, acute administration of endotoxin results in a transient repression of eIF2B activity ( 41 ), as does chronic intra-abdominal sepsis of 5 days duration ( 54 ). Reduced eIF2B activity ( 54 ), reduced eIF2Bε abundance ( 6 , 57 , 59 ), and increased eIF2Bε phosphorylation ( 53 ) have been observed and implicated as important mechanisms contributing to the repression of protein synthesis in skeletal muscle during chronic sepsis.

On the basis of the observations that eIF2B activity and eIF2Bε protein expression are increased at time points concomitant with increased protein synthesis (and vice versa), it seems likely that increasing the expression of the catalytic subunit of a rate-controlling factor in translation initiation would play an important role in stimulating global rates of protein synthesis in skeletal muscle. Moreover, in conditions where eIF2B expression and/or activity become limiting and result in repressed protein synthesis (e.g., sepsis), increasing the expression of the catalytic subunit may be able to rescue deficits in protein synthesis. Therefore, the objective of the present study was to directly manipulate the expression of eIF2Bε alone to assess whether changes in its expression are sufficient to increase protein synthesis in vivo. In this study, we provide evidence that overexpression of eIF2Bε is sufficient to enhance eIF2B GEF activity in the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle of healthy rats, as well as rescue the deficits in eIF2B activity and protein synthesis in septic animals.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals.

Animal facilities and experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The Pennsylvania State University College of Medicine. Adult male Sprague-Dawley rats (∼200–275 g body wt; Charles River Laboratories) were maintained on a 12:12-h light-dark cycle, with water and food (Rodent Chow 8604, Harlan-Teklad, Madison, WI) provided ad libitum.

Plasmid constructs and DNA preparation.

All plasmid DNA was amplified in XL-1 Blue Supercompetent Cells (Stratagene, Cedar Creek, TX) and purified with an endotoxin-free GigaPrep kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA). The pmaxGFP plasmid (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) from the copepod Pontellina plumata was utilized for fluorescence imaging and served as a control in study 1 . The pcDNA3.1 plasmid (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) served as the control plasmid in study 2 and also as the vector for the pFLAG-eIF2Bε construct containing the previously cloned ( 14 ) rat eIF2Bε cDNA with an NH 2 -terminal FLAG-epitope sequence ( 10 ).

In vivo electroporation.

Rats were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane (2–3% in O 2 ) for plasmid injection and subsequent electroporation. Once the rats were anesthetized, the lower hindlimbs were shaved with an electric razor to expose the skin, swabbed with alcohol, and air-dried to clean the injection site. Plasmid DNA (100 μl; 2 mg/ml in 0.9% sterile saline) was slowly injected directly into the TA through the skin via a 0.3-ml insulin syringe fitted with a 28-gauge, 0.5-in. needle (Becton-Dickinson). A 9-mm length of size 22 polyethylene tubing (catalog no. 3002, 3M) was used as a spacer on the needle to facilitate consistent and appropriate injection depth. Square-wave electric impulses generated by an electroporator (model ECM 830, BTX, San Diego, CA) were delivered via caliper electrodes (model 384, BTX) coated with electrode conductive gel. The electrodes were applied to the lower hindlimb with only slight pressure to ensure proper contact. Pulse trains were delivered with a 200 V/cm field strength (8 pulses, 20 ms/pulse, 1 Hz) on the basis of previously described parameters ( 44 ). The caliper electrodes were rotated for each limb, so that the negative electrode was in contact with the skin directly above the TA. Careful excision of the transfected muscles and associated procedures for studies 1 and 2 were performed 24 h following the electroporation procedure (see below).

GFP fluorescence microscopy.

After excision of the entire TA, sections of the muscle were cut, placed in Tissue-Tek Unicassettes (Sakura FineTek, Torrance, CA), and fixed in 10% buffered formalin (Fisher, Pittsburgh, PA) for ≥24 h. Formalin-fixed samples were submitted to The Pennsylvania State University College of Medicine Histology Facility for paraffin embedding, cryostat sectioning, and slide preparation. Fluorescence images of pmaxGFP-transfected TA muscles were acquired with an Eclipse E800 microscope using the FITC filter, DXM1200 digital camera, and ACT-1 software (Nikon Instruments, Melville, NY).

General procedures for study 1.

The TA muscles of each rat ( n = 18 total) were transfected via in vivo electroporation with pFLAG-eIF2Bε in one TA and pmaxGFP in the TA of the contralateral limb as a control (100 μl of 2 mg/ml plasmid DNA in saline). The design allowed for the analysis of the effects of overexpression of eIF2Bε within the same animal. The limb receiving pFLAG-eIF2Bε was alternated between the left and right TA muscles. On the day of assay (i.e., 24 h after electroporation), rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg ip). A flooding dose (1.0 ml/100 g body wt) of l -[2,3,4,5,6- 3 H]phenylalanine (150 mM containing 100 μCi/ml; GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ) was injected via the tail vein for the measurement of mixed-muscle protein synthesis ( 17 ). Rats were decapitated 10 min after tracer administration for collection of trunk blood. The blood was allowed to clot on ice prior to centrifugation at 1,800 g for 10 min at 4°C to obtain serum, which was stored at −80°C. The TA muscle was carefully excised, cleaned of connective tissue, and weighed. Approximately 0.3 g of tissue in 7 vol of muscle homogenization buffer designed for the eIF2B GEF activity assay ( 35 ) was homogenized on ice in a Polytron homogenizer. An aliquot (0.5 ml) of the homogenate was placed directly into ice-cold trichloroacetic acid for measurement of skeletal muscle protein synthesis. The actual elapsed time (∼14 min) from the injection of metabolic tracer until the muscle was homogenized was recorded for the calculation of incorporation of radiolabeled amino acid into protein. Serum samples were analyzed by The Pennsylvania State University General Clinical Research Center core laboratory to obtain phenylalanine concentration and 3 H disintegrations per minute. The specific radioactivity of serum phenylalanine was considered to be representative of the precursor pool, and the fractional rates of skeletal muscle protein synthesis were estimated on the basis of the rate of incorporation of radioactive phenylalanine into trichloroacetic acid-precipitable muscle protein. The remainder of the muscle homogenate was immediately centrifuged at 1,000 g for 3 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was used for measurement of eIF2B GEF activity as described below. An additional aliquot (0.1 ml) of supernatant was added to an equal volume of 2× SDS sample buffer [0.0625 M Tris·HCl (pH 6.8), 12.5% (vol/vol) glycerol, 1.25% SDS, 1.25% (vol/vol) β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.1% bromphenol blue] and boiled at 100°C for 3 min for Western blot analyses of protein abundance and phosphorylation state as described below.

General procedures for study 2: sterile and septic abdominal surgery.

Chronic abdominal sepsis was induced by implantation of a fecal-agar pellet (1.5 ml) inoculated with 10 4 colony-forming units of Escherichia coli and 10 8 colony-forming units of Bacteroides fragilis into the peritoneal cavity as previously described ( 27 , 52 , 53 ). Control animals received the same surgery but were implanted with a agar pellet with an equal volume of sterile saline replacing the bacterial inoculum. After the animals were allowed to recover from the surgical procedures, the intra-abdominal abscess was allowed to develop for 5 days. During this period, septic animals display leukocytosis and manifest a hyperdynamic, hypermetabolic septic condition that does not occur in rats with the nonseptic intraperitoneal abscess ( 45 ).

The TA muscles of the sterile and septic rats were transfected via the in vivo electroporation procedure on day 4 postsurgery for final death and muscle harvest on day 5 (i.e., 24 h following electroporation). The 24-h transfection period was chosen on the basis of optimal expression of the FLAG-eIF2Bε protein at this time during pilot experiments. Each rat received pcDNA3.1 (empty vector) in one TA and pFLAG-eIF2Bε in the contralateral TA (100 μl of 2 mg/ml plasmid DNA in saline). On the day of harvest, the procedures for anesthesia, muscle homogenization, and sample processing were as described for study 1 , except for the route of administration of phenylalanine and the blood collection for measurements of protein synthesis as previously described ( 52 ). Four animals were excluded from all analyses on the basis of body weight gain (>3% of initial body mass on day 5 ) and a TA-to-body mass ratio greater than control animals (i.e., those animals that evidenced rapid recovery from the septic surgery and had unimpaired muscle growth during the 5-day procedure).

Measurement of eIF2B guanine nucleotide exchange activity.

The GEF activity of eIF2B in the TA homogenate was analyzed as previously described for skeletal muscle ( 32 , 35 ) with slight modifications. The TA homogenate supernatant (120 μl) was initially incubated with nonradioactive GDP mix for 2 min at 30°C. The assay was started with the addition of the [ 3 H]GDP mixture, and aliquots (60 μl) of the reaction mixture were taken at an initial time point ( t = 0) and then at 1-min intervals for 6 min ( study 1 ) or 5 min ( study 2 ). The rate of exchange of [ 3 H]GDP for unlabeled GDP was calculated as the linear decrease in radioactivity captured on nitrocellulose filters (i.e., bound to eIF2) over time. The data were corrected for protein concentration of the homogenates as assessed in duplicate by the Bio-Rad protein assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) using crystalline BSA as a standard.

SDS-PAGE and Western blotting.

Equal volumes of TA homogenates in Laemmli sample buffer were loaded onto Criterion precast 4–15% SDS-PAGE gels (Bio-Rad). After SDS-PAGE, the resolved proteins were transferred onto a 0.45-μm polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Pall Life Sciences) for 1.5 h. Membranes were subsequently blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline + Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with one of the following primary antibodies diluted in TBST: total eIF2α [mouse monoclonal ( 51 ), 1:5,000 dilution], eIF2Bβ [mouse monoclonal, 1:500 dilution], eIF2Bδ [mouse monoclonal, 1:200 dilution], total eIF2Bε [mouse monoclonal ( 30 ), 1:200 dilution], phosphorylated (Ser 539 ) eIF2Bε (corresponding to rat Ser 535 ) and phosphorylated (Ser 51 ) eIF2α (rabbit polyclonal antibodies 44-530G and 44-728G, respectively, Biosource/Invitrogen; 1:3,000 dilution), α-tubulin (mouse monoclonal antibody sc-32293, Santa Cruz Biotechnology; 1:1,000 dilution), RGS2 (rabbit polyclonal antibody, GenWay), and anti-FLAG (M2 mouse monoclonal antibody F-1804, Sigma; 1:1,000 dilution). After overnight incubation with primary antibody, membranes were washed with TBST and probed with appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Bethyl; 1:10,000 dilution in TBST with 5% nonfat dry milk) for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were washed again with TBST and developed with ECL (Pierce) or ECL Plus (GE Healthcare) chemiluminescence detection reagents. Images were acquired using a GeneGnome HR imaging system with GeneSnap software (SynGene). Band densities were quantitated using GeneTools software (SynGene). All protein quantitation analyses were normalized to α-tubulin densitometry values for the corresponding sample unless otherwise noted. For determination of protein phosphorylation status, membranes were initially immunoblotted with phosphospecific antibodies and then stripped (using buffer containing 62.5 mM Tris·HCl, 69.35 mM SDS, and 18.3 μM β-mercaptoethanol, pH 6.7) and reprobed with antibodies directed against the respective total protein.

Statistical analyses.

Data from study 1 comparing values of control (pmaxGFP) with pFLAG-eIF2Bε-transfected TA muscles were analyzed by paired t -tests via Prism version 5 (GraphPad Software). For study 2 , unpaired t -tests were employed to evaluate the effects of surgery (i.e., control vs. septic rats), while paired t -tests were used to analyze the effects of the plasmid delivered (i.e., empty vector vs. pFLAG-eIF2Bε within each animal). A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni's posttest was employed to compare body mass data over time in study 2 . Data are presented as means ± SE. For all analyses, P < 0.05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

Several lines of evidence indicate an important role for eIF2B (and, particularly, the catalytic ε-subunit) in the control of protein synthesis. On the basis of previously reported correlations between eIF2Bε protein abundance and rates of protein synthesis in resistance-exercised ( 15 , 37 , 38 ), septic ( 6 , 57 , 59 ), and aging ( 23 , 33 ) rats, we initially tested the hypothesis that overexpression of eIF2Bε in skeletal muscle would enhance eIF2B activity and protein synthesis. To examine the role of eIF2Bε specifically, we employed in vivo electroporation to transfect a plasmid containing the coding sequence of rat eIF2Bε. In study 1 , a plasmid containing pFLAG-eIF2Bε was transfected into the TA of one leg, while the contralateral TA within the same rat received an equivalent mass of pmaxGFP plasmid. Fluorescence microscopy of transverse sections of GFP-transfected TA muscles indicated that the majority of fibers express the delivered transgene and that the protein expresses along the length of the transfected fibers ( Fig. 1 ). In study 1 , eIF2Bε expression in the TA of healthy, freely fed rats ( n = 18) was increased approximately twofold relative to the control leg ( Fig. 2 A ). The increase in eIF2Bε expression was associated with a significant increase in GEF activity compared with contralateral control muscles transfected with pmaxGFP ( Fig. 2 B ). Despite the enhanced eIF2B activity, protein synthesis was not significantly altered in healthy, freely fed rats ( Fig. 2 C ).

In an effort to further examine the potential of ectopically expressed eIF2Bε in skeletal muscle to stimulate protein synthesis, we next employed a model of chronic sepsis ( study 2 ), which has previously been shown to manifest in repressed eIF2B activity and protein synthesis in rat gastrocnemius ( 53 , 54 , 56 , 59 ). In the present study, we chose to use the TA on the basis of the high proportion of type II fibers (i.e., on the basis of previous studies, the TA should be sensitive with regard to repression of protein synthesis in septic animals), as well as our demonstrated ability to successfully transfect the majority of fibers in the TA muscle ( Fig. 1 ). Despite similar presurgery body mass, the total body mass of rats subjected to septic surgery was significantly reduced compared with control rats on days 3, 4 , and 5 of the study ( Fig. 3 A ). The results are in accordance with previous studies that established that control and septic rats demonstrate reduced food intake for 48 h after surgical implantation of the pellet; however, by 72 h postsurgery, food consumption increases and is not different between septic and control rats ( 7 , 56 ). The demonstrated body mass loss in septic rats on days 3–5 is due to the hyperdynamic, hypermetabolic septic condition. Final TA wet weight was also significantly reduced in septic vs. control animals ( Fig. 3 B ). While transfection of pFLAG-eIF2Bε did not lead to recovery of TA weight, it should be noted that the transgene was present for only 24 h (from day 4 to day 5 ), and recovery of muscle mass would not be expected in such a short time period.

Fig. 3.

Expression of eIF2Bε was increased to a similar extent in pFLAG-eIF2Bε-transfected muscle from septic rats compared with controls ( Fig. 4 A ). While the magnitude of the increase in eIF2B activity due to overexpression of eIF2Bα within control rats was similar to that of study 1 (∼20%), the difference did not reach statistical significance in study 2 . Expression of eIF2Bβ and eIF2Bδ was not affected by sepsis or by the expression of FLAG-eIF2Bε (see Supplemental Fig. S1, in Supplemental Material, which is available online at the Journal website). As expected, the septic surgery led to a significant decrease in eIF2B activity in the TA transfected with empty vector ( Fig. 4 B ). In contrast, overexpression of FLAG-eIF2Bε completely rescued eIF2B activity in the TA of septic rats. The repressed eIF2B activity in the TA of septic rats receiving empty vector compared with their corresponding controls was associated with concomitant repression of protein synthesis ( Fig. 4 C ). In contrast, protein synthesis rates in the TA of septic rats transfected with pFLAG-eIF2Bε were statistically similar to control TA muscles transfected with empty vector. Furthermore, within septic rats, overexpression of eIF2Bε led to a significant increase in protein synthesis compared with the contralateral TA receiving empty vector. Thus the expression of FLAG-eIF2Bε at least partially rescued the deficit in protein synthesis in a model of chronic sepsis.

DISCUSSION

Here, we present data showing that overexpression of eIF2Bε is sufficient to enhance guanine nucleotide exchange rates in the TA of healthy rats and to rescue deficits in GEF activity and protein synthesis in a model of chronic sepsis. Interestingly, overexpression of the single catalytic eIF2Bε subunit in the muscle of healthy rats enhanced GEF activity but did not increase protein synthesis, suggesting that eIF2B was not rate-limiting under conditions of the present study. However, on the basis of previous results from a resistance exercise model ( 37 , 38 ) and in a compensatory hypertrophy model ( 23 ), skeletal muscle appears to tightly regulate expression of eIF2Bε, unless enhanced rates of protein synthesis are required for muscle growth/hypertrophy. Thus, in resting, fed rats without hypertrophic stimuli, existing endogenous eIF2Bε protein quantities appear to be sufficient to maintain global mRNA translation at optimal rates. In contrast, under conditions where eIF2B activity is repressed (e.g., chronic sepsis), overexpression of the ε-subunit has a dramatic effect on GEF activity toward eIF2, as well as protein synthetic rates.

Previous investigations utilizing models of chronic sepsis demonstrated reductions in eIF2B activity ( 54 ), reductions in eIF2Bε mRNA and protein abundance ( 57 , 59 ), and increased eIF2Bε phosphorylation at Ser 535 ( 53 ) in the gastrocnemius of septic rats. The absence of a decrease in eIF2Bε abundance in the present study may be due to the examination of a different muscle (TA in the current study vs. gastrocnemius in previous reports) and/or the severity of the septic insult compared with previous studies ( 57 – 59 ). The loss of total body mass by days 4 and 5 was not as dramatic in the present study (in fact, it returned to presurgery body mass by day 5 ), which is indicative of a more rapid recovery from the infection. Also, gastrocnemius wet weight and protein synthesis were only slightly, but not significantly, reduced in the septic animals used in the present study (data not shown). Despite the diminished severity of the septic insult, we observed significant repression of eIF2B activity and protein synthesis in the TA muscle of septic rats, with associated reductions in TA wet weight. Previous studies showed that sepsis affects eIF2B activity and protein synthesis primarily in muscle composed mostly of fast-twitch fibers without affecting predominantly slow-twitch muscles such as the soleus ( 40 , 55 ). Since the TA consists of ∼80–90% type II fibers ( 3 ), whereas gastrocnemius contains a more mixed fiber type composition, the TA may be more sensitive to the septic insult than the gastrocnemius. Indeed, in an investigation examining a similar model of chronic sepsis, the investigators reported that the TA muscle demonstrated the greatest reduction in mass at all time points measured compared with extensor digitorum, soleus, and epitrochlearis muscles ( 58 ).

The eIF2B holoenzyme is subject to a variety of regulatory mechanisms ( 48 ), including (but not limited to) phosphorylation of its substrate eIF2α at Ser 51 (by stress-activated eIF2α kinases) ( 29 ) and eIF2Bε at Ser 535 of the rat protein by glycogen synthase kinase-3 ( 26 ) or Ser 540 in human eIF2Bε ( 61 ). Both of these phosphorylation events are considered inhibitory to the activity of eIF2B. While we observed a trend for increased phosphorylation of eIF2Bε at Ser 535 (see supplemental Fig. S2A), which might contribute to reduced eIF2B activity in muscle during sepsis, several additional mechanisms are implicated in the regulation of eIF2B. First, at least five additional phosphorylation sites have been identified, but their functions are not well characterized. Recently, Wang and Proud ( 60 ) identified a novel mechanism leading to the inhibition of eIF2B activity in response to amino acid starvation via phosphorylation of Ser 525 of the human eIF2Bε protein. Importantly, reduced amino acid availability has been shown to repress eIF2B activity in pig muscle ( 34 ) and in L6 myoblasts ( 31 ). While intramuscular amino acids were not measured in the present investigation, marked disruptions in the amino acid content of muscle occur during catabolic insults such as sepsis ( 13 ). Thus alterations in intramuscular amino acid concentration might negatively impact eIF2B activity during sepsis, perhaps via the amino acid-sensitive phosphorylation site identified by Wang and Proud. Unfortunately, a phosphospecific antibody against eIF2Bε (Ser 525 ) was not available during the present study. Also, it is unclear whether overexpressed eIF2Bε would be subject to the same inhibition.

The best-characterized mechanism for inhibition of eIF2B activity is via phosphorylation of the α-subunit of its substrate eIF2, which converts the substrate to a competitive inhibitor of eIF2B ( 36 ). Because of the substoichiometric quantities of eIF2B relative to eIF2 in most tissues ( 21 ), including muscle, even a small increase in phosphorylation of eIF2α can result in drastic reductions in eIF2B activity ( 47 ). In the present investigation, we did not observe changes in the phosphorylation of eIF2α at Ser 51 as a result of sepsis (see supplemental Fig. S2B), which is consistent with previous findings examining eIF2α phosphorylation in muscle during sepsis ( 39 , 40 , 54 ). However, a phenomenon similar to the competitive inhibition by phosphorylated eIF2α can also occur with other proteins that interact with eIF2B subunits. In such a model, exogenously expressed FLAG-eIF2Bε (presumably as excess free monomers) might act as a “sink” to sequester inhibitory interacting proteins, thus allowing the intact heteropentameric holoenzymes to resume their full catalytic activity. It is important to note in this context that there may be additional, as yet uncharacterized, mechanisms for regulating eIF2B activity, including additional interacting proteins. For example, regulator of G protein signaling 2 (RGS2) was only very recently identified as a novel inhibitory protein that represses eIF2B activity and protein synthesis via direct interaction with eIF2Bε ( 46 ). Interestingly, RGS2 expression is induced in human umbilical vein endothelial cells by treatment with LPS or TNF-α ( 43 ), both of which are also critical mediators of the adverse effects of sepsis on skeletal muscle ( 16 ). Although RGS2 expression in the TA was numerically greater in septic than in control rats (see supplemental Fig. S2C), the difference was only statistically significant when the data for all control samples (i.e., those transfected with pcDNA3.1 or pFLAG-eIF2Bε) were pooled and compared with pooled septic animals. Nevertheless, the results of the current study do not rule out the possibility that overexpression of eIF2Bε rescued GEF activity and protein synthesis in an alternative manner (i.e., not due to the direct catalytic activity of the expressed FLAG-eIF2Bε).

While overexpression of eIF2Bε did not enhance protein synthesis in healthy rats, previous results from our laboratory indicate that the translational efficiency of the eIF2Bε mRNA and the expression of eIF2Bε protein are uniquely upregulated following resistance exercise in rats, with concomitant increases in eIF2B activity and protein synthesis ( 11 , 37 , 38 ). In this context, it must be noted that eIF2B is found in rate-limiting quantities relative to its substrate eIF2 ( 47 ). Thus a rapid upregulation of the catalytic ε-subunit of eIF2B may serve as an additional point of regulation to enhance eIF2B activity during growth-promoting conditions. The significance of increased eIF2Bε expression following resistance exercise likely lies in its potential contribution to the enhanced eIF2B activity and muscle protein synthesis in the gastrocnemius of resistance-exercised rats 16 h following exercise ( 11 , 12 , 35 ). Lending further credence to the hypothesis that upregulation of eIF2Bε is important for the postexercise increase in protein synthesis, it has been shown that severely diabetic rats, which fail to increase muscle protein synthesis following resistance exercise, do not demonstrate the increase in eIF2B activity that occurs in their nondiabetic counterparts ( 35 ). In further support of the present data, overexpression of eIF2Bε alone has been shown to increase guanine nucleotide exchange activity in Sf9 ( 9 ) and Sf21 ( 10 ) insect cell lines, as well as in human HEK 293T cells ( 62 ). In COS-7 and primary mouse embryonic fibroblast cells, overexpression of eIF2Bε increases the translation of a cotransfected luciferase reporter by approximately twofold ( 5 ). Finally, in perhaps the most relevant model to the present investigation, overexpression of eIF2Bε alone in rat cardiomyocytes is sufficient to increase protein synthesis by 75% as well as induce myocyte hypertrophy in culture ( 20 ). In contrast, expression of the dominant-negative mutant of eIF2Bε has been shown to decrease translation ( 62 ), decrease myocyte size, and prevent isoproterenol-induced hypertrophy of cardiomyocytes ( 20 ).

In summary, we have demonstrated that overexpression of one subunit (ε) of the eIF2B holoenzyme is sufficient to enhance GEF activity and protein synthesis in skeletal muscle. We believe this has important implications in several conditions that exhibit dysregulated skeletal muscle protein balance (e.g., sarcopenia and sepsis) and postulate that the ability to prevent deficits in eIF2B activity would play a crucial role in a number of conditions where muscle atrophy is a concern. Further investigations of the direct contribution of enhanced eIF2Bε protein abundance and/or eIF2B activity in conditions of skeletal muscle wasting, as well as during muscle hypertrophy, would help elucidate the importance of the eIF2/eIF2B regulatory system.
