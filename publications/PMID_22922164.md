# Two functional domains in C. elegans glypican LON-2 can independently inhibit BMP-like signaling.
**Authors:** Taneja-Bageshwar S, Gumienny TL

Author information:

Glypicans are multifunctional proteoglycans with regulatory roles in several
intercellular signaling pathways. Here, we examine the functional requirements
for glypican regulation of bone morphogenetic protein (BMP)-mediated body length
in C. elegans. We provide evidence that two parts of C. elegans glypican LON-2
can independently inhibit BMP signaling in vivo: the N-terminal furin protease
product and the C-terminal region containing heparan sulfate attachment
sequences. While the C-terminal protease product is dispensable for LON-2
minimal core protein activity, it does affect the localization of LON-2.
Cleavage of LON-2 into two parts at the conserved furin protease site is not
required for LON-2 to inhibit BMP-like signaling. The
glycosyl-phosphatidylinositol (GPI) membrane anchor is also not absolutely
required for LON-2 activity. Finally, we show that an RGD protein-protein
interaction motif in the LON-2 N-terminal domain is necessary for LON-2 core
protein activity, suggesting that LON-2 inhibits BMP signaling by acting as a
scaffold for BMP and an RGD-binding protein.

Introduction

Glypicans have complex roles in regulating cell-to-cell signaling by Transforming Growth Factor-β (TGF-β) and other heparin-binding growth factor families including Wnt/Wg, Hedgehog (Hh), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and insulin-like growth factor (IGF) (Cheng et al., 2008; Hagihara et al., 2000; Lin and Perrimon, 1999; Ohkawara et al., 2003; Song et al., 2005; Topczewski et al., 2001; Yan and Lin, 2007). Glypicans are composed of a cylindrical, all α-helical protein core attached to long unbranched sugar chains, heparan sulfate glycosaminoglycans (HS GAG), and a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) link that anchors the protein to the cell membrane (Filmus et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2011; Svensson et al., 2012). Also, glypicans can be shed into the intercellular space through cleavage of the protein core at a furin cleavage site or by removal of the GPI lipid anchor (De Cat et al., 2003; Fico et al., 2011; Williams et al., 2010). How this structural complexity specifies glypican regulation of TGF-β signaling is a significant, but unresolved question relevant to both developmental and disease biology (Akiyama et al., 2008; Kirkpatrick et al., 2006; O’Connell et al., 2007; Umulis et al., 2009).

Although only a genetic interaction between glypican and TGF-β members has been demonstrated in mammals, studies in Drosophila melanogaster and Caenorhabditis elegans have proven genetic and physical interactions between glypicans and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), a group of proteins within the TGF-β superfamily (Gumienny et al., 2007; Jackson et al., 1997; Kirkpatrick et al., 2006; Paine-Saunders et al., 2000). The Drosophila glypican Dally binds BMP-like Dpp and affects Dpp’s regulation of imaginal disc size (Kirkpatrick et al., 2006; Teleman and Cohen, 2000). Too little Dpp signaling results in small wings, while overexpression of functional Dpp causes wing overgrowth. Although TGF-β members are heparan sulfate (HS)-binding molecules, and Dpp binds Dally with its HS-binding domain (Akiyama et al., 2008), the HS chain of Dally is not absolutely required for Dally to bind Dpp or to affect Dpp activity, revealing a contribution by the glypican core protein in regulating BMP activity (Kirkpatrick et al., 2006; Wu et al., 2010; Yan et al., 2009). C. elegans glypican LON-2 plays a clear role in inhibiting signaling by DBL-1, a BMP member. LON-2 binds DBL-1 and inhibits DBL-1-mediated body length development (Gumienny et al., 2007). Animals lacking functional LON-2 or overexpressing functional DBL-1 are longer than normal (Gumienny et al., 2007; Morita et al., 1999; Suzuki et al., 1999).

To better understand the role of glypicans in modulating BMP signaling, we examined the contributions of conserved glypican components to glypican’s BMP regulatory activity in the simple, established model organism C. elegans. Using C. elegans BMP-mediated body length as a tractable system for analyzing the function of lon-2 variant transgenes, we investigated the role of glypican GAG side chains, the GPI link, and the furin cleavage site. We further studied the function of the protein–protein interaction motif RGD in LON-2, which is shared within its family only by mammalian glypican-1 but is present in many other extracellular TGF-β superfamily regulatory molecules. We have been able to uncover unexpected functional requirements and redundancies within the LON-2 glypican related to its function as a BMP signaling regulator.

ResultsThe LON-2 core protein inhibits BMP signalingThe contribution of the glypican protein core in regulating heparin-binding growth factors depends on the growth factor regulated and the system. GAG attachment sites are not essential for the function of the protein core of mouse GPC3 or fly Dally-like to regulate BMP signaling (Capurro et al., 2008; Kirkpatrick et al., 2006). The role of the protein core is controversial for other signaling pathways, notably Wnt and Hh signaling pathways. The glypican core protein was shown to be insuffficient to regulate Wnt/Wg signaling by some (Baeg et al., 2001; De Cat et al., 2003; Shiau et al., 2010; Yan et al., 2009), but others have shown that the protein core can regulate Wnt/Wg signaling without heparan sulfate attachment sites (Ohkawara et al., 2003; Song et al., 2005). To regulate Hedgehog signaling, human GPC5 requires its HS side chains, but Drosophila Dally-like does not (Capurro et al., 2008; Li et al., 2011; Williams et al., 2010; Yan et al., 2010).To address the complicated role of the glypican core protein in a simpler system, we tested the ability of C. elegans LON-2 core protein to inhibit signaling by the BMP-like ligand DBL-1. Non-glycanated glypicans can inhibit BMP-like signaling (Capurro et al., 2008; Kirkpatrick et al., 2006). We have previously shown that green fluorescent protein (GFP)-tagged LON-2 inhibits DBL-1 signaling, thereby restoring body length to about normal in LON-2-deficient animals (Gumienny et al., 2007). We used lon-2(e678) strains transgenic for this tagged LON-2, LON-2(GFP) as a positive control (75–81% lon-2(e678) length, n=30 for each strain, two strains measured for each mutant construct) (Fig. 1, first construct, Figs. 3 and 4). LON-2(GFP) localizes to the cell surface of hypodermal and intestinal cells, notably to cell–cell junctions (Fig. 2C) (Gumienny et al., 2007). LON-2 has three predicted sites for attachment of heparan sulfate (HS) chains, each at the serine of an SG sequence (Fig. 1, first construct, red dotted lines, Fig. 6) (Bernfield et al., 1999). We removed the SG sites in this functional GFP-tagged LON-2 by replacing serine with alanine, either at single (LON-2(GAG)) or multiple positions (LON-2(1,2GAG) and LON-2(1,2,3GAG)). We introduced these GAG-mutant constructs into animals lacking functional LON-2 due to a deletion, lon-2(e678), that removes most of the lon-2 genomic coding sequence (Gumienny et al., 2007). Body lengths of animals from two lines transmitting each GAG-variant construct were scored and compared to the body lengths of the lon-2(e678) background strain. All lines with transgenic LON-2 lacking some or all GAG attachment sites showed a rescue of the long body length phenotype to a more normal length, at least as well as the full-length LON-2(GFP) positive control (77–83% the length of the lon-2(e678) background, 100–107% the length of LON-2(GFP), n=30 for each of two strains measured for each construct) (Fig. 1, second to fourth constructs, Supplemental Table 2). This activity was as strong as that observed in the positive control, LON-2(GFP). These results demonstrate that the LON-2 protein core can inhibit DBL-1 signaling without GAG attachment sites. This type of analysis, though, may not be quantitative enough to establish whether this activity is due to wild-type activity of the transgenic protein or from higher expression levels of a crippled protein.Because the LON-2 GAG mutants completely rescued the lon-2(e678) phenotype, we asked if the lack of HS attachment sites affected the localization or distribution of LON-2. The HS chains of other glypicans can bind proteins that may localize glypican within specific cell membrane regions (Eugster et al., 2007; Malave et al., 2003). While GFP-tagged LON-2 is required in the hypodermis to regulate DBL-1-mediated body size development, it is below the limit of detection in this tissue at later stages, when DBL-1-mediated body length phenotypes are evident (Gumienny et al., 2007). However, it visibly localizes to the surface of intestinal cells throughout development (Gumienny et al., 2007), so we analyzed cellular localization of GFP-tagged LON-2 in that tissue (Fig. 2C, see 2A for DIC location and 2B for the non-transgenic lon-2(e678) background). LON-2 lacking all three GAG attachment sites shows the same localization to the cell surface as LON-2(GFP) (Fig. 2D). This result indicates that this active form of LON-2 does not require its HS attachment sites for proper subcellular localization. Although our transgenic strains appeared to have different levels of membrane-localized LON-2 relative to LON-2(GFP) (Fig. 2), fluorescence intensity of proteins encoded by extrachromosomal multi-copy arrays varied from animal to animal within strains and did not correlate with functional ability.LON-2 is functional without GPI membrane anchor attachmentA defining feature of glypicans is their glycolipid glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor, which links the protein to the extracellular membrane (Filmus and Selleck, 2001). Shedding of glypicans from their GPI membrane anchor occurs in development and disease states (Bernfield et al., 1999; Brandan et al., 1996; Carey et al., 1993; Feng et al., 2011; Ishihara et al., 1987; Zittermann et al., 2010). Drosophila Dally-like protein (Dlp) does not require GPI linkage to inhibit Wg signaling if Dlp has a transmembrane domain instead (Yan et al., 2009). However, membrane tethering by GPI is important for Drosophila Dlp and mouse GPC3 function in Hedgehog signaling (Capurro et al., 2008; Gallet et al., 2008; Takeo et al., 2005; Williams et al., 2010). Furthermore, a secreted form of Drosophila Dally has a weak dominant negative effect on BMP-like Dpp signaling (Takeo et al., 2005). To gain a better understanding of the role of the GPI anchor in glypican function in BMP-like signaling in the C. elegans system, we asked if removal of the LON-2 GPI site would affect the ability of LON-2 to inhibit DBL-1-mediated body length signaling. We truncated the lon-2 sequence after amino acid 488 to generate LON-2(ΔGPI) (Fig. 3, first construct). This GFP-tagged construct lacks the GPI attachment sequence and fails to visibly localize to cell membranes at cell–cell junctions, but rather appears as a bright haze in cells (Fig. 2E). Extracellular LON-2(DGPI) was not detected. Nonetheless, this construct also rescues the BMP-mediated body length defect of lon-2(e678) animals as well as full-length LON-2 with a GPI attachment sequence (76–80% lon-2(e678) length, 99–101% the length of LON-2(GFP), n≥30, for two strains measured) (Fig. 3, first construct, Supplemental Table 2). Therefore, LON-2(ΔGPI) is functional despite having no GPI attachment sequence, indicating that membrane localization by GPI is not absolutely required for LON-2 to inhibit DBL-1 activity.The N-terminal LON-2 protease product is sufficient to inhibit BMP activityThe ability of a non-GPI tethered form of LON-2 (LON-2(ΔGPI)) to inhibit BMP activity in vivo led us to ask if protease solubilized LON-2 could regulate BMP signaling. All glypicans contain at least one furin proprotein convertase cleavage site sequence (De Cat et al., 2003; Filmus et al., 2008; Gumienny et al., 2007). Cleavage of glypicans by a proprotein convertase is predicted to create a soluble N-terminal fragment that remains covalently attached to its C-terminal subunit (De Cat et al., 2003). GPC3 protease fragments have been identified in the sera of hepatocellular carcinoma patients, but processing is not required for human GPC3 to induce Wnt signaling in hepatocellular carcinoma growth (Capurro et al., 2005b; Hippo et al., 2004). However, cleavage by a furin protease is essential for zebrafish GPC3 to stimulate Wnt signaling and early development (Capurro et al., 2005a; De Cat et al., 2003). Also, alteration of the Drosophila Dally furin cleavage site does not alter the effect of Dally on Hedgehog signaling (Williams et al., 2010). However, a role for proteolyzed glypican in BMP signaling has not been demonstrated in any system. To address this question, we first asked if the N-terminal part of LON-2 defined by the furin cleavage site at amino acid 368 is sufficient to restrict DBL-1 activity. The N-terminal product, LON-2(1-368), contains the globular domain but lacks the three HS attachment sequences and the GPI linkage site. Like the LON-2 variant lacking a GPI anchor site, LON-2(1-368) was not visibly associated with the membrane but did restore body length in animals lacking LON-2 (87% lon-2(e678) length, 115% the length of LON-2(GFP), n=30 for each of two strains measured) (Fig. 2F, Fig. 3, second construct, Supplemental Table 2). The body length differences of the two LON-2(1-368) strains are significantly less than those seen in the LON-2(GFP) strains and other strains with functional LON-2 constructs (Supplemental Table 1, Supplemental Fig. 2). This result suggests that LON-2(1-368), while functional, is crippled in its activity. Nonetheless, the activity of the soluble LON-2 protein core is clearly sufficient for LON-2 to inhibit DBL-1 BMP signaling, does not require N-terminus attachment to its C-terminus after processing, and does not require HS side chains. Furthermore, these results narrow core protein function to the N-terminal 368-amino acid portion of LON-2.We then asked if solubilization of this N-terminal protein core, LON-2(1-368), was required for BMP inhibition. We attached the GPI anchor sequence to the rescuing N-terminal sequence, excluding the furin protease recognition site. This construct, LON-2(1-364+GPI), was membrane bound and inhibited body length (Fig. 2G, Fig. 3, third construct, 80–83% lon-2(e678) length, n=30 for each of two strain measured). This result shows that the LON-2 minimal protein core does not need to be soluble to be active.The LON-2 furin protease site is not necessary for core protein functionHaving shown that the N-terminal furin cleavage product was sufficient for LON-2 core protein activity, we then asked if furin cleavage was necessary in the context of the full-length glypican. We altered the LON-2 furin cleavage site (FCS) RLGR at amino acids 365–368 to ALGA on the functional full length, non-glycanated LON-2(1,2,3GAG) and asked if this inactivated furin cleavage site variant, LON-2(FCS), was active in vivo. LON-2(FCS) rescued the long body length defect of lon-2(e678) animals (LON-2(FCS), 79–81% lon-2(e678) length, n=30 for each of two strains measured) (Fig. 3, fourth construct, FCS is marked in maroon dashes). This result indicates that BMP regulation by glypican does not require furin cleavage to generate an active N-terminal product. LON-2 core protein function requires the RGD tripeptide or GPI linkageLON-2, some GPC1s, and many other extracellular TGF-β superfamily regulators contain an RGD motif, a tripeptide arginine–glycine–aspartic acid sequence that regulates interactions between proteins and extracellular matrix constituents, notably integrins (Margadant and Sonnenberg, 2010; Munger and Sheppard, 2011; Saharinen et al., 1998; Wipff et al., 2007). We previously showed that the RGD sequence is not required in the context of full-length, glycanated LON-2 (Gumienny et al., 2007). Since we have shown in this work that GAG-binding sites are sufficient for LON-2 activity (Fig. 4), we asked if removing RGD (amino acids 348–350) from a LON-2 variant lacking HS attachment sites retains activity. We first deleted 21 amino acids from the C-terminus of functional LON-2(1-368) to create LON-2(1-347), which lacks the RGD sequence. This construct was no longer able to decrease the long body length of animals lacking LON-2 (101–102% lon-2(e678) length, n=30 for each of two strains measured) (Fig. 5, first construct). This result is consistent with the interpretation that soluble LON-2 requires the RGD domain. To confirm that it was loss of the RGD sequence within the deleted region that made this construct non-functional, we specifically substituted AAA for the RGD sequence in the functional LON-2(1-368) construct, making LON-2(1-368 AAA). LON-2(1-368 AAA) was expressed and visible, though not membrane bound (Fig. 2K). This variant also failed to rescue the lon-2 body length defect in three transgenic strains (99–107% lon-2(e678) length, n=30 for each of three strains measured) (Fig. 5, second construct). We then asked if the RGD sequence was required in the context of full-length, GPI-anchored but nonglycanated LON-2 core protein. We introduced the RGD-to-AAA mutation in the rescuing construct LON-2(1,2,3GAG) to make LON-2(1,2,3GAG AAA). This transgene product was visible at the cell membrane and active, rescuing the long body length defect of lon-2 loss-of-function animals (85–87% lon-2(e678) length, n=30 for each of two strains measured) (Fig. 2L, Fig. 5, third construct). The relatively weaker rescue of body length seen in strains expressing this construct may reflect a functionally crippled protein relative to LON-2(GFP), but this observation requires biochemical validation (Supplemental Fig. 2). Significantly, when the GPI anchor sequence is removed from this construct, making LON-2(1-488 1,2,3GAG AAA), this LON-2 variant is neither active nor localized to cell–cell junctions (103–104% lon-2(e678) length, n=30 for each of two strains measured) (Fig. 2M, Fig. 5, fourth construct). These results show that RGD is not the sequence in the LON-2 core protein that directly regulates BMP activity. Instead, this result indicates that the RGD facilitates BMP inhibition by another, unknown part of the N-terminal core glypican protein. However, this RGD role is not critical when the core protein is GPI linked to the membrane.

DiscussionPrevious work on C. elegans glypican LON-2 and other glypicans has clearly demonstrated a complex role for glypican in regulating BMP family signaling, but the mechanism of this interaction is not fully understood (Filmus et al., 2008; Umulis et al., 2009; Yan and Lin, 2009). Here we have focused on the contributions of the LON-2 core protein, conserved post-translational modifications, and the partially conserved RGD motif in regulating DBL-1/BMP signaling.Our results clearly demonstrate that LON-2 has at least two independent functional regions that inhibit DBL-1-mediated body size signaling in C. elegans. While the core protein of a glypican has been shown to be sufficient to regulate BMP activity (Drosophila Dally with Dpp/BMP), other work indicates that Dally lacking its HS chains is crippled in its ability to bind Dpp and regulate Dpp signaling (Dejima et al., 2011; Kirkpatrick et al., 2006). Mammalian GPC1 does need its HS chains to regulate Wnt signaling (Shiau et al., 2010). However, the mammalian GPC3 HS chains are not required for GPC3 to modulate Wnt5a or Hedgehog binding and signaling (Capurro et al., 2008; Song et al., 2005). Our work with C. elegans glypican LON-2 shows that the LON-2 protein core alone is active and contains wild-type activity when overexpressed from the lon-2 promoter (Figs. 1, 3, and 5). Furthermore, we have limited the DBL-1 regulatory region of the LON-2 protein core to the N-terminal 368 amino acids (Fig. 3). This region’s activity does not require the C-terminal portion, the GAG attachment sites, or association with the membrane through a GPI anchor. Recently, one smaller region in Drosophila Dally-like’s protein core has been shown to be necessary to mediate Hh signaling (amino acids 235–258) (Kim et al., 2011). The analogous region in LON-2 lies within the active N-terminal region (amino acids 1-368), and it will be interesting to determine if this region is also used for BMP signal regulation or if BMP and Hh regulatory regions are distinct in glypicans.The work presented here shows that the LON-2 N-terminal protein core and the C-terminus with GAG attachment sites and GPI anchor independently inhibit BMP signaling in vivo, giving rescue of the lon-2(e678) phenotype indistinguishable from full length LON-2 (Figs. 1, 3, and 4). In the context of the native full-length glypican, however, there may be functional synergy between the protein core and the GAG chains in regulating BMP signaling. In Drosophila, Dally BMP-binding and in vivo function is reduced if the core protein lacks HS side chains (Kirkpatrick et al., 2006). The core protein may also specify function of the GAG side chains by influencing the type of glycanation that occurs. The protein core of rat GPC1 directs glycanation of its GAG attachment sites with HS instead of chondroitin sulfate (Chen and Lander, 2001). Therefore, in the normal in vivo milieu, the LON-2 protein core may not only specifically bind ligand itself but also direct attachment and modification of HS chains, which, too, may confer specificity of ligand interaction.We determined that cleaved forms of glypican LON-2 are sufficient but not necessary for LON-2 activity (Figs. 3 and 4). Glypican can be shed by enzymatic processing of either its furin protease site or its GPI anchor (Fico et al., 2011). Some soluble glypican forms are active, but not necessarily in the same way as the membrane-bound protein (Eugster et al., 2007; Kreuger et al., 2004; Zittermann et al., 2010). For BMP regulation, Dally appears to act as a Dpp co-receptor when membrane-bound and as an antagonist when shed (Takeo et al., 2005). Shed glypicans have been identified as biomarkers of a variety of cancer types, too (Filmus et al., 2008; Iozzo and Sanderson, 2011; Kandil and Cooper, 2009). We addressed the functional requirement for soluble forms of LON-2 in three ways.First, we asked if the furin-solubilized N-terminal LON-2 fragment, LON-2(1-368), was sufficient for activity. As discussed above, we discovered that this LON-2 N-terminal region alone is functional, and that it does not need to be released from full-length LON-2 for its activity in our in vivo overexpression system (Fig. 3). The degree of rescuing activity in the two LON-2(1-368) strains is less than that in other rescuing lines, and together the statistics indicate that these two strains comprise a unique population compared to the other strains with constructs that restore body length to below wild-type length (Supplemental Fig. 2 and Supplemental Table 2). While this result is consistent with the interpretation that LON-2(1-368) activity is crippled relative to other functional LON-2 variants, this possibility needs to be verified biochemically. In mammalian cells, the GPC3 furin site is required to inhibit Wnt signaling, but it is dispensable for Hedgehog regulation (De Cat et al., 2003; Williams et al., 2010). A TGF-β-regulatory activity for furin-cleaved glypican had not previously been tested in any system.Second, we asked if inactivation of the conserved furin cleavage site separating the N-terminus from the smaller C-terminal GAG-linked region affected the ability of transgenic LON-2 to inhibit DBL-1-mediated body size. We found that uncleavable LON-2 inhibits body size signaling as effectively as cleavable LON-2, showing that a protease-solubilized LON-2 is not required for LON-2 activity (Figs. 1 and 3). These in vivo results parallel in vitro findings that inactivation of the furin site in Drosophila glypican Dally-like protein (Dlp) has no effect on the ability of Dlp to regulate Hedgehog signaling (Williams et al., 2010).Third, we asked if LON-2 activity requires its GPI anchor. We found that membrane tethering is not critical for the BMP-inhibitory function of LON-2 in either the otherwise full-length LON-2 (LON-2(ΔGPI)) or the N-terminal part (LON-2(1-368)) (Fig. 3). These results contrast with loss of GPI anchoring in Dally, which causes weak dominant negative effects on Dpp/BMP signaling (Takeo et al., 2005). For glypican regulation of other growth factors, though, GPI attachment is required for glypican activity. GPI anchoring of mammalian GPC3 is required to stimulate Wnt signaling, and induced cleavage of GPC3’s GPI anchor by Notum reduces GPC3 Wnt-regulatory activity (Traister et al., 2007). GPI linkage is also required for GPC3 to reduce Hedgehog signaling, and substitution of a transmembrane domain to tether GPC3 to the cell membrane does not restore GPC3 function (Capurro et al., 2008).For some extracellular TGF-β regulators that contain an RGD motif, including some TGF-β prodomains, fibrillin-1, and latent TGF-β binding proteins (LTBPs), the RGD motif has been shown to regulate TGF-β signaling by binding integrins (Munger et al., 1998, 1999; Munger and Sheppard, 2011; Robertson et al., 2011). Our work indicates that the RGD motif plays an important, albeit accessory role in helping the LON-2 N-terminal protein core inhibit BMP signaling (Fig. 5). The soluble LON-2 protein core requires the RGD sequence for activity, but the GPI-linked LON-2 core protein does not need this interaction site (Fig. 5). This conclusion that the DBL-1/BMP regulatory function of glypican in C. elegans does not absolutely require RGD is supported by previous work showing that the Drosophila glypican Dally core protein, which is GPI linked but lacks RGD, inhibits BMP function in C. elegans (Gumienny et al., 2007; Kirkpatrick et al., 2006). We propose that LON-2’s RGD inhibits DBL-1/BMP indirectly by complexing with another, unidentified RGD-binding protein.Our results support a model that LON-2 may act as a scaffold in a larger BMP-regulatory complex. Our work implicates an RGD-binding protein as a facilitator of glypican binding to BMP, though this is a mechanism that may be unique to C. elegans LON-2-DBL-1 interactions, or may be modified in other systems where the relevant glypican lacks an RGD motif. The prodomains (Latency-Associated Peptide (LAP)) of TGF-βs, but not other subfamilies, contain an RGD domain that interacts with integrin, latent TGF-β binding protein (LTBP), and other known TGF-β regulators. Fibrilllin-1, which contains an RGD motif in vertebrates but not other species, may act as a scaffold in a regulatory complex that contains BMPs or other TGF-β superfamily members, LTBP, integrins, and other extracellular matrix proteins (Robertson et al., 2011). A tantalizing possibility is that LON-2 binds to RGD-binding protein(s) and DBL-1 dimers, possibly in an inactive complex with DBL-1 prodomains, to inhibit DBL-1 signaling. These studies are the first to suggest the involvement of an additional player that interacts with the glypican protein core to control growth factor signaling, and offer insights and direction for a better understanding of the mechanism of glypican’s complex regulation of growth factor signaling.

In Drosophila, Dally BMP-binding and in vivo function is reduced if the core protein lacks HS side chains (Kirkpatrick et al., 2006). The core protein may also specify function of the GAG side chains by influencing the type of glycanation that occurs. The protein core of rat GPC1 directs glycanation of its GAG attachment sites with HS instead of chondroitin sulfate (Chen and Lander, 2001). Therefore, in the normal in vivo milieu, the LON-2 protein core may not only specifically bind ligand itself but also direct attachment and modification of HS chains, which, too, may confer specificity of ligand interaction.

We determined that cleaved forms of glypican LON-2 are sufficient but not necessary for LON-2 activity (Figs. 3 and 4). Glypican can be shed by enzymatic processing of either its furin protease site or its GPI anchor (Fico et al., 2011). Some soluble glypican forms are active, but not necessarily in the same way as the membrane-bound protein (Eugster et al., 2007; Kreuger et al., 2004; Zittermann et al., 2010). For BMP regulation, Dally appears to act as a Dpp co-receptor when membrane-bound and as an antagonist when shed (Takeo et al., 2005). Shed glypicans have been identified as biomarkers of a variety of cancer types, too (Filmus et al., 2008; Iozzo and Sanderson, 2011; Kandil and Cooper, 2009). We addressed the functional requirement for soluble forms of LON-2 in three ways.

First, we asked if the furin-solubilized N-terminal LON-2 fragment, LON-2(1-368), was sufficient for activity. As discussed above, we discovered that this LON-2 N-terminal region alone is functional, and that it does not need to be released from full-length LON-2 for its activity in our in vivo overexpression system (Fig. 3). The degree of rescuing activity in the two LON-2(1-368) strains is less than that in other rescuing lines, and together the statistics indicate that these two strains comprise a unique population compared to the other strains with constructs that restore body length to below wild-type length (Supplemental Fig. 2 and Supplemental Table 2). While this result is consistent with the interpretation that LON-2(1-368) activity is crippled relative to other functional LON-2 variants, this possibility needs to be verified biochemically. In mammalian cells, the GPC3 furin site is required to inhibit Wnt signaling, but it is dispensable for Hedgehog regulation (De Cat et al., 2003; Williams et al., 2010). A TGF-β-regulatory activity for furin-cleaved glypican had not previously been tested in any system.

Second, we asked if inactivation of the conserved furin cleavage site separating the N-terminus from the smaller C-terminal GAG-linked region affected the ability of transgenic LON-2 to inhibit DBL-1-mediated body size. We found that uncleavable LON-2 inhibits body size signaling as effectively as cleavable LON-2, showing that a protease-solubilized LON-2 is not required for LON-2 activity (Figs. 1 and 3). These in vivo results parallel in vitro findings that inactivation of the furin site in Drosophila glypican Dally-like protein (Dlp) has no effect on the ability of Dlp to regulate Hedgehog signaling (Williams et al., 2010).

Third, we asked if LON-2 activity requires its GPI anchor. We found that membrane tethering is not critical for the BMP-inhibitory function of LON-2 in either the otherwise full-length LON-2 (LON-2(ΔGPI)) or the N-terminal part (LON-2(1-368)) (Fig. 3). These results contrast with loss of GPI anchoring in Dally, which causes weak dominant negative effects on Dpp/BMP signaling (Takeo et al., 2005). For glypican regulation of other growth factors, though, GPI attachment is required for glypican activity. GPI anchoring of mammalian GPC3 is required to stimulate Wnt signaling, and induced cleavage of GPC3’s GPI anchor by Notum reduces GPC3 Wnt-regulatory activity (Traister et al., 2007). GPI linkage is also required for GPC3 to reduce Hedgehog signaling, and substitution of a transmembrane domain to tether GPC3 to the cell membrane does not restore GPC3 function (Capurro et al., 2008).

For some extracellular TGF-β regulators that contain an RGD motif, including some TGF-β prodomains, fibrillin-1, and latent TGF-β binding proteins (LTBPs), the RGD motif has been shown to regulate TGF-β signaling by binding integrins (Munger et al., 1998, 1999; Munger and Sheppard, 2011; Robertson et al., 2011). Our work indicates that the RGD motif plays an important, albeit accessory role in helping the LON-2 N-terminal protein core inhibit BMP signaling (Fig. 5). The soluble LON-2 protein core requires the RGD sequence for activity, but the GPI-linked LON-2 core protein does not need this interaction site (Fig. 5). This conclusion that the DBL-1/BMP regulatory function of glypican in C. elegans does not absolutely require RGD is supported by previous work showing that the Drosophila glypican Dally core protein, which is GPI linked but lacks RGD, inhibits BMP function in C. elegans (Gumienny et al., 2007; Kirkpatrick et al., 2006). We propose that LON-2’s RGD inhibits DBL-1/BMP indirectly by complexing with another, unidentified RGD-binding protein.

Our results support a model that LON-2 may act as a scaffold in a larger BMP-regulatory complex. Our work implicates an RGD-binding protein as a facilitator of glypican binding to BMP, though this is a mechanism that may be unique to C. elegans LON-2-DBL-1 interactions, or may be modified in other systems where the relevant glypican lacks an RGD motif. The prodomains (Latency-Associated Peptide (LAP)) of TGF-βs, but not other subfamilies, contain an RGD domain that interacts with integrin, latent TGF-β binding protein (LTBP), and other known TGF-β regulators. Fibrilllin-1, which contains an RGD motif in vertebrates but not other species, may act as a scaffold in a regulatory complex that contains BMPs or other TGF-β superfamily members, LTBP, integrins, and other extracellular matrix proteins (Robertson et al., 2011). A tantalizing possibility is that LON-2 binds to RGD-binding protein(s) and DBL-1 dimers, possibly in an inactive complex with DBL-1 prodomains, to inhibit DBL-1 signaling. These studies are the first to suggest the involvement of an additional player that interacts with the glypican protein core to control growth factor signaling, and offer insights and direction for a better understanding of the mechanism of glypican’s complex regulation of growth factor signaling.

ConclusionsIn summary, we have identified two BMP regulatory domains of a glypican that can independently act to inhibit signaling by a BMP member. While the C-terminus with consensus heparan sulfate attachment sites is sufficient to inhibit BMP activity, it requires membrane association. The N-terminal protein core is functional only if it is membrane associated or contains the protein–protein interaction motif RGD. This result indicates that LON-2 interacts with an RGD-binding protein to inhibit BMP signaling. We expect the C. elegans DBL-1 pathway will continue to be a useful system in which to challenge these models, by not only determining how glypican regulatory regions interact with BMP, but also by identifying and characterizing the RGD-interacting protein that complexes with glypican to contribute to the extracellular regulation of BMP signaling. Whether other glypicans also recruit accessory proteins to regulate BMP signaling will also be critical to understand glypican specificity and function.
