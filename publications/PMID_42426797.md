# Preliminary investigation of ghrelin in horses and ponies: receptor expression and associations with prandial state, morphometry and signalment.

Preliminary investigation of ghrelin in horses and ponies: receptor expression
and associations with prandial state, morphometry and signalment.

The hormone ghrelin has had limited attention in Equidae, despite connections
with appetite and metabolic health in other species. Ghrelin influences hunger
and food intake, energy expenditure and storage, glucose metabolism, and
thermoregulation, through its receptor-mediated effects on tissues and via
interaction with other hormones. There are two circulating forms, acylated and
des-acylated ghrelin, considered to have distinct effects on metabolism in other
species. The aims of this study were to confirm the presence of ghrelin
receptors in equine tissues, to validate an assay for the measurement of total
ghrelin (acylated and des-acylated forms) in horses, and to evaluate
associations between ghrelin concentrations and prandial state, signalment and
morphometry. Gene expression studies were conducted to identify ghrelin receptor
isoforms 1a and 1b. A validation of a commercial kit for total ghrelin was
undertaken. Finally, associations between active ghrelin concentrations and
prandial state, signalment and morphometric traits were determined in a cohort
of 35 horses and ponies with no evidence of metabolic disease. Expression of
receptor type 1a was confirmed in equine pituitary gland and adrenal medulla,
and detected in 6 other peripheral tissues. PCR product consistent with receptor
type 1b was expressed in pituitary, adrenal medulla, adrenal cortex and ileum,
but low yields prevented confirmation with sequencing. Parallelism and recovery
on addition steps of the validation resulted in values outside the acceptable
ranges, so total ghrelin concentration could not be measured. Post-prandial
active ghrelin concentration was reduced (p = 0.001) by 19% and positively
associated with age, but was not associated with bodyweight, height, or body
condition. Pre-prandial active ghrelin concentrations were higher (p = 0.0003)
in Welsh ponies compared to other horse and pony breeds. Receptor expression in
disparate tissues suggests multiple roles for ghrelin in horses. Association of
active ghrelin with age and breed warrants further investigation and could
indicate physiological diversity in metabolic pathways in this species.

Ghrelin is the endogenous ligand for the growth hormone secretagogue receptor (GHSR) [1], and has been linked with many physiological functions including, most notably, appetite and adiposity, but also cardio-protection, muscle atrophy, bone metabolism and cancer development [2]. Two forms of ghrelin have been identified: des-acylated (inactive) ghrelin and acylated (active) ghrelin, which are differentiated by a post-translational modification (the addition of a fatty-acyl group to a serine residue of the peptide) [1]. Only the active form activates the GHSR [3] so it has been measured more often. However, interest in inactive ghrelin has also emerged since it was discovered to have biological effects despite not activating the GHSR [4, 5]. The acyl form has primarily been associated with mediating orexigenic and metabolic actions such as appetite stimulation, increasing fat storage, and modulation of glucose homeostasis [6–8]. More recently the des-acyl form has been shown to oppose the action of acyl ghrelin on primary hepatoctytes by inhibiting hepatic glucose output [4] and has also been linked to enhancement of insulin sensitivity via modulation of skeletal muscle metabolism [9], acting through pathways that are independent of the classical ghrelin receptor (GHSR) [10].

In humans, ghrelin concentrations are increased by fasting [11, 12] and just prior to meals [12], and ghrelin administration increases food intake, decreases energy expenditure [13] and increases adiposity [14]. Based on existing research in humans, ghrelin secretion appears primarily regulated by patterns of feeding [12]. Similar findings have been replicated in a grazing species, where cattle exhibit predictable pre- and post-feeding oscillations in circulating ghrelin concentration, demonstrating rhythmic secretion aligned with feeding cycles [15]. Such feeding-entrained rhythmicity is thought to be consistent with intrinsic appetite signalling, not merely feed timing. There is evidence for the endogenous circadian regulation of ghrelin by the Suprachiasmatic Nucleus, the master circadian pacemaker [16], and circadian clock gene expression has been identified in gastric ghrelin cells of mice [17].

Whether ghrelin in horses shows feed-entrained or circadian rhythmicity remains unclear, as 24 h studies have reported conflicting results, with one study detecting a post-prandial peak in active ghrelin concentrations and differences between timepoints [18], while another found no time-of-day or feeding-related variation in both treated and control horses, despite structured meal provision [19]. It may be of note that despite discrete feeding of ‘meals’ (a feed ration), studies seem to vary in whether animals have access to forage outside of these, which is important as they are hindgut fermenters. This could explain the differences in diurnal and feeding-related outcomes across studies. However, Gordon and McKeever (2005) did find differences in the ghrelin response to a feed ration depending on the time of day it was eaten, despite consistent access to hay. In a controlled physiological intervention such as oral grain intake and intravenous glucose administration, circulating ghrelin was reported to respond dynamically in horses [20], suggesting its involvement in energy metabolism.

Ghrelin has been implicated in pathophysiological conditions resulting from metabolic disturbance in humans, such as type 2 diabetes [21, 22] and obesity [23, 24]. There have been some studies on the role of ghrelin in equine disease including osteoarthritis [25], critical illness in foals [26], and oral stereotypies [19]. However, knowledge of ghrelin in horses and ponies in relation to metabolic and endocrine disease lags far behind what is known in other species.

This study sought to increase our understanding of ghrelin physiology in horses and ponies, to establish a basis for future investigations of whether a connection exists between appetite, equine metabolic disease/s and ghrelin. The first aim was to report tissue gene expression of the ghrelin receptor (GHSR). No studies in horses nor ponies to date have attempted to measure and compare the two forms of the ghrelin hormone and so a second aim was to validate an assay that would enable measurement of total ghrelin in horses and ponies. The third aim was to determine whether circulating ghrelin concentration was related to feeding, animal signalment and/or body morphometry.

Associations between active ghrelin and morphometryThere was no association between baseline active ghrelin concentration and either BCS (r = -0.068; p = 0.7) or CNS (r = 0.24; p = 0.17), or between post-prandial active ghrelin and either BCS (r = 0.07; p = 0.69) or CNS (r = 0.04; p = 0.81). This study has contributed new understanding of the physiological nature of ghrelin in horses and ponies. The first aim was to report tissue gene expression of the GHSR in equine tissues, and the results confirmed GHSR1a expression in a central (pituitary gland) and peripheral (adrenal gland) neuroendocrine tissue, and additionally, indicated widespread distribution of this isoform in other peripheral tissues. The physiological activity of ghrelin depends on binding to its receptor to initiate downstream signalling pathways and outcomes, such as its orexigenic and growth hormone releasing effects, as was shown experimentally in a comparison of GHSR knockout and wild-type mice [27]. Therefore, identifying GHSR1a provides confirmation that ghrelin is likely to be functional and that signalling could occur via the GHSR pathway in horses and ponies. To date, the only other studies to have confirmed equine GHSR1a expression localised them to the corpus luteum [28] and chondrocytes [25], and there are no other reports of GHSR1b.

Inconsistent amplification across biological replicates as seen here is consistent with known features of the ghrelin receptor system. GHSR transcripts are low‑abundance so are difficult to detect reliably using end‑point RT‑PCR because targets often fall near the assay’s sensitivity limit [29]. GHSR‑expressing cells can also occur in discrete, spatially clustered populations rather than being uniformly distributed within a tissue, as demonstrated by protein‑level mapping of GHSR‑positive neurons in rat [30] and mouse brain [31]. Additionally, GHSR expression and receptor signalling are dynamically regulated, including transcriptional down regulation by ghrelin and growth hormone [32] and ligand-dependent desensitisation being a characteristic of GPCRs [33]. Accordingly, detected bands were taken to indicate transcript presence, whereas empty lanes were less informative.

In other species, expression of GHSR in central tissues such as the hypothalamus and pituitary gland, is stronger than in other tissues [34]. Thus, our confirmation of GHSR1a in the pituitary gland, although not quantitative, provides evidence for central control of ghrelin in areas of the brain known to integrate and regulate endocrine signals. Further, the appetite centre is located in the hypothalamus [35], which regulates pituitary function as part of the hypothalamic–pituitary axis [36]. Therefore, the localisation of GHSR1a in the pituitary supports the likelihood of interactions within this established regulatory circuit.

The GHSR1b isoform is expressed in the pituitary and hypothalamus of humans and pigs [37]. However, rodent studies have not identified GHSR1b [38], suggesting this isoform is present in some species and not others. Whilst GHSR1a is highly conserved across species [39], reflecting strong evolutionary pressure to maintain essential function, because GHSR1b arises from alternative splicing of the GHSR gene, it is less conserved. Alternative splicing is subject to rapid evolution [40], meaning splice derived isoforms can emerge or be lost relatively quickly across taxa. This fast evolutionary turnover provides a plausible explanation for the absence of GHSR1b in some species. We did detect bands consistent with GHSR1b presence in pituitary gland, ileum and adrenal tissues, providing pilot data for future work, but unfortunately their low intensity prevented successful sequencing. This highlights the need for future optimisation to obtain sufficient PCR product to confirm transcript presence by sequence verification. Additionally, although this study focused on transcript identification, future work would benefit from complementary protein-level analyses (e.g., Western blotting) to verify that the receptor transcript is translated and is present at detectable levels in the tissue.

Having confirmed the potential for a functional ghrelin signalling pathway in horses and ponies, next, attempts to measure ghrelin itself (in both forms it is thought to circulate in the plasma), were undertaken. Regarding the total form, two of the validation steps for the human total ghrelin assay kit chosen to measure ghrelin in equine plasma, were not within acceptable ranges and therefore validation of this kit failed. The dilutional parallelism results indicated matrix interference from equine plasma, affecting analyte quantification. Similarly, the spike and recovery experiment showed consistent under-recovery of the added kit standard (69–77% of expected), indicating suppression of the spiked analyte signal. Together, these findings suggest that components of equine plasma interfere with analyte detection in this assay.

Next, we demonstrated that active ghrelin concentration in horses and ponies was reduced after consuming a discrete feed. This outcome is consistent with previous studies in both horses [20] and other species [41]. Indeed, this phenomenon was foundational to earlier observations giving rise to the identification of ghrelin as an appetite hormone [12]. With the development of more sensitive assays and the ability to measure the acylated and des-acylated portions directly in other species, post-prandial suppression of ghrelin is now considered to be driven by a significant decline in the acylated form, but not in the des-acylated form in healthy-weight humans [42]. The present study confirmed the findings on acylated ghrelin are consistent with this in horses and ponies.

As expected [43], insulin increased between the pre- and post-prandial states and though insulin is sometimes considered to be negatively correlated with ghrelin [8], the present data did not support this. On further assessment of the literature, subsequent human work indicates the ghrelin-insulin relationship is largely temporal rather than cross-sectional [12], revealing that in healthy individuals, insulin and active ghrelin change reciprocally with feeding, but these within-individual shifts do not translate into strong single-timepoint correlations or clear between-individual associations. This supports the interpretation that the two hormones interact dynamically over time rather than exhibiting a stable linear relationship at any given sampling point, potentially explaining lack of correlation of these hormones at single timepoints seen here.

Active ghrelin did not vary depending on whether the animal was a horse or pony, but a difference in active ghrelin concentrations between breeds was observed. Welsh ponies in this cohort had higher circulating concentrations at baseline than any of the other breed groups. Due to the limitation of sample size this finding should be considered preliminary, requiring further confirmation in larger groups. It is worth noting that Shetland ponies and Arabian horses are, along with the Welsh pony, considered high risk breed groups for equine metabolic syndrome (EMS) [44, 45] but didn’t have elevated concentrations comparable to the Welsh ponies. So, no consistent pattern was identified separating high risk from low risk breeds (for EMS) in terms of baseline and post-prandial ghrelin concentration. It is notable that in other species (where larger numbers of studies have been carried out), the regulation of circulating ghrelin in metabolic disease is unresolved. In humans, acylated ghrelin has been reported to be preserved or positively associated with insulin resistance [46] whereas other cohorts demonstrate significantly reduced fasting ghrelin in cases of metabolic syndrome [22]. This, and the finding that other ‘at risk’ breeds did not also demonstrate high baseline ghrelin, might indicate there is more than one underlying endocrine pathway that imparts metabolic disease risk.

Though BCS and CNS were not correlated with ghrelin concentrations for the cohort as a whole, dividing the cohort in to breed groups revealed that Welsh ponies had a higher median CNS than the ‘other riding pony’ group (which was primarily made up of Australian riding ponies) but not than the other breed groups. The concurrent elevation of baseline ghrelin and CNS is consistent with evidence from other species in which ghrelin has been shown to promote lipid accumulation in visceral adipose depots independent of overall adiposity [47, 48], suggesting a role in regional fat partitioning. There is no evidence of causation here but it is plausible that the higher mean baseline ghrelin in the Welsh pony group could be related to fat deposition along the nuchal crest. Further, ghrelin has been shown to modulate insulin secretion [49, 50] and insulin is considered a key regulator of fatty acid availability [51], uptake by adipocytes [52] and triglyceride storage [53], suggesting a potential metabolic pathway for regional fat partitioning. Efficient uptake and storage of energy would be expected to benefit animals with an evolutionary history of survival in harsh environments. As these were small groups, confirmation in larger cohorts would be necessary to confirm these findings.

If breed differences in ghrelin concentration of ‘at risk’ groups were confirmed in future studies it may indicate ghrelin regulation contributes to differential risk for metabolic disease in horses and ponies and that it might be associated with a thrifty genotype as has been suggested previously [54]. Some breed-specific risk alleles for metabolic dysfunction have already been identified that could modulate susceptibility in certain breed groups [55]. The results presented here strengthen the rationale for adopting a breed-specific approach when investigating metabolic diseases.

Mean active ghrelin concentrations were lower than the concentrations reported previously in horses, where mean values ranged from ~ 25 pg/mL (post-prandial) to ~ 47 pg/mL (fasted) [18, 56, 57]. However, concentrations < 10 pg/mL were published for healthy young Thoroughbred mares [26] so the present results sit between these reported values. Variation in concentrations across studies likely indicate differences in the animals of the cohort and management conditions, and possibly exercise regime, which was not controlled for. Another possible limitation was the use of archived samples, although these were processed within 18 months of storage, and the assay guideline indicates that correctly handled plasma samples stored at − 80 °C remain stable during long-term storage (GHRA-88HK, Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA). It is important to note that since this experiment took place, research has identified a difference in the acyl modification of ghrelin, which exists between different species [58]. Ida et al. reported that the serine 3 acyl-modification, which is essential for ghrelin’s activity, is primarily by n-octanoyl (C8) in bovine and porcine ghrelin, but by n-butanoyl (C4) in equine ghrelin. This difference in the fatty acid that acylates ghrelin, represents both a chemical identity difference, as well as a difference in the length of the carbon chain (C4 vs. C8). These biochemical differences are supportive of the likelihood some, possibly most equine acyl‑ghrelin may not be fully recognised by commercial active‑ghrelin assays, which were developed for C8‑acylated ghrelin and are not equine‑specific. The predominance of C4-acylated ghrelin in horses is compelling because it suggests a functional shift in how the ghrelin–GHSR axis operates in this species. Such a modification implies that either the receptor has evolved altered ligand sensitivity or that horses have selected for a signalling profile better suited to their metabolic ecology as hindgut fermenters. In evolutionary terms, a change in the required acyl group points to divergence in how the ghrelin axis regulates appetite, energy balance, and feeding behaviour compared with other mammals. Future work to purify equine ghrelin and find antibodies that specifically and sensitively detect the active form of ghrelin, could uncover as yet unknown data about ghrelin regulation in this species.

The association between age and increased post-prandial ghrelin concentration in horses and ponies has not been reported previously. In humans, ageing is generally considered to reduce active ghrelin concentration [59], which is the opposite of what was found here. In humans, ageing has been reported to influence hunger, fullness and energy intake [60] so the present observation of a positive age-association with post-prandial ghrelin concentration in a healthy cohort could be associated with species-specific gastrointestinal physiology, where altered satiety, intake and/or other metabolic factors occur with increasing age. It is possible this is adaptive in a species that remains highly mobile and retains the need to graze and meet energy demands as it ages. Due to the weak association with age found here, further investigation is needed to confirm the findings.

That there appeared no influence of bodyweight, BCS or CNS on ghrelin concentrations in this healthy cohort, might reflect the limited range of body composition here as it has been associated with ghrelin concentration in numerous other species [61–64]. As this was a healthy cohort, we might have selected out weight associated effects that contribute to disturbance of ghrelin. Additionally, due to the wide variety of possible shapes and sizes of horses and ponies, as compared to a cross-section of adult humans, there may simply be too many factors mediating ghrelin, to isolate an effect of weight alone, without controlling for some of them. This is another indication that targeted investigations are required to provide adequate understanding of all aspects of ghrelin physiology.

ConclusionReceptors for the orexigenic appetite hormone ghrelin, were isolated in horse pituitary and adrenal medulla tissues, and confirmed by DNA sequencing. A splice variant of this receptor was also detected but optimisation of PCR parameters is needed for future studies. Components of equine plasma prevented accurate and reliable detection of total ghrelin and optimisation or novel approaches are needed for its accurate detection. Active ghrelin concentration exhibited variability amongst horses and ponies and was reduced by the ingestion of a feed ration. Age and breed appeared to influence active ghrelin concentration, but height, sex, and whether the animal is a horse or pony, did not. There may be a relationship between regional adiposity and ghrelin concentration in some breed groups that could be relevant to the risk of metabolic disease in Equidae and is worthy of further investigation. Overall, this study contributed fundamental data on the basic physiology of ghrelin in both horses and ponies and provided a basis for future work. Further studies to compare the present results with active ghrelin fluctuation in horses and ponies at pasture, in breed-specific cohorts, and in animals with EMS, controlling for as many factors as possible, would contribute to determining the likelihood of a role for ghrelin in equine metabolic diseases. Additionally, development and validation of an equine specific assay for both forms of ghrelin would provide greater confidence of accurate measurement in equine blood samples across studies and treatments.

There may be a relationship between regional adiposity and ghrelin concentration in some breed groups that could be relevant to the risk of metabolic disease in Equidae and is worthy of further investigation. Overall, this study contributed fundamental data on the basic physiology of ghrelin in both horses and ponies and provided a basis for future work. Further studies to compare the present results with active ghrelin fluctuation in horses and ponies at pasture, in breed-specific cohorts, and in animals with EMS, controlling for as many factors as possible, would contribute to determining the likelihood of a role for ghrelin in equine metabolic diseases. Additionally, development and validation of an equine specific assay for both forms of ghrelin would provide greater confidence of accurate measurement in equine blood samples across studies and treatments.

Statistical analysisContinuous data were assessed for normality using a Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. Pearson or Spearman correlation tests were used to examine associations between continuous and ordinal variables respectively, with mean (± SD) reported for continuous data that met normality assumptions, and median (IQR) reported for data that was categorical or didn’t meet normality criteria.
