Axonemal dynein assembly occurs in the cytoplasm and numerous cytosolic factors
are specifically required for this process. Recently, one factor (DNAAF3/PF22)
was identified as a methyltransferase. Examination of Chlamydomonas dyneins
found they are methylated at substoichiometric levels on multiple sites,
including Lys and Arg residues in several of the nucleotide-binding domains and
on the microtubule-binding region. Given the highly conserved nature of axonemal
dyneins, one key question is whether methylation happens only in dyneins from
the chlorophyte algae, or whether these modifications occur more broadly
throughout the motile ciliated eukaryotes. Here we take a phyloproteomic
approach and examine dynein methylation in a wide range of eukaryotic organisms
bearing motile cilia. We find unambiguous evidence for methylation of axonemal
dyneins in alveolates, chlorophytes, trypanosomes, and a broad range of
metazoans. Intriguingly, we were unable to identify a single instance of
methylation on Drosophila melanogaster sperm dyneins even though dipterans
express a Dnaaf3 orthologue, or in spermatozoids of the fern Ceratopteris, which
assembles inner arms but lacks both outer arm dyneins and DNAAF3. Thus,
methylation of axonemal dyneins has been broadly conserved in most eukaryotic
groups and has the potential to variably modify the function of these motors.

INTRODUCTION

Cilia are microtubule-based cellular extensions involved in motility, sensing the extracellular environment, signal processing, and the secretion of bioactive factors. These organelles occur throughout most of the eukaryotes with some exceptions such as red algae, angiosperms, ascomycetes, basidiomycetes, and amoeboid groups like the filasteria (Carvalho-Santos et al., 2011; Kumar et al., 2019). Motile cilia are usually formed from an axoneme consisting of a ring of nine outer doublet microtubules with a central pair of microtubule singlets surrounded by the ciliary membrane that is contiguous with, but distinct from, the plasma membrane. Cilia are highly complex organelles and extensive proteomic analyses in a broad array of eukaryotes suggests they contain perhaps 1000 or more distinct protein types, for example (Pazour et al., 2005; Arnaiz et al., 2009; Subota et al., 2014; Hammond et al., 2021; Sakato-Antoku and King, 2022). This represents a significant fraction of the total protein coding capacity of eukaryotic genomes (∼5% in humans). Loss of ciliary motility in mammals results in the syndrome primary ciliary dyskinesia with phenotypes including male and female infertility, severe bronchial problems in adults and neonatal respiratory distress, hydrocephalus (rare in humans), and reversal or disruption of the left-right body axis (Reiter and Leroux, 2017; Shoemark and Harman, 2021). There is also growing evidence for the involvement of dysfunctional motile cilia in congenital heart disease (Li et al., 2015), several forms of epilepsy (King, 2006; Faubel et al., 2022), and major psychiatric disorders (Alhassen et al., 2021).

Ciliary motility is powered by the axonemal dynein arms that are arranged in two rows along the length of the outer doublet microtubules. Within each axonemal outer doublet 96-nm repeat unit, there are four outer arm dyneins and an array of seven inner arm dyneins (Nicastro et al., 2006). These enzymes are highly complex and can contain 20 or more components with a combined mass up to ∼2 MDa (King, 2018). Dyneins are built around the heavy chain (HC) motors that consist of ∼4500 residues and exhibit both ATPase and microtubule translocation activities. Outer arm dyneins contain two (e.g., in metazoans and excavates) or three (e.g., in chlorophytes, stramenopiles, alveolates, haptophytes, and cryptophytes) different motor units, while there are two distinct classes of inner arms—the I1/f dynein that is based on a heterodimer of HCs and which is followed in the repeat unit by six dyneins consisting of single HC motors that are clustered in pairs around the three radial spokes that protrude toward the central pair apparatus (Lin and Nicastro, 2018; Wickstead, 2018); note that in Chlamydomonas there are two complete spokes and one truncated form.

In general, dynein HCs consist of an N-terminal region involved in binding other components and docking onto the axoneme, followed by a linker region that spans a hexameric ring of AAA+ domains (AAA1–AAA6) (Burgess et al., 2003; Carter et al., 2011; Kon et al., 2012). Each AAA unit has a distinct role. The AAA1 domain both binds and hydrolyzes ATP and provides the driving impetus for motility. AAA2 has ATP bound but does not release it, while AAA3 and AAA4 can associate with ADP and/or ATP (Chai et al., 2024) and act to modify motility properties (Liu et al., 2020); AAA5 and AAA6 do not interact with nucleotide at all. The domain involved in ATP-dependent microtubule binding and release occurs at the tip of an antiparallel coiled coil stalk that emanates from AAA4 (Carter et al., 2008), and this stalk is supported by a second shorter antiparallel coiled coil (termed the buttress) that derives from AAA5. Transitions between microtubule binding and release are mediated by alterations in the coiled coil register driven by nucleotide binding, hydrolysis, and product release at AAA1 (Carter et al., 2008; Chai et al., 2024).

Dynein motors are subject to exquisite control such that waves of motor activity propagate along the organelle and the region of active interdoublet sliding switches so as to generate both principal and reverse bends. Although a comprehensive molecular understanding of the mechanisms underlying beat frequency and waveform control has yet to be achieved, there is evidence for involvement of interdynein interactions, for example (Rao et al., 2021) and signaling through the radial spokes/central pair apparatus (Smith and Sale, 1992). In addition, dynein-driven motility can be altered by changes in cAMP, Ca2+, and redox poise (Hyams and Borisy, 1978; Hamasaki et al., 1991; Wakabayashi and King, 2006). The function of some dyneins is controlled by phosphorylation of intermediate and/or light chains (ICs and LCs) (Barkalow et al., 1994; Habermacher and Sale, 1997; King and Dutcher, 1997), and certain HCs are also phosphorylated directly at one or more sites, for example, the outer arm α HC and monomeric inner arm HCs from Chlamydomonas (King and Witman, 1994; Piperno and Luck, 1981), although the functional consequences of these additions have yet to be assessed.

Assembly of axonemal dynein complexes occurs in the cytoplasm (Mitchell, 2018), and the fully formed enzymes are kept in an inactive state until they have been trafficked and assembled into the ciliary superstructure (Mali et al., 2021). This assembly process is long and complicated with the ribosomal synthesis of HCs taking almost 15 min to complete (King, 2021). Studies in a broad array of organisms have identified numerous cytosolic factors (DNAAFs) specifically needed for axonemal dynein assembly (Braschi et al., 2022). However, not all are present in every motile ciliated eukaryote, and for example DNAAF8 is found only in vertebrates. Many of these cytosolic assembly factors contain domains involved in protein–protein interactions, recruitment of heat shock proteins and/or large segments that are predicted to be inherently disordered (Mitchell, 2018; King, 2023). Recently, one factor (DNAAF3; termed Dnaaf3 in Drosophila and PF22 in Chlamydomonas) was identified as an S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)-dependent methyltransferase (Sakato-Antoku et al., 2024). Loss of this protein leads to the failure of outer arm and some inner arm dynein assembly in Chlamydomonas, Drosophila, zebrafish, and humans, and consequent primary ciliary dyskinesia in vertebrates and infertility, uncoordinated behavior and deafness in Drosophila (Mitchison et al., 2012; zur Lage et al., 2021). Examination of ciliary dyneins from Chlamydomonas revealed that the HCs are methylated on Arg and Lys residues at multiple sites within the N-terminal regions, AAA domains, and microtubule-binding segments (Sakato-Antoku et al., 2024). However, no Arg methylation was identified on dynein HCs derived from a pf22 null mutant although some methylated Lys residues were present. These observations suggest that DNAAF3/PF22 methylates dynein HCs; these modifications would significantly affect the physicochemical properties of the targeted residues with the positive charge becoming increasingly delocalized, the number of potential hydrogen bonds reduced, and both side chain bulk and hydrophobicity increased as more methyl groups are added (Wesche et al., 2017; Luo, 2018). Furthermore, the presence of methylated Lys residues in pf22-derived Chlamydomonas dyneins suggests a second, currently unidentified, methyltransferase also acts on these motors.

These previous studies were performed using dyneins from the unicellular chlorophyte green alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii and suggested the functional importance of these modifications on dynein motors. However, it is unknown whether dyneins from other motile ciliated organisms are similarly modified by methylation and whether these additions might occur at equivalent sites thereby highlighting key targets of methyl modifications. Determining this will be essential to defining the functional consequences of dynein methylation and the mechanisms by which DNAAF3 dysfunction might lead to primary ciliary dyskinesia. Consequently, here we utilize a phyloproteomic approach to assess the methylation status of dyneins in a broad array of motile ciliated eukaryotes with representatives from four taxonomic supergroups ([Burki et al., 2020] and see Supplemental Table S1). We find complex patterns of methylation in many eukaryotic taxa, with the notable exceptions of the dipteran Drosophila melanogaster and the fern Ceratopteris richardii and identify equivalent modified sites in very distantly related organisms. These data suggest that variable levels of methylation are an ancient and highly conserved feature of axonemal dynein HCs and provide a pathway for dissecting the role of methyl marks in ciliary dynein biology.

Database searches using the default parameters within BLAST at NCBI and Phytozome revealed that orthologues of this factor are present throughout most of the motile ciliated eukaryotes (Figure 1) with the exception of the bryophytes (e.g., mosses) and some vascular plants including lycophytes (clubmoss), ferns, and the gymnosperms—specifically cycads and gingko—suggesting that DNAAF3 was lost in a charophycean ancestor of embryophytes (Bowman, 2022). To further define these deficiencies, we examined the dynein HCs present in both wild-type (CC-125) and pf22 mutant cilia by mass spectrometry (Figure 2A; Supplemental Table S2). Based on the ratio of total spectral counts, abundance of the dynein that powers retrograde intraflagellar transport (IFT; DHC16) and whose assembly does not depend on DNAAF3/PF22 was essentially unaffected by the loss of PF22 and serves as an internal control for equivalent sample loadings. In contrast, there was the almost complete loss of monomeric inner arm proteins DHC3, DHC4, DHC5, DHC8, DHC9, and DHC11 in pf22 cilia, consistent with the results of (Mitchison et al., 2012), and more modest reductions in DHC1 and DHC10 of inner arm I1/f and the monomeric dyneins DHC2, DHC6, DHC7, and DHC12. We also observed a dramatic decrease in the three outer arm dynein HCs (DHCs13-15) in pf22 cilia to ∼15% of wild-type levels. Thus, nine of the 15 axonemal dynein HCs (60%) present in Chlamydomonas exhibit a marked reduction in pf22 mutant cilia. That some outer arm HCs were still present in our whole cilia samples compared with the demembranated axonemes analyzed by (Mitchison et al., 2012) may reflect dynein subparticles being trafficked into the cilium but never incorporated into the axonemal superstructure.

Previous studies revealed that pf22 cytoplasm contains approximately wild-type levels of outer arm dynein HCs, but several-fold more IC2 IC (Mitchison et al., 2012). In addition, it was observed that reduced amounts of outer arm β HC but very little γ HC could be immunoprecipitated using HA-tagged IC2 (Mitchison et al., 2012). Fractionation of pf22 cytoplasmic extracts generated by freeze-thaw using size exclusion chromatography revealed that IC2 (detected by mAb 1869A) eluted in a single distinct peak not associated with the outer arm HCs (Figure 2B). This compares to wild-type extracts where two peaks of the IC/LC complex containing IC2 are found, one of which is tightly bound to the α and β HCs (and see Sakato-Antoku and King, 2023). Thus, the PF22 methyltransferase appears necessary for normal stable formation of the outer arm dynein holoenzyme at least under conditions imposed during gel filtration.

As axonemal dynein methylation is substoichiometric in Chlamydomonas (Sakato-Antoku et al., 2024), we then asked whether dyneins present in the ciliary matrix (i.e., those trafficked into the cilium but not inserted into the axoneme) exhibit methyl marks distinct from those stably incorporated onto the outer doublet microtubules. Analysis of two independently prepared wild-type Chlamydomonas cilia samples separated into membrane plus matrix and axoneme fractions found no evidence for methylation on detergent-extractable α and γ outer arm HCs, while methylation of those integrated into the axoneme was readily detected at numerous sites (Figure 2C). For the soluble β HC, we did find evidence for methylation on the microtubule-binding domain in one of the two samples, but again many more methylated sites on dyneins associated with the doublet microtubules (these sites are highlighted in Supplemental Table S3).

Within the active site of Rossmann fold–based SAM-dependent methyltransferases, there is an essential Glu residue that coordinates the ribose hydroxyls of SAM and thereby orients the donor methyl group (Chouhan et al., 2019). This key residue is present in DNAAF3 orthologues from a broad array of organisms ranging from vertebrates and sea urchins to alveolates and algae. However, in the dipteran Drosophila melanogaster, this Glu residue is replaced by Asp (Supplemental Figure S1A). Structural modeling of the effects of this conservative substitution using Crassostrea (oyster) DNAAF3 and Drosophila Dnaaf3 revealed a subtle relocation of the side chain carboxylate group that might potentially alter the orientation of SAM in the binding pocket thereby affecting activity (Supplemental Figure S1B). Intriguingly, BLAST searches revealed that this active site alteration is not a feature of all Drosophila Dnaaf3s. While an Asp is also present in Dnaaf3 from D. simulans, D. yakuba, and D. grimshawi and also another Drosophilidae genus Zaprionus, a Glu and other alterations in adjacent sequences are found in some other Drosophila species (e.g., D. willistoni, D. ironensis, and D. pseudoananassae). An Asp also occurs at this position in Dnaaf3s from some other dipterans and within the lepidoptera (e.g., Monarch and Swallowtail butterflies; Danaus plexippus and Iphiclides podalirius, respectively), while a Glu residue is present in the 20 hymenopteran species examined (e.g., Vespa mandarinia; Asian giant hornet) and in six orthopterans (e.g., Schistocerca gregaria; desert locust). However, in some other insect groups such as coleoptera (beetles) and hemiptera (true bugs), examples of both residues were again found for example, a Glu in Tribolium castaneum (red flour beetle), and Diaphorini citri (Asian citrus psyllid) but Asp in Leptinotarsa decemlineata (Colorado potato beetle) and Macrosteles quadrilineatus (aster leafhopper). Based on the inferred relationships between these insect clades (Misof et al., 2014), this pattern is not consistent with a single evolutionary origin of the E→D transition.

DISCUSSIONMethyl modifications of dyneinsMethylation on Arg and Lys residues is a common posttranslational modification (PTM) that occurs on a very broad array of proteins throughout the bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes (Murn and Shi, 2017). Methyl group additions lead to delocalization of side chain charge, reduction in hydrogen bond capacity and increased side chain bulk and hydrophobicity (Wesche et al., 2017; Luo, 2018). As basic residues can be monomethylated, dimethylated and, in the case of Lys, trimethylated this leads to tunable effects on these physicochemical parameters that can be detected by interacting partners and are reversible by the action of demethylases.Previous studies in Chlamydomonas identified numerous instances of methylation on axonemal dynein HCs (Sakato-Antoku et al., 2024) and also revealed that many other ciliary components are modified in this manner and that cilia contain all the enzymes needed for SAM metabolism and tetrahydrofolate recycling (Sloboda and Howard, 2009; King et al., 2024). Here we demonstrate that these dynein modifications occur throughout most of the motile ciliated eukaryotes including green algae, alveolates, excavates, and metazoans implying that methylation as a generalized posttranslational process impacting axonemal dyneins is of ancient origin likely dating to before the last eukaryotic common ancestor. That this type of PTM on dyneins has survived for such deep time suggests methylation plays key roles in axonemal dynein biology and this concept is reinforced by the observation that equivalent residues are modified on HCs in very distantly related organisms from separate taxonomic supergroups. The two cases where methylation was not observed likely represent independent secondary loss events—one within the metazoa and a second during embryophyte evolution.In addition to methylation, other PTMs also occur on some dynein HC proteins including phosphorylation (Piperno and Luck, 1981; King and Witman, 1994; Wang et al., 2014) and N-terminal acetylation (Sakato-Antoku et al., 2023); the latter is generally considered irreversible as it often includes proteolytic processing by a methionine aminopeptidase and no specific N-terminal deacetylases have been identified (Ree et al., 2018). Analysis of numerous and varied PTMs in proteins such as histones, tubulins, and actin has revealed the presence of structural codes that are written and erased by modifying enzymes and read by interacting partners; PTM codes dramatically impact the function of these key cellular components (discussed in Jenuwein and Allis, 2001; Janke and Magiera, 2020; Mu et al., 2020). The broadly conserved occurrence of PTMs on dyneins suggests a similar concept might apply to this motor class. Dyneins were found to be methylated in cytoplasm (Sakato-Antoku et al., 2024), but the modified motors function in the cilium where methylation might change interactions either within or between individual dynein particles or other ciliary components. Although ciliated cells express numerous protein demethylases, currently none have been identified in the numerous ciliary proteomes available. Consequently, any potential erasure of dynein methyl marks presumably must also occur in the cytoplasm. In Chlamydomonas, for example, dynein motors undergo recycling in the cytoplasm following ciliary disassembly. This suggests there may be a spatial separation between the writers/erasers and potential readers of dynein methyl marks.Methylated dyneins are not ubiquitous throughout all the motile ciliated eukaryotesDynein methylation was identified in an extensive array of organisms including chlorophyte alga, alveolates, excavates, and numerous metazoans consistent with an ancient origin for these dynein PTMs and the dynein modifying enzyme(s). However, no methylated dynein peptides were found in Drosophila melanogaster sperm dyneins even though we obtained very extensive sequence coverage from two independent samples. This was a surprising observation given that this organism does encode Dnaaf3 (zur Lage et al., 2021). However, sequence alignments show that an essential Glu residue within the active site of other DNAAF3 orthologues is changed to Asp in Drosophila melanogaster (but not some other Drosophila species), and structural modeling suggests this potentially might change the orientation of SAM within the binding pocket and thus affect enzymatic activity. To test this, we examined dyneins from Drosophila willistoni, a species that retains the essential Dnaaf3 active site Glu residue and identified methylated outer arm dynein HCs consistent with this prediction. Sequence alignments of insect Dnaaf3s revealed that this E→D alteration also occurs in some other diptera (flies) and lepidoptera (butterflies and moths) but apparently not in the hymenoptera (ants, bees, and wasps) or orthoptera (crickets and katydids), while in several other insect clades including coleoptera (beetles) and hemiptera (true bugs) examples of both residues in the active site of Dnaaf3 orthologues were identified. Based on their resolved relationships (Misof et al., 2014), the E→D transition must have occurred multiple times within insect lineages. Insect sperm are often enormous (in the mm to even cm range), and relatively little is known of their detailed motility parameters (Werner and Simmons, 2008). Furthermore, insect sperm axonemes exhibit a stunning array of architectural adornments and modifications (Dallai et al., 2016). Consequently, although our data from two Drosophila species suggests the E→D alteration results in an inactive methyltransferase, it is currently difficult to predict what factor(s) drove this Dnaaf3 change. As both insect species we examined assemble dyneins into sperm axonemes it appears that methylation per se is not essential for their axonemal incorporation.Dyneins from spermatozoids of the model fern Ceratopteris also appear to lack methylated dyneins. Even though the sequence coverage obtained was somewhat less compared with other samples, given the number of methyl groups found in other organisms we would have expected to detect perhaps 10 or more methylated peptides from these fern dynein samples if they were modified at a similar frequency. Fern genomes do not encode a DNAAF3/PF22 protein, which is consistent with the observed lack of Arg methylation. Previously, we found some Lys methylation on Chlamydomonas dyneins from the Arg methyltransferase null mutant pf22 suggesting a second Lys-specific enzyme also acts on these HCs. During annotation of the original Chlamydomonas genome, a series of genes were identified encoding proteins of uncertain affinity with the distinctive phylogenetic signature of being present only in motile ciliated eukaryotes (Merchant et al., 2007); several of these have since been identified as encoding integral dynein components or assembly factors, for example (Tanner et al., 2008; Yamamoto et al., 2020). One of these genes, now termed MOT56 (originally MOT1), encodes a Lys methyltransferase with an N-terminal SAM-binding SET domain and a large C-terminal disordered segment. Although an apparently orthologous gene is present in Volvox, a colonial relative of Chlamydomonas, searches of the Ceratopteris, Physcomitrium (moss) and Selaginella (clubmoss) genomes did not identify clear orthologues; although SET domain–containing protein sequences were retrieved by BLAST searches from these organisms using MOT56 as the query, all had very close homologues with much higher probability scores in the angiosperm Arabidopsis and so do not represent MOT56 orthologues. Thus, it is possible that MOT56 represents a second dynein HC methylating enzyme that is also missing in these ciliated bryophyte and vascular plant lineages.Conserved sites of methyl marks on dynein motorsThe phyloproteomic analysis detailed here has revealed that many axonemal dynein HCs are extensively methylated, for example, some outer arm β HCs have in excess of 20 or more methylated residues. Furthermore, we found evidence for 22 sites at which methylation occurred on the equivalent residues of HCs from phylogenetically diverse organisms, for example, between mammals and algae or mollusks and trypanosomes. That these PTM sites have been preserved implies that they play important roles in axonemal dynein function.These conserved sites occur in three general HC regions: near the N-terminus where they might affect HC assembly with other components of these dynein complexes; within the linker and AAA domains where they may impact domain folding, nucleotide-mediated processes or structural transitions; and, at several locations on the coiled coil stalks that support the microtubule-binding domains. Most intriguingly, in multiple dynein HCs we identified methylation at sites on the coil inbound to AAA4 involved in interactions with a conserved acidic residue in the buttress derived from AAA5; predictably methylation would weaken these associations. As the stalk-buttress conformation changes during the mechanochemical cycle (Chai et al., 2024) and is linked to alterations in microtubule-binding affinity, methylation here has the potential to affect the connectivity between the AAA ring and microtubule-binding site.We observed previously that methylation of Chlamydomonas dynein HCs was substoichiometric (Sakato-Antoku et al., 2024). Analysis of dynein HCs from other species revealed that this is a highly consistent feature and leads to several possibilities. For example, all methyl modifications might occur on a subset of dynein HCs while other HCs are completely unmodified. Alternatively, individual dynein HCs might have different patterns of residues bearing methyl marks. Given the number of sites identified in some dynein HCs (in many cases 10 or more), this latter possibility would imply that each HC of a particular class might incorporate a unique pattern of methylation and thus be subtly distinct from all others of that class within the same cilium.Consequences of DNAAF3/PF22 loss and its role in dynein assemblyAlthough many factors specifically needed for axonemal dynein assembly and axonemal incorporation have been identified (Braschi et al., 2022), only six (DNAAF1/LRRC50/ODA7, DNAAF5/HEATR2, DNAAF7/ZMYND10, DNAAF10/WDR92, DNAAF13/SPAG1, and DNAAF16/CFAP298/FBB18) appear to be absolutely indispensable, with others, some with overlapping functions, being missing in one or more eukaryotic group(s). Loss of the DNAAF3 methyltransferase in vertebrates results in the classic features of primary ciliary dyskinesia following the disruption of dynein assembly within the axoneme (Mitchison et al., 2012). In humans, both frameshift mutations (likely nulls) and point mutations (S20Y, L61P, and V116E) two of which alter residues near the SAM binding pocket of DNAAF3 have been found to result in primary ciliary dyskinesia (Mitchison et al., 2012; Guo et al., 2019; Wan et al., 2023). In a null allele of Dnaaf3 in Drosophila melanogaster, sperm were observed to be immotile and both outer and inner dynein arms were missing as assessed by transmission electron microscopy (zur Lage et al., 2021). Proteomics revealed that all dynein HC subtypes were significantly depleted in dnaaf3 mutant testes; in addition, a chordotonal neuron-specific Dnah5 homologue was strongly reduced or absent (zur Lage et al., 2021). This contrasts with Chlamydomonas which undergoes programmed ciliary disassembly and dynein recycling, and where essentially wild-type levels of dynein HCs were present in cytoplasm of the pf22 null mutant even though most dyneins were severely depleted in the cilia (Mitchison et al., 2012; Sakato-Antoku et al., 2024 and data reported here).Currently, DNAAF3/PF22 is the only cytoplasmic assembly factor that has been clearly identified as an enzyme. It consists of an N-terminal Rossmann fold methyltransferase domain followed by a region that is predicted to be inherently disordered; this segment consists of eighty residues in human DNAAF3 (King, 2023). Analysis of Chlamydomonas dyneins from pf22 null mutant cytoplasm found no Arg methylation but did find that Lys methylation was retained (Sakato-Antoku et al., 2024). Whether the general assembly failure of all these various dyneins in pf22 cilia derives from the loss of arginine methyltransferase activity per se or from the absence of the C-terminal inherently disordered region or some other feature of this protein remains uncertain. For example, while neither inner arm dyneins DHC3 nor DHC12 are detectably methylated, only DHC3 exhibits a highly altered ciliary abundance in the absence of PF22 suggesting a nonenzymatic role. A similar argument can be made for the fern Ceratopteris which does not appear to have methylated spermatozoid inner arm dyneins and lacks a DNAAF3 gene. Future studies using site-directed mutations at conserved methylation sites and the analysis of enzyme-dead and/or truncated forms of DNAAF3 will be useful to assess this.In conclusion, we demonstrate here that methylation is a highly abundant PTM of axonemal dynein HCs that occurs throughout most of the motile ciliated eukaryotes with a few intriguing exceptions. These observations suggest that dynein methylation is of ancient origin and dates to the dawn of the ciliated eukaryotes. Furthermore, we find examples where the equivalent sites are modified in HCs from very divergent organisms providing a conservation-based guide for analyzing the roles they play in dynein biology.

The two cases where methylation was not observed likely represent independent secondary loss events—one within the metazoa and a second during embryophyte evolution.

In addition to methylation, other PTMs also occur on some dynein HC proteins including phosphorylation (Piperno and Luck, 1981; King and Witman, 1994; Wang et al., 2014) and N-terminal acetylation (Sakato-Antoku et al., 2023); the latter is generally considered irreversible as it often includes proteolytic processing by a methionine aminopeptidase and no specific N-terminal deacetylases have been identified (Ree et al., 2018). Analysis of numerous and varied PTMs in proteins such as histones, tubulins, and actin has revealed the presence of structural codes that are written and erased by modifying enzymes and read by interacting partners; PTM codes dramatically impact the function of these key cellular components (discussed in Jenuwein and Allis, 2001; Janke and Magiera, 2020; Mu et al., 2020). The broadly conserved occurrence of PTMs on dyneins suggests a similar concept might apply to this motor class. Dyneins were found to be methylated in cytoplasm (Sakato-Antoku et al., 2024), but the modified motors function in the cilium where methylation might change interactions either within or between individual dynein particles or other ciliary components. Although ciliated cells express numerous protein demethylases, currently none have been identified in the numerous ciliary proteomes available. Consequently, any potential erasure of dynein methyl marks presumably must also occur in the cytoplasm. In Chlamydomonas, for example, dynein motors undergo recycling in the cytoplasm following ciliary disassembly. This suggests there may be a spatial separation between the writers/erasers and potential readers of dynein methyl marks.

Conserved sites of methyl marks on dynein motors

The phyloproteomic analysis detailed here has revealed that many axonemal dynein HCs are extensively methylated, for example, some outer arm β HCs have in excess of 20 or more methylated residues. Furthermore, we found evidence for 22 sites at which methylation occurred on the equivalent residues of HCs from phylogenetically diverse organisms, for example, between mammals and algae or mollusks and trypanosomes. That these PTM sites have been preserved implies that they play important roles in axonemal dynein function.

These conserved sites occur in three general HC regions: near the N-terminus where they might affect HC assembly with other components of these dynein complexes; within the linker and AAA domains where they may impact domain folding, nucleotide-mediated processes or structural transitions; and, at several locations on the coiled coil stalks that support the microtubule-binding domains. Most intriguingly, in multiple dynein HCs we identified methylation at sites on the coil inbound to AAA4 involved in interactions with a conserved acidic residue in the buttress derived from AAA5; predictably methylation would weaken these associations. As the stalk-buttress conformation changes during the mechanochemical cycle (Chai et al., 2024) and is linked to alterations in microtubule-binding affinity, methylation here has the potential to affect the connectivity between the AAA ring and microtubule-binding site.

We observed previously that methylation of Chlamydomonas dynein HCs was substoichiometric (Sakato-Antoku et al., 2024). Analysis of dynein HCs from other species revealed that this is a highly consistent feature and leads to several possibilities. For example, all methyl modifications might occur on a subset of dynein HCs while other HCs are completely unmodified. Alternatively, individual dynein HCs might have different patterns of residues bearing methyl marks. Given the number of sites identified in some dynein HCs (in many cases 10 or more), this latter possibility would imply that each HC of a particular class might incorporate a unique pattern of methylation and thus be subtly distinct from all others of that class within the same cilium.

Gel pieces were dehydrated with acetonitrile, dried, and then rehydrated with 12.5 ng/µl trypsin solution (Promega porcine sequencing grade trypsin #V5113) or 12.5 ng/µl endoproteinase AspN (Roche #V1621) in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate. Proteolysis was allowed to proceed for 16 h at 37°C. Peptides were extracted using two sets of alternating washes with 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate and 5% formic acid in 50% acetonitrile followed by two sets of alternating washes using 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate or 100% acetonitrile. The wash solutions were pooled, dried and the peptides resuspended in 0.1% formic acid in water.

Peptides were analyzed using nanoflow ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) and MS/MS using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano UPLC system and Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were loaded onto a 75 µm x 25 cm nanoEase m/z Peptide BEH C18 analytical column (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA), separated using a 1 h reversed-phase UPLC gradient, and directly eluted into the Orbitrap Eclipse using positive mode electrospray ionization. MS/MS data were acquired using a data-dependent Top15 acquisition method.

Raw MS/MS data were searched against the various organismal proteomes using the Andromeda search engine embedded within MaxQuant v.1.6.10.43. Proteome databases searched were Ceratopteris richardii v2.1 (Phytozome), Chlamydomonas reinhardtii CC-4532 v6.1 (Phytozome), Drosophila melanogaster (FlyBase), Drosophila willistoni (UniProt), Trypanosoma brucei (TriTrypDB TRUE927), Rattus norvegicus (UniProt), Mnemiopsis leidyi (Mnemiopsis genome project portal at the National Human Genome Research Institute), Hemicentrotus pulcherrimus (DDBJ/EMBL/GenBank at the National Library of Medicine under BioProject accession PRJDB6441), Crassostrea gigas (DDBJ/EMBL/GenBank at the National Library of Medicine under BioProject accession PRJNA629593), Ciona intestinalis (DDBJ/EMBL/GenBank at the National Library of Medicine under BioProject accession PRJNA187185), Takifugu rubripes (DDBJ/EMBL/GenBank at the National Library of Medicine under BioProject accession PRJNA543527), Tetrahymena thermophila (DDBJ/EMBL/GenBank at the National Library of Medicine under BioProject accession PRJN543527), and Oncorhynchus mykiss (DDBJ/EMBL/GenBank at the National Library of Medicine under BioProject accession PRJNA639144); all searched proteome databases are available in fasta format (see Data availability statement). All searches included the following variable modifications: Met oxidation, N-terminal acetylation, methyl Lys/Arg, dimethyl Lys/Arg, trimethyl Lys and Ser/Thr/Tyr phosphorylation; carbamidomethylation of Cys was included as a fixed modification. Identification and quantification results generated by MaxQuant were visualized and further analyzed using the Scaffold v.5 (Proteome Software, Inc., Portland, OR).
