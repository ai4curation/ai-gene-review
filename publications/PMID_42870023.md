Activation of heterotrimeric G proteins by G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs)
requires large-scale opening of the Gα α-helical domain (AHD) to expose the
nucleotide-binding site and facilitate GDP-GTP exchange. Although orthosteric
ligands are known to modulate GPCR conformation, how these effects propagate to
the bound G protein remains unclear. Using single-molecule Förster resonance
energy transfer imaging, we monitored AHD motions in Gi proteins coupled to the
μ-opioid receptor across different ligand- and nucleotide-bound states. We find
that receptor ligands differentially modulate these dynamics from a binding site
over 70 Å away, with higher-efficacy agonists accelerating transitions to an
open, low-nucleotide-affinity conformation. These data also capture transient
AHD intermediates during nucleotide exchange and suggest that allosteric control
over the G protein conformational states provides an additional mechanism for
encoding opioid efficacy and signaling outcomes.

The most notable conformational change observed for G protein activation by a GPCR is the separation of its Ras-like and α-helical domains (RLD and AHD) in the Gα subunit (Fig. 1a,b), as revealed by early double electron–electron resonance (DEER) spectroscopy10 and X-ray crystallography11 studies. In all inactive-state crystal structures, the AHD—a six-α-helical bundle (αA–αF)—packs against the RLD, burying the bound GDP in the interface between the domains12. Following receptor coupling and GDP release, this interface opens by tens of angstroms10,11,13–15, resulting in an intrinsically dynamic AHD that has largely eluded high-resolution structural characterization16.

Multiple lines of evidence suggest that AHD movements are critical for facilitating GDP–GTP exchange across all G protein isoforms, yet their role in signaling efficacy remains poorly understood17. While molecular dynamics (MD) simulations indicate that domain separation alone may not be sufficient for nucleotide exchange, a variety of functional18–20, spectroscopic13,21,22 and computational23,24 studies demonstrate that AHD movements are tightly linked to both GDP release and the subsequent binding of GTP16,25,26. Here, we use total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) single-molecule Förster resonance energy transfer (smFRET) imaging to directly monitor AHD motions in Gi1 proteins bound to μOR activated by diverse orthosteric ligands, in the absence and presence of nucleotide. Our results reveal how ligand-specific signals are allosterically encoded in the G protein conformational ensemble, a pattern that also applied to Gi bound to neurotensin type 1 receptor (NTSR1). These data suggest a mechanistic basis for opioid signaling efficacy that extends beyond simple receptor–G protein coupling efficiency.

To investigate the kinetics of AHD motions within μOR–Gi, we used a hidden Markov model (HMM) to idealize the traces and quantify transitions between high-FRET and low-FRET states (Figs. 1e and 2c and Methods). In basal (inactive)-state structures of heterotrimeric G proteins, the Gα AHD tightly encloses the bound GDP38,39, yet spontaneous domain separation must occur to permit GDP release, as G proteins still slowly exchange nucleotide even without GPCR catalysis40. To investigate these basal dynamics, we immobilized labeled Gi heterotrimers using an N-terminal histidine tag and imaged their AHD motions in a physiological concentration of GDP (30 μM)41 (Extended Data Fig. 5a and Methods).

These results demonstrate that the G protein conformation can be dynamically tuned by the GPCR, sampling a continuum of states rather than discrete ‘off’ or ‘on’ conformations (Fig. 2b). We showed that opioid ligand efficacy extends beyond initial receptor–G protein coupling to include the modulation of G protein conformational equilibria within the fully formed signaling complex. These results demonstrate that the G protein conformation can be dynamically tuned by the GPCR, sampling a continuum of states rather than discrete ‘off’ or ‘on’ conformations (Fig. 2b). While these insights may be relevant to efforts to develop safer opioid analgesics48–51, they also suggest a broader framework for how the interplay between receptor and effector conformational states can influence signaling response, which is important across GPCR targets52,53.

Our findings reveal that ligand-dependent conformational changes propagate from the receptor to the engaged G protein, modulating its AHD across open, closed and transient intermediate states. Consistent with the loss of contacts upon separation of the Gα domains, our measurements showed that the high-FRET (closed) state corresponds to higher nucleotide affinity, while the low-FRET (open) state possesses reduced affinity (Fig. 3c,d). This aligns with recent complementary cryo-EM structures of μOR–Gi revealing ligand-dependent GDP-bound intermediates with distinct AHD conformations54, as well as prior findings that mutations of the interdomain interface or hinge regions accelerate basal nucleotide exchange25,40,55,56 and increased interdomain motion correlates with G protein activation57–59. The energetic bias toward AHD opening we observe in full agonist-bound complexes (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 3a) should reduce their nucleotide affinity, driven by the shift in the G protein conformational equilibria.

To test this, we measured GTPγS binding to nucleotide-free μOR–Gi complexes by fluorescence dequenching (Methods). BUP-occupied complexes exhibited 1.5-fold faster GTPγS binding than those bound to the full agonist LFT (Fig. 4a), consistent with extensive earlier evidence that higher-efficacy ligands reduce the nucleotide affinity of coupled G proteins5,60–65. In the cytoplasm, where GTP is approximately tenfold more abundant than GDP, G protein activation is primarily limited by GDP release, as GTP loading occurs rapidly afterward. Full agonists promote AHD opening, facilitating GDP release and, therefore, efficient GTP binding. In contrast, partial agonists restrict open-state occupancy, favoring GDP retention or rebinding, which prolongs its residence time in the interdomain pocket. This competitively inhibits GTP binding and delays G protein activation, ultimately explaining why partial agonists exhibit lower signaling efficacy despite faster intrinsic GTPγS binding rates (Fig. 4a). The relative difference between ligands is also greater for GDP than for GTP binding, further suggesting that G protein dynamics are primarily selected to regulate the GDP release step, although opioid-dependent regulation of GTP exchange has also been recently reported.

At a structural level, these ligand-induced G protein conformational changes require allosteric communication spanning over 70 Å from the receptor’s orthosteric site to the G protein AHD. Differences in G protein dynamics observed with different orthosteric ligands can be understood as alterations in the complementarity between the binding surfaces where the RLD and AHD meet. While GPCR-mediated G protein activation is known to involve remodeling of the α5 helix in the Gα subunit67–70, studies also link this helix to AHD opening10,23,71, minimally through the α1 helix in the RLD72,73 (Fig. 4b). The α1 helix directly connects to the AHD through the αA helix and helps to stabilize the inactive G protein conformation through conserved interdomain contacts with αF and the hinge region74. During GPCR engagement, α5 moves away from the nucleotide-binding pocket toward the intracellular vestibule of the receptor, causing rotation and partial disorder of α1. These rearrangements sterically disrupt the interdomain interface, opening the AHD and priming the ternary complex for GDP release13,15,70 (Fig. 4b). In addition, the fact that the AHD is partially displaced from the RLD in the receptor-bound high-FRET state suggests that some steric disruption has already occurred. This ‘preactivated’ conformation may lower the barrier for GDP release during the early stages of ternary complex assembly, with the extent of this priming potentially varying across different Gi-coupled receptors.

smFRET and DEER studies of β2-adrenergic and μOR receptors have shown that full agonists promote greater outward movement of transmembrane helix 6 (TM6)5,6, which expands the intracellular vestibule where the α5 helix binds. This TM6 displacement, along with other ligand-dependent changes to the receptor’s cytosolic surface, may facilitate AHD opening by making it more energetically favorable for the G protein α5 to fully engage15,75–77. In contrast, partial agonists have been shown to induce less efficient α5 insertion78, which could weaken α1-mediated disruption of the Gα interdomain interface and prolong the dwell time in the high-FRET, high-nucleotide-affinity state preceding GDP release (Fig. 4c). This mechanism helps explain an earlier observation that partial agonists at β2–Gs act to stabilize transient GDP-bound GPCR–G protein intermediates5, which could function as kinetic traps retarding productive complex assembly and activation. As for the low-affinity open state, the similar low-FRET dwell times observed across opioid agonists (Fig. 2d) suggest that, once the AHD is fully open, ligand identity has minimal influence on its dynamics, possibly because the AHD is affixed against Gβ, as seen in a recent cryo-EM structure14.

Unlike real-time insights from smFRET, resolution of the AHD in cryo-EM studies of GPCR–G protein complexes is limited by the conformational heterogeneity captured upon freezing16,59. As such, it may be unsurprising that structures with ligands of varying efficacies often show minimal differences in Gα61,79,80, capturing primarily the lowest-energy conformations while even subtle changes at the interdomain interface can greatly alter domain separation dynamics74. Lipid bilayers have also been shown to promote AHD opening in Gαs independent of receptor engagement81 and certain small molecules that target G proteins have been demonstrated to select for a specific state of the AHD, such as the Gq inhibitor YM-254890 (ref. 82). Interplay between such direct perturbations of G protein conformation and the ligand-dependent bias observed here may ultimately help to explain previously unappreciated signaling outcomes. Together, these findings point to the G protein itself as an additional locus through which ligand-specific signaling behavior can emerge, providing a mechanistic rationale for how certain opioid ligands could produce partial agonism with potentially fewer adverse effects.

Photons emitted from LD555 and JFX673 were collected through a ×60 (numerical aperture: 1.27) water-immersion objective (Nikon) and split onto two cooled electron-multiplying charge-coupled device cameras (Andor iXon Ultra 888) connected by a 640-nm dichroic mirror (ZT640rdc, Chroma) housed in a TwinCam (Cairn). All imaging was performed at 19 °C and videos were recorded with Andor Solis (i) software (Andor) at a 50-ms time resolution (20 fps). Microfluidic chambers for smFRET imaging were constructed from passivated quartz slides and coverslips using a modified protocol from Ha et al.86. Briefly, aminated glass surfaces passivated with mPEG-SVA (MW 5,000; Laysan Bio) doped with 0.6% w/w biotin-PEG-SVA (MW 5,000; Laysan Bio) were exposed to two additional rounds of passivation reactions using only mPEG-SVA.

For experiments with receptor–Gi ternary complexes, passivated imaging chambers were incubated for 5 min with 0.2 mg ml−1 NeutrAvidin (Thermo Scientific), followed by 5 min with 25 nM biotinylated anti-Flag M1 Fab fragment. Unbound M1 Fab and NeutrAvidin were washed out using imaging buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 0.01% L-MNG, 0.001% CHS and 2 mM CaCl2). Fluorophore-labeled μOR–Gi and NTSR1–Gi complexes were filtered using a 0.22-μm PVDF membrane (Millipore Sigma) and loaded onto the microscope slide at 200 pM by pulling down the N-terminal Flag tag on the receptor. Unbound complexes were washed out at an immobilization density of ~0.1 molecules per μm2. All experiments were performed in imaging buffer containing saturating concentrations of tested ligands and without enzymatic oxygen scavenging. Measurements of each condition collected on different days using independent chambers were aggregated, with total molecule counts for each condition reported in the figures or legends.

For smFRET of basal Gi heterotrimer, NeutrAvidin-coated chambers were incubated for 5 min with 0.02 mg ml−1 biotinylated anti-mouse IgG1 antibody (BioLegend, 406603; 1:25 dilution) followed by 5 min with 0.02 mg ml−1 mouse anti-His epitope tag antibody (Qiagen, 34660; 1:50 dilution). Purified, LD555/JFX673-peg4-labeled Gi with an uncleaved N-terminal 6×His tag on the α subunit was loaded onto the microscope slide at ~200 pM and measured in imaging buffer containing 30 μM GDP, 1 mM MgCl2 and 100 μM TCEP. The procedure for anti-His tag immobilization was reported previously87,88 and is specific. Nucleotide-free measurements were obtained by exchanging the heterotrimer into buffer without GDP on the surface for 30 min before imaging.

smFRET data analysisFluorescence time trajectories of single molecules were extracted from the videos and processed using a custom software suite implemented in MATLAB that is available on GitHub. Donor traces were scaled by a constant factor (γ) to account for the apparent unequal fluorophore brightnesses, calculated as γ = ΔIA/ΔID, where ΔIA and ΔID are the changes in donor and acceptor intensity after acceptor photobleaching, respectively89. FRET trajectories were calculated as (1 + (γID/IA))−1, where ID and IA are the background-corrected donor and acceptor fluorescence intensities at each frame. Traces lasting at least 0.5 s and exhibiting single-step donor and acceptor photobleaching and overall anticorrelated donor and acceptor intensities were manually selected for further analysis. Stable low-FRET traces with FRET close to the donor-leakage background were excluded. The selected datasets had a mean signal-to-noise ratio (mean/s.d. of the total fluorescence intensity before photobleaching) of ~11 across all samples. Population histograms summed over the first 3 s of the selected FRET traces were fit to a mixture of 3–4 Gaussian functions in Python using sci-kit learn (version 1.3.2) to derive the state mean FRET values and occupancies. Initial component centers were estimated from the observed distributions, informed by k-means-initialized fits of the full agonists. The idealized FRET traces (viterbi) were clustered using a Gaussian mixture model (GMM) to remove overfitting of fluctuations within the same FRET state and the resulting corrected state sequences were used to calculate transition density plots from transitions corresponding to ΔFRETideal > 0.1. The number of states k for each condition was determined on the basis of the lowest value for which a k + 1 state HMM fit did not change the clusters in a transition density plot of the uncorrected traces. For three-state fits, vbFRET camera-blur correction was enabled. To explore the exchange kinetics between the fully open and closed FRET states within μOR–Gi, the state sequences resulting from a two-state HMM and Gaussian mixture fit were concatenated (‘stitched’) and the dwell times in each state were extracted from the change points. Population dwell time distributions were calculated using a kernel density estimate (Gaussian kernel; bandwidth determined by Scott’s rule), which was normalized to reflect the frequency of dwells in time bins along the x axis91. The mean lifetime in each state was estimated as the integral of the normalized Kaplan–Meier estimate of the dwell time survival curves restricted to <25 s for all ligands. Dwell times and their distributions are reported as the mean ± s.d. from 100 bootstrap samples of the idealized FRET traces, where the resampling probability of each trace is weighted by its relative duration92. While kinetics were analyzed with only γ correction, conversions of FRET efficiency to relative interdye distances used additional corrections for channel crosstalk and direct acceptor excitation, as outlined by Hellenkamp et al.93. BODIPY (BDP)–GTPγS binding assayBinding of GTPγS–BDP-FL (Jena Bioscience) to nucleotide-free μOR–Gi complexes was measured as previously described94. Briefly, complexes of ligand-bound μOR, unlabeled Gαi-Δ6-90/238C and Gβγ were purified by anti-Flag and added at a final concentration of 500 nM to a 500-μl quartz cuvette containing 1 μM GTPγS–BDP-FL. Fluorescence was recorded at 512 nm (495 nm excitation) for 600 s at room temperature using a Fluorolog spectrophotometer (HORIBA) and Horiba FluorEssence with magnetic stirring. All experiments were performed in a buffer containing saturating ligands and 20 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl, 0.01% L-MNG, 0.001% CHS, 2 mM MgCl2 and 100 μM TCEP. Mean association times for GTPγS–BDP-FL binding were calculated from resulting kinetic spectra by fitting a pseudo-first-order association function using nonlinear least squares in Python. Data were background-corrected using the mean of the first ten time points. We note that the C325A substitution contained in the Δ6 cysteine construct reportedly affects the rate of spontaneous GDP release but not GTP binding95.

Statistics and reproducibilityNo statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses; the experiments were not randomized; the investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Reproducibility of the single-molecule data was assessed across at least two independent experiments on different days with different imaging chambers. Biological replication (separate protein preparations starting from expression) was confirmed for the LFT complex. Sample sizes are reported in the figures or legends as the pooled number of molecules (n) observed for each condition. For bootstrap analysis, individual molecules were treated as the observational unit and resampled with replacement, preserving all FRET observations associated with each selected molecule. This procedure estimates uncertainty in the molecular state distributions arising from the finite number of molecules sampled and variation among molecules. Parametric curve fits and linear regression were performed in Python using SciPy (version 1.11.4).
