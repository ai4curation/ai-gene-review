Abstract

Pax6 and c-Maf regulate multiple stages of mammalian lens development. Here, we identified novel distal control regions (DCRs) of the αA-crystallin gene, a marker of lens fiber cell differentiation induced by FGF-signaling. DCR1 stimulated reporter gene expression in primary lens explants treated with FGF2 linking FGF-signaling with αA-crystallin synthesis. A DCR1/αA-crystallin promoter (including DCR2) coupled with EGFP virtually recapitulated the expression pattern of αA-crystallin in lens epithelium and fibers. In contrast, the DCR3/αA/EGFP reporter was expressed only in ‘late' lens fibers. Chromatin immunoprecipitations showed binding of Pax6 to DCR1 and the αA-crystallin promoter in lens chromatin and demonstrated that high levels of αA-crystallin expression correlate with increased binding of c-Maf and CREB to the promoter and of CREB to DCR3, a broad domain of histone H3K9-hyperacetylation extending from DCR1 to DCR3, and increased abundance of chromatin remodeling enzymes Brg 1 and Snf 2h at the αA-crystallin locus. Our data demonstrate a novel mechanism of Pax6, c-Maf and CREB function, through regulation of chromatin-remodeling enzymes, and suggest a multistage model for the activation of αA-crystallin during lens differentiation.

Keywords: c-Maf, chromatin remodeling, histone acetylation and methylation, lens differentiation, Pax6

Introduction

Multicellular organisms achieve cellular differentiation through precisely regulated gene expression. The specification of individual cell lineages occurs through a myriad of operations including cell-to-cell interactions, and extracellular signaling that result in the activation and repression of specific genes. The lens provides an excellent system to study this process at the molecular level, because the expression of any gene in the lens can be temporally and spatially characterized as a consequence of the lens' unique morphology ( Lovicu and McAvoy, 2005 ).

Crystallins are the most abundant proteins in terminally differentiated lens fiber cells. Their high level of expression in lens fibers together with their distinct spatial distribution is essential for lens transparency and its refractive properties. αA-crystallin represents as much as 17% of all newborn mouse lens water-soluble proteins, while all crystallins represent about 90% of total lens proteins ( Bloemendal et al , 2004 ). Loss of expression of αA-crystallin or expression of its mutants causes lens opacification and formation of cataracts ( Bloemendal et al , 2004 ).

Lens differentiation commences as soon as the lens vesicle, comprised of a single layer of cuboidal epithelial cells, pinches off from the surface ectoderm. The lens vesicle is polarized with its anterior cells retaining their epithelial morphology and proliferative capacity, whereas the posterior lens precursor cells initiate terminal differentiation forming the primary lens fibers. Lens differentiation is initially regulated by secreted growth factors including members of the FGF family originating from the prospective retina ( Lovicu and McAvoy, 2005 ). Secreted FGFs interact with four types of tyrosine kinase receptors, FGFR1–4, all of them expressed in lens. The mechanism by which FGFs regulate lens fiber cell differentiation is not known. One possibility is that FGF-signaling regulates abundance and activity of members of Ets and large Maf families of transcription factors ( Barolo and Posakony, 2002 ). Recent genetic studies also suggested roles of Wnt pathway ( Stump et al , 2003 ; Lyu and Joo, 2004 ) and TGFβ-signaling ( de Iongh et al , 2001 ) in ‘late' stages of lens fiber cell differentiation.

αA-crystallin mRNA appears first in the invaginating lens placode followed by uniform expression in the lens vesicle ( Robinson and Overbeek, 1996 ). Dramatic upregulation of αA-crystallin expression is associated with primary lens fiber cell differentiation. Previous studies have shown that a −366 to +46 αA-crystallin promoter fragment is active in primary lens fibers with positional and copy-number effects in transgenic mice ( Cvekl et al , 2004 ). In addition, this promoter supported levels of expression two to three orders of magnitude lower than that of the endogenous αA-crystallin gene ( Duncan et al , 1996 ), suggesting that additional regulatory regions are necessary.

Three distinct DNA-binding transcription factors, Pax6, c-Maf, and CREB, were implicated as αA-crystallin regulatory factors ( Cvekl et al , 1995 ; Kawauchi et al , 1999 ; Ashery-Padan et al , 2000 ; Ring et al , 2000 ; Yang and Cvekl, 2005 ). Pax6 is critical in establishing the lens lineage from surface ectoderm ( Grindley et al , 1995 ; Ashery-Padan et al , 2000 ). Multiple Pax6 binding sites were identified in the lens-specific promoter of the mouse αA-crystallin gene ( Yang and Cvekl, 2005 ). However, expression of αA-crystallin requires additional factors as Pax6 is expressed in other tissues, including brain, olfactory epithelium and pancreas not expressing αA-crystallin ( Walther and Gruss, 1991 ). Genetic studies of the bZIP protein, c-Maf, established its essential role in the transcription of many crystallin genes during lens fiber cell differentiation ( Kawauchi et al , 1999 ; Ring et al , 2000 ). In c-Maf null lenses, expression of αA-crystallin is reduced to about 1% of its normal level ( Ring et al , 2000 ). Like Pax6, c-Maf can interact with multiple regions of the αA-crystallin promoter ( Yang and Cvekl, 2005 ) and CREB interactions with a region of −110/−103 was shown in vitro ( Cvekl et al , 1995 ). Regardless of the established roles of Pax6, c-Maf and CREB in various developmental contexts, the molecular basis of their activities during differentiation and in chromatin structure is unclear.

Herein, we examined mechanisms that regulate mouse αA-crystallin gene expression in vivo . We identified novel distal regulatory regions of this locus and determined their activities in transgenic mice. We also characterized interactions of specific DNA-binding transcription factors and ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling enzymes in a 16 kb region in cells/tissues exhibiting high, moderate or no expression of this gene.

Results

Identification of three distal control region (DCRs) in the mouse α A-crystallin locus

To identify putative DCRs, we aligned the mouse and human αA-crystallin loci using the VISTA algorithm for comparative genomics to identify evolutionary conserved noncoding regions as described in Supplementary data . We found three such regions termed DCR1, DCR2 and DCR3, as schematically shown in Figure 1A . To test their possible enhancer-like activities, we generated a series of reporter constructs containing the lens-specific αA-crystallin promoter fragment −111 to +46 driving luciferase expression combined with individual DCRs (see Figure 1B ). It has been shown previously that treatment of explanted lens epithelia with FGF2 can induce endogenous αA-crystallin expression ( Leenders et al , 1997 ). To assess whether any of these DCRs could confer induction of reporter activities in the presence of FGF2, we performed a series of transfections in primary lens epithelial explants and evaluated their promoter activities in the presence or absence of 100 ng/ml of FGF2. In the absence of FGF2, DCR1 activated the promoter 1.6-fold ( Figure 1C ). In contrast, both DCR2 and 3 reduced this promoter activity approximately two-fold. In the presence of FGF2, DCR1 increased the promoter activity approximately seven-fold compared to that of the promoter alone ( Figure 1D ). In contrast, the promoter activities of constructs containing DCR2 and DCR3 were similar to the activity of the promoter alone. Thus, DCR1 is likely to play a role in αA-crystallin gene regulation downstream of FGF-signaling.

DCR1 and DCR3 confer different temporal and spatial patterns of expression in lens vesicle, lens epithelium and primary lens fibers

To assess the roles of DCR1 and DCR3 in αA-crystallin gene regulation in vivo , we generated transgenic mice expressing EGFP under the control of DCR1, DCR3 and DCR1/DCR3 in combination with a 1.9 kb genomic fragment harboring DCR2 and the promoter (see Figure 1A and Supplementary data ). We rationalized that the integrity of the genomic region including the promoter and its proximal DCR2 would be more comparable to their endogenous functions. Modest expression of EGFP, driven by the 1.9 kb genomic region of αA-crystallin and assessed by direct fluorescence, was observed in lens fibers from E13.5 (see Figure 2 ). In contrast, DCR1, DCR3 and DCR1/DCR3 strongly activated this promoter in four, two and four transgenic lines, respectively ( Figure 2 ). Analysis of RNAs and proteins from transgenic lenses confirmed the differences in fluorescence intensities (data not shown). No ‘leaky' expression of EGFP was found in the brain, kidney and muscle in any of the mouse lines (data not shown). We thus concluded that both DCR1 and DCR3 act in combination with the 1.9 kb αA-crystallin promoter as potent lens-specific enhancers.

Figure 2.

Analysis of the temporal and spatial expression of an EGFP reporter gene driven by DCR1 and the 1.9 kb αA-crystallin promoter during eye development revealed expression of EGFP in the lens vesicle of E11.5 embryos ( Figure 2A ) and expression upregulated in primary fibers at E12.5, while EGFP expression was maintained in the anterior lens epithelium (see Figure 2B ). In contrast, expression driven by DCR3 and the 1.9 kb αA-crystallin promoter was delayed approximately 24 h in relation to the onset of EGFP expression driven by DCR1 ( Figure 2 ). However, this expression preceded the expression driven by the 1.9 kb promoter alone. A combination of both DCR1 and DCR3 yielded expression pattern of EGFP comparable to the DCR1/1.9 kb promoter. Our data therefore suggest that DCR1 and DCR3 play distinct temporal roles in the regulation of the αA-crystallin gene in vivo : DCR1 directs both early expression in the lens vesicle and the initial increase during primary fiber cell differentiation. DCR3 contributes to this differentiation-associated expression pattern, although expression directed by DCR3 in mature lens fiber cells is delayed compared with that directed by DCR1. Our data do not formally exclude the possibility that DCR3 is also capable of supporting expression in lens epithelium, albeit below the detection limit of the EGFP assay.

Histone H3 acetylation and methylation mark the mouse α A-crystallin locus

Next, it was of interest to study the distribution of specific DNA-binding proteins and other regulators of chromatin structure and transcription at the αA-crystallin locus to understand the molecular mechanisms regulating αA-crystallin expression in vivo . However, the small amount of lens tissue available at the developmental stages of interest made ChIP experiments impractical in this system. In lieu of this, we used three cell and/or tissue models exhibiting high, moderate, and no expression of the αA-crystallin gene. One-day old mouse lens (P1) represents a tissue with high levels of expression. Cultured mouse lens αTN4-1 epithelial cells express moderate levels of αA-crystallin expression. In contrast, mouse L929 fibroblasts do not express αA-crystallin ( Kantorow et al , 1993 ). Using quantitative RT–PCR, we found approximately 19-fold higher expression of αA-crystallin in the P1 mouse lens compared to αTN4-1 cells ( Table 1 in Supplementary data ). Therefore, these materials can provide nuclei for study of αA-crystallin chromatin in vivo using ChIPs.

Since high level histone H3 lysine 9 acetylation is a hallmark of transcriptionally active genes ( Litt et al , 2001 ), we first examined its distribution at the αA-crystallin locus ( Figure 3A ). In fibroblasts, as expected, we detected a marginal presence of acetylated H3 K9 ( Figure 3B ). In lens epithelial cells, the highest levels of H3 K9 acetylation were observed in the promoter region with moderately increased levels of H3 K9 acetylation at positions of −9 kb, DCR1 and +4 kb. In contrast, in mouse lens, we found that the highest H3 K9 acetylation occurred in DCR1 and the lowest was detected at +4 kb ( Figure 3B ). Thus, moderate levels of αA-crystallin expression are associated with a narrow region of H3 K9 acetylation in the promoter and coding regions of this gene. This H3 K9 acetylation domain was expanded in mouse lens chromatin and included both DCR1 and DCR3. The highest local level of H3 K9 acetylation in DCR1 correlates with its functional significance in temporal and spatial regulation of the αA-crystallin gene in vivo .

Figure 3.

To further characterize epigenetic regulation of the αA-crystallin gene, we analyzed distribution of trimethylated lysine 4 histone H3 (H3K4Me3), since it is also associated with transcriptionally active genes ( Kim et al , 2005 ). The localization of H3K4Me3 at the αA-crystallin locus was predominantly in the promoter and in its downstream region (see Figure 3C ). As with H3 K9 acetylation, the intensity of this modification in the promoter region of cultured lens cells was lower compared to lens chromatin. The most likely reason for this finding is a depletion of histone H3, and, hence entire nucleosomes, from the promoter region in lens chromatin ( Carey, 2005 ; Mito et al , 2005 ).

Binding of Pax6, c-Maf and CREB in the mouse α A-crystallin locus

Previous studies suggested Pax6, c-Maf and CREB as specific DNA-binding regulatory factors of the mouse αA-crystallin gene ( Cvekl et al , 2004 ). To determine binding of Pax6, c-Maf, CREB and TBP in the mouse αA-crystallin locus, we prepared chromatin from fibroblasts, lens epithelial cells and 1-day old lenses, and evaluated in vivo binding of these factors in a region from −10 kb to +6 kb of the mouse αA-crystallin locus extending from DCR1 to DCR3. We started our analysis with Pax6, a key regulator of lens development ( Ashery-Padan et al , 2000 ). We did not find Pax6 in chromatin from mouse fibroblasts (data not shown), in agreement with lack of Pax6 expression in these cells ( Cvekl et al , 1995 ). In lens, epithelial cells, we found Pax6 at DCR1, DCR2 and the promoter at comparable levels ( Table I and Supplementary data ). We also found Pax6 at DCR3, albeit at lower levels. In contrast, in mouse lens chromatin, we detected the presence of Pax6 only at DCR1 and at the promoter. These data established that Pax6 associates with important regions of the mouse αA-crystallin locus in vivo.

We next determined the occupancy of c-Maf in the 16 kb αA-crystallin locus. We did not find any c-Maf at this locus in fibroblasts (data not shown). In the chromatin of lens epithelial cells, we detected higher binding of c-Maf at DCR1, DCR2, the promoter and exon 2 compared to a slightly reduced binding at DCR3 ( Table I and Supplementary data ). In the mouse lens, we detected approximately 20-fold increased abundance of c-Maf in the promoter while the amount of c-Maf in DCR1 remained almost unchanged from its levels in lens epithelial cells. No evidence of occupancy of MafA, MafB and NRL at the αA-crystallin locus was obtained (data not shown). These data demonstrate that the increased levels of c-Maf in the promoter correlate with strong transcriptional activity of the αA-crystallin gene in P1 lens compared to lens epithelial cells.

Previously, we have shown CREB binding to the distal promoter region of the mouse αA-crystallin promoter ( Cvekl et al , 1995 ). Although CREB proteins were detected in L929 nuclear extracts ( Cvekl et al , 1995 ), we did not find evidence for in vivo interactions between CREB and the αA-crystallin promoter in vivo in mouse fibroblasts (data not shown). However, in cultured lens epithelial cells, we detected CREB in the promoter ( Table I and Supplementary data ). In the lens, we found increased abundance of CREB both in the promoter, exon 2 and DCR3. These data established that strong enrichments of CREB in both the promoter and DCR3 in the lens correlated with higher transcriptional activity of αA-crystallin in lens compared to lens epithelial cells.

Expression of EGFP directed by DCR1–promoter is reduced in Pax6 heterozygous lens

To demonstrate whether Pax6 regulates early embryonic expression of the αA-crystallin gene, we crossed mice harboring the DCR1–promoter–EGFP construct with Pax6 heterozygous mice, and determined both the temporal-spatial and quantitative levels of transgenic EGFP and endogenous αA-crystallin genes. Pax6 haploinsufficiency delayed the process of lens placode formation. As a result, smaller lens vesicles are produced ( van Raamsdonk and Tilghman, 2000 ). Our data show that primary lens fiber cell differentiation was delayed in DCR1–promoter × Pax6+/− eye at E12.5 (see Figure 4 ) compared to more elongated fibers found at the DCR1–promoter–EGFP eye (see Figure 2A ). Next, we analyzed the transcript levels for EGFP driven by the DCR1–αA–promoter and the endogenous αA-crystallin genes in newborn transgenic and Pax6+/− lenses and found that Pax6+/− animals had lower transcript levels for both of these genes (see Table II ), demonstrating that transgenic expression of the entire DCR1–promoter–EGFP is sensitive to Pax6 gene dosage.

Pax6 and c-Maf activate transcription via DCR1

Given the function of DCR1 in the above developmental studies and its association with Pax6 and c-Maf in vivo , we analyzed its sequence for such binding sites. The results show the presence of two candidate Pax6- and three Maf-binding sites (MAREs) in DCR1 (see Figure 5A ). To test if DCR1 mediates transcriptional activation in the presence of Pax6 and c-Maf, we prepared a reporter containing DCR1 cloned 5′ of the heterologous β A -globin minimal promoter (see Figure 5A ) that is known to be un-responsive to these factors ( Czerny and Busslinger, 1995 ). Pax6 activated this reporter seven-fold and c-Maf activated this reporter 41-fold as shown in Figure 5B . These data further support the idea that DCR1 acts as a distal enhancer of the αA-crystallin locus, and at least a part of its function can be mediated by interactions with Pax6 and c-Maf. In summary, our data suggest interactions of key DNA-binding transcription factors with novel regulatory regions of the αA-crystallin gene, and show notable differences in interactions of c-Maf and CREB with the mouse αA-crystallin locus in vivo . The function of DCR1 in the lens vesicle and lens epithelium is consistent with binding of Pax6 and c-Maf in vivo and its ability to be activated by these factors in reporter assays. In contrast, DCR3 function correlated with strong binding of CREB, supporting the idea that DCR1 and DCR3 play distinct roles as shown above.

Recruitment of RNA polymerase II to the α A-crystallin locus

Identification of regions harboring RNA polymerase II provides information regarding the recruitment of this enzyme to the αA-crystallin locus. We first assessed binding of TBP/TFIID to the αA-crystallin promoter and locus. In ChIP assays of lens epithelial cells, we detected the majority of TBP in DCR1 ( Table I ) although DCR1 does not contain any obvious TATA-box. We also found smaller amounts of TBP in DCR2, the TATA-containing promoter and DCR3. In contrast, in mouse lens, we found a substantial fraction of TBP in the promoter region with appreciable levels also in DCR1. In lens epithelial cells, we detected enrichments of RNA polymerase II in DCR1, DCR2, the promoter and DCR3 ( Table I ). This pattern of enrichment correlated with enrichments of TBP. In mouse lens, we found increased abundance of RNA polymerase II in two regions, the promoter and DCR1. This profile was almost identical to the profile of TBP in the same chromatin. In addition, novel enrichments of RNA polymerase II were found in exon 2, and regions +2 and +4 kb downstream from the promoter. These enrichments could signal an active transcriptional process.

An alternate explanation for the presence of TBP in DCR1 is that this region harbors a cryptic promoter. To address this possibility, we performed 5′-RACE mapping and conducted amplification of a series of regions between DCR1 and the promoter using total RNAs from mouse E12.5 embryos, newborn lenses and cultured lens epithelial cells (see Supplementary data ). We did not obtain any evidence for transcription in this region supported by analysis of ESTs in various databases.

Differential recruitment of Brg1 and Snf2h to the α A-crystallin locus mark its transcriptional status

Condensed chromatin characterized by a regular nucleosomal organization represents a major structural barrier for transcription that interferes with many phases of the transcriptional process ( Khorasanizadeh, 2004 ). It has been shown that active chromatin remodeling mediated by SNF/SWI and ISWI ATP-dependent complexes is required for transcription; however, different genes and their variable levels of expression are likely to function in a broad continuum of remodeling activities. Herein, we examined the presence and distribution of Brg 1, a catalytic subunit of SWI/SNF, and Snf 2h, a similar subunit of the ISWI complex in the αA-crystallin locus ( Table I ). In lens epithelial cells, we found low levels of Brg 1 at the majority of sites (8 of 12) along the αA-crystallin locus, particularly between DCR2 to +6 kb. In contrast, in mouse lens chromatin, we observed significantly higher amounts of Brg 1 across the entire 16 kb of the analyzed region. The amounts of Brg 1 were on average 30 times higher in lens compared to lens epithelial cells. In both systems, Brg 1 was present at DCR1, DCR2, the promoter and DCR3, which we have shown are all occupied by Pax6 and c-Maf. However, we also found high levels of Brg 1 at regions –10, −9, −7, −4 and +4 kb in lens chromatin. These regions did not associate with Pax6, c-Maf, CREB or TBP (see Table I ). These results suggest that high levels of αA-crystallin expression are associated with an abundance of Brg 1, and by inference the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complexes, throughout the 16 kb of the locus.

As Brg 1 and Snf 2h are thought to play nonredundant roles in chromatin remodeling during development ( Bultman et al , 2000 ; Stopka and Skoultchi, 2003 ; Fan et al , 2005 ), we next examined the distribution patterns of Snf 2h. In the chromatin of lens epithelial cells, we found Snf 2h at a single region, the promoter (see Table I ). In contrast, in lens chromatin, we found a broad region of Snf 2h covering positions –10 to +6 kb of the αA-crystallin locus. Enrichment of Snf 2h dropped nearly three-fold in the promoter region in lens epithelial cells compared to the lens chromatin. Limited abundance of Snf 2h to the αA-crystallin promoter in lens epithelial cells raised the possibility that Snf 2h is needed for a distinct step in chromatin remodeling in the promoter to achieve the moderate levels of αA-crystallin expression in epithelial cells prior to broad recruitment of SWI/SNF and ISWI complexes to the whole αA-crystallin locus in lens fiber cells. It was shown earlier that SWI/SNF and ISWI complexes use different mechanisms for chromatin remodeling ( Fan et al , 2005 ). To further test this model, we determined which DNA-binding factors recruit Brg 1 and Snf 2h. Using co-immunoprecipitation assays, we found that Pax6 bound Brg 1 ( Figure 6A ) and c-Maf bound Snf 2h ( Figure 6B ). In addition, CREB interacted with both Brg 1 and Snf 2h ( Figure 6C and D ). In contrast, no interactions between Pax6 and Snf 2h, and c-Maf and Brg 1 were detected (data not shown). Inclusion of ethidium bromide to disrupt protein–DNA interactions did not prevent formation of Brg 1/Pax6 complexes but Snf 2h/c-Maf complexes were disrupted (data not shown). These data raise the possibility that c-Maf and CREB directly recruit Snf 2h and Pax6 lures Brg 1 to the αA-crystallin locus. However, it is not known if these complexes are direct or mediated by other proteins.

Discussion

DCR1 and DCR3 temporally and spatially control α A-crystallin gene expression

The transcription of tissue-restricted genes during development often depends on the function of a promoter in combination with multiple DCRs. This modular organization of regulatory regions within the locus allows various external signals to control the expression of specific genes. Here, we identified two such regions DCR1 and DCR3 that regulate the expression of the mouse αA-crystallin gene in the lens. We show that DCR1 is sufficient for EGFP reporter gene expression in combination with the 1.9 kb αA-crystallin promoter in lens vesicle and lens epithelium of transgenic mice. Both expression patterns directed by DCR1- and DCR1/DCR3-transgenes virtually recapitulated the spatial and temporal pattern of expression of the endogenous αA-crystallin gene ( Robinson and Overbeek, 1996 ). The early activity of DCR1 in primary lens fibers in transgenic mice was consistent with the roles of FGFs as key lens differentiation signals ( Faber et al , 2001 ) and its capacity to mediate this signaling in primary lens explants ( Leenders et al , 1997 ). In contrast, DCR3/transgenes were activated 24 h later compared to DCR1/transgenes, and their expression was restricted to more elongated lens primary fiber cells. In transfected primary lens cells, DCR3 did not increase the luc reporter gene activity in the presence of FGF2. Thus, DCR3 could be a downstream target for Wnt- ( Stump et al , 2003 ; Lyu and Joo, 2004 ; Smith et al , 2005 ) and TGFβ- ( de Iongh et al , 2001 ) signaling operating after FGF-triggered lens fiber cell differentiation ( Lovicu and McAvoy, 2005 ).

Changes in binding of Pax6, c-Maf and CREB to the α A-crystallin locus

Analysis of positions and quantities of transcription factors in the 16 kb region of αA-crystallin locus in chromatin with no, moderate or high levels of expression of αA-crystallin provided novel insights into the mechanism of up-regulation of this gene during lens fiber cell differentiation. Although each DCR and the promoter interacted with Pax6, a redistribution of Pax6 was evident in chromatin expressing moderate and high levels of αA-crystallin. Stable association of Pax6 proteins with the αA-crystallin promoter and DCR1 in the lens chromatin is in agreement with Pax6's dual roles in the establishment of lens lineage and direct regulation of the αA-crystallin gene.

The high level of αA-crystallin expression in the lens correlated with a nearly 20-fold higher focal abundance of c-Maf in the promoter in lens compared to its amount detected in the promoter in lens epithelial cells. This finding agrees both with the upregulation of c-Maf in primary lens fibers ( Kawauchi et al , 1999 ; Ring et al , 2000 ), dramatic reduction of αA-crystallin expression in c-Maf null lenses and higher expression of αA-crystallin in the lens compared to lens epithelial cells. Our data show that c-Maf associates with the same regions of the αA-crystallin locus as Pax6; however, we found nearly 20-fold higher binding of c-Maf in the promoter in the lens compared to lens epithelial cells. Since DCRs occupied by c-Maf do not undergo this dramatic change, some selective mechanism recruiting c-Maf to the promoter is involved.

In spite of the ability of chicken L-Maf/MafA to induce transdifferentiation of neuroretina to lens and its activation of the chicken αA-crystallin promoter (−244 to +89) in cultured cells ( Ogino and Yasuda, 1998 ), post-translational regulation of quail MafA via FGF signaling ( Benkhelifa et al , 2001 ) and expression of MafB and NRL in lens ( Coolen et al , 2005 ), we could not establish interactions of MafA/L-Maf, MafB and NRL with the mouse αA-crystallin locus using ChIPs. Furthermore, variable expression patterns of large Maf genes in mouse, chicken, frog and zebrafish lenses, termed ‘expression domain shuffling' ( Coolen et al , 2005 ) suggest that c-Maf and L-Maf/MafA play distinct roles during mouse and chick lens differentiation.

Similar to c-Maf, an increased abundance of CREB was also found in the αA-crystallin promoter in lens chromatin. Owing to sequence similarity between Maf- and CREB-binding sites and their ability to form heterodimers ( Vinson et al , 2002 ), it is possible that either homodimers and/or heterodimers of these proteins are present in the αA-crystallin promoter in vivo . The only molecular marker of the active DCR3 was a 26-fold higher enrichment of CREB in the lens compared to lens epithelium. Therefore, our data suggest CREB and its upstream signaling as one of the possible mechanisms of DCR3 function in ‘late' lens primary fibers ( Figure 2 ). Notably, TGFβ and Wnt signaling also regulate lens fiber cell differentiation ( de Iongh et al , 2001 ; Stump et al , 2003 ), and both these pathways can operate via protein kinase A, an activator of CREB ( Schiller et al , 2003 ; Chen et al , 2005 ). Our data provide a possible downstream target region for TGFβ and Wnt signaling pathways, DCR3, for future studies of lens fiber cell differentiation.

The pattern of TBP binding to DCR1, 2 and 3 correlates with that of Pax6 in chromatin of lens epithelial cells and in lens. We have shown earlier that Pax6 could interact with TBP/TFIID in lens nuclear extracts ( Cvekl et al , 1999 ). Other studies showed interactions between large Maf proteins, including c-Maf, with TBP ( Friedman et al , 2004 ), and, thus, both Pax6 and c-Maf may recruit TBP/TFIID to theses DCRs. In lens chromatin, Pax6 and TBP co-localized in the promoter and DCR1, and RNA polymerase II co-localized with TBP/Pax6. Identification of RNA polymerase II enrichments in DCR1 requires further explanation: one possibility is that a DNA loop formed between DCR1 and the αA-crystallin promoter ( Wijgerde et al , 1995 ; Carter et al , 2002 ) or recruitment of RNA polymerase II via Pax6/c-Maf/TBP to DCR1 serve as an ‘intermediate' storage compartment for the enzyme. Additional studies of these nonmutually exclusive mechanisms are necessary.

Distribution of modified histones and chromatin remodeling enzymes Brg1 and Snf2h in the α A-crystallin locus

Epigenetic regulation of gene expression ultimately changes nucleosomal organization of individual genes by chromatin remodeling ( Narlikar et al , 2002 ). Chromatin remodeling can be divided into covalent histone modifications ( Berger, 2002 ) and ATP-dependent movement of nucleosomes ( Kingston and Narlikar, 1999 ). Herein we found a 13 kb domain of acetylated histone H3 K9 in lens chromatin marked at its 5′/3′ borders by DCR1 and DCR3. In contrast, we detected a less than 5 kb long H3 K9 hyperacetylated domain in lens epithelial cells (see Figure 7 ). Methylated histone H3 (H3K4Me3) was mostly found in the αA-crystallin locus at the promoter with asymmetric extension into the transcribed region in chromatin from both lens epithelial cells and whole lens. Interpretation of local intensities of H3 K9 acetylation and K4 trimethylation along the αA-crystallin locus requires identification of positions of individual nucleosomes and enzymes catalyzing post-translational modifications of histones. It is likely that reduced amounts of H3 K9 acetylated and K4 methylated histones in the promoter region of αA-crystallin in whole lens chromatin are caused by the removal of multiple nucleosomes from this region ( Carey, 2005 ; Mito et al , 2005 ).

Figure 7.

Removal of nucleosomes is performed by structurally and functionally distinct SWI/SNF and ISWI chromatin remodeling complexes ( Fan et al , 2005 ). Our data show localized recruitment of Snf 2h to the αA-crystallin promoter (see Figure 7 ) in combination with a broad and low presence of Brg 1 in chromatin from lens epithelial cells. In contrast, in whole lens chromatin, high abundance of both Brg 1 and Snf 2h was found through the entire 16 kb region of the αA-crystallin locus. There is a great demand for crystallins in lens fibers as they accumulate to an average concentration of 450 mg/ml of total protein ( Horwitz, 2003 ). As αA-crystallin is a single-copy gene, its high level expression in lens fibers is likely to require elimination of all structural barriers to transcription. The sustained recruitment of both SWI/SNF and ISWI complexes to the entire αA-crystallin locus may be required to fulfill this need.

A multistage model of α A-crystallin gene activation

The molecular mechanisms that regulate expression levels of differentiation markers are still poorly characterized. Our data are most consistent with a multistage model, whereby the lens progenitor cells forming the lens placode do not express the αA-crystallin gene ( Robinson and Overbeek, 1996 ). The transcriptionally inactive αA-crystallin locus is then converted into an ‘open' chromatin domain suitable for transcription. Recent studies of early development and initiation of tissue-specific transcription showed that specific ‘decisive/pioneer' transcription factors initiate chromatin opening ( Cosma, 2002 ; Lomvardas and Thanos, 2002 ). Genetic studies of Pax6 and its Drosophila homologue eyeless established Pax6 as an early developmental ‘stage-selector' gene ( Graw, 2003 ). We hypothesize that some stage-selector proteins including Pax6 aid chromatin remodeling of their target genes.

High levels of Pax6 and Brg 1 expression in the lens placode ( Chauhan et al , 2002 ) prior to the expression of c-Maf ( Ring et al , 2000 ) indirectly suggests an early role for Pax6 via recruitment of Brg 1 to initiate chromatin remodeling of the αA-crystallin locus in the lens placode. In addition, recent studies have implicated Pax6 in the regulation of c-Maf ( Sakai et al , 2001 ) and Brg 1 ( Chauhan et al , 2002 ) expression. αA-crystallin expression would then begin as Snf 2h/ISWI complexes complete the chromatin remodeling necessary for its transcriptional initiation in the invaginating lens pit. Nevertheless, our data cannot exclude the alternate possibility that c-Maf/ Snf 2h/ISWI localize to the αA-crystallin first during the invagination of the lens placode facilitating binding of Pax6 to the locus. We hypothesize that remodeling of the locus requires DCR1 as the promoter alone is inactive in transgenic mice at this stage.

Our data show that the increase of αA-crystallin expression in response to FGF-signaling is mediated by both DCR1 and the αA-crystallin promoter. We propose that FGF-signaling directly or indirectly increases the selective abundance of c-Maf in the promoter and boosts promoter strength in primary lens fibers. While c-Maf abundance at DCR1 did not increase in the whole lens as compared to lens epithelial cells, the presence of two consensus Ets-binding sites in this element suggest that Ets factors, nuclear executors of FGF-signaling ( Barolo and Posakony, 2002 ), are responsible for FGF mediated activation of DCR1. Recruitment of Snf 2h via c-Maf and Brg 1 via Pax6 may then increase the abundance of chromatin remodeling enzymes in the αA-crystallin locus. The highest transcriptional state of the αA-crystallin gene may be reached by CREB activation triggered by activated Wnt- and TGFβ-cooperating with FGF-signaling in ‘late' primary lens fibers and recruitment of CREB to both DCR3 and its promoter. Additional studies will be needed to probe various aspects of this model.

Materials and methods

Materials

Antibodies used for ChIPs: anti-Pax6, anti-c-Maf, anti-TBP and anti-RNA polymerase II were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology; anti-CREB-1 from ActiveMotif, anti-acetylated H3 K9 and anti-trimethylated H3 K4 from Upstate Biotechnology and rabbit IgG from Oncogene. Recombinant FGF2 was from Sigma, phalloidin and DAPI were from Molecular Probes.

Reporter plasmids and transient transfections

DCRs generated by PCR were subcloned 5′ of the mouse αA-crystallin promoter (−111 to +46) in pGL2 ( Yang and Cvekl, 2005 ). Primary lens explants were obtained from 3-day-old rat lenses, 2 μg of reporter and 50 ng of CMV Renilla reference plasmids were transfected with Effecten system (Qiagen) as described elsewhere ( Golestaneh et al , 2004 ). Transfections of 293T cells by Lipofectamine Plus (Invitrogen) and their analyses were described earlier ( Yang et al , 2004 ). Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Transgenic mice production and analysis of EGFP expression

Four reporter plasmids were generated in peGFP-1 (Clontech) as diagrammatically shown in Supplementary Figure 2A . The reporter cassettes were released from the plasmids by Mlu I and Afl II digestion to generate transgenic mice by pronuclear injection of fertilized eggs at the AECOM Transgenic Core Facility. Pax6 heterozygous mice (Pax6+/−lacZ) were obtained from P Gruss ( St-Onge et al , 1997 ). EGFP fluorescence was directly visualized using a Leica AOBS confocal microscope. DNA/nuclei and cell morphology were visualized by DAPI and phalloidin ( Beebe et al , 2001 ) counter-staining. For qRT–PCR analysis, the following primers were used: αA-crystallin (5′-GAGATTCACGGCAAACACAA and 5′-ACATTGGAAGGCAGACGGTA) and EGFP (5′-ACGACGGCAACTACAAGACC and 5′-GTCCTCCTTGAAGTCGATGC).

qChIP

Formaldehyde crosslinked chromatin was obtained from two different cultivations of mouse lens epithelial (αTN4-1) and fibroblast (L929) cells, and from two groups of pooled 400 1-day-old lenses as described earlier ( Yang and Cvekl, 2005 ). The sheared chromatin (average size 600 bp) was generated by sonication ( Stopka et al , 2005 ). Aliquots of chromatin prepared from 2.5 × 10 6 cells and 20 lenses were incubated with 1–2 μg of antibody bound to 8 μl of a 1:1 mixture of protein G- and protein A-Sepharose beads (Sigma). The immunoprecipitates were washed three times and resuspended in a buffer containing 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 25 mM EDTA supplemented with 0.1 mg/ml RNaseA and 0.2 mg/ml proteinase K. After the 2 h incubation at 55°C, the crosslinks were reversed by overnight incubation at 65°C. Genomic DNA was eluted into 250 μl of water using QIAquick Spin Gel Purification kit (Qiagen).

The amounts of each specific DNA fragment in immunoprecipitates were determined by quantitative PCR reactions using a standard curve generated for each primer set with 0.05, 0.2, 0.5 and 1% input DNA samples ( Stopka et al , 2005 ). Using a standard curve, we transformed C t values into DNA copy numbers. The copy number of a specific DNA fragment in each assay was compared to the copy number of that fragment before immunoprecipitation (‘input DNA'). The values obtained with control antibody (IgG) were subtracted from that corresponding value.

Primers were designed using Primer 3 ( http://frodo.wi.mit.edu/cgi-bin/primer3 ) and their sequences are given in Supplementary data . The PCR products were between 60 and 130 bp. The reactions were analyzed using a 7900 ABI PRISM PCR Instrument and a 2xSYBR mix (ABI). The parameters were: 95°C/10 min followed by 45 cycles of (94°C/10 s, 60°C/20 s and 72°C/30 s) ( Stopka et al , 2005 ).

Co-immunoprecipitations

Co-immunoprecipitations were performed using 3xFlag tagged Pax6, c-Maf and CREB proteins ( Yang and Cvekl, 2005 ), Flag tagged Snf 2h (provided by Dr GJ Narlikar) and Brg 1 co-expressed in 293T cells as described elsewhere ( Wu et al , 2003 ). Briefly, the cells grown on 10 cm dishes were transfected with 10 μg of each plasmid DNA using calcium phosphate precipitates. The transfected cells were cultured for 48 h and lysed in a buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 140 mM NaCl, supplemented with 1% Triton X-100, and proteinase inhibitor mixture (Roche). In total, 500 μg of total protein was incubated with anti-FLAG M2 (Sigma), anti- Brg 1, anti-Pax6, anti-c-Maf and anti-CREB antibody followed by incubation with protein G-Sepharose beads. The beads were washed five times with the above buffer containing 0.5% Tween-20. The precipitated proteins were released by boiling for 5 min in a buffer containing 100 mM Tris, 10 mM DTT and 4% SDS. The proteins were subjected to SDS-gel electrophoresis in 6% precast gels. Specific experiments included 100 μg/ml ethidium bromide to disrupt the protein–DNA interactions ( Lai and Herr, 1992 ).
