Plant hypocotyls are central to seedling establishment in variable light
environments. Although core photoreceptor pathways regulating hypocotyl
elongation are well defined, the molecular basis of natural variation in this
trait-particularly through posttranscriptional regulation-remains poorly
understood. Here, we report that a high-altitude, high-light-adapted Arabidopsis
ecotype from Tibet exhibits longer hypocotyls under low light compared to a
low-altitude ecotype from Sichuan. Using recombinant inbred lines combined with
fine-mapping and near-isogenic line validation, we identified the major locus
LA1 (Light Adaptation 1) and pinpointed IAMH2 as the causal gene. Full-length
transcriptomics revealed a natural structural variant in the IAMH2 3' UTR of
high-altitude ecotypes that alters its length and disrupts a poly(U) motif. We
further show that in low-altitude ecotypes, the RNA-binding proteins C3H14/C3H15
directly bind this poly(U)-rich region to posttranscriptionally downregulate
IAMH2 mRNA levels, thereby attenuating IAMH2-dependent IAA accumulation and
hypocotyl elongation. Analysis of 38 globally representative ecotype genomes
confirmed that IAMH2 3' UTR variation correlates with light intensity and
elevation, supporting its role in environmental adaptation. Collectively, our
work reveals a previously unknown C3H14/C3H15-IAMH2 posttranscriptional module
that dynamically adjusts hypocotyl elongation in response to light, providing
new insights into the plasticity and adaptive evolution of seedling
photomorphogenesis.

INTRODUCTION

The hypocotyl serves as a pivotal environmental sensing and signal‐integrating organ in plants, capable of synergistically interpreting diverse external cues such as light, temperature, and soil mechanical stress (Krahmer & Fankhauser, 2024). Light signals are transduced into intracellular responses via specific photoreceptors, including phytochromes, cryptochromes, and UVR8, thereby regulating multiple developmental phases from seed germination and seedling emergence to floral transition (Chen et al., 2004; Paik & Huq, 2019; Podolec et al., 2021). By integrating light information into endogenous genetic networks, plants dynamically reprogram their developmental trajectories (Yadav et al., 2020). The elongation capacity of the Arabidopsis hypocotyl is crucial for adaptation to variable light environments. Under darkness or low light, seedlings undergo rapid hypocotyl elongation to reach the soil surface, driven by the alleviation of light‐imposed growth suppression (e.g., via gibberellin‐promoted degradation of DELLA proteins) (Bawa et al., 2020; Feng et al., 2008). Upon perceiving sufficient light, phytochrome/cryptochrome‐mediated signaling pathways are rapidly activated, suppressing cell elongation and promoting photomorphogenesis (Alabadí et al., 2004; de Wit et al., 2016). This developmental switch relies on extensive crosstalk between light signaling and phytohormone networks, with auxin (indole‐3‐acetic acid, IAA) acting as a central metabolic and morphogenetic integrator (Oh et al., 2014; Casanova‐Sáez et al., 2021). Studies demonstrate that auxin regulation of hypocotyl elongation exhibits marked dose‐ and light‐dependence: light conditions systematically alter the hypocotyl response curve to auxin concentration, thereby enhancing seedling adaptability to auxin‐mediated growth inhibition and enabling precise acclimation to the ambient light environment (Wang et al., 2025).

The spatial concentration gradient of IAA is precisely maintained by a dynamic equilibrium among its biosynthesis, polar transport, and reversible inactivation (Ljung, 2013; Casanova‐Sáez et al., 2021). Among the four known tryptophan‐dependent IAA biosynthetic pathways, the indole‐3‐pyruvate (IPyA) pathway is regarded as the primary source of IAA in plants (Casanova‐Sáez et al., 2021). Concurrently, the role of the indole‐3‐acetamide (IAM) pathway has become increasingly defined. Initially characterized in plant‐pathogenic bacteria, this pathway has now been validated across diverse plant species (Mano et al., 2010; Mano & Nemoto, 2012). The conversion of IAM to IAA depends on specific IAM hydrolases. In Arabidopsis thaliana, two tandem IAM hydrolase genes—IAMH1 and IAMH2—have been reported to catalyze the terminal step in the IAM‐to‐IAA conversion reaction, and the iamh1 iamh2 double mutant exhibits insensitivity to exogenous IAM and suppresses the high‐auxin phenotype induced by overexpression of the bacterial IAM synthase gene iaaM (Gao et al., 2020).

Alternative splicing and differential mRNA stability—often determined by cis‐regulatory elements within the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR)—represent key posttranscriptional layers in the genetic regulation of quantitative traits (Litterman et al., 2019; Mayr, 2017, 2019). For instance, under high‐light stress in cucumber seedlings, the preferential production and accumulation of a nonfunctional splice variant, CsGA2ox8.2, sustains rapid growth by attenuating the degradation of active gibberellins (Liu et al., 2021). In poplar, the RNA‐binding protein PdGRP1 precisely modulates brassinosteroid‐mediated secondary growth by binding to GU‐rich elements in the 3′ UTR of PdCPD1 transcripts to promote their decay (Wang et al., 2023). Similarly, in A. thaliana, tandem CCCH‐type zinc finger proteins C3H14 and C3H15 instigate the decay of target mRNAs by recognizing poly(U) and AU‐rich elements (AREs) in their 3′ UTRs, thereby positively regulating secondary wall formation and anther development (Chai et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2014); furthermore, C3H15 also transcriptionally represses BZR1 and BES1 to negatively regulate cell elongation (Chai et al., 2022). Notably, within the ethylene signaling pathway, the 3′ UTR of EBF1/2 mRNA functions as a molecular relay; its unique poly(U) motif, recognized by the EIN2 protein, directs the transcript into cytoplasmic processing bodies (P‐bodies) for translational repression (Li et al., 2015). These examples collectively underscore the central role of 3′ UTRs in integrating environmental signals with developmental programs. However, whether analogous posttranscriptional regulatory mechanisms are involved in fine‐tuning hypocotyl elongation and thereby influencing plant adaptability to light environments remains unclear.

In this study, we found that under low‐light conditions, the hypocotyl of the A. thaliana Tibet ecotype (Tib) from high‐light habitat was significantly longer than that of the Sichuan ecotype (Sic) from low‐light habitat. To identify the key genetic locus regulating hypocotyl elongation in response to light intensity, designated as Light Adaptation 1 (LA1), we conducted a structural variant genome‐wide association study (SV‐GWAS) using recombinant inbred lines (RILs) derived from a cross between the Sic and Tib ecotypes (Lou et al., 2022). This study identifies IAMH2 (LA1) as the causal gene underlying natural variation in hypocotyl elongation. In the Tib ecotype, the IAMH2 3′ UTR carries an additional 269‐bp sequence that disrupts a poly(U) loop. This structural alteration disrupts mRNA decay mediated by C3H14 and C3H15, ultimately promoting hypocotyl elongation, at least in part through enhanced IAA accumulation. We thus link a noncoding structural variant to adaptive growth through posttranscriptional regulation, revealing a previously uncharacterized mechanism in the IAM pathway that elucidates how plants modulate organ growth to adapt to varying light conditions.

To determine whether C3H14 and C3H15 directly interact with the poly(U) motif within the 3′ UTR of IAMH2, we performed RNA electrophoretic mobility shift assays (RNA‐EMSAs). The results confirmed specific binding of both C3H14 and C3H15 proteins to the IAMH2 RNA probe of Col‐0/Sic (Figure 4D, E). We next assessed the functional consequence of this interaction using a dual‐luciferase reporter system. Effector plasmids expressing C3H14 or C3H15 were co‐transfected with a reporter in which the Col‐0/Sic IAMH2 3′ UTR was fused to a 35S‐driven LUC gene (Figure 4F). The IAMH2 3′ UTR alone substantially reduced basal LUC activity relative to a UTR‐less control. Co‐expression of C3H14 or C3H15 further and significantly repressed reporter expression (Figure 4G). These results demonstrate that the IAMH2 3′ UTR contains cis‐acting repressive elements and that C3H14/C3H15 act as trans‐regulators that bind directly to this region to enhance posttranscriptional repression.

To confirm this regulatory relationship in vivo, we examined IAMH2 transcript levels in the c3h14 c3h15 double mutant. IAMH2 expression was significantly elevated in the mutant compared with Col‐0 under both normal and low light (Figure 4H), demonstrating that C3H14 and C3H15 repress endogenous IAMH2 expression. To further determine whether C3H14 and C3H15 regulate hypocotyl elongation through IAMH2, we generated the iamh2 c3h14 c3h15 triple mutant. Under low light, the triple mutant exhibited hypocotyl lengths comparable to those of the iamh2 single mutant (Figure 4I, J), indicating that IAMH2 is epistatic to C3H14 and C3H15. Under normal light, hypocotyl lengths of iamh2, c3h14 c3h15, and iamh2 c3h14 c3h15 did not differ significantly from those of Col‐0 (Figure 4I, J), indicating that this regulatory module exerts its phenotypic effect specifically under low light. Collectively, these in vivo genetic data establish that C3H14 and C3H15 regulate hypocotyl elongation under low light conditions via IAMH2.

DISCUSSIONRapid hypocotyl elongation is critical for seedling emergence and the transition to autotrophic growth (Krahmer & Fankhauser, 2024). Through comparative analysis of A. thaliana ecotypes from high‐light (Tib) and low‐light (Sic) habitats, this work delineates a complete regulatory pathway from natural variation to adaptive phenotype (Figure S1; Figure 1A, B). We demonstrate that a 269‐bp structural variant in the 3′ UTR of IAMH2 underlies the divergence in hypocotyl elongation response (Figure 5H). This variant shortens the transcript and, critically, ablates a conserved poly(U)‐containing loop that serves as a cis‐regulatory motif for the RNA‐binding proteins C3H14 and C3H15. Consequently, the Tib allele escapes posttranscriptional repression, leading to enhanced IAMH2 mRNA level and driving exaggerated hypocotyl elongation under low light (Figure 5H). Although IAMH1 and IAMH2 catalyze the conversion of IAM to IAA (Gao et al., 2020), recent studies have questioned whether this pathway contributes to auxin biosynthesis under standard conditions (Moya‐Cuevas et al., 2026). IAA levels were significantly reduced in iamh2 hypocotyls under low light, whereas the reduction under normal light was not significant, and IAM accumulated in the mutant under normal light (Figure 2H). We therefore propose that the IAM pathway is not essential for basal auxin homeostasis, but is conditionally recruited under low‐light stress to provide local IAA for adaptive growth. This model reconciles the lack of a strong IAA phenotype in iamh1 iamh2 mutants under normal growth (Gao et al., 2020) with the pronounced hypocotyl divergence between ecotypes specifically under low light. Tib and Sic differed in hypocotyl length even under normal light, whereas iamh2 did not differ from Col‐0, suggesting that natural variation in IAMH2 expression can modulate growth, while complete loss of IAMH2 is buffered under normal light. Notably, Sic and Tib inhabit brighter environments than our experimental normal light, which may thus already constitute low light for these ecotypes. The c3h14 c3h15 double mutant (Col‐0 background) was kindly provided by Prof. Jing‐Shi Xue (Xue et al., 2026). MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditionsSeeds of the Sichuan ecotype (CS79063) and the Tibet ecotype (CS79062), together with the iamh2 mutant (SALK_208590C; Col‐0 background), were bulk‐propagated in a controlled growth chamber (16 h 22°C light/8 h 19°C dark, 60 ± 5% RH) to minimize maternal effects. The c3h14 c3h15 double mutant (Col‐0 background) was kindly provided by Prof. Jing‐Shi Xue (Xue et al., 2026). After surface‐sterilization (75% ethanol, 2 min; five sterile‐water rinses), seeds were sown on 0.65% agar‐solidified half‐strength Murashige & Skoog medium (Sigma, USA) containing 1% sucrose (pH 5.7). To synchronize germination, seeds were stratified in darkness at 4°C for 3 days (Col‐0 and iamh2) or 10 days (Sic and Tib) as previously described (Gao et al., 2025). Plates were then incubated for 48 h at 22°C under the same long‐day photoperiod used for bulking (16 h light/8 h dark, 160 ± 2 μmol m−2 s−1) before being transferred to experimental light conditions. Seedlings were subsequently cultured for 7 days under the same long‐day photoperiod (16 h light/8 h dark) at either 160 ± 2 μmol m−2 s−1 (normal light) or 7 ± 0.2 μmol m−2 s−1 (low light). Hypocotyl length was measured from the root–hypocotyl junction to the cotyledon base using ImageJ (v1.54). At least 10 seedlings per genotype and light condition were analyzed in each of three independent biological replicates.

Genome‐wide association analysisHypocotyl lengths were measured by 146 recombinant inbred lines (RILs) under standardized low‐light conditions, with a minimum of 10 seedlings evaluated per line (Data S4). SV‐GWAS was conducted using the EasyOmics platform (Han et al., 2025), which implements GCTA (Yang et al., 2011) to perform association analysis with a linear mixed model (LMM) that accounts for population structure and relatedness. Phenotypic data from the RILs were integrated with genome‐wide structural variant calls in VCF format. A total of 21 341 high‐quality structural variants (minor allele frequency ≥0.05, missing rate ≤0.3, depth ≥2) were retained for analysis.
