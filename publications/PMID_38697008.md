More than 1200 genes have been shown in the database to be expressed 
predominantly in the mouse testes. Advances in genome editing technologies such 
as the CRISPR/Cas9 system have made it possible to create genetically engineered 
mice more rapidly and efficiently than with conventional methods, which can be 
utilized to screen genes essential for male fertility by knocking out 
testis-enriched genes. Finding such genes related to male fertility would not 
only help us understand the etiology of human infertility but also lead to the 
development of male contraceptives. In this study, we generated knockout mice 
for 12 genes (Acrv1, Adgrf3, Atp8b5, Cfap90, Cfap276, Fbxw5, Gm17266, Lrrd1, 
Mroh7, Nemp1, Spata45, and Trim36) that are expressed predominantly in the 
testis and examined the appearance and histological morphology of testes, sperm 
motility, and male fertility. Mating tests revealed that none of these genes is 
essential for male fertility at least individually. Notably, knockout mice for 
Gm17266 showed smaller testis size than the wild-type but did not exhibit 
reduced male fertility. Since 12 genes were not individually essential for male 
fertilization, it is unlikely that these genes could be the cause of infertility 
or contraceptive targets. It is better to focus on other essential genes because 
complementary genes to these 12 genes may exist.

Introduction

Infertility, which is defined as when a couple is unable to conceive after more than one year of regular and unprotected intercourse, is a serious health problem affecting one in every six couples worldwide (Li et al., 2022; Vander Borght and Wyns, 2018). Statistical study provides evidence that the male factor accounts for nearly half of all infertility cases (Krausz, 2011). To clarify the cause of this, it is important to understand how fertile spermatozoa form, a process called spermatogenesis. Spermatogenesis is a complicated cellular differentiation process that is tightly regulated to generate spermatozoa in the seminiferous tubules of the testis. Any defects in this process could result in male infertility.

Genetically engineered mice are a useful experimental tool in identifying and characterizing genes that contribute to spermatogenesis and male fertility. In particular, the generation and analysis of knockout (KO) mice can provide a clear answer as to whether the gene is essential for male fertility. In addition, the detailed analyses of the causative gene using KO mice may allow us to discover its molecular function. However, it was costly and time-consuming to generate KO mice with the conventional methods that used homologous recombination in embryonic stem cells and generated chimeric mice. Recently, the situation has changed because it has become possible to generate KO mice in a shorter time with less cost using the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9) system (Abbasi et al., 2017).

Transcriptome analysis has shown that 77% (n = 15481) of all human proteins (n = 20162) are expressed in the testis, with 1992 of these genes showing elevated expression in the testis compared to other tissue types (https://www.proteinatlas.org/humanproteome/tissue/testis. Accessed December 12, 2023). Further, our analyses of RNA-seq datasets show that 1262 genes are expressed predominantly in the mouse testes (Oura et al., 2022; Robertson et al., 2020). Among these genes, we have already reported that more than 100 genes are not required for male fertility in mice despite being highly expressed in the testes (Holcomb et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2019; Miyata et al., 2016; Oyama et al., 2021; Park et al., 2020; Robertson et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2020). In this study, KO mice were newly generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system to individually disrupt 12 genes, all of which were found not to be required for male fertility.

3.Results3.1.In silico validation of testis-enriched expressions of candidate genesIt has been shown in the database that more than 1200 genes are expressed predominantly in the mouse testes (Oura et al., 2022). Among them, we have generated more than 200 line genes to date. In this study, we used multiple bioinformatic searches, including the RNA profiling data generated by the Mouse ENCODE project (https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene. Accessed November 16, 2022), to confirm 12 genes predominantly or specifically expressing in mouse testis (Supplementary Fig. S1). These candidate mouse genes included 7 genes, such as Acrosomal vesicle protein 1 (Acrv1), Adhesion G protein-coupled receptor F3 (Adgrf3), ATPase class I type 8B member 5 (Atp8b5), Cilia- and flagella-associated protein 276 (Cfap276), F-box and WD repeat domain containing 5 (Fbxw5), Nuclear envelope integral membrane protein 1 (Nemp1), and Tripartite motif containing 36 (Trim36), for which some biological information has been reported, and 5 unreported genes, such as Cilia and flagella associated protein 90 (Cfap90), Gm17266, Leucine-rich repeats and death domain containing 1 (Lrrd1), Maestro heat-like repeat family member 7 (Mroh7), and Spermatogenesis associated 45 (Spata45), regardless of whether they are involved in male reproduction. The isPCR using the MRGDv2 database revealed that all candidate genes, except for Gm17266, are highly expressed in mouse testis and round spermatids (Supplementary Fig. S2A). The value of Gm17266 was not found in mouse RNA-seq datasets. Notably, Fbxw5 and Nemp1 were markedly enriched in Sertoli cells and leptotene spermatocytes, respectively, although these genes were expressed in other organs as well. All of these candidate genes, except for Atp8b5, were conserved in human (Supplementary Fig. S3). Interestingly, unlike in mouse, the expressions of C18orf63 (a human homolog of Gm17266) and NEMP1 were not or faintly found in human spermatids, whereas they were enriched in human spermatocytes and/or spermatogonia (Supplementary Fig. S2B). S4 and S5). All mutant alleles were verified by gPCR with specific primer sets and Sanger sequencing. We here chose four genes, such as Nemp1, Spata45, Fbxw5, and Gm17266, and then showed their data on in vivo functional analyses.NEMP1 is an evolutionally conserved gene encoding an inner nuclear membrane protein with a RanGTP-interacting domain and regulates nuclear shape (Tsatskis et al., 2020). Nemp1-deficient mice targeting exon 3, were previously reported to exhibit abnormal erythropoiesis, splenomegaly, and female infertility (Tsatskis et al., 2020). We disrupted most of the Nemp1 coding region by electroporation into pronuclear eggs with two gRNAs targeted to exons 1 and 9 (Fig. 1A). The genotypes and mutation site of generated KO mice were confirmed by gPCR and sequencing, respectively (Fig. 1B). Homozygous and heterozygous KO mice were viable, with no significant differences in appearance between them. Testicular morphology, size, and weight of KO mice were compared to those of wild-type (WT), but no differences were found between them (Fig. 1C and 1D). Histological analysis of testes showed no differences between WT and KO mice (Fig. 1E). Observation using a light microscope also showed no abnormal morphology of spermatozoa extracted from cauda epididymides of KO mice (Fig. 1F). In the motility assay of spermatozoa using the computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) system, the percentage of motile spermatozoa of KO mice was comparable to that of WT mice (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Table S3).SPATA45 has been reported that the expression was significantly associated with shorter survival of T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia patients (Peng et al., 2022), but there are no reports of the biological function. Therefore, Spata45 was disrupted with two gRNAs targeted as shown in Fig. 2A. The genotypes and the mutation site of KO mice were confirmed by gPCR and sequencing, respectively (Fig. 2B). Homozygous and heterozygous KO mice were viable, with no significant differences in appearance between them. Testicular morphology, size, weight, and histological features of KO mice were comparable to those of WT (Fig. 2C–E). We found no significant differences between WT and KO male mice in sperm morphology and the percentage of motile spermatozoa (Fig. 2F, 2G, and Supplementary Table S3).FBXW5, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, has been reported to be highly expressed in various human cancers, including prostate adenocarcinoma, and functions as an oncogene to ubiquitinate tumor suppressors, such as DLC1 and LATS1 (Kim et al., 2013; Lan et al., 2020; Yao et al., 2022). The expression of FBXW5 is significantly downregulated in a patient group classified as maturation arrest in nonobstructive azoospermia, compared with the control (Fang et al., 2020). However, the roles of FBXW5 in male fertility remain unclear. To clarify this, mouse Fbxw5 was disrupted with two gRNAs targeted as shown in Fig. 3A. The genotypes and the mutation site of KO mice were confirmed by gPCR and sequencing, respectively (Fig. 3B). Homozygous and heterozygous KO mice were viable. Testicular morphology, size, weight, and histological features of KO mice were comparable to those of WT (Fig. 3C–E). We also found no significant differences between WT and KO male mice in sperm morphology and the percentage of motile spermatozoa (Fig. 3F, 3G, and Supplementary Table S3).Gm17266 is an uncharacterized gene and has no reports of its biological functions, thus was disrupted with two gRNAs targeted as shown in Fig. 4A. The genotypes and the mutation site of KO mice were confirmed by gPCR and sequencing, respectively (Fig. 4B). Homozygous and heterozygous KO mice were viable, with no significant differences in appearance between them. Interestingly, testes derived from homozygous KO mice were significantly smaller in size and lower in weight than those of WT control mice (Fig. 4C and 4D), but the mean body weight was comparable between two groups (WT, 27.17 ± 0.93 g; KO, 31.45 ±16.79 g; P = 0.169). Of note, we did not find any apparent differences between WT and KO epididymis. To compare the gene expression profiles in testes between homozygous KO and control WT mice, we conducted RNA-Seq analysis with these testis samples. The results were shown as a scatter plot under conditions of minimum fold change of 2 (Supplementary Fig. S6A). These samples were prepared in duplicate. We identified 60 genes (19 up-regulated and 41 downregulated genes) differentially expressed between the WT and KO samples. The data was displayed as a heat map image (Supplementary Fig. S6B). The analysis revealed a decrease in a long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) named SRY (sex determining region Y)-box 5, opposite strand 3 (Sox5os3), and an increase in a subfamily of Kallikrein 1 (Klk1), a subgroup of serine proteases involved in blood pressure regulation, in KO mice (Supplementary Fig. S6C and S6D). However, the differences in these gene expression levels were not markedly large between the WT and KO samples (log2 fold change of Sox5os3 is −3.69 and that of Klk1 family is 2.77 to 3.31). The upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were significantly enriched for gene ontology (GO) categories related to the digestion and activation of proteins, such as zymogen activation, proteolysis, protein processing, and protein maturation (Supplementary Fig. S6E). In contrast, no significant differences between WT and KO male mice were found in histological features of testes, sperm morphology, and the percentage of motile spermatozoa (Fig. 4E–4G, and Supplementary Table S3).3.3.All 12 candidate genes are not essential for male fertility in miceTo determine whether the 12 genes of interest are required for male fertility, we performed fertility tests using KO mice for these genes (Table 1). We found that the average number of pups sired by each KO male was 7 to 10 pups per litter during the mating period, comparable to the WT control (about 9 pups per litter), suggesting that there was no significant difference between the fecundity of KO and WT males. Therefore, these results suggest that all 12 of these genes are not essential for male fertility, at least individually.

Discussion and conclusion

In this study, 12 genes of interest were found to be dispensable for male mouse reproduction, at least individually, by generating KO mice with the CRIPR/Cas9 system. Among 12 genes, some genes or their homologs have some interesting information about their biological functions as follows.

It has been reported that two genes of Nemp exist in mice: Nemp1 is highly expressed in oocytes, whereas Nemp2 is ubiquitously expressed in various tissues. It has also been shown by mating tests that Nemp1 KO females were infertile or severely subfertile, although the males were fertile (Tsatskis et al., 2020), consistent with our results. In contrast, the males or females with homozygous mutations of dNemp, which is a single homologous gene in fruit flies, were reported to be infertile (Tsatskis et al., 2020). A mutant with double disruption of zNemp1 and zNemp2, which are two kinds of homologous genes in zebrafish, was also shown to be infertile in both males and females. Therefore, the double KO male mice of Nemp1 and Nemp2 may exhibit infertile or severely subfertile phenotypes.

Acrv1 is an evolutionarily conserved gene encoding the sperm protein SP-10, which abundantly exists in the acrosomal matrix and the inner acrosomal membrane in mammals (Reddi, 2023). In vitro fertilization assay showed that an anti-human SP-10 antibody prevented the binding of human spermatozoa to hamster oolemma but not to human zona pellucida (Hamatani et al., 2000). Furthermore, the immunization with anti-human SP-10 antibody in male rodents did not induce the arrest of spermiogenesis (Sehgal et al., 1996).

Atp8b5, also known as FetA (flippase expressed in testis splicing form A), is a gene coding a member of the P4-ATPase family with phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine flippase activity, which is predicted to be involved in phospholipid translocation. Mouse ATP8B5 localizes at the ridge of the acrosome in spermatozoa (Xu et al., 2009), whereas human ATP8B5 seems to be a pseudogene (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/158381. Accessed September 29, 2023). Thus, the loss of function of Atp8b5 in the KO mice might be complemented by expression of intact another P4-ATPase, such as Atp8b3 (Wang et al., 2004).

Cfap276 is an evolutionally conserved gene encoding a Ca2+ regulator. The transcriptome analysis has previously shown that the expression of a human homolog in the asthenozoospermic infertile group was downregulated compared to that in the normozoospermic infertile group (Salehi et al., 2021).

Trim36, also known as Haprin, is a gene coding an E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase with RING finger and U-box motifs, which is involved in the acrosome reaction of mouse spermatozoa (Kitamura et al., 2003). However, Haprin KO male mice generated by a conventional method exhibited normal fertility in vivo, consistent with our study (Aoki et al., 2020).

Cfap90 and Gm17266 are uncharacterized genes. RNA-Seq analysis with testis samples of Gm17266 KO mice revealed that the upregulated DEGs, including Klk1b family genes, were also enriched for GO categories for blood pressure and blood circulation (Supplementary Fig. S6). Notably, Klk1b21, Klk1b24, and Klk1b27 have been reported to be expressed in mouse Leydig cells, which produce testosterone (Lawrence et al., 2010). These genes are also downregulated under conditions of a reduced androgen or androgen receptor. Among them, Klk1b21 seems to be upregulated by the treatment of cultured mouse Leydig cells with testosterone. Therefore, it suggests that the upregulation of Klk1b family genes might result from a secondary effect due to increased concentration of androgen or testosterone associated with low testicular volume in Gm17266 KO mice. However, even if so, since this event appears to be irrelevant to male fertility, we did not proceed with further analyses on this gene in this study.

Taken together, our results revealed that all 12 genes abundant in mouse testes and/or round spermatids are unnecessary for male fertility in mice by generating knockout mice for each gene. These findings will reduce the amount of effort and time spent by other researchers focusing on genes essential for male fertility and will help to efficiently promote research on male reproduction.
