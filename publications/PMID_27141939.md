Abstract

Glucan-branching enzyme plays an essential role in the formation of branched polysaccharides, glycogen, and amylopectin. Only one type of branching enzyme, belonging to glycoside hydrolase family 13 (GH13), is found in eukaryotes, while two types of branching enzymes (GH13 and GH57) occur in prokaryotes (Bacteria and Archaea). Both of these types are the members of protein families containing the diverse specificities of amylolytic glycoside hydrolases. Although similarities are found in the catalytic mechanism between the two types of branching enzyme, they are highly distinct from each other in terms of amino acid sequence and tertiary structure. Branching enzymes are found in 29 out of 30 bacterial phyla and 1 out of 5 archaeal phyla, often along with glycogen synthase, suggesting the existence of α-glucan production and storage in a wide range of prokaryotes. Enormous variability is observed as to which type and how many copies of branching enzyme are present depending on the phylum and, in some cases, even among species of the same genus. Such a variation may have occurred through lateral transfer, duplication, and/or differential loss of genes coding for branching enzyme during the evolution of prokaryotes.

Keywords: Alpha-glucan, Branching enzyme, Bacteria, Archaea, Glycoside hydrolase, Glycosyltransferase, Carbohydrate-active enzymes

Introduction

Glucan-branching enzyme (abbreviated as BE; EC 2.4.1.18) catalyzes the formation of α-1,6-bonds in the branched polysaccharides, glycogen in animals and microorganisms, or amylopectin (the major component of starch) in plants [ 1 , 2 ]. The frequency and position of the branch points are important determinants for the structure and properties of these reserve carbohydrates, and BE plays a pivotal role in specifying these characteristics [ 3 , 4 ]. The BE reaction proceeds in two steps: first, a preexisting α-1,4-glucan chain is cleaved and the non-reducing portion of the donor chain is covalently attached to carboxyl group of the catalytic residue (Asp in GH13 BE or Glu in GH57 BE; see below) at the active site of the enzyme [ 5 , 6 ]. The glucan moiety is then transferred to the C-6 hydroxyl group of the same or another glucan chain (the acceptor chain) [ 4 ]. Through this double displacement mechanism, the α-configuration of anomeric carbon is retained, resulting in the formation of α-1,6-linkage. If a water molecule instead of the C-6 hydroxyl group of a sugar is involved in the latter step of the reaction, the enzyme serves as a hydrolase rather than a transferase. Therefore, BE is mechanistically similar to amylolytic enzymes. BEs are actually homologous to α-amylases and grouped into glycoside hydrolase (GH) families (although only a limited number of BEs show hydrolytic activity).

Notable distinction has been recognized concerning the distribution of BE in eukaryotes and prokaryotes: BEs in animals, fungi, and plants show higher sequence similar to each other than to BEs in bacteria [ 7 , 8 ]. This situation is in sharp contrast to some of the other enzymes responsible for starch metabolism in plants, including ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (AGPase), starch synthase (SS), and debranching enzyme (DBE). The latter enzymes in plants were acquired from bacteria during endosymbiosis that gave rise to plastids, and do not have homologs in animals or fungi (in the case of AGPase and DBE), or they show much higher similarities to those in bacterial rather than animal/fungal counterparts (SS) [ 8 ]. In contrast to rather uniform distribution of one type of BE in eukaryotic linages, two distinct types of BE, one being moderately related to the eukaryotic counterpart, and the other showing little sequence similarity to eukaryotic BEs, have been found across two Domains of prokaryotes, Bacteria and Archaea.

According to “List of Prokaryotic Names with Standing in Nomenclature” ( http://www.bacterio.net ), the Domains Bacteria and Archaea are divided into 30 and 5 phyla (or divisions), respectively, and most of these phyla have representative organisms with completed genomes. In this review, current knowledge of structural and functional features of BEs, and distribution of BEs in bacterial and archaeal phyla are described.

Occurrence of two distinct types of glucan-branching enzyme

The primary structure of BE was first elucidated in Escherichia coli [ 9 ]. It was noted that the deduced amino acid sequence of BE showed similarity to those of amylolytic enzymes [ 10 – 12 ]. The amylolytic enzymes include α-amylase (EC 3.2.1.1), pullulanase (EC 3.2.1.41), isoamylase (EC 3.2.1.68), and cyclomaltodextrin glucanotransferase (EC 2.4.1.19), in addition to BE, thus enzymes catalyzing all four types of reaction; transfer and hydrolysis of α-1,4- and α-1,6-linkages [ 13 ]. They shared some conserved sequence regions (CSRs), thus collectively constituting a protein family, which was designated as α-amylase family [ 13 , 14 ]. When CAZy database, a classification system of carbohydrate-active enzymes based on amino acid sequence similarities ( http://www.cazy.org/ ) was founded, the α-amylase family proteins were classified into GH family 13 [ 15 ]. As the number of members increased considerably, the GH13 family was subdivided into 35 subfamilies [ 16 ] (currently expanded to 40 subfamilies). Until now, BEs have been classified into only two subfamilies, GH13_8 and GH13_9, that include BEs in Eukaryotes and Bacteria, respectively (although with exceptions as described below).

Apart from the identification and expansion of GH13 α-amylase family, α-amylases with distinct sequences were described in a thermophilic bacterium, Dictyoglomus thermophilum [ 17 ] and a thermophilic archaeon Pyrococcus furiosus [ 18 ]. Both of these enzymes, currently considered to be 4-α-glucanotransferase (EC 2.4.1.25) [ 19 , 20 ], were classified into the GH57 family [ 21 ]. Together with α-amylase and 4-α-glucanotransferase, GH57 family also contains enzymes with other catalytic specificities, amylopullulanase (EC 3.2.1.41), and even α-galactosidase (EC 3.2.1.22) [ 22 ]. Finally, a GH57 protein with unknown function in a thermophilic archaeon, Thermococcus kodakaraensis , was identified as BE [ 23 ]. Homologs of T. kodakaraensis GH57-type BE were found to occur in several linages of Bacteria (Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Cyanobacteria) [ 23 ]. Two types of BE (belonging to GH13 and GH57) may have derived from distinct origins and have undergone convergent evolution to show the common catalytic function along with the related enzymes involved in modification, and degradation of α-glucans.

Primary and tertiary structures of glucan-branching enzymes

Domain composition and sequence motifs of BEs are schematically represented in Fig. 1 . Both GH13 and GH57 BEs are multidomain proteins. GH13 BEs consist of, from N to C termini, carbohydrate-binding module (CBM) 48, central catalytic domain referred to as domain A, and domain C. In some GH13_9 BEs, CBM48 is further preceded by domain N, which is generally absent from GH13_8 BEs. Indeed, GH13_9 BEs are divided into group 1 and group 2, depending on the presence or absence, respectively, of domain N [ 24 ] (Fig. 3 ). Domain N, CBM48, and domain C consist of approximately 100 amino acid residues, and all these domains adopt a β-sandwich fold [ 25 , 26 ]. CBM48 is also found in related protein of GH13 (referred to as pullulanase subfamily) including pullulanase (GH13_12, GH13_13, and GH13_14), isoamylase (GH13_11), and maltooligosyltrehalose trehalohydrolase (EC 3.2.1.141, GH13_10) [ 27 ]. The central domain A is composed of a (β/α) 8 barrel although α5 is not obvious between β5 and β6 (Fig. 1 ) [ 25 ]. Four conserved sequence regions (CSRs) I to IV are located at β strands (β3, β4, β5, and β7, respectively) and their succeeding loops [ 28 ] (Figs. 1 , 2 ). The two essential residues at the active site, the nucleophile aspartate and the acid/base glutamic acid are located in CSRs II and III, respectively [ 5 , 14 , 29 , 30 ]. Among other conserved residues, another aspartate is located in CSR IV, and serves to stabilize the reaction intermediate [ 5 ]. Additional CSRs V, VI, and VII have been proposed [ 31 ]. The CSRs VI and VII correspond to β strands (β2 and β8, respectively) and the following loops, whereas the CSR V is located at a loop facing the end of CSR I, constituting a wall of the active site cleft [ 26 ].

GH57 BEs are composed of a (β/α) 7 barrel domain (also referred to as domain A), an α-helical domain (domain B) inserted between β2 and α5 of domain A, and another α-helical domain (domain C) following domain A [ 6 ] (Fig. 1 ). Five CSRs (1 through 5) are distributed in domain A and domain C [ 32 ]. When GH57 BE of T. kodakaraensis was initially characterized, 6 CSRs (A through F) were proposed [ 23 ]. Among them, CSRs A, B, C, E, and F corresponded to CSRs 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5, respectively [ 32 ], while CSR D was found specifically in BE, but not in the other enzymes of GH57 family. The catalytic residues glutamate (nucleophile) and aspartic acid (acid/base) are located in CSRs 3 and 4, respectively [ 6 ]. Extra loop regions were found between β7 and β9 in a limited number of BEs including those from T. kodakaraensis [ 33 ] and Thermus thermophilus [ 6 ] (Fig. 4 ). Some members of GH57 BEs have extra sequence at their C termini: (1) Repeated helix–hairpin–helix (HhH) motif was reported in GH57 BE from T. kodakaraensis [ 23 ]. The (HhH) 2 sequence also occurs in other GH57 BEs (if not all) from Thermococcales archaea. (2) Some GH57 BEs from Firmicutes (Bacilli and Clostridia) are fused to glycosyltransferase (GT) family 4 sequence at the C terminus. GT4 is the second largest GT family in the CAZy database (next to GT2) and contains 48,000 members, represented by nearly 20 different enzyme specificities. In terms of tertiary structure, proteins in GT4 family adopt the GT-B fold, one of the two major folds in glycosyltransferase families, along with other GT families, GT3, GT5, and GT35 [ 34 , 35 ]. The GT-B fold proteins consist of two lobes of Rossmann fold [ 36 ] indicated as N- and C-domains in Fig. 1 c (the last segment of the protein is folded as a part of the N-domain) [ 37 ]. A glutamate, putative catalytic residue of GT-B fold enzymes is conserved, but enzymatic specificity conferred by the GT4 domain of this fusion protein remains to be elucidated. As glycogen synthases (GSs) belonging to GT4 family have been identified in some bacteria (Table 1 ), it is tempting to speculate that the GH57-GT4 protein has BE and GS activities responsible for glycogen synthesis. However, the organisms containing this fusion protein also have GS independently (Table 1 ).

Overall architecture and geometry around the active site of BEs are shown in Fig. 2 . Crystal structure of GH13_9 BE has been elucidated for E. coli [ 25 , 38 ] and Mycobacterium tuberculosis [ 26 ]. Based on these structures, GH13-type BE exhibits an elongated structure, as CBM48, domain A, and domain C are arranged linearly along a longitudinal axis of the protein. The structure and the arrangement of domain N in the E. coli BE have not been solved, while in M. tuberculosis BE, domain N was located at the far end of the enzyme preceding CBM48, and the whole protein adopts a highly elongated shape [ 26 ]. The catalytic residues aspartate and glutamic acid (indicated as D405 and E458, respectively, in Fig. 2 b) are located in adjacent loops (immediately following β4 and β5 strands) [ 25 , 26 ]. Binding of oligosaccharides was observed at several sites in the crystal structure of E. coli BE [ 38 ]. These binding sites can be regarded as surface/secondary binding sites (SBSs) [ 39 ], in which oligosaccharides are bound at a certain distance of the active site. SBSs have been described for a wide range of glycoside hydrolases [ 39 ], and are particularly prominent in GH13 [ 40 ]. Crystal structures have also been reported for BEs from rice (one of three isoforms, BEI) [ 41 , 42 ] and human [ 43 ]. The structure of these eukaryotic BEs, belonging to GH13_8 subfamily, is similar to those of bacterial counterparts, except for the presence of α-helical segment at the N terminus instead of domain N, and the arrangement of two loops (β3-α3, and β4-α4) in domain A [ 41 , 43 ].

Crystal structures of GH57 BE were elucidated for the enzymes from Thermococcus kodakaraensis [ 33 ] and Thermus thermophilus [ 6 ]. In these structures, two α-helical domains (domains B and C) are folded together with the (β/α) 7 barrel domain (domain A), so that the whole protein exhibits a compact, triangular shape. The central β strands constitute an incomplete barrel structure by two distorted sheets, arranged as β10–β1–β2–β3 and β4–β7–β9, with a gap between β9 and β10 [ 6 , 33 ]. The catalytic residues glutamate and aspartic acid (indicated as E183 and D354, respectively, in Fig. 2 d) are from loops following β4 and β10 strands, respectively, and are located at the opposite sides of the active site pocket [ 6 , 33 ] (Fig. 2 ). In GH57 BE, subsites were predicted based on a binding of modeled maltotriose [ 6 ], or on the positions of glucose and glycerol located around the active site [ 33 ].

In spite of a number of studies as described above, the binding of oligosaccharides at the active site cleft has not been observed in any of the BE crystal structures. The relationship between tertiary structure of the enzyme and substrate specificity, therefore, remains unsolved.

The catalytically critical residues are located on the concave surface of the enzyme. The geometry around the catalytic residues is compared between GH13 and GH57 BEs by calculating the “width” and “depth” of the active site. As shown in Fig. 2 e and f, the active site cleft is wider and shallower in GH13 BEs than in GH57 BEs. The structural distinction around the active site may significantly affect the specificity of the enzyme. However, comparative biochemical characterization of BE across different families has not been extensively performed [ 6 ].

Catalytic specificity of branching enzymes

Determination of the catalytic property of BE is essential to understand the mechanism, by which the molecular structure of the reserve polysaccharide is formed. However, the enzymatic characterization of BE is not very simple, as the action of BE is tightly coupled with the elongation of the glucan chain by glycogen/starch synthase. In the in vitro assays, artificial substrates (e.g. commercial amylose and amylopectin) are generally used, which may or may not well reproduce the actual situation in vivo. Nevertheless, a number of studies have been carried out to characterize the catalytic specificity of BEs, mostly belonging to GH13 family (Fig. 3 ). Comparative characterization of bacterial and eukaryotic BEs has been reported [ 44 , 45 ].

In many instances, BE specifically shows α-1,6-transferase activity (as opposed to α-1,4-transferase or α-1,4-hydrolase activities). Hydrolase activity is thus below the detection limit for BEs from Arthrobacter globiformis [ 46 ], Butyrivibrio fibrisolvens [ 47 ], Deinococcus geothermalis and D. radiodurans [ 48 ], Anaerobranca gottschalkii [ 49 ], Vibrio vulnificus [ 50 ]. In contrast, BE from Streptococcus mutans showed α-1,4-hydrolytic activity in addition to α-1,6-branching activity [ 51 ]. A GH57 BE in Thermus thermophilus showed hydrolase activity, which was 4 % of the total activity (measured as the amount of reducing ends before and after isoamylase treatment of the reaction product, respectively) [ 6 ]. Besides α-1,6-transferase activity, thermostable BEs from Rhodothermus marinus [ 52 ] and R. obamensis [ 53 ] showed activity of α-1,4-transferase [ 52 , 53 ] and α-1,4-hydrolase [ 52 ].

Concerning the substrate specificity, BEs from A. globiformis [ 46 ], B. fibrisolvens [ 47 ], and S. mutans [ 51 ] showed activities both on amylose and amylopectin. The BE activity of D. geothermalis was much higher on amylose than amylopectin, while the activity of D. radiodurans was higher on amylopectin than amylose [ 48 ]. R. obamensis BE was six times more active on amylose than amylopectin, whereas it showed little activity on glycogen [ 53 ]. Among three isoforms of GH13_9 BE in Cyanothece sp. ATCC 51142, two isoforms (BE1 and BE2) showed higher activity on amylose than amylopectin, while the activity of BE3 was much lower but comparable on amylose and amylopectin [ 54 ]. The activity of V. vulnificus BE was ten times higher with amylopectin than amylose [ 50 ]. E. coli BE showed higher activity on amylose than amylopectin [ 55 ], which was opposed by a different study reporting that E. coli BE had higher activity on amylopectin than amylose [ 50 ]. R. marinus BE showed α-1,6-branching, α-1,4-disproportionating, and α-1,4-hydrolytic activities on various substrates including amylose, starch, pullulan, and maltooligosaccharides as short as maltotriose (the mode of catalysis differed depending on the substrate) [ 52 ]. A GH57 BE in T. thermophilus was active on amylose, showed only hydrolytic activity (and no transferase activity) on amylopectin, and was virtually inactive on glycogen [ 6 ].

The preferred lengths of glucan chains transferred by BE are also variable depending on the source organism. V. vulnificus BE transferred very short chains of degree of polymerization (DP) 3–5, comprising greater than 20 % of all chains transferred [ 50 ]. R. obamensis BE also transferred short glucan chains of DP 3–8 [ 51 ]. D. geothermalis and D. radiodurans BEs transferred glucan chains of DP 4–17 (predominantly DP 6–7) [ 48 ]. B. fibrisolvens BE gave rise to branches of DP 5–10 (with a maximum peak at DP 7) [ 47 ]. S. mutans BE primarily transferred oligosaccharides of DP 6–7 [ 51 ]. R. marinus BE produced branched glucan from amylose, with the most abundant chains of DP 12 [ 52 ]. Incubation of amylose with A. gottschalkii BE resulted in the production of glucan chains estimated to be ranging from DP 6 to 60 [ 49 ]. Two isoforms of BE (BE1 and BE2) in Cyanobacterium sp. NBRC 102756 and Cyanothece sp. ATCC 51142 produced glucans of DP 6–7 and DP 10 (as a major and minor peak, respectively), while BE3 produced short (DP 5–11) as well as long (DP 30–35) glucan chains [ 54 , 56 ]. BEs of Aquifex [ 48 , 57 , 58 ] and E. coli [ 44 , 45 , 48 , 50 ] primarily transferred much longer chains of DP 10–14. As the GH57 enzymes, BE in Thermococcus kodakaraensis produced glucans of DP 5–30 with two local maxima at DP 6 and DP 11 [ 23 ], whereas BE in Thermus transferred glucan chains of DP 4–16 with a clear preference at DP 6 [ 6 ]. Based on the catalytic property, BEs from various bacteria and eukaryotes were classified into three types, corresponding to three BE isoforms of rice: BEI producing short (DP 6–15) and long (DP 26–39) glucan chains, BEIIa predominantly producing short chains (DP 6 and 7) as well as longer chains (DP 8–15), and BEIIb exclusively producing short chains (DP 6 and 7) [ 44 , 45 , 54 , 56 , 59 ]. BE isoforms of rice hardly produce glucan chains shorter than DP 6 [ 59 ], and some bacterial enzymes (e.g. V. vulnificus BE that transfers very short chains with DP ≤5 at high rates [ 50 ]) apparently deviate from the classification scheme.

Other aspects on the catalytic specificity of BEs have been demonstrated using elaborate experimental techniques, i.e., treatment of substrate or product polysaccharides by exoglycolytic digestion using phosphorylase or β-amylase. (1) BEs from E. coli [ 44 , 45 ], Synechococcus elongatus [ 44 , 45 , 56 ], and three isoforms of GH13_9 BE from Cyanobacterium sp. NBRC 102756 [ 56 ] showed activity on phosphorylase-limit dextrin, which uniformly had short branches with four glucose residues from the branch points. The result suggested that these BEs transferred glucan chains bearing branch(es), provided that these enzymes could not attack short linear chains of DP ≤4. This property was also observed for BEI isoform of rice, but not for the other eukaryotic BEs examined [ 44 , 45 ]. (2) Treatment of reaction products of BEs from E. coli [ 45 ] or S. elongatus [ 45 , 60 ] by phosphorylase [ 45 ] or β-amylase [ 60 ] for analysis of chain length profile indicated that these enzymes formed the α-1,6-linkages preferentially at the second or third glucose residue from the reducing end of the acceptor glucan chain. In contrast, BEs from plants (including green or red algae), human, and yeast, all belonging to GH13_8, formed branch points with lower specificities in terms of the distance from the reducing end [ 45 , 60 ].

BEs are multidomain proteins, and roles of non-catalytic domains in determining reaction specificity have also been investigated. Truncation at the N terminus of E. coli BE by 112 amino acid residues (corresponding to the removal of domain N) resulted in the preference for transfer of longer glucan chains (from DP 8–14 to 15–20) [ 61 ]. Exchange of domains derived from BEs in D. geothermalis and D. radiodurans showed that CBM48, but not domain C influenced substrate specificity (preference on amylose or amylopectin) and branching pattern (preferred lengths of glucans transferred) [ 48 ]. Domain N of Vibrio vulnificus BE was shown to determine the preference toward amylose over amylopectin, and transfer of very short chains [ 50 ]. As a comparison, experiments with chimeric proteins of maize BEI and BEIIb (both belonging to GH13_8) showed that C-terminal portion of these BEs (a part of domain A after CSR IV and domain C) determined the substrate preference (amylose or amylopectin), whereas the central portion and/or N terminus (CBM48 and a part of domain A before CSR I) influenced the length of the chains transferred [ 62 ].

So far, no obvious correlation has been found between the primary structure and catalytic specificity of BE [ 45 ]. It may be possible that the enzyme specificity of BEs is substantially altered by slight changes in the geometry around the active site (e.g. presence of a loop, or a specific amino acid side chain contributing to form the substrate binding site) rather than the entire amino acid sequence of the protein.

Distribution of glucan-branching enzymes in Bacteria and Archaea

Overview

As of October 2015, the GH13 family contains 28,024 members, of which 2994, accounting for more than 10 % of the whole GH13 family, are GH13_9 BEs in Bacteria. Compared to the large number in Bacteria, only three each of the GH13_9 BEs are found in Archaea (in the phylum Euryarchaeota) and Eukaryotes (apicomplexan parasites). While 488 members of GH13_8 BE are in Eukaryotes, mostly represented by animals, fungi, and plants, 103 members of GH13_8 BE (dubbed hereafter as “eukaryotic type BE”) are also found in a limited number of bacterial taxa (in the phyla Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, δ-Proteobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia). GH57 family, currently consisting of 1,335 members, is exclusive to prokaryotes, and includes 410 and 21 BEs in Bacteria and Archaea, respectively. It should be emphasized here that most of these BEs (without triangles in Figs. 3 and 4 ) remain to be characterized. Particularly, only three members of GH57 BE and none of GH13_8 BE (from prokaryotes) have been experimentally investigated.

Distribution of GH13_9 and GH57 BEs in prokaryotes is summarized in Table 1 . BEs are found in many prokaryotic phyla, but their distribution (as to which type and how many copies are present) is extremely variable within and between phyla. Such variability is observed even between species in the same genus (Table 1 ). A phylogenetic tree based on the amino acid sequences of GH13_9 BEs (Fig. 3 ) shows that the primary structure of BEs does not necessarily reflect taxonomy. In some cases, BEs from a single organism or from organisms in the same taxa are located in distant branches of the tree, and in other cases, BEs from organisms in distantly related taxa are found close to each other (e.g. Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria). These observations suggest that the evolution of BE involves ancient gene duplication and diversification, lateral gene transfer (LGT), and/or differential gene loss.

Distribution of BE in individual phyla

In Actinobacteria (Gram-positive bacteria with high G + C content), Corynebacterium glutamicum [ 63 ] and Frankia alni have one GH13_9 BE. Species in the genus Streptomyces have two BEs belonging to GH13_9. In Streptomyces coelicolor A3(2), two BEs (GlgBI and GlgBII) are differentially expressed in distinct cell types (in the boundary of substrate and aerial mycelia, and aerial hyphae, respectively), and the expression of the latter was controlled by WhiG (a sigma factor σ whiG responsible for the developmental regulation of sporulation) [ 64 , 65 ]. The above Actinobacteria do not have any member of GH57 family. In contrast, Frankia sp. EuI1c and Mycobacterium tuberculosis [ 66 , 67 ] have one each of GH13_9 and GH57 BE. Conexibacter woesei and Saccharomonospora viridis have BE as the single member of GH57 family in these organisms.

In Bacteroidetes, some species unusually contain “eukaryotic type” GH13_8 BE. Bacteroides fragilis and Cytophaga hutchinsonii have one GH13_8 BE, while Tannerella forsythia has two GH13_8 BEs. Flavobacterium johnsoniae has one GH13_9 BE, whereas Sphingobacterium sp. 21 has two GH13_9 BEs.

The phylum Chlorobi includes green sulfur bacteria performing anoxygenic photosynthesis. Neither GH13 nor GH57 BE has been found in Chlorobaculum tepidum TLS and Chlorobium limicola DSM 245, although glycogen accumulation has been reported in C. limocola [ 68 ]. A gene annotated as BE occurs in a heterotrophic bacterium, Ignavibacterium album JCM 16511 (accession number AFH48672 ), but the sequence similarity to proteins in GH13_9 is poor.

Cyanobacteria are group of organisms that carry out oxygenic photosynthesis. Most of the species in this phylum including Prochlorococcus marinus CCMP1986 and Synechococcus elongatus PCC 7942 have one each of GH13_9 and GH57 BE [ 69 ]. Cyanothece sp. PCC 8802 and Cyanothece sp. ATCC 51142 have two and three isoforms of GH13_9 BE, respectively, in addition to the single GH57 BE [ 54 , 56 , 66 ]. Multiplication of BE is correlated with a specific trait in these species to accumulate insoluble, semi-crystalline α-glucan (cyanobacterial starch) instead of glycogen [ 70 , 71 ]. Except for the multiplication of GH13_9 isoform in a limited number of species, the distribution pattern of BE is uniform throughout the phylum.

Deinococcus (radiation-resistant bacteria) and Thermus (thermophilic bacteria) constitute a single phylum, but their BE content is distinct to each other. Deinococcus radiodurans has one GH13_9 BE [ 48 ] and does not possess any GH57 members. On the other hand, one GH57 BE is present in Thermus thermophilus . The protein ( BAD71725 ) is the only GH57 BE, for which enzymatic characteristics and the crystal structure have been reported in Bacteria [ 6 ].

Distribution of BE in Firmicutes (Gram-positive bacteria with low G + C content) shows remarkable diversity. All known types of BE are found, sometimes in combination, and differences are seen even between species of the same genus (e.g. Bacillus and Clostridium ). Clostridium clariflavum DSM 19732 and Lactococcus lactis CV56 have one GH13_8 (eukaryotic type) BE. Selenomonas ruminantium TAM6421 represents a rare example in that it has one each of GH13_8 and GH13_9 BE. Bacillus subtilis str. 168 [ 50 ], Clostridium botulinum B str. Eklund 17B, and Lactobacillus casei BD-II have a single GH13_9 BE. Paenibacillus sp. JDR-2 and Ruminococcus obeum A2-164 have two and three copies of GH13_9 BE, respectively. These organisms in Firmicutes listed above do not have any members of GH57. Paenibacillus mucilaginosus has one each of GH13_9 and GH57 BE. Bacillus halodurans C-125 and Clostridium acetobutylicum ATCC 824 have a single GH57 BE, while Acetohalobium arabaticum DSM 5501 and Desulfotomaculum kuznetsovii DSM 6115 have two GH57 BEs. Several GH57 BEs in Firmicutes, including one in B. halodurans , and one of two isoforms in D. kuznetsovii have GT4 domain fused at the C terminus to the GH57 domain, as described above (Figs. 1 , 4 ).

Proteobacteria are a diverse group of Gram-negative bacteria, and most species have one or two copies of GH13_9 BEs and no GH57 BE, with several exceptions across five Classes of the phylum (α- through ε-Proteobacteria). Most α-Proteobacteria do not have any members of GH57. Species of α-Proteobacteria including Paracoccus denitrificans PD1222 possess just one GH13_9 BE. Organisms in the Order Rhizobiales have two GH13_9 BEs, one each on chromosome and large plasmid. Some photosynthetic purple bacteria also have two copies of GH13_9 BE, but the situation varies depending on species. For example, Rhodobacter sphaeroides 2.4.1 has one GH13_9 BE, while R. sphaeroides ATCC 17025 has two isoforms. Magnetospira sp. QH-2 is an exception among α-Proteobacteria in that it has a GH57 BE in addition to a GH13_9 BE. In β-Proteobacteria, Burkholderia multivorans ATCC BAA-247 and Thiobacillus denitrificans ATCC 25259 have one GH13_9 BE. Azoarcus sp., Herbaspirillum seropedicae , Sulfuricella denitrificans skB26 and Variovorax paradoxus B4 have two GH13_9 BE isoforms. In a BE isoform of Azoarcus sp. ( CAL94413 , Fig. 3 ), the catalytic aspartate is substituted by serine. The protein may not have the catalytic activity on its own, but may serve as a component of hetero-oligomeric complex with other protein(s) (e.g. with its active isoform CAL94342 ), as has been reported for the complex between isoamylase 1 (active) and isoamylase 2 (inactive) proteins in plants [ 72 ]. In γ-Proteobacteria, a basally branched Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans ATCC 23270 contains one GH13_9 BE. Escherichia coli MG1655 and Vibrio cholerae IEC224 also have one GH13_9 BE, and these extensively studied bacteria do not possess any member of GH57. Moritella viscosa and Xanthomonas oryzae KACC 10331, together with other species of Xanthomonas , have two GH13_9 BEs. Methylomicrobium alcaliphilum 20Z and Nitrosococcus halophilus Nc 4 have one GH57 BE in addition to two GH13_9 BEs. Several species in δ-Proteobacteria including Desulfovibrio desulfuricans ATCC 27774 have a eukaryotic type GH13_8 BE. D. vulgaris Miyazaki F has one GH13_9 BE, while D. africanus Walvis Bay has two isoforms of GH13_9 BE. Sorangium cellulosum So ce56 and Myxococcus xanthus DK 1642 have one each of GH13_9 BE and GH57 BE. In ε-Proteobacteria, Nitratifractor salsuginis DSM 16511 and Sulfurimonas autotrophica DSM 16494 have a GH13_9 BE, as the sole member of GH13.

In Thermotogae (mostly thermophilic bacteria), Petrotoga mobilis SJ95 has one each of GH13_9 and GH57 BE. Thermotoga maritima MSB8 does not have GH13 BE, but it has one GH57 family protein designated as AmyC ( AHD18669 ). The protein was characterized as amylase [ 73 ] and its crystal structure was reported [ 74 ]. Based on the amino acid sequence, AmyC is classified as a member of GH57 BE [ 22 ]. It remains to be examined whether AmyC shows BE activity in addition to the hydrolase activity.

Among other bacterial phyla, a single GH13_9 BE, and no GH57 BE is found in Chlamydiae, Gemmatimonadetes, Lentisphaerae, Candidate phylum NC10, Synergistetes and Tenericutes. Chlamydia trachomatis D/UW-3/CX (Chlamydiae) and Mycoplasma mobile 163K (Tenericutes) have one GH13_9 BE and do not possess any members of GH57 (many other species of Mycoplasma do not have any enzymes involved in glycogen synthesis). Gemmatimonas aurantiaca T-27 (Gemmatimonadetes), Candidatus Methylomirabilis oxyfera (Candidate phylum NC10), and Thermanaerovibrio acidaminovorans DSM 6589 (Synergistetes) have a GH13_9 BE as the sole member of GH13. One GH13_9 BE is found on the genome of Lentisphaera araneosa HTCC2155 (Lentisphaerae), although its genome sequence remains to be completed.

In contrast, a single GH57 BE and no GH13 BE is present in the organisms from the phyla Armatimonadetes, Caldiserica, Chrysiogenetes, Defferibacteres, Dictyoglomi, Elusimicrobia, Spirochaetes, and Thermodesulfobacteria. Caldisericum exile AZM16c01 (Caldiserica), Desulfurispirillum indicum (Chrysiogenetes), Denitrovibrio acetiphilus (Defferibacteres), and Thermodesulfatator indicus DSM 15286 (Thermodesulfobacteria) do not have a GH13 member, while they possess a GH57 BE among three GH57 family members. Chthonomonas calidirosea T49 (Armatimonadetes), Fimbriimonas ginsengisoli Gsoil 348 (Armatimonadetes), Dictyoglomus thermophilum (Dictyoglomi), Elusimicrobium minutum Pei191 (Elusimicrobia), and organisms in Spirochaetes, Brachyspira pilosicoli WesB, Spirochaeta thermophila DSM 6578, and Treponema pallidum Nichols also have a GH57 BE. In BE from T. pallidum ( AAC65344 ), the catalytic residue aspartic acid is substituted by proline. As T. pallidum is obligate human parasite and glycogen metabolism is apparently lacking [ 75 ], the gene coding for BE may be in the process of inactivation by mutations leading eventually to its loss.

Complex distribution patterns are observed in other bacterial phyla. Candidatus Solibacter usitatus (Acidobacteria), Aquifex aeolicus (Aquificae) [ 48 , 57 , 58 ], Fusobacterium nucleatum ATCC 25586 (Fusobacteria), Candidatus Nitrospira defluvii (Nitrospirae), and Planctomyces brasiliensis DSM 5305 (Planctomycetes) have a single GH13_9 BE, while from the same phyla, Candidatus Chloracidobacterium thermophilum (Acidobacteria), Thermovibrio ammonificans (Aquificae), Leptotrichia buccalis C-1013-b (Fusobacteria) Leptospirillum ferrooxidans (Nitrospirae), and Phycisphaera mikurensis NBRC 102666 (Planctomycetes) possess one GH57 BE. One each of GH13_9 and GH57 BE coexists in Fibrobacter succinogenes (Fibrobacteres). In Chloroflexi, Thermobaculum terrenum has one GH13_9 BE, Chloroflexus aurantiacus has one each of GH13_9 and GH57 BE (the catalytic glutamate residue in the GH57 BE, ABY33308 , is substituted by glycine), and Herpetosiphon aurantiacus has two GH57 BEs. Due to the amino acid substitution, ABY33308 may be an inactive enzyme. The mutation may result from the occurrence of the redundant BEs in C. aurantiacus . However, it is also possible that the inactive protein is involved in the functional association with other protein(s) for glycogen metabolism. In Verrucomicrobia, Akkermansia muciniphila ATCC BAA-835 possesses a eukaryotic type GH13_8 BE, Opitutus terrae PB90-1 has one GH13_9 BE, and Methylacidiphilum infernorum V4 has one each of GH13_9 BE and GH57 BE.

In contrast to the large list for Bacteria as described above, a very limited number of BEs are known to occur in Archaea. Especially, no known type of BE has ever been identified in the phyla Crenarchaeota, as well as in Korarchaeota, Nanoarchaeota, and Thaumarchaeota. In Euryarchaeota, a GH13_9 BE is found in three species including Methanoculleus bourgensis MS2 and Picrophilus torridus DSM 9790, as exceptions in the archaeal Domain. Thermococcus kodakaraensis and other Archaea in the Order Thermococcales have one GH57 BE. The enzyme from T. kodakaraensis has been identified as the first GH57 BE [ 23 ] and its crystal structure has also been reported [ 33 ]. In Euryarchaeota GH57 BEs are found exclusively in Thermococcales consisting of only three genera ( Paleococcus , Pyrococcus , and Thermococcus ). BE has not been identified in the other taxa of Euryarchaeota including halophiles and methanogens (other than the above mentioned exceptions). Despite the absence of the known types of BE, accumulation and metabolism of glycogen have been observed in various species of Crenarchaeota [ 76 ], and methanogens [ 77 , 78 ].

Correlation to the distribution of glycogen synthase

Substrate of BE, α-1,4-glucan, is produced through the action of glycogen synthase (GS). GS in animals and fungi (EC 2.4.1.11) uses UDP-glucose as the substrate, whereas GS in prokaryotes, as well as its homolog starch synthase (SS) in plants (EC 2.4.1.21) use ADP-glucose [ 2 ]. In terms of primary structure of these enzymes, animal/fungal GSs and prokaryotic/plant GSs (SSs) are classified into GT3 and GT5, respectively [ 79 ]. The 3D structures of these glycosyltransferases show GT-B fold consisting of two β/α/β Rossmann-fold domains connected by a short linker [ 36 ]. By the catalysis of GT-B fold enzymes the α-configuration of anomeric carbon in the substrate is retained, resulting in the formation of α-1,4-glucoside linkage in GS (SS) reaction [ 35 ].

Distribution of GS in prokaryotes is shown in Table 1 . Most prokaryotes have a GT5 GS (nearly 3000 and 90 members of GT5 are found in Bacteria and Archaea, respectively, in CAZy database). In some instances, two or even three copies of GT5 GS are found in species belonging to Chlorobi, Chloroflexi, Cyanobacteria, Deferribacteres, Firmicutes, Gemmatimonadetes, Proteobacteria, Spirochaetes, and Verrucomicrobia. It is noteworthy that GSs are found in organisms in Chlorobi, Crenarchaeota, and Euryarchaeota (methanogens), where no BEs have been found. It is possible that these organisms have BE belonging to as-yet-unidentified protein families.

In addition to the standard GS in GT5 family, GSs belonging to GT3 family are observed in a small number of prokaryotes (110 members in CAZy database) including the phyla Bacteroidetes, Planktomycetes, and δ-Proteobacteria. It should be noted that in Bacteroidetes and δ-Proteobacteria, GT3 GSs coexist with GH13_8 BE, the combination being the same as that seen in animals and fungi. These enzymes may be acquired from Eukaryotes through lateral gene transfer (LGT).

Furthermore, GSs characterized in species of Actinobacteria, Corynebacterium glutamicum [ 80 ], Streptomyces coelicolor [ 81 ], and Mycobacterium tuberculosis [ 82 ] are classified into GT4 family. Proteins showing high sequence similarity to GT4 GSs in Actinobacteria are found in Chloroflexus aurantaricus and Lentisphaera araneosa , and they have been tentatively assigned here as GT4 GS (Table 1 ).

Novel routes for α-glucan biosynthesis

Apart from the classical AGPase–GS pathway, two other routes of α-glucan production have been described in Mycobacterium tuberculosis and other Actinobacteria: (1) synthesis of methylglucose lipopolysaccharides (MGLPs) and (2) maltose-1-phosphate maltosyltransferase (GlgE) pathway involved in the production of glycogen and/or other α-glucans (see below), starting from trehalose.

MGLPs are composed of 15–20 α-1,4-linked glucoses, with numerous modifications by 6- O -methylation, 3- O -methylation (at the non-reducing end), branching with two glucoses via β-1,3-linkages (attached at second and fourth glucose residues from the reducing end), and esterification at 6- O - and 4- O -positions (the latter being only at the non-reducing end) with acetate, propionate, isobutylate, succinate, or octanoylate [ 83 – 85 ]. The reducing end of MGLPs is composed of α-glucosyl-1,2- d -glycerate, which is linked through an α-1,6-bond to the rest of the molecule [ 83 , 84 ]. MGLPs are postulated to assume a helical conformation with hydrophobic cavity that can accommodate fatty acid derivatives, thereby regulating lipid metabolism [ 83 , 84 ]. A GT4 α-glucosyltransferase (distinct from GS) was shown to be responsible for elongation of the glucan chain in MGLPs [ 86 ]. A GH57 BE, whose gene lies next to that of the α-glucosyltransferase in mycobacteria is thought to catalyze the formation of the single α-1,6-linkage in MGLPs [ 83 , 86 ], although experimental proof remains to be obtained.

The GlgE pathway consists of four reaction steps, isomerization of trehalose to maltose by trehalose synthase (TreS, EC 5.4.99.16), conversion to maltose-1-phosphate by maltokinase (Mak or Pep2, EC 2.7.1.175), transfer of maltosyl unit for α-glucan polymerization by maltose-1-phosphate maltosyltransferase (GlgE, EC 2.4.99.16), and formation of branched polysaccharide by BE (belonging to GH13_9) [ 67 , 87 , 88 ]. Among the enzymes in the pathway, TreS and GlgE have been classified into GH13_16 and GH13_3, respectively, in the CAZy database.

In addition to the intracellular α-glucans, mycobacteria synthesize extracellular capsule glucan, structurally similar to glycogen, for the evasion of host immune system [ 66 ]. Three enzymes coexist for α-glucan elongation in mycobacteria, GS, α-glucosyltransferase, and GlgE, as described above. Studies in M. tuberculosis showed that α-glucosyltransferase (also responsible for the synthesis of MGLPs) and GS were involved in the production of glycogen and extracellular capsule glucan, respectively, although they seemed to have partially overlapping roles [ 66 , 67 ]. While the occurrence of extracellular capsule glucan and MGLPs is confined in mycobacteria and related Actinobacteria, enzymes in GlgE pathway are widely distributed in prokaryotes, mostly in Bacteria [ 88 ]. In the CAZy database, approximately 1000 members each of TreS and GlgE are found. In contrast to the glucan elongation step, the role of branch formation by BE seems to be less redundant. The gene coding for GH13_9 BE in M. tuberculosis could not be inactivated, indicating its essentiality for viability [ 66 ]. In contrast, GH13_9 BE in Corynebacterium glutamicum was shown to be dispensable for viability, but required for glycogen accumulation [ 63 ].

Future prospects

Despite the accumulated information obtained from biochemical and structural studies, interrelationship between the reaction specificity and protein structure of BE remains still elusive. Precise pictures for the mode of substrate binding at the active site, and selective mechanism for the specific lengths of oligosaccharides are required. While GH13 family enzymes have been investigated for virtually any types of organism, GH57 family enzymes, including BE, have been characterized only for thermophilic bacteria and archaea. The GH57 BEs found in various mesophilic bacteria remain as unexplored frontiers for future research. As BE has not been identified in some prokaryotic linages (e.g. Chlorobi and Crenarchaeota), it would be possible that novel BE(s) will be discovered belonging to still unrecognized protein family. Such new repertoires of BE, possibly exhibiting novel catalytic properties, will bring us new opportunities for the development of basic glycoscience and industrial application.
