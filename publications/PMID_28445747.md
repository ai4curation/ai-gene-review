Abstract

Genome packaging is strongly conserved in the complex double-stranded DNA viruses, including the herpesviruses and many bacteriophages. In these cases, viral DNA is packaged into a procapsid shell by a terminase enzyme. The packaging substrate is typically a concatemer composed of multiple genomes linked in a head-to-tail fashion, and terminase enzymes perform two essential functions: 1) excision of a unit length genome from the concatemer (genome maturation) and 2) translocation of the duplex into a procapsid (genome packaging). While the packaging motors have been described in some detail, the maturation complexes remain ill characterized. Here we describe the assembly, physical characteristics, and catalytic activity of the λ -genome maturation complex. The λ -terminase protomer is composed of one large catalytic subunit tightly associated with two DNA recognition subunits. The isolated protomer binds DNA weakly and does not discriminate between nonspecific DNA and duplexes that contain the packaging initiation sequence, cos . The Escherichia coli integration host factor protein (IHF) is required for efficient λ -development in vivo and a specific IHF recognition sequence is found within cos . We show that IHF and the terminase protomer cooperatively assemble at the cos site and that the small terminase subunit plays the dominant role in complex assembly. Analytical ultracentrifugation analysis reveals that the maturation complex is composed of four protomers and one IHF heterodimer bound at the cos site. Tetramer assembly activates the cos -cleavage nuclease activity of the enzyme, which matures the genome end in preparation for packaging. The stoichiometry and catalytic activity of the complex is reminiscent of the type IIE and IIF restriction endonucleases and the two systems may share mechanistic features. This study, to our knowledge, provides our first detailed glimpse into the structural and functional features of a viral genome maturation complex, an essential intermediate in the development of complex dsDNA viruses.

Introduction

Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites whose developmental pathways are conserved in many virus classes and especially in the complex double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) viruses ( 1 , 2 , 3 ). In the majority of these viruses (phage ϕ29 being a notable exception), DNA is replicated via a rolling circle mechanism to afford linear concatemers of the viral genome, linked in a head-to-tail fashion (immature DNA) ( 2 , 3 , 4 ). Terminase enzymes serve to package viral DNA and perform two essential functions: 1) excision of individual genomes from the concatemer (maturation reaction); and 2) concomitant translocation of DNA into a preassembled procapsid shell, fueled by ATP hydrolysis (packaging reaction) ( 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ).

The genome packaging pathways and the structure and function of the terminase enzymes are strongly conserved in the dsDNA viruses, both eukaryotic and prokaryotic ( 5 , 6 , 9 , 10 ). The characterized terminase enzymes are composed of two subunits. The larger TerL subunit contains a C-terminal nuclease domain that is responsible for genome end maturation and an N-terminal DNA packaging domain that contains the DNA translocation and packaging ATPase catalytic sites ( 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ). Thus, TerL possesses all of the catalytic activities required to excise and package monomeric genomes from a multigenome concatemer. The smaller TerS subunit is responsible for specific recognition of the packaging initiation site within the concatemer ( pac , or cos ) and assembly of the maturation complex at that site ( 6 , 8 , 9 ). Structural studies for a number of isolated TerS subunits and subdomains reveal heterogeneous oligomeric complexes ranging from 2 to 12 subunits, typically in ringlike structures and in some cases double-ring complexes (e.g., phage T4) ( 8 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ). While it is clear that the functional holoenzymes are heterooligomers assembled from both the catalytic (TerL) and DNA recognition (TerS) subunits, there is a dearth of information on the catalytically competent maturation complexes that initiate genome packaging. McNulty et al. ( 22 ) have recently reported the isolation of a phage P22 terminase complex composed of a nonameric TerS ring associated with one to three TerL subunits; however, the catalytic activity of the complex was not reported.

Bacteriophage λ has historically served as a model system for gene regulation and in recent years has emerged as a powerful system for interrogating mechanistic features of viral genome packaging. Specifically, we have isolated a λ -terminase protomer composed of one large TerL catalytic subunit tightly associated with two small TerS DNA recognition subunits (TerL 1 ⋅TerS 2 ); however, the protomer possesses weak catalytic activity ( 23 ). In the absence of DNA, the protomer is in slow equilibrium with a tetramer of protomers assembled into a ringlike complex ( K D ,app ∼ 4 μ M); ring assembly activates the catalytic activities of the enzyme ( 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 ). Viral DNA similarly activates the catalytic activities of the protomer, but at much lower and physiologically relevant enzyme concentrations ( 26 , 27 , 28 ). Based on this observation, we have proposed that viral DNA, specifically the cos sequence, nucleates cooperative assembly of four protomers into a functional maturation complex.

The Escherichia coli integration host factor protein (IHF) is required for λ -development in vivo ( 29 ) and strongly stimulates genome maturation and packaging reactions in vitro ( 25 , 27 , 30 ). IHF is a stable heterodimer that belongs to a class of histone-like proteins capable of bending and wrapping DNA ( 31 , 32 ). The dimer binds DNA weakly and nonspecifically, but with positive cooperativity to afford an ensemble of complexes of increasing mass ( 28 ). In this mode, the protein plays a major role in condensation of the bacterial nucleoid ( 33 , 34 , 35 ). In addition, IHF binds with high affinity to specific DNA elements ( 28 , 36 , 37 ); in this mode the protein plays an important role in regulating cellular processes including DNA replication ( 38 ), transcription ( 39 ), and site-specific integration events ( 36 , 40 , 41 ). Indeed, IHF was originally identified as a host protein required for λ -integration into the E. coli genome during lysogeny ( 31 , 36 ). In all characterized cases, binding of IHF to its specific recognition sequence introduces a strong bend in DNA (>160°), which provides a duplex architecture conducive to the recruitment of regulatory proteins to the site ( 29 , 42 ).

We have shown that IHF specifically recognizes a single consensus element within the λ cos sequence ( I1 ) and introduces a strong bend at that site ( 28 , 43 ). We have further demonstrated that while the isolated λ TerS subunit discriminates only weakly between nonspecific and cos -containing duplexes, TerS and IHF cooperatively bind at cos to afford a specific and strongly bent duplex structure ( 43 ). We have suggested that these cooperative interactions are central to the assembly of a functional maturation complex at cos ; however, this remains speculative, because the catalytic TerL subunit was not utilized in the published studies.

Recent studies have revealed structural and functional features of the packaging motors in several systems ( 16 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 ); however, the equally important genome maturation complexes have been virtually ignored. Here we describe the assembly, physical characteristics, and catalytic activity of the λ -genome maturation complex. Analogous maturation complexes are essential in the development of virtually all of the complex dsDNA viruses, from herpesvirus to phage, and the results are of broad biological significance.

Materials and Methods

Protein and DNA preparation

The terminase protomer and tetramer species were isolated by published protocol ( 25 , 26 , 50 ) and IHF was purified as described in Sanyal et al. ( 28 ). A 274-bp duplex that encompasses the minimal cos sequence ( cos DNA) and a nonspecific 274-bp duplex of random sequence ( ns DNA) were prepared by large-scale preparative PCR as described in Sanyal et al. ( 28 ). The purity of amplified products was verified by agarose gel analysis and the concentration of DNA was determined via spectroscopic methods. Primers containing a 5′-cyanine (Cy3) dye were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA) and used to prepare labeled DNA for the analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC) studies. 5′ 32 P-end labeled duplex substrates for the electrophoretic mobility shift (EMS) studies were prepared as described in Ortega and Catalano ( 43 ).

EMS assay

Unless otherwise indicated, these studies were performed in buffer containing 20 mM Tris, pH 8.6 (at 4°C), 50 mM NaCl, 1.5 μ M bovine serum albumin, 1.5 nM salmon sperm DNA, 1 mM EDTA, 7 mM β -mercaptoethanol, 10% glycerol, and 30 pM radiolabeled DNA substrate ( cos 274 or ns 274). IHF and terminase were added as indicated in each individual experiment and the binding mixtures were incubated at 7°C for 1 h. For the reactions that involved duplex cleavage, 10 mM MgCl 2 was added and incubation was carried out at 30°C for 20 min. In all cases, the binding mixtures were fractionated by nondenaturing PAGE and the radioactive bands were visualized and quantified as described in Ortega and Catalano ( 43 ). In those cases where the bound DNA ran as an apparent nonspecific smear on the gel, the region consisting of free DNA was boxed in a consistent manner and the region extending from immediately above the free DNA band to the well was boxed to determine the retarded counts. The raw binding data were analyzed according to a phenomenological Hill model as described in Sanyal et al. ( 28 ) and Ortega and Catalano ( 43 ).

Sedimentation equilibrium analytical ultracentrifugation

Sedimentation equilibrium (SE-AUC) studies were performed using an XLA analytical ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA). Unless otherwise indicated, the binding studies were performed in buffer containing 20 mM Tris, pH 8.6 (at 4°C), 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 7 mM β -mercaptoethanol, 5% glycerol, 200 nM Cy3-labeled duplex substrates ( cos 274 or ns 274). IHF was included at 250 nM and terminase was added as indicated in each individual experiment. The binding mixtures were loaded into either two-sector (150 μ L) or six-sector (120 μ L) Epon charcoal-filled centerpieces and unless otherwise indicated spun at 4°C at rotor speeds of 4000, 6000, and 9000 rpm. Sedimentation was monitored by absorbance at 550 nm ( λ max of Cy3), which allows selective monitoring of Cy3-DNA without pollution of the absorbance signal by the protein; thus only free DNA and the nucleoprotein complexes are observed ( 51 ). Sedimentation equilibrium was confirmed by the absence of deviation between successive scans using the software Winmatch (David Yphantis, University of Connecticut, Storrs, CT; Michael Johnson, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA; Jeff Lary, National Analytical Ultracentrifuge Center, Storrs, CT); all experiments reached equilibrium within 50–60 h for the first speed, and absorbance data were then collected every 0.001 cm in the step mode, with 20 averages per step.

cos -cleavage assay

The cos- cleavage endonuclease assay was performed as described in Andrews and Catalano ( 25 ), with minor modification. Results

Binding of the terminase protomer to DNA

We first utilized an EMS assay to examine protomer-DNA binding interactions. The data presented in Fig. 1 A show that the protomer binds to a duplex of random sequence ( ns DNA) to afford a series of complexes that are initially smeared on the gel, but that ultimately collapse into discrete, strongly retarded bands at elevated protein concentrations. This binding pattern is unchanged in the presence of 5 nM IHF ( Fig. 1 B ), consistent with the observation that IHF does not bind to ns DNA at this concentration ( 28 ). We next examined protomer binding to a duplex that contains the λ -packaging initiation sequence, cos . In the absence of IHF, the protomer binds to cos DNA to afford a binding pattern similar to that observed with the ns DNA substrate, which we suggest reflects a nonspecific binding interaction ( Fig. 1 C ).

Figure 1.

The smeared banding patterns preclude rigorous characterization of thermodynamic binding constants ( K D ,app ). Notwithstanding, quantitation of the gels as described in Materials and Methods and analysis of the data according to a phenomenological Hill model indicates that the protomer discriminates poorly, if at all, between the specific and nonspecific DNA substrates ( Fig. 2 ; Table 1 ). Moreover, the data suggest that the protomer binds to both duplexes in a weak and nonspecific manner, as previously demonstrated for IHF ( 28 , 43 ).

The terminase protomer and IHF cooperatively bind to cos DNA

We previously demonstrated that 1) IHF binds to the specific I1 element within cos to afford a 1:1 binary complex ( K D ,app = 1–5 nM) and that 2) IHF and the isolated TerS subunit cooperatively bind and bend cos -containing duplexes ( 28 , 43 ). Here we examine cooperative assembly of IHF and the terminase protomer (TerL⋅TerS 2 heterotrimer) at cos using the EMS assay. Fig. 1 D demonstrates that the protomer and IHF bind to cos DNA to afford a series of specific (i.e., nonsmeared), but increasingly retarded nucleoprotein complexes. Quantification of the specific binding data as described above affords a K D ,app = 19.8 ± 0.8 nM ( Fig. 2 ; Table 1 ). This represents a sixfold decrease in K D ,app relative to cos DNA in the absence of IHF, which corresponds to a ΔΔ G ∼ 1 kcal/mol cooperative binding energy.

The K D ,app values determined here for the terminase protomer are quite similar to those observed with the isolated TerS subunit ( Table 1 ) ( 43 ). This indicates that TerS plays the dominant role in protomer binding to DNA and that specific recognition of cos is mediated by cooperative binding of IHF and TerS at that site. That said, the derived Hill coefficients are generally larger with the protomer compared to the isolated TerS subunit ( Table 1 ); this suggests that the TerL subunit also plays a role in facilitating maturation complex assembly at cos . Unfortunately, rigorous quantitation of the EMS binding data is not possible (see above) and the mechanistic implications of this observation are unclear.

Stoichiometry of terminase in the maturation complex

We next employed SE-AUC to interrogate the stoichiometry of proteins assembled into the genome maturation complex. These studies used a DNA concentration of 200 nM, which is in excess of the derived K D ,app values ( Table 1 ) and a stoichiometric binding event is anticipated. We first characterized the stoichiometry of IHF bound to cos DNA and Fig. 3 A shows the sedimentation equilibrium distribution for the binary IHF⋅ cos DNA complex. Analysis of the data according to a single-species model ( Eq. 1 ) affords an apparent weight average buoyant molecular weight M B = 85.8 (82.3–88.7). This value closely matches the anticipated value of a complex composed of one IHF heterodimer bound to cos DNA ( M B = 84.3; see Table S1 in the Supplemental Information ), as depicted in Fig. 3 A .

Next, the terminase protomer was incrementally added to the binary IHF⋅ cos DNA complex assembled as above; a representative sedimentation equilibrium distribution for the protomer⋅IHF⋅ cos DNA complex (i.e., the maturation complex) is shown in Fig. 3 B . The apparent weight average buoyant molecular weight as a function of protomer/DNA ratio was determined according to a single species model and the data are plotted in Fig. 4 . The binding data show a linear increase in M B with increasing protomer concentration as expected for a stoichiometric binding event, and the data are well described by a stoichiometric binding model ( Eq. 6 ). The titration curve saturates at a protomer/DNA ratio of ∼5, which, on the surface, suggests that five protomers are incorporated into the complex; however, this interpretation presumes that the titrant (i.e., protomer) possesses 100% binding activity, which is rarely the case. Fortunately, the value of M B at saturation can be used to rigorously determine the actual stoichiometry of the protomer in the maturation complex.

The derived M B values for the complexes assembled at saturation (protomer/DNA ratios of 5–10; Fig. 4 ) were averaged, which affords M B = 207.0 (185.6–230.0); this value closely matches the anticipated value of a complex containing one IHF heterodimer and four protomers bound to cos DNA ( M B = 201.5; see Table S1 ). Explicit calculation of the complex stoichiometry according to Eq. 5 affords n = 4.1 ± 0.2, demonstrating that four protomers and one IHF heterodimer are incorporated into the maturation complex, as depicted in Fig. 3 B . We note that comparison of this stoichiometry with the breakpoint in the titration curve (∼5 protomers per duplex, Fig. 4 ) indicates that the purified protomer possesses ∼80% DNA binding activity.

Stoichiometry of terminase bound to cos DNA in the absence of IHF

The protomer binds to cos DNA to afford an apparent nonspecific binding pattern in the absence of IHF ( Fig. 1 ). Here we use SE-AUC to determine the stoichiometry of this nonspecific complex. The protomer was incrementally added to cos DNA in the absence of IHF and M B as a function of protomer/DNA ratio was determined according to a single-species model ( Eq. 1 ). While the data show a concentration-dependent increase in M B with increasing protomer concentration, the curvilinear titration profile is inconsistent with a simple stoichiometric binding event. The mechanistic basis for this observation is not clear, but likely reflects heterogeneity in the nonspecific complexes. Analysis of the data at an excessive protomer/DNA ratio of 10 affords an apparent weight average buoyant molecular weight M B = 152.5 (135.7–169.7). Comparison to the theoretical values for likely oligomeric complexes suggests that this complex contains two to three terminase protomers per duplex, on average (see Table S1 ). Stoichiometry of the catalytically competent maturation complex

The AUC data presented above suggests that at 1 μ M terminase, 2–3 protomers assemble on cos DNA in the absence of IHF while a tetramer is recruited in the presence of IHF. Efficient duplex nicking is dependent on IHF ( 50 ), conditions where tetramer assembly is favored. This predicts that the tetramer represents the catalytically competent nuclease complex. To test this hypothesis, we assembled the dimeric and tetrameric protomer complexes on cos DNA in the absence and presence of IHF, respectively, under conditions identical to those used in the AUC studies above (1 μ M protomer). Magnesium was then added to the reaction mixtures and nuclease activity monitored. The data presented in Fig. 5 A demonstrate that the cos- cleavage reaction is strongly stimulated by IHF. We interpret these data to indicate that the catalytically competent and biologically relevant maturation complex is composed of four protomers and IHF assembled at the cos site of λ -DNA.

Figure 5.

The upstream D R duplex is ejected from the post-cleavage complex

In the presence of Mg 2+ , the maturation complex nicks the duplex at cos to afford the post-cleavage maturation complex (the cos -cleavage nuclease reaction) and we next examined this packaging intermediate. We first examined the reaction using the EMS assay (30 pM DNA). The protomer was incrementally added to the cos DNA⋅IHF complex as described in Fig. 1 D , except that Mg 2+ was included. The mixtures were incubated at 30°C for 20 min to allow duplex cleavage and then loaded on an EMS gel. Consistent with our prior studies ( 50 , 54 , 55 ), the duplex is fully cleaved in the presence of Mg 2+ and the D R strand is ejected from the complex, even at the lowest concentration of enzyme examined ( Fig. 5 B ). The post-cleavage complex appears as a series of bands whose migration is increasingly retarded in a protomer concentration-dependent manner. The nature of these EMS complexes is uncertain, but may reflect higher-order, nonspecific binding of multiple protomers at elevated protein/DNA ratios, superimposed with bending/wrapping of the duplex as we have previously demonstrated for IHF ( 28 ). Importantly, DNA bending and wrapping by TerS subunits has also been reported in the phage T4 ( 56 ) and SPP1 ( 57 ) systems, and this may be a common feature of the packaging initiation complexes.

Stoichiometry of terminase in the post-cleavage complex

The EMS studies clearly demonstrate that the upstream, unpackaged D R duplex is ejected from the post-cleavage complex; however, the protein(s) that remain bound to the matured D L end are unknown ( D L is the mature left end of the λ -genome formed by terminase cleavage at cos ). Here we examine this post-cleavage complex using SE-AUC. The maturation complex was generated under stoichiometric binding conditions (1 μ M protomer, 250 nM IHF, 200 nM cos DNA) and magnesium was added. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 4 h to complete duplex nicking and SE-AUC was then performed at 4°C; the equilibrium sedimentation distribution of the products are shown in Fig. 3 C . EMS analysis of the reaction products at the end of the experiment confirmed that the upstream D R duplex had been ejected from the complex (data not shown), and two species are thus present in the reaction mixture at equilibrium, the D R duplex, and the protomer⋅IHF⋅ D L post-cleavage complex. The AUC data were therefore analyzed according to a noninteracting, two-species model ( Eq. 3 ) as described in Materials and Methods . This affords an apparent weight average buoyant molecular weight M B = 190.4 (182.8–197.9) for the postcleavage maturation complex. This value closely matches the anticipated value of a complex containing one IHF heterodimer and four protomers bound to the matured D L end of the duplex (180.4; see Table S2 ). Explicit calculation of the protomer stoichiometry according to Eq. 4 affords n = 4.2 ± 0.2 ( Table 2 ), which indicates that one IHF heterodimer and four protomers remain bound to the matured D L end in the post-cleavage complex, as depicted in Fig. 3 C .

Discussion

Genome recognition and packaging in the complex dsDNA viruses is catalyzed by a terminase enzyme. In this study, we demonstrate that 1) the λ -terminase protomer discriminates poorly between ns DNA and cos DNA substrates, 2) one IHF heterodimer and four protomers cooperatively assemble at the cos sequence, 3) assembly of this maturation complex activates the cos -cleavage nuclease activity of the enzyme, and that 4) the post-cleavage complex retains the four terminase protomers tightly bound to the matured D L strand in preparation for packaging. This study, to our knowledge, provides our first glimpse into the structure and function of two essential packaging intermediates common to the complex dsDNA viruses, and provides insight into the packaging pathway in an infected cell.

Pathway for maturation complex assembly in vivo

We previously demonstrated that the K D ,app for ring-tetramer assembly in the absence of DNA is ∼4 μ M ( 26 ). This is significantly greater than the estimated protomer concentration during a productive virus infection (150–600 nM) ( 27 ) and the concentration of the preassembled ring is thus expected to be very low in vivo. In contrast, the K D ,app for cooperative binding of four protomers and IHF at a cos -sequence is 20 nM, which is well below presumed in vivo concentrations ( 27 , 32 , 58 ). Thus, the data suggest that the preferred pathway for assembly of a catalytically competent maturation complex in vivo is via the cooperative binding of terminase and IHF at the cos- sequence of λ -DNA. Importantly, this pathway prevents premature assembly of a potentially damaging nuclease because terminase activity is only activated in the ring tetramer, which only assembles at cos and in the presence of IHF, as depicted in Fig. 6 .

Figure 6.

Maturation of viral DNA

Once assembled, the maturation complex cuts the duplex at cos in preparation for packaging. We show here that the product of the cos -cleavage reaction, i.e., the post-cleavage maturation complex, is similarly composed of one IHF heterodimer and four terminase protomers tightly bound to the matured duplex end ( D L ). This nuclease reaction and the stoichiometry of terminase protomers in the maturation complex is reminiscent of the Type IIE and Type IIF restriction endonucleases ( 59 , 60 , 61 ). In these enzymes, a dimeric complex can assemble at a specific, symmetric duplex nicking site but this intermediate possesses weak nuclease activity. The catalytic activity of the dimer is strongly activated by direct association with a second dimer bound to a second recognition sequence, to yield a dimer-of-dimers nuclease complex. In the case of the IIE enzymes, the second dimer acts as an allosteric effector for cleavage at only one site; in the case of the IIF enzymes, both sites are cut. In analogy, cooperative assembly of four protomers at cos is required to activate the site-specific nuclease complex. We suggest that DNA bending by IHF binding to the I1 element positions the duplex such that it can interact with a second dimer in the context of a tetrameric protomer ring to afford an activated dimer-of-dimers nuclease complex. Within this context, we note that synapse initiation models have been proposed in the T7 and T4 systems where it has been suggested that the maturation complex binds to two sequential packaging sequences in the genome concatemer (P. Serwer, personal communication) ( 8 , 62 ). Thus, it is feasible that in the context of a multigenome concatemer, a λ -terminase dimer of dimers interacts with two sequential cos sequences to engender an activated synapse maturation complex as proposed in other systems. Indeed, this model has been previously considered in the λ -system ( 63 ) and studies that seek to directly test this hypothesis are currently underway in our lab.

The nature of packaging motor complexes

The next step in the packaging pathway is binding of the post-cleavage maturation complex to the portal of a procapsid to afford the activated packaging motor. The most parsimonious model is a direct interaction between the maturation complex and the portal such that the tetrameric stoichiometry is retained in the packaging motor, as depicted in Fig. 6 . This stoichiometry differs from the pentameric motors proposed in the phage ϕ 29 ( 64 ), T4 ( 14 ), T3 ( 65 ), and P74-26 ( 16 ) systems (we note that there is controversy as to the stoichiometry of the packaging motor in ϕ 29. Structural studies indicate a pentameric motor ( 49 ), whereas biophysical studies suggest a hexameric motor ( 64 ).) Although we cannot rigorously exclude the possibility that an additional λ -protomer is recruited to the portal at the motor assembly step, we find this unlikely, based on a variety of biophysical ( 24 , 55 ) and kinetic ( 30 ) data. Whatever the case and despite these differences, it is likely that the essential features of mechanochemical coupling of ATP hydrolysis, and DNA translocation are conserved in these fascinating genome packaging motors. These fundamental questions remain an active area of research in our lab.
