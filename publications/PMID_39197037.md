## Full Text

Abstract

Photorespiration is an energetically costly metabolic pathway in plants that responds to environmental stresses. The molecular basis of the regulation of the photorespiratory cycle under stress conditions remains unclear. Here, we discovered that FERONIA (FER) regulates photorespiratory flow under salt stress in Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ). FER mutation results in hypersensitivity to salt stress, but disruption of ferredoxin-dependent glutamate synthase 1 (GLU1), an enzyme that participates in the photorespiratory pathway by producing glutamate, greatly suppresses fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress primarily due to reduced glycine yield. In contrast, disrupting mitochondrial serine hydroxymethyltransferase1 (SHM1), which is supposed to increase glycine levels by hampering the conversion of glycine to serine in the photorespiratory cycle, aggravates fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress. Biochemical data show that FER interacts with and phosphorylates SHM1, and this phosphorylation modulates SHM1 stability. Additionally, the production of proline and its intermediate △ 1 -pyrroline-5-carboxylate (P5C), which are both synthesized from glutamate, also contributes to fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress. In conclusion, this study elucidates the functional mechanism of FER in regulating salt tolerance by modulating photorespiratory flux, which greatly broadens our understanding of how plants adapt to high salinity.

In response to salt stress, a receptor-like kinase modulates photorespiratory flux by controlling the stability of a mitochondrial enzyme, ultimately improving plant salt tolerance.

IN A NUTSHELL.

Background : Soil salinity is detrimental to plant growth and productivity due to its negative impacts on various biological processes within plant cells. Currently, the physiological, metabolic, and biochemical processes that are affected by salt stress and the underlying regulatory mechanisms are still not fully understood. Photorespiration is one such pathway that is influenced by salt stress, but our knowledge of its role in plant salt tolerance and how it is regulated under salt stress is still quite limited. FERONIA (FER) is a key regulator of salt tolerance in Arabidopsis, but the internal pathways that are controlled by FER to mediate salt stress responses remain largely unknown.

Question : What role does the photorespiratory pathway play in plant salt tolerance? How the photorespiratory pathway is regulated under salt stress at the molecular level.

Findings : We found that FER plays an important role in regulating the photorespiratory pathway in Arabidopsis. The salt hypersensitivity of fer-4 mutant was considerably alleviated when GLU1 was disrupted. GLU1 is an Fd-GOGAT enzyme that catalyses the conversion of glutamine to glutamate, and glutamate is a precursor of glycine within the photorespiratory pathway. Amino acid profiling revealed the importance of glycine homeostasis in the FER-mediated regulation of salt tolerance, which was reinforced by both genetic analysis and external glycine application. Further investigation indicated that FER biochemically controls photorespiratory flux through the modulation of SHM1 protein stability via direct phosphorylation.

Next steps : Our study shows that glycine homeostasis in the photorespiratory pathway is a critical determinant of plant salt tolerance. However, we still do not know whether glycine directly affects plant salt tolerance as a signaling molecule, or its downstream metabolites, such as ammonium, cause salt hypersensitivity in plants.

Introduction

Photorespiration is a high-flux metabolic pathway that connects a number of metabolic processes, including photosynthetic carbon assimilation, ammonium assimilation, and primary metabolism ( Eisenhut et al. 2019 ). Photorespiration occurs in four compartments, i.e. cytosol, chloroplast, peroxisome, and mitochondrion, and requires eight core enzymes ( Foyer et al. 2009 ). Initially, ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP) in the chloroplast is catalyzed by bifunctional ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco) to produce 2-phosphoglycolate (2-PG) via an oxygenation reaction, and 2-PG is then dephosphorylated by 2-phosphoglycolate phosphatase 1 (PGLP1) to produce glycolate ( Schwarte and Bauwe 2007 ). Glycolate is oxidized to glyoxylate by glycolate oxidases (GOXs) and subsequently converted to glycine (Gly) by glutamate:glyoxylate aminotransferases (GGTs) in the peroxisome ( Kerchev et al. 2016 ). In the metabolic step of glycine production, glutamate that is yielded by glutamine synthetase (GS)/ferredoxin-dependent glutamate synthase (Fd-GOGAT) cycle is utilized as a nitrogen donor ( Bauwe et al. 2010 ). The synthesized glycine is taken up into the mitochondria, where it is switched to serine (Ser) with the synergistic action of glycine decarboxylase (GDC) ( Bauwe and Kolukisaoglu 2003 ) and serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT) ( Voll et al. 2006 ), and during this metabolic process, CO 2 and ammonium are released ( Coschigano et al. 1998 ; Linka and Weber 2005 ). Serine is then shuttled back into the peroxisomes to produce glycine and hydroxypyruvate by serine:glyoxylate aminotransferase (SGAT) ( Modde et al. 2016 ). Finally, hydroxypyruvate is converted to glyceral and 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PG) through the catalytic actions of hydroxypyruvate reductases (HPRs) and glycerate kinase (GLYK) ( Boldt et al. 2005 ; Timm et al. 2008 ). Plants with mutations in photorespiratory genes usually exhibit chlorosis or lethality under ambient air conditions ( Eisenhut et al. 2019 ), suggesting that interrupting the photorespiratory flux negatively impacts plant growth and vitality under normal conditions. For certain photorespiratory mutants, however, deleterious effects can be substantially mitigated under nonphotorespiratory conditions, such as high CO 2 levels ( Voll et al. 2006 ; Schwarte and Bauwe 2007 ; Dellero et al. 2015 ).

Environments with harsh conditions such as high salinity, drought, and extreme temperatures can cause dehydration, which in turn triggers stomatal closure ( Roychoudhury et al. 2013 ). Consequently, the photorespiratory flux intensifies due to reduced CO 2 absorption and an elevated O 2 /CO 2 ratio ( Hagemann and Bauwe 2016 ). Therefore, photorespiratory pathway must be accurately modulated to optimize plant adaptation to challenging conditions. The involvement of photorespiration-related components in regulating salt tolerance has been reported in several studies. For instance, gs2 mutant that is deficient in GS/Fd-GOGAT cycle exhibits an improved capacity to withstand salt stress compared with the wild-type in Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ) ( Ferreira et al. 2019 ). Conversely, the photorespiratory mutant shm1-1 displays a hypersensitive phenotype under salt stress, mainly due to increased H 2 O 2 and cytochrome c production ( Moreno et al. 2005 ; Zhou et al. 2012 ). Transgenic Arabidopsis plants overexpressing SGAT exhibit increased salt tolerance compared with the wild-type ( Wang et al. 2019 ). These findings suggest that different photorespiratory components may play distinct roles in plant salt tolerance and their functional mechanisms need to be further clarified in the context of the entire photorespiratory pathway.

The conversion of glycine to serine during photorespiration is accompanied by the production of ammonium, which is considered a major nitrogen source for plants ( Keys et al. 1978 ). Photorespiratory ammonium is refixed by the plastidial GS/Fd-GOGAT cycle before entering into primary metabolism ( Bauwe et al. 2010 ). Within this cycle, GS2 catalyzes ammonium and glutamate into glutamine, while Fd-GOGAT converts glutamine and 2-oxoglutarate into glutamate ( Lancien et al. 2002 ). In Arabidopsis, there are two Fd-GOGAT homologs, namely glutamate synthase 1 (GLU1) and glutamate synthase 2 (GLU2), and GLU1 accounts for more than 90% of Fd-GOGAT activity ( Coschigano et al. 1998 ). Excess ammonium, as a sole nitrogen source, can be harmful to plants, which is linked to reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation ( Sun et al. 2020 ) and ethylene signaling pathway ( Li et al. 2019 ). It was recently found that ammonium toxicity is caused by acidic stress due to proton production in the GS2 reaction ( Hachiya et al. 2021 ). Because salt stress promotes the production of ammonium ( Skopelitis et al. 2006 ; Debouba et al. 2007 ), the salt-tolerant phenotype of the gs2 mutant can be explained by reduced ammonium toxicity. It is noteworthy that, in addition to participating in photorespiration, glutamate from the GS/Fd-GOGAT cycle also serves as a precursor of proline, an amino acid that acts as an important compatible osmolyte under environmental stresses ( Skopelitis et al. 2006 ; Szabados and Savouré 2010 ).

The receptor-like kinase FERONIA (FER), a member of Catharanthus roseus RECEPTOR-LIKE KINASE 1-LIKE ( Cr RLK1L) subfamily, functions as a receptor for the secreted peptides RAPID ALKALINIZATION FACTORs (RALFs) ( Haruta et al. 2014 ; Stegmann et al. 2017 ). Recent researches have shown that FER directly binds to demethylesterified pectin ( Feng et al. 2018 ; Lin et al. 2022 ; Liu et al. 2024 ), positioning it as a potential sensor of cell wall integrity. Our previous studies indicate that the cell wall-localized glycoproteins leucine-rich repeat extensin 3/4/5 (LRX3/4/5) and FER function in a same pathway to regulate salt tolerance, as both lrx345 and fer-4 mutants exhibit hypersensitivity to salt stress ( Zhao et al. 2018 , 2020 ). Genetic studies indicate that disruption of ABA biosynthesis gene ABA2 or phytochrome B ( phyB ) suppresses the hypersensitivity of the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants to salt stress, suggesting that ABA accumulation and overstimulation of phyB-driven signaling pathways contribute to the salt-hypersensitivity of the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants ( Zhao et al. 2020 ; Liu et al. 2023 ). It should be noted that neither aba2 nor phyB mutation restores the salt tolerance of the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants to wild-type level, implying that additional intracellular components and pathways might be involved in LRX3/4/5- and FER-mediated salt tolerance regulation. Hence, identifying such components or pathways can offer a better understanding of how the LRX3/4/5-FER module regulates plant salt tolerance.

In the past, we have conducted ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) mutagenesis on the lrx345 mutant to screen for suppressors with improved salt tolerance compared with the lrx345 mutant. In this study, we identified four independent suppressors that all had mutations in GLU1 gene. Phenotypic analysis indicated that glu1 mutation also reduced fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress. Amino acid profiling revealed that extreme sensitivity to salt in the fer-4 mutant was largely caused by the excessive buildup of glycine and proline, both of which are produced in GLU1-driven metabolic pathways. Further study showed that FER modulates photorespiratory pathway via the phosphorylation of SHM1, a modification necessary for regulating SHM1 protein stability. Overall, our study uncovers an important role of FER in controlling photorespiratory flux during salt stress responses in Arabidopsis.

Results

Glu1 mutation suppressed the salt-hypersensitivity of lrx345 and fer-4 mutants

In an attempt to find out the causes behind the salt-hypersensitivity of lrx345 and fer-4 mutants, we performed genetic screening to search for suppressors that survived better under salt stress. Given that the salt-hypersensitive phenotype of the lrx345 mutant is less severe than that of the fer-4 mutant, we conducted EMS-mediated mutagenesis on the lrx345 mutant, which was expected to be easier to identify suppressors. Here, we characterized one of the suppressors, designated slrx821 ( suppressor of lrx345-821 ). The slrx821 exhibited a notably improved salt tolerance compared with the lrx345 mutant ( Supplementary Fig. S1, A and B ), but this suppressor displayed an obvious chlorotic phenotype and exhibited a smaller plant size and less weight than the lrx345 mutant ( Supplementary Fig. S1, C to E ). To pinpoint the mutated gene, slrx821 was backcrossed with lrx345 mutant to generate an F 2 population before conducting bulk segregant analysis (BSA). Mapping result revealed a peak at the beginning of chromosome 5 ( Supplementary Fig. S1F ), where a mutation (G6744A) was found in GLU1 gene that causes aberrant splicing ( Supplementary Fig. S1G ). Notably, previous studies have shown that glu1 mutant exhibits a chlorosis phenotype under normal air conditions ( Coschigano et al. 1998 ; Jamai et al. 2009 ), the trait of which is mirrored in the slrx821 mutant. In addition to slrx821 , another three suppressors ( slrx28 , slrx85 , and slrx111 ) that displayed chlorosis but with increased salt tolerance were identified in our mutant screening ( Supplementary Fig. S1, H and I ), and BSA-seq revealed that all these three suppressors had mutations in the GLU1 gene but at distinct sites ( Supplementary Fig. S1G ). Altogether, these results indicated that GLU1 gene mutation could suppress lrx345 hypersensitivity to salt stress.

To verify that the glu1 mutation is responsible for mitigating the salt-hypersensitivity of the lrx345 mutant, three independent glu1 mutant alleles, designated glu1-1 (CS8611), glu1-2 (SALK_011035C), and glu1-3 (SALK_019917), were ordered from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC) ( Supplementary Fig. S2A ). All these three glu1 mutant alleles exhibited chlorosis when grown under ambient air conditions ( Fig. 1, A and B ; Supplementary Fig. S2B ), and all of them could suppress the hypersensitivity of both the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants to salt stress ( Fig. 1, A to C ; Supplementary Fig. S2B ), but the retarded growth of the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants could not be alleviated ( Fig. 1, D and E ; Supplementary Fig. S2C ). Moreover, the expression of wild-type GLU1 fully restored the growth and salt-tolerant phenotypes of the glu1-1 fer-4 mutant to that of the fer-4 mutant ( Fig. 1, D to G ), further corroborating that glu1 mutation is responsible for suppressing fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress. To determine whether GLU1 is specifically involved in the LRX3/4/5- and FER-mediated regulation of salt tolerance, the impact of glu1-1 mutation on the salt-hypersensitive phenotype of sos1 ( salt overly sensitive 1 ) mutant was assessed. SOS1 encodes a Na + /H + antiporter and its mutation results in hypersensitivity to salt stress ( Wu et al. 1996 ). Unlike the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants, the salt-hypersensitivity of the sos1 mutant could not be rescued by the glu1-1 mutation ( Supplementary Fig. S2, D and E ), suggesting that LRX3/4/5-FER module and SOS1 might function in distinct salt stress signaling pathways, and GLU1 is specifically involved in the LRX3/4/5- and FER-mediated regulation of salt tolerance.

Figure 1.

Given that the glu1 mutation suppressed the salt-hypersensitive phenotype of the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants, we were curious to find out whether glu1 single mutant exhibits improved salt tolerance. For this assay, five-day-old wild-type and glu1-1 seedlings grown vertically on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium were transferred to a medium containing 180 m m NaCl. After growing for additional 4 d, we found that glu1-1 mutant performed much better than the wild-type ( Supplementary Fig. S3, A and B ). We then assessed the salt tolerance of three glu1 mutant alleles grown on soils irrigated with high NaCl content and found that they all outperformed the wild-type plants ( Supplementary Fig. S3C ). Measurement of chlorophyll content and the maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II (Fv/Fm) showed that these two physiological indicators were greater in the glu1 mutants compared with the wild-type ( Supplementary Fig. S3, D and E ), corroborating that glu1 mutation confers enhanced salt tolerance.

Suppression of the salt-hypersensitivity of fer-4 mutant by the glu1 mutation is not linked to ROS

Since the salt-hypersensitivity of lrx345 and fer-4 mutants is partially caused by increased ROS accumulation ( Zhao et al. 2020 ), and GLU1-facilitated photorespiration pathway is a major source of H 2 O 2 production in plants ( Queval et al. 2007 ), we examined whether the improved salt tolerance of glu1-1 lrx345 and glu1-1 fer-4 mutants was linked to ROS. Through diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining, an increase of H 2 O 2 content was observed in the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants both before and after NaCl treatment compared with the wild-type, but the increase of H 2 O 2 could not be alleviated by the glu1-1 mutation ( Supplementary Fig. S4A ). Surprisingly, H 2 O 2 content was also increased in the glu1-1 mutant compared with the wild-type ( Supplementary Fig. S4A ), but the cause of H 2 O 2 elevation in this mutant is currently unknown. Singlet oxygen is another ROS variant that is known to trigger cell death ( Wagner et al. 2004 ), and a recent study pointed out that singlet oxygen is obviously increased in the fer-4 mutant ( Shin et al. 2021 ), so we investigated whether singlet oxygen is linked to the reduced salt-hypersensitivity of the glu1-1 fer-4 double mutant. Here, we found that singlet oxygen was indeed increased in the fer-4 mutant, but its elevation was not lessened by the glu1-1 mutation ( Supplementary Fig. S4B ). To further investigate the impact of singlet oxygen increase on fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress, we crossed executer 1 ( ex1 ) mutant with the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants. EX1 protein has been demonstrated that is required for singlet oxygen-triggered cell death ( Wagner et al. 2004 ). Our result showed that ex1 mutation could not rescue salt stress-triggered death phenotype in either the lrx345 or fer-4 mutant ( Supplementary Fig. S4C ). Similarly, the mutation of OXIDATIVE STRESS INDUCIBLE 1 ( OXI1 ), another integral element involved in singlet oxygen-induced cell death ( Shumbe et al. 2016 ), did not suppress fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress ( Supplementary Fig. S4D ). These findings suggested that ROS are not linked to the suppression of salt-hypersensitivity in the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants facilitated by the GLU1 mutation.

Fer-4 mutation results in an increased glycine content

LRXs function as structural proteins in the cell wall, and FER serves as a plasma membrane-localized receptor-like kinase ( Duan et al. 2010 ; Herger et al. 2019 ), so it is most likely FER rather than LRX3/4/5 has a direct association with GLU1. Hence, in following study, we focused on investigating the functional mechanism of FER in regulating GLU1-mediated metabolic pathways. The main role of GLU1 is to catalyze the conversion of glutamine to glutamate within the GS/Fd-GOGAT cycle ( Somerville and Ogren 1980 ), and glutamate is a key metabolite that is necessary for synthesizing amino acids like glycine and serine in the photorespiratory cycle ( Fig. 2A ). Here we defined glutamine, glutamate, glycine, and serine as photorespiratory amino acids. To decipher how GLU1 mutation reduced fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress, we measured amino acids in the wild-type, fer-4 , and glu1-1 fer-4 mutants with or without salt exposure. To align with the observation that the fer-4 mutant usually began to die at 6 d of growth on NaCl medium, we collected seedlings for amino acids testing after 5 d of growth on MS media or the same media supplemented with 100 m m NaCl. Compared with that grown on MS medium, a substantial reduction in glycine content and a considerable increase in serine content were observed in the wild-type seedlings grown on NaCl medium ( Fig. 2, B and C ), suggesting that salt stress exposure promotes the conversion of glycine to serine. In addition, a moderate decrease in glutamine and an obvious rise in glutamate were observed in the wild-type plants post-salt treatment, and as a result, the glutamine/glutamate ratio was reduced after salt exposure ( Fig. 2, B and C ). These findings suggested that salt stress might accelerate photorespiratory flux, which is probably caused by the decreased absorption of CO 2 . For other amino acids, significant increases were noticed in alanine, phenylalanine, threonine, and proline, while a significant decrease in asparagine was observed after salt treatment ( Fig. 2B ; Supplementary Fig. S5 ). No significant change was found in the remaining amino acids in the wild-type after salt exposure ( Fig. 2B ; Supplementary Fig. S5 ).

Figure 2.

In the fer-4 mutants, all the four photorespiratory amino acids were increased both before and after salt treatment compared with the wild-type, with glycine showing the largest rise among all the tested amino acids ( Fig. 2, B and C ). As a result, despite the increase in both glycine and serine, the ratio of Gly/Ser in the fer-4 mutant was much higher than in the wild-type ( Fig. 2C ), suggesting that the conversion of glycine to serine was disrupted in the fer-4 mutant. For the rest of amino acids, aminobutyric acid, threonine, and histidine were increased in the fer-4 mutant both before and after salt treatment compared with the wild-type, while proline, alanine, valine, and ornithine only rose in the fer-4 mutant after salt exposure ( Fig. 2B ; Supplementary Fig. S5 ). Among all the elevated amino acids in the fer-4 mutant, the levels of glycine, serine, proline, and threonine, as well as Gly/Ser ratio, were significantly reduced in the glu1-1 fer-4 mutant ( Fig. 2, B and C ). Unexpectedly, in the glu1-1 fer-4 mutant, glutamate content remained at a high level under normal conditions and was only restored to the wild-type level under salt stress ( Fig. 2C ). In this assay, whole seedlings were collected for the quantification of amino acids, so the likelihood of the high level of glutamate content in the glu1-1 fer-4 mutant could be that GLU2, the homolog of GLU1 ( Coschigano et al. 1998 ), or NADH-dependent glutamate synthase (NADH-GOGAT) that predominantly synthesizes glutamate in roots ( Lancien et al. 2002 ), may compensate for the absence of GLU1. Considering that both glycine and serine were highly reduced in the glu1-1 fer-4 mutant compared with the wild-type and fer-4 mutant, it is possible that glutamate produced by the GLU2 or NADPH-GOGAT in roots has a minor contribution to the production of glycine and serine in the photorespiratory cycle.

Application of external glycine aggravates fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress

Because glycine was markedly increased in the fer-4 mutant but was substantially diminished in the glu1-1 fer-4 mutant, we then tested whether application of exogenous glycine could increase fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress. On MS medium, addition of 2 m m glycine did not confer an obvious effect on the growth of wild-type seedlings ( Fig. 3A ). However, under the same conditions, the seedling growth of the fer-4 mutant was notably inhibited, indicative of the increased sensitivity of fer-4 mutant to glycine. Under salt stress, we found that external glycine aggravated fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress ( Fig. 3, A and B ). Similar to the fer-4 mutant, the glu1-1 fer-4 double mutant showed less tolerance on the medium containing both NaCl and glycine compared with that with NaCl alone ( Fig. 3, A and B ). These results suggested that fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress, to some extent, is caused by elevated glycine content.

Figure 3.

Glycine can be catalyzed to produce ammonia in the photorespiratory cycle, and excessive accumulation of photorespiratory ammonium is harmful to plants ( Britto and Kronzucker 2002 ). We then sought to understand whether the increased sensitivity of fer-4 mutant to salt stress is linked to ammonium toxicity. Our results showed that addition of external ammonium increased the sensitivity of both the wild-type and fer-4 plants to salt stress ( Fig. 3, C and D ). In the glu1-1 fer-4 double mutant, salt-tolerant capacity was also weakened when exogenous ammonium was introduced ( Fig. 3, C and D ). Intriguingly, compared with the wild-type, the glu1-1 single mutant showed higher tolerance to NaCl combined with external ammonium ( Fig. 3, C and D ). These results indicated that excessive photorespiratory ammonium negatively impacts plant salt tolerance.

To gain further insight into the behavior of ammonium in the fer-4 mutant, a reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis was performed to analyze the expression of two ammonia-responsive genes CMCU and PHD2 ( Hachiya et al. 2021 ). The results showed that the expression of these two genes was considerably increased in the fer-4 mutant compared with the wild-type both before and after salt treatment, but this increase was largely compromised in the glu1-1 fer-4 double mutant ( Fig. 3, E and F ), reinforcing the notion that the ammonium response via GLU1-mediated photorespiratory pathway is amplified in the fer-4 mutant.

Fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress is suppressed by ggt1 mutation but aggravated by shm1 mutation

As shown above, fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress is linked to elevated glycine in the photorespiratory pathway, and glu1 mutation that decreased glycine content could reduce fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress. We proceeded to study whether disruptions in other photorespiratory genes can also change fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress. Over the past four decades, significant progresses have been made in identifying photorespiratory components ( Oliver and Raman 1995 ; Liepman and Olsen 2001 ; Igarashi et al. 2003 ; Boldt et al. 2005 ; Voll et al. 2006 ; Schwarte and Bauwe 2007 ; Timm et al. 2008 ). These components include PGLP1, GOXs, GGT1, GDC, SHM1, SGAT, HPRs, and GLYK ( Fig. 2A ). Here, GGT1 , SHM1 , HPRs , and SGAT genes were chosen to analyze their genetic relationships with FER in regulating plant salt tolerance. It is worth mentioning that GGT1 functions in a metabolic step before glycine production, while SHM1, SGAT, and HPRs manage metabolic processes following glycine production ( Fig. 2A ). Akin to the glu1 mutation, ggt1 mutation was capable of rescuing fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress ( Fig. 4, A and B ). However, hpr1-2 hpr2 double mutations could not mitigate fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress ( Supplementary Fig. S6, A and B ). Because sgat mutation results in a conditional lethal phenotype ( Liepman and Olsen 2001 ), we generated SGAT -overexpressing plants in the fer-4 mutant and found no change in sensitivity to high salinity ( Supplementary Fig. S6C ). In line with previous studies ( Moreno et al. 2005 ; Zhou et al. 2012 ), null mutant shm1-2 exhibited hypersensitivity to salt stress ( Fig. 4, C and D ). Here, we found that fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress was drastically intensified by the shm1-2 mutation ( Fig. 4, C and D ), but was largely suppressed by SHM1 over-expression ( Fig. 4, E and F ). Intriguingly, the shm1-2 fer-4 double mutant displayed a lethal phenotype even under normal conditions ( Fig. 4C ), suggesting that simultaneous mutations of SHM1 and FER genes lead to lethality in Arabidopsis. A past study has shown that glycine level increases about 8-fold in the shm1 mutant under photorespiratory conditions ( Somerville and Ogren 1981 ), promoting us to hypothesize that the increased sensitivity of the fer-4 mutant to salt stress by the shm1-2 mutation could be due to a rise in glycine level or disrupted Gly/Ser ratio. Altogether, based on these genetic analyses, we can understand that disruption of metabolic processes prior to Gly-to-Ser conversion, but not those after this conversion, could suppress fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress, further solidifying the conclusion that excess glycine is detrimental to plant survival under salt stress.

The function of GLU1 is not altered in the fer-4 mutant

Genetic data clearly showed that FER regulates salt tolerance through the modulation of GLU1-mediated photorespiratory flux. To further explore this connection, we examined whether FER regulates GLU1 at a biochemical level. We first inspected the interaction of FER with GLU1 using split-LUC and Co-IP assays, but neither of these two assays produced positive results ( Supplementary Fig. S7, A and B ), indicating that FER and GLU1 might not have physical interaction. Using an anti-Fd-GOGAT antibody that recognizes endogenous GLU1, we found that GLU1 protein abundance was not affected in both the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants ( Supplementary Fig. S7C ). As a control, GLU1 protein was almost entirely depleted in the glu1-1 , glu1-1 lrx345 , and glu1-1 fer-4 mutants ( Supplementary Fig. S7C ). In addition, salt stress did not affect GLU1 protein abundance in either the wild-type, lrx345 , or fer-4 seedlings ( Supplementary Fig. S7D ). Subcellular localization analysis revealed that GLU1 was exclusively localized in the chloroplast in the wild-type, and this localization pattern remained unmodified in the fer-4 mutant ( Supplementary Fig. S7E ). Assessment of Fd-GOGAT activity showed that overall Fd-GOGAT activity stayed the same in the fer-4 mutant compared with the wild-type with or without salt exposure ( Supplementary Fig. S7F ). Collectively, these results indicated that the fer mutation did not influence the protein abundance, subcellular localization, and activity of GLU1, inferring that FER might be involved in the regulation of other photorespiratory components.

FER interacts with and phosphorylates SHM1

In our IP-MS assay performed for FER-GFP transgenic plants, we noticed that SHM1 protein was co-purified with FER ( Fig. 5A ), hinting a potential interaction between FER and SHM1. To validate the physical association between these two proteins, split-LUC, pull-down, and Co-IP assays were performed, and all showed that FER interacts with SHM1 ( Fig. 5, B to D ). SHM1 has been shown that is primarily localized in the mitochondria ( Jamai et al. 2009 ; Zhou et al. 2012 ). To understand the cellular association of SHM1 with FER, we transformed 35S::SHM1-GFP and 35S::FER K565R -GFP constructs into Arabidopsis protoplasts, and MitoTracker Red mitochondrial marker was used to stain the mitochondria. FER K565R is a kinase-dead version, and the reason we used FER K565R is because over-expression of wild-type FER may have a negative impact on cell survival ( Guo et al. 2018 ). We found that SHM1-GFP was indeed localized in the mitochondria ( Supplementary Fig. S8A ). Interestingly, FER K565R -GFP was mainly localized at the plasma membrane, but GFP fluorescence close to the mitochondria was also observed ( Supplementary Fig. S8A ), suggesting that FER is tightly associated with the mitochondria, and perhaps FER interacts with SHM1 prior to the import of SHM1 into the mitochondria.

Figure 5.

To further examine whether FER, being a kinase, is able to phosphorylate SHM1, an in vitro kinase assay was carried out, which showed that the cytoplasmic kinase domain of FER (FERCD) was capable of phosphorylating SHM1 ( Fig. 5E ). In our earlier study, we have conducted an in vivo quantitative phosphoproteomic analysis in the wild-type and fer-4 mutant with or without salt treatment ( Supplementary Data Set 1 ), and from this assay, we found that the Ser12 and Ser350 residues of SHM1 protein underwent phosphorylation in planta, and a considerable decrease in the phosphorylation levels of these two residues was observed in the fer-4 mutant compared with the wild-type under normal conditions ( Fig. 5F ; Supplementary Data Set 1 ). After salt treatment, the phosphorylation of Ser12, but not Ser350, was detected in both the wild-type and fer-4 mutant, and the phosphorylation level of Ser12 was also lower in the fer-4 mutant ( Supplementary Fig. S8B ; Supplementary Data Set 1 ). To ascertain that these two residues are the phosphorylation sites of FER, each of them was substituted with Alanine before being subjected to an in vitro kinase assay, and the result showed that replacing either of these two Ser residues to Ala largely abolished the phosphorylation of SHM1 by FER ( Fig. 5G ). To further validate that FER phosphorylates SHM1 in vivo, we examined the phosphorylation level of SHM1 in the wild-type and fer-4 mutant using an anti-pSer antibody, which showed that SHM1 phosphorylation level was reduced in the fer-4 mutant ( Fig. 5H ). Collectively, these results indicated that FER interacts with and phosphorylates SHM1 both in vitro and in vivo, and it is plausible that FER modulates photorespiratory flux through the phosphorylation of SHM1.

FER-mediated phosphorylation is required for regulating SHM1 protein stability

In order to understand the biological significance of the FER-mediated SHM1 phosphorylation, 35S::SHM1-GFP transgenic plants in both the wild-type and fer-4 mutant background were constructed. 35S::SHM1-GFP / fer-4 transgenic plants were created through the crossing of 35S::SHM1-GFP /WT with fer-4 mutant, and transgenic lines with a comparable SHM1 transcript level were selected for further study ( Fig. 6A ). Immunoblotting assays showed that SHM1 protein abundance in the fer-4 mutant was less than that in the wild-type ( Fig. 6B ), suggesting that FER acts as a positive regulator of SHM1 protein stability. Since SHM1 is detected in both the cytosol and mitochondria ( Zhou et al. 2012 ), we separated cytosolic and mitochondrial proteins from 35S::SHM1-GFP /WT and 35S::SHM1-GFP / fer-4 seedlings after being exposure to high salt for 24 h. Immunoblotting assays showed that SHM1 was detected in both the cytosolic and mitochondrial fractions, and in each fraction, the presence of SHM1 protein in the fer-4 mutant was less than that in the wild-type ( Fig. 6C ), further substantiating the role of FER in regulating SHM1 protein stability.

Figure 6.

To decipher the contribution of Ser12 and Ser350 phosphorylations to the biological functions of SHM1, 35S::SHM1 WT -GFP and 35S::SHM1 S12AS350A -GFP transgenic plants in the shm1-2 mutant background were generated, and the transgenic lines with a similar SHM1 transcript level were selected for further analysis ( Fig. 6D ). Immunoblotting assays showed that the protein abundance of SHM1 S12AS350A was considerably lower than the SHM1 WT ( Fig. 6E ), corroborating that SHM1 phosphorylations at these two residues are vital for maintaining SHM1 protein levels. Phenotypic observations showed that SHM1 WT transgene could complement the phenotypes of the shm1-2 mutant in terms of plant size, chlorosis, and salt-hypersensitivity, but SHM1 S12AS350A only partially rescued the dwarfism and chlorosis of the shm1-2 mutant and failed to reverse the salt-hypersensitivity of the shm1-2 mutant ( Fig. 6, F to H ). These results supported that phosphorylation modification mediated by FER is crucial for the biological function of SHM1 protein.

Elevation of proline content causes the salt-hypersensitivity of fer-4 mutant

During our genetic analyses, we noted that the glu1-1 mutation was more effective than the ggt1 mutation in reducing fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress, suggesting that perhaps GLU1-mediated metabolic products other than glycine also contribute to fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress. In addition to entering into photorespiratory pathway, glutamate produced by GLU1 protein is also necessary for proline production ( Skopelitis et al. 2006 ; Szabados and Savouré 2010 ) ( Fig. 2A ). Our amino acid profiling revealed a higher proline level in the fer-4 mutant than in the wild-type after salt stress exposure, and this elevation was fully reversed in the glu1-1 fer-4 double mutant ( Fig. 7A ). To understand whether the increased proline level is the cause of fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress, we observed the phenotype of plants grown on MS or MS + NaCl media supplemented with or without different concentrations of proline (1.5, 5, and 10 m m ) ( Fig. 7B ; Supplementary Fig. S9A ). Intriguingly, the growth of fer-4 mutant was largely inhibited on the MS medium supplemented with 1.5 m m proline ( Fig. 7B ). When exposed to 5 and 10 m m concentrations of proline, the growth of fer-4 mutant was almost entirely stunted, exhibiting a severe deficiency in cotyledon expansion ( Supplementary Fig. S9A ). In contrast, external proline with the same concentrations had little or no effect on the growth of the wild-type plants ( Fig. 7B ; Supplementary Fig. S9A ). These findings indicated that fer-4 mutation results in hypersensitivity to external proline in a dosage-dependent manner. Under salt stress, we found that addition of exogenous proline resulted in fer-4 super-sensitivity to salt stress ( Fig. 7, B and C ; Supplementary Fig. S9, A and B ). Similarly, the salt tolerance of the glu1-1 fer-4 double mutant was also compromised when exogenous proline was applied ( Fig. 7, B and C ). These results indicated that elevated proline is responsible for the salt-hypersensitivity of the fer-4 mutant. Proline elevation is known to boost the production of P5C, an intermediate that is thought to be toxic to plant cells ( Hellmann et al. 2000 ; Deuschle et al. 2001 ). In Arabidopsis, P5C can be converted to cytoplasmic glutamate by P5C dehydrogenase (P5CDH) ( Elthon and Stewart 1982 ; Szabados and Savouré 2010 ), thus alleviating its adverse impact on plants ( Deuschle et al. 2001 ). We generated P5CDH -overexpressing plants in the fer-4 mutant and found that P5CDH over-expression considerably rescued fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress ( Fig. 7, D and E ). These results suggested that the increased sensitivity of the fer-4 mutant to salt stress is partly due to the increased accumulation of proline and probably also its metabolic product P5C.

Discussion

The photorespiratory cycle is generally regarded as an energy-costing process, which is estimated to lead to a reduction of yield in soybean and wheat by 36% and 20%, respectively, and thus it is considered an important genetic target for breeding high-yield crops ( Walker et al. 2016 ). The photorespiratory flux rate is mainly determined by O 2 /CO 2 ratio in the chloroplast ( Hagemann and Bauwe 2016 ). As such, environmental stresses that influence photosynthetic CO 2 assimilation can speed up the photorespiratory flow, ultimately leading to diminished photosynthesis and stunted plant growth. This escalated photorespiratory flux during stressful conditions is supported by our findings showing that salt stress promoted the conversion of glycine to serine, as well as glutamine to glutamate. Another research reported that under the conditions of 150 m m NaCl, there is a noticeable increase in the protein levels of GDCH, GS2, and SGAT in C3 plant Pancratium maritimum L. ( Abogadallah 2011 ), corroborating the enhancement of photorespiratory flux under salt stress. While the photorespiratory cycle is beneficial in removing harmful metabolites like 2-PG and glycolate and protecting plants against photoinhibition ( Wingler et al. 2000 ), this process needs to be properly managed under stress conditions to prevent over-inhibition of plant growth. Currently, little is known about the molecular regulation of the photorespiratory flux during environmental stresses. In this study, we found that FER plays a critical role in regulating metabolic flux in the photorespiratory cycle, which reveals a molecular linker that synchronizes environmental stimuli and photorespiratory responses.

In the photorespiratory pathway, the metabolic level of glycine is relatively high and shows the highest change in the conditions that enhance photorespiratory shift, such as elevated light and high O 2 /CO 2 ratio ( Heineke et al. 2001 ; Timm et al. 2013 ; Eisenhut et al. 2017 ; Abadie et al. 2018 ; Fu et al. 2023 ). The accumulation of glycine during the switch from low to high photorespiratory flux is because of the inadequate capacity of GDC to handle the large flux of glycine, which is supported by evidence that restriction of GDC activity by 50% leads to a 100-fold rise in glycine content, whereas increasing GDC activity substantially reduces glycine levels ( Heineke et al. 2001 ; Timm et al. 2012 ; Fu et al. 2023 ). A recent study indicated that the large pool size of glycine in the photorespiratory cycle is due to its function as a buffer metabolite, and thus glycine is considered a nonharmful metabolite in plants ( Fu et al. 2023 ). In our study, we also found that glycine is one of the amino acids that changed the most in both the wild-type and the fer-4 mutant upon salt exposure. However, our data showed that excessive glycine is detrimental to plant survival under salt stress. A possible scenario is that excessive glycine leads to the overproduction of ammonium during its conversion to serine under salt stress, and thus causes toxicity to plants. This is evidenced by the data showing that application of exogenous ammonium exacerbated plant death under salt stress in a manner that depends on GS/Fd-GOGAT cycle.

The GS/GOGAT cycle is essential for the conversion of inorganic nitrogen into glutamine and glutamate, and the produced glutamate serves as a primary nitrogen donor for other nitrogen-containing components, such as other amino acids, chlorophyll, nucleic acid, and secondary metabolites ( Takabayashi et al. 2016 ). In plants, GOGAT is classified into two types, Fd-GOGAT and NADPH-GOGAT. Fd-GOGAT consists of GLU1 , primarily expressed in leaves at a relatively high level, and GLU2 , expressed at a low level in leaves and preferentially accumulated in roots ( Coschigano et al. 1998 ). The involvement of GLU1 in the photorespiratory cycle is supported by the fact that disruption of GLU1 gene results in chlorosis, a typical phenotype of photorespiratory mutants, and this phenotype can be rescued under high CO 2 conditions that attenuate photorespiratory flux ( Coschigano et al. 1998 ; Lancien et al. 2002 ). There is only one NADPH-GOGAT (GLT1) in Arabidopsis, and GLT1 is predominantly expressed in roots ( Lancien et al. 2002 ). In the glu1-1 fer-4 mutant, glutamate content was only restored to the wild-type level under salt stress, but glycine and serine contents were much lower compared with the wild-type. These results suggest that a large proportion of glutamate in glu1-1 mutant might be synthesized by GLU2 or NADPH-GOGAT in the root, and these pools of glutamate are perhaps not efficiently used in producing glycine and serine in the photorespiratory cycle. Given that fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress is primarily caused by increased glycine level, so although overall glutamate was not highly reduced in the glu1-1 fer-4 double mutant, dramatically reduced glycine content due to the glu1-1 mutation contributes to the mitigation of fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress.

SHM1 is an essential enzyme in producing serine in the photorespiratory cycle, and serine has been shown that acts as a metabolic input for the transcriptional regulation of genes related to photorespiration ( Timm et al. 2013 ). The positive role of SHM1 in promoting salt tolerance has been documented in different organisms ( Moreno et al. 2005 ; Mishra et al. 2016 , 2019 ; Nogués et al. 2020 ; Gao et al. 2022 ). In Arabidopsis, shm1 mutant exhibits increased sensitivity to salt stress ( Moreno et al. 2005 ; Zhou et al. 2012 ). In rice, SHMT gene expression is highly induced under salt stress, and the strength of salt tolerance is associated with the expression level of SHMT gene ( Mishra et al. 2016 ; Mishra et al. 2019 ). In photosynthetic bacteria, heterologously expression of SHMT gene from halotolerant cyanobacterium in Escherichia coli leads to improved salinity tolerance ( Waditee-Sirisattha et al. 2012 ). The increased sensitivity of the shm1 mutant to salt stress has been attributed to the elevated levels of ROS and cytochrome c ( Moreno et al. 2005 ; Zhou et al. 2012 ). Here, we propose that excessive accumulation of glycine might also contribute to salt-hypersensitivity in the shm1 mutant. Nevertheless, a more detailed exploration of the functional mechanism of SHM1 in regulating plant salt tolerance is still required. Since SHM1 plays a fundamental role in regulating photorespiratory flow under stressful conditions, its activity must be precisely controlled to optimize plant fitness under environmental stress. It has been reported that SHM1 protein stability is enhanced under salt stress due to the removal of conjugated ubiquitin by ubiquitin-specific protease 16 (UBP16) ( Zhou et al. 2012 ). In peas, a reduction in the S-nitrosylation of mitochondrial SHMT was observed under salt stress ( Camejo et al. 2013 ). Our study concluded that phosphorylation modification mediated by FER is critical for the regulation of SHM1 protein stability, suggesting that alteration of protein abundance could be an important regulatory mode to adjust the functional activity of SHM1 in response to salt stress. Our data revealed that the Ser12 and Ser350 residues of SHM1 were phosphorylated by FER, and similar to the impact of fer-4 mutation, replacing these two residues with Ala to create a phospho-deficient variant of SHM1 resulted in decreased protein abundance, further highlighting the importance of SHM1 phosphorylation in maintaining protein stability.

Proline is generally considered a metabolite that is beneficial for stress tolerance because it can serve as a compatible osmolyte, shielding plant cells from osmotic stress. However, it has been pointed out that the positive association between proline and abiotic stress tolerance is not consistently evident, which needs to be evaluated in the context of environmental circumstances, plant species, genotypes, and overall proline level ( Szabados and Savouré 2010 ). For instance, the sos1 mutant that is extremely susceptible to salt stress accumulates twice the amount of proline as the wild-type when exposed to NaCl ( Liu and Zhu 1997 ). In the sugar-signaling mutant rsr1-1 , a moderate concentration of external proline (15 m m ) can cause severe toxicity, and a detailed investigation indicates that rsr1-1 hypersensitivity to proline is due to the production of P5C ( Hellmann et al. 2000 ). In this study, we found that the fer-4 mutant was extremely vulnerable to proline, even under normal conditions, and application of external proline obviously accelerated the death of the fer-4 mutant under salt stress. It has been shown that the fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress is mainly caused by the disrupted balance between plant growth and stress responses ( Zhao et al. 2020 ), so we assume that addition of exogenous proline may intensify stress responses that have already been present in the fer-4 mutant, thereby further disrupting the equilibrium between plant growth and stress responses. Considering that over-expression of P5CDH gene that participates in the catabolism of P5C substantially suppressed fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress, we can deduce that, similar to that observed in the rsr1-1 mutant, the intermediate P5C in the proline metabolic pathway is also a factor that causes fer-4 hypersensitivity to salt stress.

Our earlier studies have uncovered that the disruption of ABA2, a key enzyme vital for ABA biosynthesis, and the disruption of phyB, a phytochrome necessary for detecting red/far-red light, can alleviate the salt-hypersensitivity of the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants ( Zhao et al. 2020 ; Liu et al. 2023 ). It appears that ABA2, phyB, and GLU1 belong to separate pathways that are not closely linked. Therefore, it remains puzzling how interrupting any of these three genes can similarly reverse the salt-hypersensitivity of the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants. Given that all these three components or their regulated pathways play a part in responding to salt stress ( Wingler et al. 2000 ; Shi and Zhu 2002 ; Liu et al. 2023 ) and the increased sensitivity of the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants to salt stress results from the over-activation of stress responses ( Zhao et al. 2020 ; Liu et al. 2023 ), it is plausible that the mitigation of the salt-hypersensitivity of the lrx345 and fer-4 mutants by mutating either of these three genes is due to the weakening of a certain salt stress-responsive pathway. Nevertheless, we also cannot rule out the possibility that there might be an undisclosed pathway that is commonly regulated by these three proteins to modulate salt tolerance in plants. Altogether, this study provides mechanistic insights into the FER-driven regulation of salt tolerance and uncovers a regulatory mechanism of photorespiratory flux in response to salt stress. These discoveries will greatly deepen our understanding of how plants adapt to high salinity.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The Columbia (Col-0) of Arabidopsis ( A. thaliana ) was used as the wild-type. lrx345 , fer-4 , and proFER::FER-GFP transgenic plant have been described previously ( Zhao et al. 2018 ). glu1-1 (CS8611), glu1-2 (SALK_011035C), glu1-3 (SALK_019917), ggt1 (CS2044347), hpr1-2 (CS799961), hpr2 (SALK_123913C), shm1-2 (SALK_089133C), ex1 (SALK_002088), and oxi1 (GK335H08) mutants were ordered from ABRC. The sos1-1 (CS3862) mutant was obtained from Prof. Jian-Kang Zhu (Southern University of Science and Technology, Shenzhen, China). slrx821 , slrx28 , slrx85 , and slrx111 mutants were selected from the mutagenized population of lrx345 . Transgenic plants expressing 35S::GLU1-GFP , 35S::P5CDH-GFP , 35S::SHM1-GFP , and 35S::SHM1 S12AS350A -GFP were generated by Agrobacterium tumefaciens strains GV3101-mediated transformation. Primers used for genotyping are listed in Supplementary Data Set 2 .

For the cultivation of plants on plates, seeds were sterilized by 15% (v/v) bleach and sown on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) (M524, PhytoTechnology Laboratories) solid medium with 0.8% (w/v) agar and 1% sucrose (w/v), and with pH adjusted to 5.8 by KOH. After stratification at 4 ℃ for 2 d, the plates were placed in a growth chamber at 22 ℃ with a long-day light cycle (16 h light/8 h dark with light intensity of 100 μmol m −2 s −1 ). For the cultivation of plants on soil, 6-day-old seedlings grown on half-strength MS medium were transferred to nutrient soil and grown in a greenhouse with 16 h light/8 h dark cycle.

Mutant screening and BSA

The lrx345 mutant seeds were subjected to mutagenesis by EMS, and suppressors that exhibited improved salt tolerance were screened in the M2 generation. slrx821 , slrx28 , slrx85 , and slrx111 were backcrossed with lrx345 to generate F 2 mapping populations. For each population, more than 100 seedlings that survived better than the lrx345 mutant on NaCl medium were collected for genomic DNA extraction and sequencing. BSA-seq was performed as described previously ( Li and Durbin 2009 ).

Plasmid construction

For the generation of proSHM1::SHM1-GFP transgenic plants, 2,000 bp promoter region together with the genomic sequence of SHM1 was amplified and cloned into pCambia1305 vector between Eco RI and Bam HI restriction sites. To generate 35S::GLU1-GFP and 35S::SHM1-GFP transgenic plants, the CDS of GLU1 and SHM1 were amplified and cloned into pCambia1305 vector between Kpn I and Sal I restriction sites. To generate 35S::SGAT-GFP and 35S::P5CDH-GFP transgenic plants, the CDSs of SGAT and P5CDH were first amplified and cloned into pDONR207 ENTRY using a Gateway BP Clonase II kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and then they were recombined into the destination vector pEarleyGate 101 using Gateway LR Clonase II Enzyme Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For split luciferase complementation assay, the CDS of SHM1 was amplified and cloned into pDONR207 ENTRY before being recombined into the destination vector cLUC. To generate GLU1-cLUC, the CDS of GLU1 was amplified and cloned into pCambia1300-cLUC vector between Bam HI and Sal I restriction sites. For Arabidopsis protoplast transformation assay, the FER K565R fragment was firstly amplified using the plasmid 35S::FER K565R -mCherry as a template and cloned into pDONR221 vector using a Gateway BP Clonase II kit, and then FER K565R was recombined into the destination vector pEarleyGate 103 using Gateway LR Clonase II Enzyme Mix to obtain 35S::FER K565R -GFP plasmid. The constructs of FER-nLUC , FERCD-nLUC (470-895 aa), and 35S::FER K565R -mCherry have been described previously ( Liu et al. 2023 ).

For recombinant protein expression, the CDS of SHM1 was first amplified by using primer pairs pMAL-c2X-SHM1-LP and pMAL-c2X-SHM1-RP, and then cloned into pMAL-c2X vector between Bam HI and Hind III restriction sites to generate MBP-SHM1 plasmid. The MBP-SHM1 S12A and MBP-SHM1 S350A constructs were generated by PCR-based point mutation using MBP-SHM1 plasmid as a template according to the protocol of Mut Express II Fast Mutagenesis Kit V2 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). His-FERCD used for in vitro kinase assay has been described previously ( Liu et al. 2023 ). To generate 35S::SHM1 S12AS350A -GFP transgenic plants, SHM1 S12AS350A DNA fragment was first amplified based on the fusion PCR using MBP-SHM1 S12A plasmid as a template and the two primer pairs, Xba I-SHM1-LP/pMAL-c2X-SHM1-S350A-RP and pMAL-c2X-SHM1-S350A-LP/ Sal I-SHM1-RP. Then, SHM1 S12AS350A was cloned into pCambia1305 vector between Xba I and Sal I restriction sites.

All PCR amplifications were performed using Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Plasmids were extracted using a FastPure Plasmid Mini Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The genetic transformation of Arabidopsis was conducted using Agrobacterium tumefaciens (GV3101 strain)-mediated floral dip method ( Clough and Bent 1998 ). The homozygous lines of transgenic plants were screened at T 2 generation on the half-strength MS solid medium supplemented with hygromycin B (25 μ g mL −1 ) or glufosinate (10 μ g mL −1 ). All constructs were confirmed by sequencing and the primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Supplementary Data Set 2 .

Measurement of plant size and fresh weight

For petiole length measurement, 6-day-old seedlings were transferred to soil. After growth for 15 d. The largest expanded rosette leaf was excised from each plant and the length of petiole was measured. For fresh weight measurement, 6-day-old seedling were transferred to soil and grown for additional 16 or 19 d. The whole rosette leaves of each plant were collected for fresh weight determination.

Measurement of Fv/Fm and chlorophyll content

The 6-day-old seedlings of each genotype were transferred to soil, and after growth for 9 d, they were treated with 400 m m NaCl for 21 d before the measurement of Fv/Fm and chlorophyll content. Fv/Fm was measured using a FluorCam 800MF instrument (Photo Systems instruments) as described previously ( Dogra et al. 2022 ). Total chlorophylls in plant leaves were extracted with 95% (v/v) ethanol. After a 5 min centrifugation (4000 rpm), the supernatant was used to measure absorbance at 665 and 649 nm. The chlorophyll a and b contents were calculated as follow equations: Chlorophyll a = 13.95*A 665 –6.8*A 649 , and Chlorophyll b = 24.96*A 649 –7.32*A 665 . Results are presented as mg chlorophyll g −1 fresh weight.

Quantification of amino acids

Amino acids were analyzed by the company of Suzhou PANOMIX Biomedical Tech Co., LTD using a liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) method as described previously ( Virág et al. 2020 ). Five-day-old seedlings grown on half-strength MS solid medium supplemented with or without 100 m m NaCl were sampled for the amino acid measurement. Briefly, 50 mg of each sample was extracted in 600 μL of 10% formic acid in methanol-water (1:1, v/v), and the solution was mixed with two steel balls for 30 s, and then placed in a tissue grinder and grind at 55 Hz for 90 s. The sample was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4 ℃ for 5 min, and an appropriate amount of supernatant was collected and diluted 100 times by 10% formic acid in methanol-water (1:1, v/v). Take 100 μL of supernatant and add 100 μL Trp-d3 (100 ng/mL), and the solution was vortex-mixed for 30 s. The supernatant was filtered through 0.22 μm membrane, and the filtrate was added to LC-MS bottle. LC analysis and mass spectrometric detection of metabolites were performed using standard protocols on ACQUITY Liquid chromatography (Waters, USA) and AB5000 (AB SCIEX, USA), respectively.

Quantification of Fd-GOGAT activity

For Fd-GOGAT activity measurement, the seedlings of wild-type and fer-4 mutant were grown on half-strength MS solid medium supplemented with or without 100 m m NaCl for 5 d. Fd-GOGAT activity was detected using a commercial Fd-GOGAT enzyme activity detection kit (Suzhou Michy Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). Theoretically, Fd-GOGAT catalyzes the transfer of ammonium to glutamine and α-ketoglutaric acid to produce two molecules of glutamate. Then glutamate dehydrogenase catalyzes the dehydrogenation of glutamate to NADH, and NADH binds to WST-8 to produce an orange-yellow compound. The compound was quantified by measuring absorbance at 450 nm. The higher absorbance represents the higher enzyme activity of Fd-GOGAT.

Split luciferase complementation assay

The split luciferase complementation assays were performed using Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. The tested genes fuzed with either nLUC or cLUC were transformed into A. tumefaciens (GV3101 strain). Each strain clone was incubated in liquid Lysogeny Broth (LB) medium overnight, and bacterial solution was then resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 m m MgCl 2 , 10 m m MES (pH 5.7) and 200 μM acetosyringone) and diluted to an OD 600 of 1.0. Following a 4-h incubation in the dark at room temperature, the mixed strains were infiltrated into the leaves of N. benthamiana . After 48 h, D-Luciferin was injected into the leaves and fluorescence was visualized using living plant imaging system (NightShade LB985) after 10 min.

RT-qPCR analysis

To analyze the transcript levels of ammonium-responsive genes CMCU and PDH2 in the wild-type, fer-4 , and glu1-1 fer-4 plants, 6-day-old seedlings were transferred into liquid half-strength MS medium for overnight. After treatment with 0 or 150 m m NaCl for 0, 6 and 72 h, the seedlings of each genotype were collected for RNA isolation. To assess the transcript level of SHM1 in the wild-type, shm1-2 , and various SHM1 transgenic plants, 7-day-old seedlings of each genotype grown on half-strength MS solid medium were sampled for RNA isolation. Total RNAs were extracted from whole seedlings using an EasteopTM Total RNA Super Extraction Kit (Promega). Reverse transcription was performed using the ABScript III RT Master Mix for qPCR with gDNA Remover kit (ABclonal). RT-qPCR was performed using iTaqTM Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad). Primers used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Data Set 2 .

Total protein extraction and immunoblotting

Total proteins were extracted from 7-day-old seedlings using a protein extraction buffer containing 100 m m Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 m m NaCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol, 1 m m dithiothreitol (DTT), 1 m m phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 1× complete protease inhibitor cocktail, and 0.5% (v/v) NP-40. Immunoblottings were performed as described previously ( Zhao et al. 2020 ). Anti-Fd-GOGAT (Agrisera; Cat#AS07242, 1:20000 dilution), anti-GFP (Sigma, Cat#11814460001, 1:3,000 dilution) and anti-ACTIN (Agrisera, Cat#AS10702, 1:5,000 dilution) were used as primary antibodies. Goat anti-mouse HRP conjugate (Bio-Rad, Cat#1721011, 1:10,000 dilution) or goat anti-rabbit HRP conjugate (Bio-Rad, Cat#1721019, 1:10,000 dilution) was used as a secondary antibody.

Co-IP assay

To test the interaction of FER with GLU1 by Co-IP assay, the seedlings of wild-type and proFER::FER-GFP/fer-4 were grown on half-strength MS solid medium for 7 d and then transferred to half-strength MS liquid medium overnight. The 8-day-old seedlings before and after 150 m m NaCl treatment for 3 h were collected for protein extraction. To confirm the interaction of FER with SHM1 by Co-IP assay, the 10-day-old seedlings of wild-type and proSHM1::SHM1-GFP grown on half-strength MS solid medium were collected for protein isolation. Plant tissues were frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground completely. The ground samples were transferred into 50 mL tubes and mixed with lysis buffer (100 m m Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 m m NaCl, 0.5 m m EDTA (pH 8.0), 10% (v/v) glycerol, 5 m m MgCl 2 , 1 m m DTT, 1 m m NaF, 1 m m PMSF, 1× complete protease inhibitor cocktail, and 0.1% (v/v) triton X-100) by vortex, and then incubated on ice for 15 min. After centrifugation at 4 °C for 15 min at 12,000 rpm, the supernatants were transferred into new 50 mL tubes. The total protein concentration was measured by a NanoDrop 2000c bioPhotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Quick Start Bradford 1× Dye Reagent (BIO-RAD). After adjusting the total protein amount of each sample to a same level using lysis buffer, preequilibrated anti-GFP beads (Smart-lifesciences, Cat#SM038001) were added in supernatants and incubated at 4 ℃ for 4 h. With the help of a DynaMag-2 magnetic bracket (Thermo Fisher Scientific), the beads were then washed four times with wash buffer (100 m m Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 m m NaCl, and 1 m m DTT, 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20) and once with 1× phosphate buffer saline (PBS) buffer (Sangong Biotech, China). After centrifugation, the beads were resuspended with an appropriate amount of 1× PBS buffer and then boiled with 5× SDS loading buffer for 10 min. The immunoprecipitated proteins were detected by SDS-PAGE using anti-GFP, anti-Fd-GOGAT, and anti-FER (ABclonal, China) antibodies.

Recombinant protein expression and in vitro kinase assay

The plasmids of MBP-SHM1, MBP-SHM1 S12A , and MBP-SHM1 S350A were transformed into strain BL21 Star (DE3) (WEDI). The bacteria were cultured in LB liquid medium at 37 ℃ until OD 600 reached 0.8, and the cell cultures were treated with 1 m m isopropyl b-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and incubated at 16 °C for 18 h. The bacterial suspensions were centrifuged at 4 ℃ for 15 min with a spin speed of 4,000 rpm, and then pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (50 m m HEPES, 300 m m NaCl, 20 m m imidazole, 5% (v/v) glycerol, and 1 m m TCEP at pH 7.5) and lysed using a high-pressure homogenizer. In vitro kinase assay was conducted according to the protocol described previously ( Allen et al. 2007 ). MBP-SHM1, MBP-SHM1 S12A , or MBP-SHM1 S350A proteins were mixed with His-FERCD in a 25 μL kinase buffer (50 m m HEPES (pH 7.5), 5 m m MgCl 2 , and 1 m m ATP-gamma-S). The mixture was incubated at 30 ℃ for 30 min followed by mixing with 2.5 m m P -Nitrobenzyl mesylate (PNBM, Abcam) for 2 h. After adding 5× SDS loading buffer, samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE and protein phosphorylation was examined using anti-thiophosphate ester antibody (Abcam, Cat#ab92570, 1:5,000 dilution). Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining was conducted to indicate total protein inputs.

In vivo quantitative phosphoproteomics

Seven-day-old seedlings of wild-type and fer-4 mutant were treated without or with 150 m m NaCl for 30 min and then ground into powder with liquid nitrogen. The ground tissues were lysed in 500 µL of GdmCl lysis buffer (6 m GdmHCl (guanidine hydrochloride), 100 m m Tris-HCl (pH 8.5), 10 m m TECP, and 40 m m CAA (2-chloroacetamide)). The lysates were heated at 95 °C for 5 min, cooled on ice for 10 min, and heated at 95 °C for 5 min again. The samples were centrifuged at 16,000 g at 4 °C for 10, and 100 µL of supernatants were transferred to new tubes with the addition of 400 µL of MeOH, 100 µL of chloroform, and 300 µL of ddH 2 O. After vortex and centrifugation at 16,000 g for 3 min, the upper layer of solution was removed, and the pellets were rinsed with 400 µL of MeOH twice. The dry pellets were resuspended with 100 µL of digestion buffer (12 m m SDC (sodium deoxycholate), 12 m m SLS (sodium lauroyl sarcosinate), and 100 m m Tris-HCl (pH 8.5)) with sonication at 4 °C until the pellets were dissolved. The protein concentration was quantified by a BCA kit.

For protein digestion, 400 µL of 50 m m TEAB (triethylammonium bicarbonate) and Lys-C with 1:100 (w/w) ratio were added into the samples, and the mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. Trypsin was added with 1:100 (w/w) ratio, and the mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for another 12 h. After incubation, ethyl acetate was added with 1:1 (v/v) ratio and subsequently 10% (v/v) TFA was added (final concentration is 0.5%, v/v). After centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 5 min, the supernatants were removed, and the pellets were dried by evaporation in a SpeedVac. The dry pellets were resuspended with 500 µL of 0.1% (v/v) TFA, and then loaded into a preequilibrated SEP-PAK Cartridge. The peptides were eluted by 80% (v/v) ACN (anhydrous acetonitrile)/0.1% (v/v) TFA and finally dried by a SpeedVac.

TMT labeling was performed using TMT10plex reagents according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 200 µg of dried peptides were resuspended with 30 µL 0.2 m m TEAB buffer (pH8.5) and vortexed at room temperature. Each TMT reagent vial was resuspended with 70 µL of ACN, and 20 µL reagent was added to each peptide solution. After incubation at room temperature for 1 h, 5% (v/v) oxyammonia was added to quench TMT reaction and the samples were incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Then, all TMT reagent-labeled sample tubes were combined into one tube, and the labeled samples were dried by a SpeedVac.

Phosphopeptide enrichment was performed according to the IMAC StageTip protocol ( Tsai et al. 2013 ). Briefly, in-house-constructed IMAC tip was made and activated with 100 m m FeCl 3 and equilibrated with loading buffer (6% (v/v) acetic acid at pH 3.0). The TMT-labeled peptides were resuspended in 100 µL of loading buffer and loaded onto the IMAC tip. After rinsing with 200 µL of washing buffer (4% (v/v) TFA and 25% (v/v) ACN) and 100 µL of loading buffer, the enriched phosphopeptides were eluted with 150 µL of 200 m m NH 4 H 2 PO 4 , and then loaded into a C18 beads StageTip. The samples were separated into eight fractions using high-pH reverse phase fractionation, and the fractionated phosphopeptides were collected and dried by a SpeedVac. The dried peptides were then subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis, and mass spectra raw files were processed using Maxquant software for peptide identification.

In vivo phosphorylation assay

Eight-day-old seedlings grown vertically on 1/2 m S medium were collected for in vivo phosphorylation assay. To extract total protein, seedlings were ground in liquid nitrogen and mixed with lysis buffer (50 m m Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 m m NaCl, 1% (v/v) Triton X-100, 1 m m DTT, 1 m m PMSF, 1×complete protease inhibitor cocktail, and 1× phosphatase inhibitor cocktail set II (Millipore, 524625)). The extraction buffer was centrifuged twice at 12,000× rpm at 4 °C for 15 min. The protein concentration of supernatant was measured using Quick Start Bradford 1× Dye Reagent. After adjusting the total protein concentration of each sample to a same level using lysis buffer, preequilibrated anti-GFP beads were added in supernatants and incubated at 4 ℃ for 4 h. The beads were washed five times with washing buffer (50 m m Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 m m NaCl, and 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100) and twice with 1× PBS buffer. The control group was treated with lambda protein phosphatase (NEB, Cat#P0753S) at 30 ℃ for 1 h. The beads were boiled with 5× SDS loading buffer for 10 min and the immunoprecipitated proteins were detected by immunoblotting using anti-phosphoserine (Abcam, Cat#ab9332) and anti-GFP antibodies.

In vitro pull-down assay

The equal amounts of MBP-SHM1 and His-FERCD proteins were mixed in 800 µL binding buffer (50 m m HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 m m NaCl, 0.5 m m EDTA, 1× phosphatase inhibitor cocktail set II (Millipore, Cat#524625), and 1 m m PMSF). The mixture was incubated at 4 ℃ for 4 h with anti-MBP beads (Amylose Resin High Flow, NEB, Cat#E8032S). After centrifugation at 4 ℃ for 1 min at 1,000 × g , the beads were washed four times with washing buffer (50 m m HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 m m NaCl, 0.5 m m EDTA, and 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20) and once with 1× PBS buffer. After centrifugation, the beads were boiled with an equal volume of 2× SDS loading buffer for 10 min. The proteins were detected by SDS-PAGE using anti-MBP (NEB, Cat#E8032S, 1:5,000 dilution) and anti-His (Abmart, Cat#M30111L, 1:2,000 dilution) antibodies.

Protoplast preparation and transformation

The mesophyll protoplasts of Arabidopsis were isolated as described previously ( Yoo et al. 2007 ). Briefly, the leaves of 4-week-old wild-type plants were cut into 1 mm strips. These leaf strips were immersed in an enzyme solution (20 m m MES (pH 5.7), 1.5% (w/v) cellulase R10, 0.4% (w/v) macerozyme R10, 0.4 m mannitol, 20 m m KCl, 10 m m CaCl 2 , and 5 m m β-mercaptoethanol) before being subjected to vacuum infiltration for 30 min. After staying in the dark for at least 3 h at room temperature without shaking, protoplasts were washed with W5 buffer (2 m m MES (pH 5.7), 154 m m NaCl, 125 m m CaCl 2 , and 5 m m KCl) and resuspended in MMg buffer (4 m m MES (pH 5.7), 0.4 m mannitol, and 15 m m MgCl 2 ). The 35S::SHM1-GFP and 35S::FER K565R -GFP recombinant plasmids were transformed into protoplasts using PEG-calcium transfection solution (40% (w/v) PEG4000, 0.2 m mannitol, and 100 m m CaCl 2 ), and the protoplasts were placed at room temperature for 30 min. After incubation in WI buffer (4 m m MES (pH 5.7), 0.5 m mannitol, and 20 m m KCl) for around 20 h in the dark, protoplasts were prepared for confocal imaging.

Confocal imaging

To observe the subcellular localization of GLU1-GFP in wild-type and fer-4 mutant, seedlings were vertically cultured on half-strength MS solid medium for 3 d. GFP fluorescence and chloroplast auto-fluorescence in the hypocotyl of each plant were visualized by using a Leica TCS SMD FLCS confocal laser-scanning microscope (Leica, USA) with a 40 × water immersion objective. GFP fluorescence was excited at 488 nm (intensity of 69.3874%) using an argon laser and collected with a detector at 498 to 548 nm (gain 111.8). The chloroplast autofluorescence was excited at 488 nm and collected with a detector at 648 to 701 nm (gain 10). The localization of SHM1-GFP and FER K565R -GFP in Arabidopsis protoplasts was observed using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica, USA) with a 40 × oil immersion objective. GFP was excited with a 488 nm laser (intensity of 60.0042%) and detected at 500 to 570 nm (gain 900). MitoTracker Deep Red FM (Yeasen, China) was used as an indicator of mitochondria. After staining with 500 n m MitoTracker Deep Red FM for 30 min, MitoTracker fluorescence in protoplasts was excited at 633 nm (intensity of 15.0007%) and collected with a detector at 660 to 670 nm (gain 600).

Detection of ROS

Five-day-old seedlings grown on half-strength MS solid medium were transferred to half-strength MS liquid medium. After incubation overnight, seedlings were treated with 0 or 150 m m NaCl for 6 h before they were subjected to chemical staining. To perform diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining, the seedlings were immersed in 1 mg/mL DAB solution (Sigma, Cat#D8001, 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20), and then vacuum infiltrated for 5 min and incubated in the dark for 8 h. The seedlings were destained with 70% (v/v) ethanol and finally stored in water. Images were photographed using a stereomicroscope (Lecia, S8 AP0). To conduct SOS staining, the seedlings were immersed in 50 μM Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green (SOSG) (Invitrogen, Cat# S36002 ) dissolved in 50 m m phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). After vacuum infiltration for 5 min and incubation in the dark for 5 h, images were taken using a stereoscopic fluorescence microscope (Lecia, M205FA).

Cellular fractionation

The cellular fractionation of Arabidopsis was performed as described previously ( Zhou et al. 2012 ). Briefly, 10-day-old seedlings of SHM1 transgenic plants in the wild-type and fer-4 were ground in extraction buffer (0.4 m mannitol, 1 m m EDTA, 50 m m Tricine, 20 m m β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.1% BSA (w/v), pH 7.8) for 1 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 4 °C for 5 min at 15,000 × g . Five percentage of supernatants were collected as total fractions. The remaining supernatants were centrifuged again at 4 °C for 15 min at 16,000 × g . Then the supernatants were collected as the cytosolic fraction and the pellet was resuspended in extraction buffer and collected as the mitochondrial fraction. The total, cytosolic, and mitochondrial fractions of each sample were boiled with 1× SDS loading buffer for 10 min and subjected to SDS-PAGE. Immunoblotting was performed using anti-GFP, anti-MAPK3 (Sigma, Cat#M8318, 1:5,000 dilution), and anti-VDAC1 (Agrisera, Cat#AS07212, 1:5,000 dilution) antibodies. MAPK3 and VDAC1 were used as the protein markers of cytosolic and mitochondria fractions, respectively.

Data analysis and statistical methods

One-way ANOVA or Student's t -test was performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0.1. Values are represented as means ± standard deviation. * and ** indicate statistically significant differences at P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively. Different letters represent significant differences at P < 0.05. The results of Student's t -test and one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons test are listed in Supplementary Data Set 3 .
