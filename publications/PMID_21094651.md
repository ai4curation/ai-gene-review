Gap junction channels are unique in that they possess multiple mechanisms for
channel closure, several of which involve the N terminus as a key component in
gating, and possibly assembly. Here, we present electron crystallographic
structures of a mutant human connexin26 (Cx26M34A) and an N-terminal deletion of
this mutant (Cx26M34Adel2-7) at 6-Å and 10-Å resolutions, respectively. The
three-dimensional map of Cx26M34A was improved by data from 60° tilt images and
revealed a breakdown of the hexagonal symmetry in a connexin hemichannel,
particularly in the cytoplasmic domain regions at the ends of the transmembrane
helices. The Cx26M34A structure contained an asymmetric density in the channel
vestibule ("plug") that was decreased in the Cx26M34Adel2-7 structure,
indicating that the N terminus significantly contributes to form this plug
feature. Functional analysis of the Cx26M34A channels revealed that these
channels are predominantly closed, with the residual electrical conductance
showing normal voltage gating. N-terminal deletion mutants with and without the
M34A mutation showed no electrical activity in paired Xenopus oocytes and
significantly decreased dye permeability in HeLa cells. Comparing this closed
structure with the recently published X-ray structure of wild-type Cx26, which
is proposed to be in an open state, revealed a radial outward shift in the
transmembrane helices in the closed state, presumably to accommodate the
N-terminal plug occluding the pore. Because both Cx26del2-7 and Cx26M34Adel2-7
channels are closed, the N terminus appears to have a prominent role in
stabilizing the open configuration.

Introduction

Investigations of gap junction channels have used independent but complementary experimental approaches to elucidate the gating mechanism of these intercellular conduits. Structural insights into the gating mechanism have been limited, however, by the lack of isolated channels in distinctly closed and open states. Previous electron crystallographic studies yielded a three-dimensional (3D)* structure of a C-terminal truncated connexin43 (Cx43) gap junction channel with each half containing 24 alpha helices arranged with a 6-fold symmetry.1, 2 Assignment of the helices to the primary sequence has been controversial, however, because functional mapping based on chemical pore blocks and structural models has led to varying conclusions.3–6 The recent 3.5Å crystal structure of the wild-type Cx26 (Cx26WT) provides a more detailed model that should serve as a basis to resolve these issues.6 We recently found that the 3D structure of a mutant human connexin26 (Cx26M34A) channels determined by cryo-electron crystallography showed an unexpected density within the vestibule of each hemichannel, which we called a “plug”.7 Our original experiments with this mutant showed significantly reduced dye coupling between HeLa cells transiently expressing Cx26M34A gap junctions.8 Projection maps calculated from images of two-dimensional (2D) crystals demonstrated a clear reduction in the plug density in the pore of an N-terminal deletion mutant of Cx26M34A, suggesting that the plug is composed, at least in part, of the N-terminus.9 Tracing of the Cα backbone in the recent X-ray crystal structure of Cx26WT showed that the N-terminus lies within the pore, against the pore wall.6 This “looping” of the N-terminus back into the pore is consistent with NMR structures of the N-terminal peptide of Cx2610 and Cx37, which contains a slightly longer sequence.11

The role of the N-terminus in gap junction channel gating and trafficking has been further analyzed by mutagenesis studies. Within the connexin family, the N-terminus is the most conserved part of the three cytoplasmic domains. Deletions of up to 9 amino acids at the start of the Cx37 N-terminus do not interfere with trafficking to the plasma membrane in either Xenopus oocytes or mammalian cells, but N-terminal deletion eliminates hemichannel currents in single Xenopus oocyte experiments, indicating that this domain is required to maintain the open conformation of Cx37 hemichannels.12 Mutagenesis studies of key residues in the Cx26 N-terminus suggest that this domain serves a critical role in channel gating as part of the transjunctional voltage sensor.13–15 Formation of the plug density by the N-termini could be an important feature of gap junction gating dynamics. Because connexins have multiple and complex gating sensitivities in response to voltage, pH, calcium, and various chemical modulators,16 however, this is probably not the only mechanism, and concerted movement of other parts of the structure is also likely to be involved in some gating responses.

In the present study, we present two electron crystallographic structures of the Cx26M34A mutant to determine the contribution of the N-terminus to the structure and function of gap junction channels. The first is a 6Å resolution structure of Cx26M34A channels, and the second is a 10Å map of an N-terminal deletion of Cx26M34A in which amino acids 2–7 were deleted (Cx26M34Adel2-7). We complemented these structural comparisons with electrical coupling and dye transfer experiments to assess the functionality of these mutants and to provide insight into the nature of this particular closed state of the channel. Finally, we fit the atomic model of the X-ray crystal structure of Cx26WT6 into a cryo-electron microscopy (EM) structure of Cx26M34A. No one structural technique or static view of a structure can fully explain complex gating movements of any channel and each crystallographic technique (2D EM versus 3D X-ray) delivers unique information about the channel structure and environment. The studies presented here indicate that connexin channels can adopt non-equivalent configurations with independent conformations of the six N-termini that cause an overall subunit rearrangement across the hexamer.

The N-terminus appears to be critical for stable assembly of connexin channels, as slightly larger deletions (e.g., 2–8 residues) reduced assembly of gap junction plaques, while deletions of 2 to 10 residues and 2 to 11 residues resulted in an almost complete loss of plaque formation and the accumulation of connexin protein in the cytoplasm (data not shown).Fitting the X-ray structure of Cx26WT to the electron crystallographic structure of Cx26M34AWe compared the atomic model of Cx26WT channels as determined by X-ray crystallography,6 to our 6Å Cx26M34A channel EM crystallographic structure. The first N-terminal 17 residues in the crystal structure were not included, as this region showed clear differences between the two structures, and we elected to focus on changes in the transmembrane segments. The transmembrane helices in all 6 subunits of Cx26M34A spread beyond the X-ray crystal structure through a radial tilt utilizing the extracellular docking surface as a fulcrum (Fig. 6, Supplementary movie). This was an unexpected observation, because the channels in the 3D crystals were surrounded by detergent micelles, whereas channels in the 2D crystals were embedded in the membrane. This expansion gave rise to a 45Å-diameter pore at the cytoplasmic entrance, 5Å larger than that in the X-ray crystal model structure (Supplementary movie). Our analysis suggests that connexin monomers are flexible enough to alter the channel diameters through a global subunit rearrangement.

The N-terminus appears to be critical for stable assembly of connexin channels, as slightly larger deletions (e.g., 2–8 residues) reduced assembly of gap junction plaques, while deletions of 2 to 10 residues and 2 to 11 residues resulted in an almost complete loss of plaque formation and the accumulation of connexin protein in the cytoplasm (data not shown).

Functional analysis of Cx26 mutants

To correlate our structures with the functional state of the pore, the channel activity of Cx26 mutants was assessed electrophysiologically in paired Xenopus oocytes. Cx26 channels were expressed by injecting cRNA into paired Xenopus oocytes and junctional currents were monitored and compared to oligonucleotide-injected negative controls. Cx26M34A failed to induce measurable intercellular conductance in homotypic pairings (Fig. 4a). Heterotypic pairings of the mutant with Cx26WT revealed that this mutant formed functional gap junction channels, albeit with 90% reduced conductance. This is most easily explained if each hemichannel has an open probability (Po) of ~0.1, which would mean that in homotypic mutant pairings, the open probability would be 0.01 (the product of the Po of each hemichannel), and therefore below the limits of detection of the oocyte system. The electrical coupling of heterotypic pairings showed current decays in response to transjunctional voltage, similar to Cx26WT (Fig. 4b). Thus, Cx26M34A channels favor a closed state, but one that is distinct from that induced by transjunctional voltage, because the remaining open channels retain normal voltage gating.

Functional analysis of the N-terminal deletion mutants revealed no electrical activity for any 2 to 7-residue deletion mutant, with or without the M34A mutation (Fig. 4c). Dye coupling of these mutants was also significantly reduced, although not eliminated. Fusion constructs of Cx26WT, Cx26del2-7, and Cx26M34Adel2-7 with a green fluorescent protein-tetracysteine tag (GFP-4C) at the C-terminus were also used in transiently transfected HeLa cells to monitor protein localization using GFP fluorescence. While all constructs formed gap junctions at cell apposition areas (Fig. 5d),9 less than 40% of the Alexa598-injected cells were permeable in Cx26del2-7-GFP-4C and Cx26M34Adel2-7-GFP-4C (Figs. 5a and 5e) compared to 80% in Cx26WT-GFP-4C (Figs. 5a, 5b, and 5c). The X-ray crystal structure of the Cx26WT channel was reported to be in an open conformation based on the crystallization conditions and the diameter of the pore in the 3.5Å resolution map.6 In our “closed” Cx26M34A structure, the diameter of the pore constriction (~15Å) formed at the TM1/E1 border was not very different from that of the “open” Cx26WT channel, whereas the cytoplasmic entrance of Cx26M34A was wider than that of Cx26WT. This plug would be expected to result in a significant loss of electrical coupling (Fig. 4a) and decreased dye transfer.8 The plug density, which resides deeply in the vestibule and is asymmetric, appears to cause elastic spreading of the subunits laterally, but no significant change of the diameter deeper in the pore at the TM1/E1 border (Fig. 7). 2D crystallization of Cx26 channels and sample preparation for cryo-EMAll Cx26 constructs described in this paper were made using the human Cx26 sequence. Cx26 channels of Cx26M34A and Cx26M34Adel2-7 were purified8 and crystallized as described before.7,9 Briefly, purified histidine-tagged Cx26 proteins were mixed with decyl maltoside-solubilized 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabaster, AL) at a lipid-to-protein ratio of 0.8~1.2 (w/w). Dialysis was started against 10 mM MES, pH 5.8, 100 mM NaCl, 50 mM MgCl2, 5 mM CaCl2, 2 mM dithiothreitol, 100 μM carbenoxolone (Sigma, St. Louis, MO), 0.005% NaN3, and 1% glycerol. The concentration of NaCl was increased to 300 mM 3 days later. The dialysis solution was exchanged on the third day. The incubation temperature was kept at 20°C for the first day, 37°C for the next 4 days, and 20°C for the final day. The resulting 2D crystals were harvested from the crystallization button and mixed with a buffer containing a cryoprotectant (10% trehalose (Sigma, St. Louis, MO), 10 mM MES, pH 5.8, 300 mM NaCl, 50 mM MgCl2, 5 mM CaCl2, 2 mM dithiothreitol, 100 μM carbenoxolone, 0.005% NaN3, and 1% glycerol). The specimens were applied to carbon-coated molybdenum grids. The grids were blotted with filter paper and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen using a plunge freezing device (KF-80, Reichert Jung, Austria).

Cryo-EM and image processingAll cryo images of Cx26 2D crystals were obtained at 4 kelvin using a JEOL JEM-3000SFF electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV.39 For Cx26M34A crystals, images of 0°, 20°, 45° and 60° tilt were recorded on Kodak SO-163 film at a magnification of 40,000× or 60,000× with an electron dose of 25 electrons/Å2. The Cx26M34Adel2-7 images of 0°, 20°, and 45° tilt were recorded at a magnification of 40,000×. Images that diffracted to better than 10Å resolution using an optical diffractometer during an initial screening were then digitized with a SCAI scanner (Zeiss) at a step size of 7 μm per pixel. All images were processed with the MRC 2D crystal processing package40 to correct lattice distortions. The contrast transfer function was corrected using square frequency filtering41 in combination with periodogram averaging.42 The statistics for the EM crystallographic structure determination of Cx26M34A and Cx26M34Adel2-7 crystals are shown in Table 1. The 6-fold NCS map of Cx26M34A was calculated by averaging three maps with rotations of 0°, 60°, and 120° around the c-axis. An inverse B-factor of −100 was applied to the 3D map of Cx26M34A to enhance the amplitudes of the high-resolution reflections. The 3D maps were visualized with the program Pymol (http://pymol.sourceforge.net/).

Electrophysiological measurementOocytes were examined by voltage clamp experiments 18 to 48 hours after cRNA injection. Single oocyte voltage clamp recordings were taken using a two-electrode technique, while oocyte pairs were analyzed with dual electrode voltage clamps. GeneClamp 500B voltage clamps (Axon Instruments, Inc., Foster City, CA) were used in both single and paired oocyte arrangements. A 1× L head stage was used for voltage recording and a 10× MG head stage was used to pass current. The bath potential was actively clamped to 0 mV using a 100× VG head stage. A horizontal puller (Flaming-Brown P-87, Sutter Instruments, Novato, CA) was used to make borosilicate glass electrodes used for passing current and recording voltage. Resistances for electrodes were between 0.5 and 3 megaohms when using the internal pipette solution (150 mM KCl, 10 mM EGTA, 10 mM HEPES at pH 7.2). The amplifiers were interfaced to a personal computer through a Digidata 1320 A/D converter. Data were acquired and analyzed using pClamp 8 software (Axon Instruments). Currents were acquired directly from the hard drive and filtered at 50 to 200 Hz. Attempts to induce current by injecting 5-fold the amount of RNA and waiting for periods of up to 72 hours did not lead to measurements significantly greater than those of control pairs.

Dye transfer assayParental HeLa cells were transiently transfected using Lipofectamine (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) with human Cx26, Cx26del2-7, and Cx26M34Adel2-7 expressed as fusion proteins with GFP-4C in a pcDNA vector system. A solution of Alexa568 dye (2 mM in 200 mM KCl, Invitrogen) was injected into cells expressing each of the constructs as gap junctions (as first identified based on the GFP fluorescence at cell appositional areas). Parental HeLa cells were also injected in each experiment to serve as negative controls. Dye injections were performed on a BX50WI Olympus infrared differential interference contrast (DIC)/epifluorescent microscope (Olympus, Melville, NY) equipped with a 40X water immersion objective. A fluorescein isothiocyanate filter set was used before injection to image the Cx-GFP-4C expression, and a rhodamine filter set was used after injection using to image the Alexa 568. Epifluorescent images of the Alexa568 dye were captured 5 minutes after each injection with a Nikon D300 digital camera and before and after images were superimposed. Dye transfer between adjacent cells was examined and statistics were then tabulated from each set of experiments.
