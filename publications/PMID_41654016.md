AIMS: Human adipose tissue is central to obesity-associated metabolic
dysfunction. ANKRD53 is a human-specific, adipocyte-enriched ankyrin repeat
scaffold protein with largely unknown function. We investigated its role in
human adipocyte metabolism and the underlying mechanism.
METHODS: RNA-seq analysis of subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) and visceral
adipose tissue (VAT) from 236 individuals quantified ANKRD53 expression and its
association with metabolic traits. In human primary adipocytes, we assessed
lipolysis (free fatty acid and glycerol release) and mitochondrial respiration
(oxygen consumption rate) after ANKRD53 overexpression or knockdown. An AAV was
used to overexpress ANKRD53 in mouse inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT).
Protein interactors were identified by immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry,
and knockdown experiments confirmed a functional role of ACSL1.
RESULTS: ANKRD53 expression in both adipose depots was markedly reduced in
obesity and inversely correlated with BMI, adiposity measures, insulin
resistance indices, and circulating triglycerides, while positively associated
with adiponectin and HDLc. In human adipocytes, ANKRD53 overexpression enhanced
forskolin-stimulated lipolysis and mitochondrial respiration, whereas silencing
impaired these processes. Adipose-targeted ANKRD53 overexpression in mice
increased lipolysis in vivo. Mechanistically, ANKRD53 interacted with ACSL1 and
promoted its mitochondrial localization, channeling lipolysis-derived FFAs into
β-oxidation; silencing ACSL1 abrogated ANKRD53's effects.
CONCLUSIONS: ANKRD53 is reduced in obesity and coordinates lipolysis with
mitochondrial oxidative metabolism in human adipocytes, promoting efficient use
of lipolysis-derived FFAs via ACSL1. These findings establish ANKRD53 as a key
regulator of adipocyte energy metabolism and a potential therapeutic target for
improving metabolic health in obesity.

Introduction

Obesity is a major risk factor for metabolic disorders, including type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease, constituting a significant global health burden [1]. In humans, adipose tissue serves not only as the principal energy reservoir but also as a dynamic endocrine organ, regulating energy homeostasis through the storage of excess nutrients and the mobilization of lipids via lipolysis [2]. Efficient coupling between lipolysis and mitochondrial oxidative metabolism ensures that fatty acids released from triglyceride hydrolysis are promptly channeled into oxidative pathways to meet energy demands [3,4]. In obesity, defects in these processes impair this coordination, leading to adipose tissue metabolic inflexibility and contributing to systemic metabolic disturbances [5,6].

Lipolysis and mitochondrial oxidative metabolism are tightly coordinated processes that allow adipocytes to supply energy during fasting, exercise, or adrenergic stimulation by sequentially mobilizing and oxidizing fatty acids [4,7]. Upon lipolytic stimulation, triglycerides are hydrolyzed by adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL), and monoacylglycerol lipase (MGL), releasing glycerol and free fatty acids (FFAs) [8]. These FFAs must be rapidly converted to fatty acyl-CoAs by enzymes such as acyl-CoA synthetase long chain family member 1 (ACSL1) before entering mitochondria for β-oxidation and ATP generation [9]. Efficient substrate channeling from lipid droplets to mitochondria is therefore critical for sustaining adipocyte energy output and preserving metabolic health.

Ankyrin repeat domain (ANKRD) proteins represent a structurally conserved family characterized by tandem ankyrin repeat motifs that mediate protein–protein interactions [[10], [11], [12]]. These proteins frequently function as scaffolds, providing structural platforms that organize enzymatic or signaling complexes to ensure specificity and spatial coordination across biological pathways. While some ANKRD proteins are involved in diverse cellular functions, including signal transduction [13], transcriptional regulation [14], cytoskeletal organization [15], cell cycle progression [16], and membrane trafficking [17], most remain uncharacterized in adipose metabolism. ANKRD53, in particular, has previously been linked only to mitotic processes [18]. However, its potential role in adipose tissue and metabolic regulation remains unexplored.

Here, we hypothesized that ANKRD53 serves as a metabolic scaffold coordinating lipolysis with mitochondrial oxidative metabolism in human adipocytes. To examine this, we combined large-cohort human adipose transcriptomics with mechanistic studies in primary human adipocytes, an adiponectin-promoter AAV to overexpress human ANKRD53 directly in mouse iWAT, and unbiased proteomics to define ANKRD53-interacting partners. We show that ANKRD53 augments lipolysis via the PKA-HSL pathway and enhances mitochondrial respiration in vitro, is sufficient to elevate circulating FFAs and glycerol and increase p-HSL in vivo upon adipose-targeted AAV delivery, and physically engages ACSL1—promoting its mitochondrial localization—to channel fatty-acid release toward β-oxidation. Collectively, these data identify ANKRD53 as a scaffold linking lipolysis to mitochondrial oxidative metabolism through ACSL1 and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for restoring adipose metabolic flexibility and improving systemic metabolic health in obesity.

4DiscussionIn this study, we identify ANKRD53 as a previously uncharacterized, adipocyte-enriched scaffold protein linking lipolysis to mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. Transcriptomic analysis of human adipose tissue revealed that ANKRD53 expression is markedly reduced in obesity and inversely correlated with adverse metabolic traits, highlighting its potential clinical relevance. Functional studies in human primary adipocytes demonstrated that ANKRD53 enhances lipid mobilization via activation of the PKA-HSL pathway and increases mitochondrial respiratory capacity, thereby facilitating efficient utilization of lipolysis-derived fatty acids. Adipose-targeted AAV delivery confirmed its sufficiency in vivo, increasing circulating FFAs and glycerol as well as HSL phosphorylation without affecting adiposity. Mechanistically, ANKRD53 physically interacts with ACSL1, promotes its mitochondrial localization, and coordinates fatty acid release with mitochondrial β-oxidation. These findings position ANKRD53 as a metabolic scaffold protein that couples lipid mobilization with mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, providing new insight into the regulation of adipocyte metabolic flexibility in obesity (Figure 7e).ANKRD53 is highly enriched in human adipose tissue but negligible expression in murine depots, reflecting a species-specific expression pattern similar to other adipocyte-related genes. For example, resistin is secreted by murine adipocytes but in humans is produced mainly by macrophages [[24], [25], [26]]. FNDC5 (irisin) translation is greatly limited in humans due to a non-canonical start codon, whereas mice efficiently produce irisin from Fndc5 [27]. CIDEA also shows such divergence—abundant in human white adipocytes [24,28], and reported to be linked to smaller lipid droplets, enhanced mitochondrial capacity, and improved insulin sensitivity [29,30], but undetectable in murine white adipocytes and confined to thermogenic depots [31,32]. Such differences highlight the limitations of rodent models for studying certain aspects of human adipose biology. Accordingly, the human-specific expression of ANKRD53, together with its pronounced metabolic effects, underscores it as a regulator of human adipose biology that is unlikely to be adequately captured in mice. These findings underscore the importance of human-relevant systems, such as primary adipocyte cultures, for elucidating its biological function and translational relevance in metabolic disease.Lipolysis and mitochondrial oxidative metabolism are tightly interconnected processes underlying adipose tissue metabolic flexibility. Basal lipolysis is a slow and continuous process that maintains steady intracellular FFAs levels, providing a consistent energy source [2,4]. These FFAs are rapidly re-synthesized or utilized and oxidized in mitochondria to sustain basal respiration, without substantial accumulation in the supernatant [5,8]. Recent studies have identified scaffold proteins as critical mediators of this inter-organelle coupling [33]. For instance, Perilipin5 (PLIN5) exemplifies a multi-compartment metabolic scaffold that coordinates lipid mobilization and oxidation through integrated actions. Foremost, via its C-terminal tethering domain, PLIN5 anchors lipid droplets to mitochondria and, together with FATP4, channels lipolysis-derived fatty acids directly to β-oxidation sites, thereby coupling fatty-acid release to mitochondrial activation under energy-demanding conditions [34]. At the lipid droplet surface, PLIN5 organizes the ATGL/ABHD5 lipase complex and modulates PLIN5-ATGL interactions to shift lipolysis from a repressed to an activated state [35,36]. In this study, we identify ANKRD53 as a previously uncharacterized, adipocyte-enriched scaffold protein linking lipolysis to mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. Transcriptomic analysis of human adipose tissue revealed that ANKRD53 expression is markedly reduced in obesity and inversely correlated with adverse metabolic traits, highlighting its potential clinical relevance. Functional studies in human primary adipocytes demonstrated that ANKRD53 enhances lipid mobilization via activation of the PKA-HSL pathway and increases mitochondrial respiratory capacity, thereby facilitating efficient utilization of lipolysis-derived fatty acids. Adipose-targeted AAV delivery confirmed its sufficiency in vivo, increasing circulating FFAs and glycerol as well as HSL phosphorylation without affecting adiposity. Mechanistically, ANKRD53 physically interacts with ACSL1, promotes its mitochondrial localization, and coordinates fatty acid release with mitochondrial β-oxidation. These findings position ANKRD53 as a metabolic scaffold protein that couples lipid mobilization with mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, providing new insight into the regulation of adipocyte metabolic flexibility in obesity (Figure 7e).

ANKRD53 is highly enriched in human adipose tissue but negligible expression in murine depots, reflecting a species-specific expression pattern similar to other adipocyte-related genes. For example, resistin is secreted by murine adipocytes but in humans is produced mainly by macrophages [[24], [25], [26]]. FNDC5 (irisin) translation is greatly limited in humans due to a non-canonical start codon, whereas mice efficiently produce irisin from Fndc5 [27]. CIDEA also shows such divergence—abundant in human white adipocytes [24,28], and reported to be linked to smaller lipid droplets, enhanced mitochondrial capacity, and improved insulin sensitivity [29,30], but undetectable in murine white adipocytes and confined to thermogenic depots [31,32]. Such differences highlight the limitations of rodent models for studying certain aspects of human adipose biology. Accordingly, the human-specific expression of ANKRD53, together with its pronounced metabolic effects, underscores it as a regulator of human adipose biology that is unlikely to be adequately captured in mice. These findings underscore the importance of human-relevant systems, such as primary adipocyte cultures, for elucidating its biological function and translational relevance in metabolic disease.

Lipolysis and mitochondrial oxidative metabolism are tightly interconnected processes underlying adipose tissue metabolic flexibility. Basal lipolysis is a slow and continuous process that maintains steady intracellular FFAs levels, providing a consistent energy source [2,4]. These FFAs are rapidly re-synthesized or utilized and oxidized in mitochondria to sustain basal respiration, without substantial accumulation in the supernatant [5,8]. Recent studies have identified scaffold proteins as critical mediators of this inter-organelle coupling [33]. For instance, Perilipin5 (PLIN5) exemplifies a multi-compartment metabolic scaffold that coordinates lipid mobilization and oxidation through integrated actions. Foremost, via its C-terminal tethering domain, PLIN5 anchors lipid droplets to mitochondria and, together with FATP4, channels lipolysis-derived fatty acids directly to β-oxidation sites, thereby coupling fatty-acid release to mitochondrial activation under energy-demanding conditions [34]. At the lipid droplet surface, PLIN5 organizes the ATGL/ABHD5 lipase complex and modulates PLIN5-ATGL interactions to shift lipolysis from a repressed to an activated state [35,36]. Other scaffolds, such as mitofusin-2 (Mfn2) and MIGA2, perform similar functions by positioning lipid oxidation machinery at droplet-mitochondria contact sites, enabling efficient FFAs delivery [[37], [38], [39]]. Additionally, as scaffold protein coordinate the spatial assembly of multiple partners, allowing even modest changes in interaction strength to generate substantial functional effects in complex metabolic and signaling. For example, the scaffold protein Ste5 converts subtle interaction differences into marked downstream signaling outputs. [40]. This paradigm supports our view that ANKRD53 functions as a metabolic scaffold in human adipocytes: by activating the PKA-HSL axis and engaging ACSL1 at sites of fatty-acid activation, ANKRD53 couples lipolysis to mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, providing a mechanistic basis for the coordinated increases of FFAs release and respiratory flux observed in our study.

Our findings identify ACSL1 as a pivotal effector mediating the mitochondrial oxidative action of ANKRD53 in human adipocytes. While ACSL1 is recognized as a mitochondria-associated enzyme that activates long-chain fatty acids to acyl-CoAs for β-oxidation, our data extend this understanding by placing ACSL1 within an ANKRD53-dependent regulatory context. In mice, adipose-specific deletion of ACSL1 reduces fatty acid oxidation by 50–90% and impairs cold-induced thermogenesis, despite preserved triglyceride synthesis, highlighting its essential role in oxidative fuel utilization [9]. Moreover, as ankyrin repeat proteins, including ANKRD1, can modulate acyl-CoA synthetases via protein–protein interaction networks [43], ANKRD53 may recruit additional partners to fine-tune fatty acid channeling. Identifying these partners through proteomic mapping, combined with localization and functional assays, will be essential for fully defining ANKRD53 as a structural organizer of adipocyte lipid metabolism and to assess its potential for preserving metabolic flexibility under fluctuating energy demands.

We acknowledge several limitations of our study. First, the absence of ANKRD53 expression in murine adipose tissue limits the applicability of conventional rodent models for in vivo validation. Second, although ANKRD53 expression was strongly associated with systemic metabolic traits, we did not directly asses adipocyte size or immune cell infiltration. Future studies incorporating histological and single-cell resolution analyses may clarify whether ANKRD53 also influences adipocyte remodeling and local inflammatory signaling. Third, while ACSL1 as a key ANKRD53 interactor mediating its metabolic effects, ANKRD53 may engage additional lipolytic enzymes or directly regulate mitochondrial function. Given that scaffold proteins often coordinate multiple partners at lipid droplet–mitochondria interfaces, integrated approaches combining high-resolution imaging, subcellular fractionation, proximity-labelling proteomics, and functional perturbations will be necessary to further delineate ANKRD53's role in adipocyte metabolism. Fourth, as ACSL1 likely acts downstream of lipolysis initiation, dissecting the specific contributions of the ANKRD53-ACSL1 axis to lipid flux will require high-resolution metabolic flux analysis and in vivo kinetic modelling using stable isotope tracers. Finally, investigating ANKRD53 function in brown/beige adipocytes will be important for defining its role across different adipocyte types.

In conclusion, our study identifies ANKRD53 as a human adipocyte-specific scaffold protein. Its expression is markedly reduced in obesity, and functionally it promotes lipolysis and mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. By interacting with the fatty acid activation enzyme ACSL1, ANKRD53 likely facilitates the routing of lipolysis-derived fatty acids into oxidative pathways. These findings position ANKRD53 as a regulator of adipocyte function with relevance in therapeutic target for obesity-related metabolic disorders.
