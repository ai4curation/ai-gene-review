The γ-carboxylation of glutamate residues enables Ca2+-mediated membrane
assembly of protein complexes that support broad physiological functions,
including haemostasis, calcium homeostasis, immune response and endocrine
regulation1-4. Modulating γ-carboxylation levels provides prevalent treatments
for haemorrhagic and thromboembolic diseases5. This unique post-translational
modification requires vitamin K hydroquinone (KH2) to drive highly demanding
reactions6 catalysed by the membrane-integrated γ-carboxylase (VKGC). Here, to
decipher the underlying mechanisms, we determined cryo-electron microscopy
structures of human VKGC in unbound form, with KH2 and four haemostatic and
non-haemostatic proteins possessing propeptides and glutamate-rich domains in
different carboxylation states. VKGC recognizes substrate proteins through
knob-and-hole interactions with propeptides, thereby bringing tethered
glutamate-containing segments for processive carboxylation within a large
chamber that provides steric control. Propeptide binding also triggers a global
conformational change to signal VKGC activation. Through sequential
deprotonation and KH2 epoxidation, VKGC generates a free hydroxide ion as an
exceptionally strong base that is required to deprotonate the γ-carbon of
glutamate for CO2 addition. The diffusion of this superbase-protected and guided
by a sealed hydrophobic tunnel-elegantly resolves the challenge of coupling KH2
epoxidation to γ-carboxylation across the membrane interface. These structural
insights and extensive functional experiments advance membrane enzymology and
propel the development of treatments for γ-carboxylation disorders.

## Full Text

Posttranslational modifications significantly enhance the functionality and complexity of proteins beyond genomic encoding. One key modification is carried out by the vitamin K-dependent γ-carboxylase (VKGC), which converts glutamate residues (Glu) to γ-carboxyglutamates (Gla) that have high affinity for Ca2+ binding. This capability enables the modified substrate proteins to associate with phospholipid membranes and subsequently assemble into functional complexes (Fig. 1a). This process was first recognized in hemostasis, where coagulation and anticoagulation proteins form membrane assemblies that either promote the coagulation cascade or inhibit excessive thrombosis1. These hemostatic proteins also engage in pathways that counteract inflammation and apoptosis7, modulate immune response, and support vascular integrity2. Other vitamin K-dependent proteins (VKDPs) maintain Ca2+ homeostasis in bone and soft tissues3, promote Ca2+ signaling for sperm maturation8, and prevent Ca2+ overfilling to ensure insulin secretion4. The γ-carboxylation is ancient in evolutionary history (predating the Cambrian explosion) and widespread across the animal kingdom9. For example, venomous cone snails produce numerous γ-carboxylated peptides targeting nerve receptors of their prey, offering a wealth of precise and fast-acting drug leads.

The γ-carboxylation is a unique posttranslational modification that is vitamin K-dependent, a landmark discovery that has saved countless lives from bleeding and thrombosis disorders5. Newborn mortality due to hemorrhagic disease is nearly eliminated by administering vitamin K immediately after birth to promote γ-carboxylation of coagulation factors. Conversely, reducing their carboxylation by vitamin K antagonists, such as warfarin, is one of the most prevalent anticoagulation therapies. Mutations in hemostatic proteins that interfere with γ-carboxylation have been identified in hemophilia B10, thrombophilia11, and other bleeding complications12,13, whereas deficient γ-carboxylation of non-hemostatic proteins leads to bone disorders and vascular calcification associated with atherosclerosis and chronic kidney diseases14. VKGC mutations result in vitamin K clotting factor deficiency 1 (VKCFD1), which exhibits spontaneous hemorrhages15 and aberrant tissue calcification, along with cardiac or skeletal defects16 (Supplementary Table 1). However, the molecular mechanisms underlying these physiological and pathogenic processes remain unclear due to the challenges of studying VKGC, which is integral to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane and lacks homology with any other enzyme families.

VKGC catalyzes highly complex and demanding reactions (Fig. 1a). Within a single binding event, VKGC processively carboxylates 3 to 15 Glu residues in various VKDPs17,18, which are typically recognized through a N-terminal propeptide upstream of the Glu-rich region19. Each carboxylation reaction requires the γ-carbon of Glu to be deprotonated by an exceptionally strong general base (pKa 25–28) rarely found in biological systems20. VKGC generates this superbase from the epoxidation of vitamin K hydroquinone (KH2) within the lipid bilayer6, and couples this reaction to the γ-carbon deprotonation and CO2 addition in the aqueous phase. This catalytic process is tightly regulated, with KH2 epoxidation triggered by the binding of the propeptide and linked Glu-rich region (Prop-Glu)21. These critical regulatory and catalytic mechanisms of VKGC, however, remain elusive due to the lack of structural insights.

Here, we report a series of cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of human VKGC in unbound form, in complex with Prop-Glu of coagulation factors IX (FIX) and X (FX), anticoagulation protein C (PC), and a nonhemostatic protein, transmembrane Gla protein 2 (TMG2), in different carboxylation states, and with (and without) KH2 (Extended Data Table 1). These structures and extensive functional analyses reveal the mechanisms underlying multiple substrate specificity, coupling of epoxidation with carboxylation, and allosteric motions during VKGC catalysis. These mechanistic understandings represent a breakthrough for membrane enzymology and inspire novel therapeutic strategies targeting γ-carboxylation disorders.

We linked this C-terminal tail to the N-terminus of VKGC in the cytoplasm, thereby allowing the C-terminal lumenal region of VKGC to bind23 the Prop-Glu of TMGs, which in turn stabilizes VKGC conformation. Consistently, propeptides predominantly possess an aliphatic residue at position −15 and a near-neutral polar residue at −9 (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 6c), whereas the reduced propeptide affinities of PC and FII are due to S−15 and R−9 substitutions, respectively37. Taken together, these favorable interactions account for the varying affinities observed for VKDP propeptides.Missense mutations at propeptide-binding sites underlie bleeding complications10. The A−10D mutation has been identified both in FIX, causing severe hemophilia B38, and in FVII deficiency, associated with easy bruising39. Consistently, we find that A−10D nearly abolishes the γ-carboxylation of FIX (Fig. 3e), similar to the effect of A−10E34. Substituting other hydrophobic residues with charged ones at the three key sites also largely reduce the activity (Fig. 3e), including L−6R associated with hemophilia B40. Alanine substitutions at the conserved F−16 and L−6 sites show reduced activities, but are better tolerated at V−17, L−15, and I−7, likely due to the retention of hydrophobic interactions. Combined mutations, V−17A/F−16A/L−15A or I−7A/L−6A, result in loss of activity (Fig. 3e). Compared to A−10V35,41 at site II, we find that L−6A at site III causes more drastic decrease in warfarin IC50 (Fig. 3f), suggesting increased bleeding risks during warfarin therapy for mutations at this newly identified −6 position. Such mutations may compromise the ability of FIX propeptide to stimulate KH2 epoxidation21 (Fig. 1f), an effect that can become more pronounced under the low KH2 conditions induced by warfarin treatment. In VKGC, our extensive mutagenesis experiments show that site III disruption by Y601A/Y603A causes the most substantial loss of activities (Extended Data Fig. 6d). Consistently, a few VKCFD1 mutations are clustered around the site III (Extended Data Fig. 5c) and reduce carboxylation activities28,29. However, substitutions at sites I and II show moderate effects on carboxylation activity (Extended Data Fig. 6d), probably because these binding pockets can form similar or new hydrophobic interactions with the propeptide despite the mutational changes. As the Glu-rich region continuously extends from the propeptide (except TMG2), a near N-terminal Glu residue, E+8 in FIX or E+6 in FX and PC, is positioned for γ-carboxylation (named c-Glu). This c-Glu residue is in the middle of a 5-AA segment that shares a similar backbone conformation among the four VKDPs (Fig. 4b, c), a length matching the pentapeptides (e.g., FLEEL) commonly used as Glu-containing substrates for VKGC20. Residues flanking c-Glu in this segment can vary in size and polarity, as the binding pocket features accommodating grooves for each residue and permits diverse interactions (Fig. 4d and Extended Data Fig. 7a, b). For instance, E+7 in FIX is salt bridged to R406 and F+9 interacts with F294, whereas the corresponding L+5 and E+7 in FX stack with Y395 and F294, respectively (Fig. 4c). Both H160 and Y395 are required for the catalysis, as their mutations, including Y395F, diminish carboxylation activity (Fig. 4f)42,43. In contrast, mutating peripheral residues interacting with the 5-AA segments, such as N158A and F294A, show moderate effects. Nevertheless, the unsharpened map shows continuous, elongated densities that suggest helical structures (Extended Data Fig. 4g), which may form within the large chamber of VKGC (Extended Data Fig. 4c).Coupling of epoxidation and γ-carboxylationThe VKGC structures were determined with Prop-Glu of VKDPs and KH2 (Extended Data Fig. 2b–e and Extended Data Table 1) to reveal how KH2 epoxidation drives γ-carboxylation. Consistent with the coupled reactions, the c-Glu residue is positioned adjacent to KH2, with a tunnel connecting the c-Glu and KH2 binding sites (Fig. 5a, b). The VKGC conformation remains essentially unchanged upon binding KH2 (Extended Data Figs. 2a, b, 8a, b and Extended Data Table 1), which is oriented with its naphthoquinone ring facing the connecting tunnel and its isoprenyl tail descending towards the membrane interior (Fig. 5c). The naphthoquinone ring is nestled between HL9 and the four-helix assembly of TM5-RH6-RH7-TM8. The isoprenyl chain, capped by HL5–6, resides in another tunnel that opens to the lipid bilayer (Fig. 5a, b). Extensive mutagenesis of residues forming this pocket shows that reducing the pocket size, such as with the pathogenic S300F, causes the most drastic reduction in both epoxidation28 and carboxylation, whereas S300A largely retains the carboxylation activity (Extended Data Fig. 8c). Several other pathogenic mutations, such as V255M48, are also located in the KH2-binding pocket (Extended Data Fig. 5e). While most binding interactions with KH2 are hydrophobic, its 1- and 4-hydroxyl groups are specifically recognized by hydrogen bonding with N290 and the D263-K218 pair, respectively (Fig. 5a, d).Amplification to a superbase for γ-carbon deprotonation, known as the Dowd mechanism, must begin with a weaker general base6. K218, identified for this function49, is ideally positioned in the structures to deprotonate the 4-hydroxyl group of KH2 (Fig. 5a, d). To act as a general base, K218 itself needs to be deprotonated at neutral pH6, a process facilitated by the interacting D263 likely through the acid-base relay (Fig. 5a, e). In support of these roles, K218 and D263 mutants nearly lose both epoxidation and γ-carboxylation activities (Fig. 5f, g). As an exception, D263E maintains ~25% of carboxylation activity, probably through retaining the acid-base interaction with K218. Although K217 has been suggested to deprotonate K218, the TM5 helix turns this preceding residue away from the active site (Fig. 5d), and K217A nearly retains both activities (Fig. 5f, g). Instead, D263 facilitates K218 deprotonation for subsequent base amplification; both residues are conserved in VKGC homologs from bacteria to humans (Extended Data Fig. 9).The base amplification proceeds with K218 deprotonating the 4-hydroxyl of KH2 to generate a negative charge, leaving the neutral 1,2-side of the quinone ring susceptible to oxygenation (Fig. 5a, e). This regioselectivity has been demonstrated by 18O labeling, which shows that the oxygenation occurs adjacent to the 2-methyl group50. Chemical modeling suggests that once KH− (i.e., deprotonated KH2) is formed, its oxygenation can occur independently of enzymatic catalysis6. Nevertheless, this process may be facilitated in VKGC because the negatively charged reaction intermediates can be stabilized by the positively charged K218 and additional hydrogen bonding (Fig. 5e).The VKGC structure reveals an elegant strategy that addresses the challenges of generating the superbase and coupling it to γ-carboxylation across the membrane interface. Dowd et al. proved that the superbase is either a free hydroxide ion (OH−) (Fig. 5e) or a dialkoxide depending on whether one or both hydroxyls of KH2 are deprotonated, respectively6. However, generating the dialkoxide would be challenging because it requires efficient deprotonation of the 1-hydroxyl group as well6, yet the interacting N290 cannot serve as another general base, and unlike K218A, the N290A mutant retains both activities (Fig. 5f, g). Furthermore, direct attack by dialkoxide is unlikely given a ~7 Å distance from the quinone ring to the γ-carbon (Fig. 5a, b), which are also separated by I296, F299, M401, and M402 in between. Remarkably, the side chains of these hydrophobic residues arrange in a circle to form a narrow, sealed tunnel that may protect and guide the diffusion of OH− as the superbase for γ-carbon deprotonation (Fig. 5a, b). Moreover, the 1-hydroxyl group, from which OH− is generated (Fig. 5e), directly faces the tunnel opening towards the γ-carbon (Fig. 5a, b). However, a leaky tunnel would lead to OH− being quenched by nearby protons, resulting in futile epoxidation without γ-carboxylation. Indeed, this decoupling (inactive γ-carboxylation but active epoxidation) is observed when any of the ‘sealing’ residues is mutated to alanine, as reducing the side chain size causes leakage (Fig. 5f, g). The dysfunctional F299S found in VKCFD1 patients28,29 (Supplementary Table 1) exhibits a similar pattern, probably also due to the tunnel leakage. In contrast, side chain substitutions with a similar size, such as I296L or F299L, largely retain the γ-carboxylation activity (Fig. 5f). Taken together, the OH− diffusion, protected by the sealed hydrophobic tunnel, is central in coupling the epoxidation and carboxylation reactions, which take place over a distance across the membrane interface.Allosteric regulationComparison of the VKGC structures in the propeptide bound and unbound states (Extended Data Fig. 2a, g and Extended Data Table 1) reveals the allosteric control that triggers KH2 epoxidation. In the absence of the propeptide, the jellyroll and α/β domains contact only at one end of their β-sheets, leaving a gap on the opposite side (Extended Data Fig. 8d). The binding of the propeptide, not only bridges this gap, but also induces rotation of the jellyroll domain and adaptive movement of the α/β domain (Fig. 5h, Extended Data Fig. 8e and Supplementary Video 1). Consequently, the bent helix (associated with jellyroll) and its preceding loop (L11 in α/β), a critical region that clamps the two domains together (Extended Data Fig. 8f), are pushed towards the membrane (Fig. 5h). This motion in turn compresses HL9 and HL5–6 below, both components of the KH2 binding pocket, thereby reshaping the pocket to facilitate KH2 epoxidation (Fig. 1f). These backbone movements, discernible from the 4.4 Å density map (Extended Data Fig. 8g–i), resemble the operation of a telegraph key (Fig. 5i). The jellyroll and α/β domains function as the knob, while the bent helix serves as the lever, compressing HL9 and HL5–6 to signal KH2 binding and catalysis. These conformation changes are consistent with hydrogen-deuterium exchange data showing protection of HL9 and L11 upon propeptide binding (Fig. 5h)51. Overall, although the γ-carboxylation and epoxidation sites are distant from the propeptide-binding site (Fig. 5h), the sequential allosteric motions promote the KH2 epoxidation21,52. This regulatory mechanism ensures that the epoxidation is initiated only after Prop-Glu binding, thereby preventing futile reactions or the undesired production of cytotoxic K intermediates.

Structure determination

Structural studies of human VKGC face challenges of its intrinsic flexibility21 and relatively small size (88 kDa) for cryo-EM analysis (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). To tackle both challenges, we took advantage of the folding topology of transmembrane Gla proteins (TMGs)22, which features a transmembrane helix (TM) that connects their N-terminal Prop-Glu in the ER lumen to their C-terminal tail in the cytoplasm (Fig. 1b). We linked this C-terminal tail to the N-terminus of VKGC in the cytoplasm, thereby allowing the C-terminal lumenal region of VKGC to bind23 the Prop-Glu of TMGs, which in turn stabilizes VKGC conformation. After further optimizations to remove flexible ends, we identified a construct of human TMG2 (1–139 AA; full length 202 AA) linked to human VKGC (27-end), with a BRIL sequence inserted in between that improves the expression level (Extended Data Fig. 1c–f).

The TMG2-BRIL-VKGC fusion construct retains both γ-carboxylase and epoxidase activities. Immunoblots using an anti-Gla antibody show γ-carboxylation of this construct in VKGC-knockout cells after vitamin K treatment (Extended Data Fig. 1g). In vitro, the purified uncarboxylated fusion protein and wildtype VKGC exhibit similar 14C γ-carboxylation activity of a Glu-containing pentapeptide (Fig. 1c). Moreover, this TMG2 Prop-Glu fusion, and a similar fusion of FIX Prop-Glu with VKGC, both self-incorporate 14CO2 following NaH14CO3 and KH2 treatment (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1h). This γ-carboxylation activity can be competitively inhibited by a free Prop-Glu peptide (Fig. 1d), and a free propeptide alone can competitively bind to the fusion protein (Extended Data Fig. 1i), suggesting that the fused Prop-Glu can exit VKGC binding sites, similar to Prop-Glu release in wildtype VKGC. Overall structure of VKGC

The VKGC structure contains an N-terminal transmembrane domain, followed by a lumenal α/β domain situated laterally on top, and a jellyroll domain layered further above (Figs. 1h and 2a, b). A pillar-shaped helical region then descends towards the membrane surface and connects to a horizontal helix hairpin. This hairpin interacts with the transmembrane domain, akin to a door threshold. These lumenal domains are arranged in a left-handed twist to create an arch-like architecture and an open chamber inside (Fig. 2a). This chamber is spacious enough to accommodate the ~60 AA Prop-Glu region in an extended conformation (Extended Data Fig. 4a). The lower part of this chamber is positively charged (Fig. 2a), encircled by a belt of basic residues that may attract acidic Glu or Gla residues24 (Extended Data Fig. 4b) during processive γ-carboxylation.

The membrane domain of VKGC adopts a unique 9-helix fold, with helices 1–8 comprising the core structure and helix 9 situated at the periphery (Fig. 2c, d). The 8-helix core is arranged clockwise into two layers with a pseudo two-fold symmetry. Across the two layers, helices 1 and 8 intersect with corresponding helices 5 and 4 to form a grid-like structure. Flanking this grid, helices 2/3 and 6/7 each form a helical hairpin. PPM analysis25 suggests that these four helices do not fully penetrate the lipid bilayer and are instead reentrant helices (RHs, Fig. 2b, c), reconciling previous topology experiments implying that VKGC contained only 5–7 TMs23. These RHs are capped by amphipathic regions that extend toward the bilayer surface.

The VKGC structure is characterized by many amphipathic helices and loops (HL) on both sides of the ER membrane (Fig. 2c). The lumenal HL regions form the bottom of the large chamber (Fig. 2a, c), where a cluster of VKCFD1 mutations is located (Extended Data Fig. 5a, b), suggesting that these regions support VKGC catalysis at the membrane interface. On the cytoplasmic side, HL1 (preceding TM1) caps on L6–7 and interacts with HL4–5 (connecting TM4 and TM5), which in turn caps on L2–3 and is overlaid by HL8–9. Near HL1 and adjacent TMs/RHs, a non-protein density fits well to a phosphatidylcholine molecule (Fig. 2e), confirmed by high-resolution MS analyses that show high levels of phosphatidylcholine species in purified VKGC (Supplementary Table 2). Phosphatidylcholine is required for the activity of purified VKGC (Fig. 1f)26 and this bound lipid is an integral part of the structure (Fig. 2c). The lipid phosphate and head groups are recognized by charged residues, including R204 (Fig. 2e). Patients carrying homozygous R204C mutations were diagnosed with VKCFD1 and midfacial hypoplasia27 due to impaired γ-carboxylation of VKDPs28–30 (Supplementary Table 1), implying pathogenic outcomes from disrupting the lipid interaction.

The C-terminal lumenal architecture of VKGC begins with an α/β domain, in which a sheet-like structure of β-strands and loops folds with a connecting helix (Fig. 2b, f). The α/β domain interacts with HL9 and with HL1–2 through a C99-C450 disulfide31. Next, a connecting bent helix engages HL5–6 and also wraps around the jellyroll domain, which comprises two stacked β-sheets, each with four β-strands (Fig. 2g). Four N-glycosylation sites (Fig. 2f, g), required for VKGC folding32, are found in the α/β and jellyroll domains. The subsequent pillar domain, comprising three helices, leads to the ‘threshold’ helix hairpin (Fig. 2c, h) that binds along the 8-TM/RH core and creates a lateral opening to the lipid bilayer (Fig. 2a), which may allow the entry of membrane-anchored VKDPs4 or the exit of membrane-associated Gla domains.

Propeptide recognitionThe propeptides of VKDPs serve as their primary recognition site for VKGC and exhibit varied affinities19,33 that impact γ-carboxylation efficiency34,35. To investigate this key interaction, we determined VKGC structures with propeptides from four representative VKDPs, with affinities ranging from high (<1 nM) for FX, medium (2–5 nM) for TMG2 and FIX, to relatively low (~20 nM) for PC19 (Fig. 3a–d). Despite different affinities, these propeptides show highly similar binding patterns, varying only in specific interactions (Extended Data Fig. 6a, b). All bound propeptides adopt an L-shape with a central helical region flanked by two extended termini, binding to the α/β and jellyroll domains of VKGC at three primary sites (Fig. 3b, c). Side chains of F−16 and V/L−17 (reversely counted from mature FIX34,36) in these propeptides form a knob that nestles into a hydrophobic pocket (site I) at one end of the α/β domain (Fig. 3d). Similarly, L−6 and I/V/F−7 form knob-and-hole interactions within a deep cavity (site III) in the jellyroll domain. The interface of the two domains binds the central region of the propeptide, which consistently adopts a helix-like conformation from positions −13 to −8 (Fig. 3a, b). This helical conformation of propeptides is further defined by the groove formed between the two domains (Fig. 3d), with the central A−10 contacting Y425 (site II; Fig. 3c). Consistently, sequence alignment of VKDPs shows constant spacing and near invariance of L−6, A−10, and F−16 (Extended Data Fig. 6c). In contrast, V−6 and G−10 substitutions in the osteocalcin propeptide result in poor affinity34,36, which can be rescued to the level of the FIX propeptide by back mutations to V−6L and G−10A37. Moreover, L/I−15 inserts hydrophobically between A−10 and N427 aliphatic chain, whereas N/Q/H−9 form hydrogen bonds that bridge K412 and E541 from the two domains (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 6b). Consistently, propeptides predominantly possess an aliphatic residue at position −15 and a near-neutral polar residue at −9 (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 6c), whereas the reduced propeptide affinities of PC and FII are due to S−15 and R−9 substitutions, respectively37. Taken together, these favorable interactions account for the varying affinities observed for VKDP propeptides.Missense mutations at propeptide-binding sites underlie bleeding complications10. The A−10D mutation has been identified both in FIX, causing severe hemophilia B38, and in FVII deficiency, associated with easy bruising39. Consistently, we find that A−10D nearly abolishes the γ-carboxylation of FIX (Fig. 3e), similar to the effect of A−10E34. Substituting other hydrophobic residues with charged ones at the three key sites also largely reduce the activity (Fig. 3e), including L−6R associated with hemophilia B40. Alanine substitutions at the conserved F−16 and L−6 sites show reduced activities, but are better tolerated at V−17, L−15, and I−7, likely due to the retention of hydrophobic interactions. Combined mutations, V−17A/F−16A/L−15A or I−7A/L−6A, result in loss of activity (Fig. 3e). Warfarin hypersensitivity is another clinical manifestation of propeptide mutations; in patients with A−10T/V or N−9K, warfarin treatment reduces active FIX to <1% of the normal level12. Compared to A−10V35,41 at site II, we find that L−6A at site III causes more drastic decrease in warfarin IC50 (Fig. 3f), suggesting increased bleeding risks during warfarin therapy for mutations at this newly identified −6 position. Such mutations may compromise the ability of FIX propeptide to stimulate KH2 epoxidation21 (Fig. 1f), an effect that can become more pronounced under the low KH2 conditions induced by warfarin treatment. In VKGC, our extensive mutagenesis experiments show that site III disruption by Y601A/Y603A causes the most substantial loss of activities (Extended Data Fig. 6d). Consistently, a few VKCFD1 mutations are clustered around the site III (Extended Data Fig. 5c) and reduce carboxylation activities28,29. However, substitutions at sites I and II show moderate effects on carboxylation activity (Extended Data Fig. 6d), probably because these binding pockets can form similar or new hydrophobic interactions with the propeptide despite the mutational changes. Taken together, identification of pathogenic mutations in both VKGC propeptide-binding pockets and VKDP propeptides underscores the critical role of propeptide interactions, which stimulate KH2 epoxidation21 (Fig. 1f) and bring the Glu-rich substrates for processive carboxylation18.

The γ-carboxylation site

The Glu-rich regions of VKDPs bind at the bottom of the large chamber, interacting along the β1 strand (in α/β domain) and then HL9 (Fig. 4a, b). This Glu-binding pocket is well-suited for accommodating the diverse sequences (Extended Data Fig. 7c) found in the Glu-rich region.

The c-Glu residue poised for carboxylation is specifically recognized via hydrogen bonding with H160 imidazole ring, Y395 hydroxyl group, and backbone carbonyls (Fig. 4e). Several VKCFD1 mutations, such as W157R and L394R (Extended Data Fig. 5d), are found in proximity and reduce the carboxylation activity28,29 and the binding of Glu-containing substrates44, probably by altering the conformations of HL9 and HL3–4. These relatively flexible regions constitute the c-Glu binding site and may also be allosterically modulated during catalysis.

Coupling of epoxidation and γ-carboxylationThe VKGC structures were determined with Prop-Glu of VKDPs and KH2 (Extended Data Fig. 2b–e and Extended Data Table 1) to reveal how KH2 epoxidation drives γ-carboxylation. Consistent with the coupled reactions, the c-Glu residue is positioned adjacent to KH2, with a tunnel connecting the c-Glu and KH2 binding sites (Fig. 5a, b). The VKGC conformation remains essentially unchanged upon binding KH2 (Extended Data Figs. 2a, b, 8a, b and Extended Data Table 1), which is oriented with its naphthoquinone ring facing the connecting tunnel and its isoprenyl tail descending towards the membrane interior (Fig. 5c). The naphthoquinone ring is nestled between HL9 and the four-helix assembly of TM5-RH6-RH7-TM8. The isoprenyl chain, capped by HL5–6, resides in another tunnel that opens to the lipid bilayer (Fig. 5a, b). Extensive mutagenesis of residues forming this pocket shows that reducing the pocket size, such as with the pathogenic S300F, causes the most drastic reduction in both epoxidation28 and carboxylation, whereas S300A largely retains the carboxylation activity (Extended Data Fig. 8c). Several other pathogenic mutations, such as V255M48, are also located in the KH2-binding pocket (Extended Data Fig. 5e). While most binding interactions with KH2 are hydrophobic, its 1- and 4-hydroxyl groups are specifically recognized by hydrogen bonding with N290 and the D263-K218 pair, respectively (Fig. 5a, d).Amplification to a superbase for γ-carbon deprotonation, known as the Dowd mechanism, must begin with a weaker general base6. K218, identified for this function49, is ideally positioned in the structures to deprotonate the 4-hydroxyl group of KH2 (Fig. 5a, d). To act as a general base, K218 itself needs to be deprotonated at neutral pH6, a process facilitated by the interacting D263 likely through the acid-base relay (Fig. 5a, e). In support of these roles, K218 and D263 mutants nearly lose both epoxidation and γ-carboxylation activities (Fig. 5f, g). As an exception, D263E maintains ~25% of carboxylation activity, probably through retaining the acid-base interaction with K218. Although K217 has been suggested to deprotonate K218, the TM5 helix turns this preceding residue away from the active site (Fig. 5d), and K217A nearly retains both activities (Fig. 5f, g). Instead, D263 facilitates K218 deprotonation for subsequent base amplification; both residues are conserved in VKGC homologs from bacteria to humans (Extended Data Fig. 9).The base amplification proceeds with K218 deprotonating the 4-hydroxyl of KH2 to generate a negative charge, leaving the neutral 1,2-side of the quinone ring susceptible to oxygenation (Fig. 5a, e). This regioselectivity has been demonstrated by 18O labeling, which shows that the oxygenation occurs adjacent to the 2-methyl group50. Chemical modeling suggests that once KH− (i.e., deprotonated KH2) is formed, its oxygenation can occur independently of enzymatic catalysis6. Nevertheless, this process may be facilitated in VKGC because the negatively charged reaction intermediates can be stabilized by the positively charged K218 and additional hydrogen bonding (Fig. 5e).The VKGC structure reveals an elegant strategy that addresses the challenges of generating the superbase and coupling it to γ-carboxylation across the membrane interface. Dowd et al. proved that the superbase is either a free hydroxide ion (OH−) (Fig. 5e) or a dialkoxide depending on whether one or both hydroxyls of KH2 are deprotonated, respectively6. However, generating the dialkoxide would be challenging because it requires efficient deprotonation of the 1-hydroxyl group as well6, yet the interacting N290 cannot serve as another general base, and unlike K218A, the N290A mutant retains both activities (Fig. 5f, g). Furthermore, direct attack by dialkoxide is unlikely given a ~7 Å distance from the quinone ring to the γ-carbon (Fig. 5a, b), which are also separated by I296, F299, M401, and M402 in between. Remarkably, the side chains of these hydrophobic residues arrange in a circle to form a narrow, sealed tunnel that may protect and guide the diffusion of OH− as the superbase for γ-carbon deprotonation (Fig. 5a, b). Moreover, the 1-hydroxyl group, from which OH− is generated (Fig. 5e), directly faces the tunnel opening towards the γ-carbon (Fig. 5a, b). However, a leaky tunnel would lead to OH− being quenched by nearby protons, resulting in futile epoxidation without γ-carboxylation. Indeed, this decoupling (inactive γ-carboxylation but active epoxidation) is observed when any of the ‘sealing’ residues is mutated to alanine, as reducing the side chain size causes leakage (Fig. 5f, g). The dysfunctional F299S found in VKCFD1 patients28,29 (Supplementary Table 1) exhibits a similar pattern, probably also due to the tunnel leakage. In contrast, side chain substitutions with a similar size, such as I296L or F299L, largely retain the γ-carboxylation activity (Fig. 5f). Taken together, the OH− diffusion, protected by the sealed hydrophobic tunnel, is central in coupling the epoxidation and carboxylation reactions, which take place over a distance across the membrane interface.

The base amplification proceeds with K218 deprotonating the 4-hydroxyl of KH2 to generate a negative charge, leaving the neutral 1,2-side of the quinone ring susceptible to oxygenation (Fig. 5a, e). This regioselectivity has been demonstrated by 18O labeling, which shows that the oxygenation occurs adjacent to the 2-methyl group50. Chemical modeling suggests that once KH− (i.e., deprotonated KH2) is formed, its oxygenation can occur independently of enzymatic catalysis6. Nevertheless, this process may be facilitated in VKGC because the negatively charged reaction intermediates can be stabilized by the positively charged K218 and additional hydrogen bonding (Fig. 5e).

The VKGC structure reveals an elegant strategy that addresses the challenges of generating the superbase and coupling it to γ-carboxylation across the membrane interface. Dowd et al. proved that the superbase is either a free hydroxide ion (OH−) (Fig. 5e) or a dialkoxide depending on whether one or both hydroxyls of KH2 are deprotonated, respectively6. However, generating the dialkoxide would be challenging because it requires efficient deprotonation of the 1-hydroxyl group as well6, yet the interacting N290 cannot serve as another general base, and unlike K218A, the N290A mutant retains both activities (Fig. 5f, g). Furthermore, direct attack by dialkoxide is unlikely given a ~7 Å distance from the quinone ring to the γ-carbon (Fig. 5a, b), which are also separated by I296, F299, M401, and M402 in between. Remarkably, the side chains of these hydrophobic residues arrange in a circle to form a narrow, sealed tunnel that may protect and guide the diffusion of OH− as the superbase for γ-carbon deprotonation (Fig. 5a, b). Moreover, the 1-hydroxyl group, from which OH− is generated (Fig. 5e), directly faces the tunnel opening towards the γ-carbon (Fig. 5a, b). However, a leaky tunnel would lead to OH− being quenched by nearby protons, resulting in futile epoxidation without γ-carboxylation. Indeed, this decoupling (inactive γ-carboxylation but active epoxidation) is observed when any of the ‘sealing’ residues is mutated to alanine, as reducing the side chain size causes leakage (Fig. 5f, g). The dysfunctional F299S found in VKCFD1 patients28,29 (Supplementary Table 1) exhibits a similar pattern, probably also due to the tunnel leakage. In contrast, side chain substitutions with a similar size, such as I296L or F299L, largely retain the γ-carboxylation activity (Fig. 5f). Taken together, the OH− diffusion, protected by the sealed hydrophobic tunnel, is central in coupling the epoxidation and carboxylation reactions, which take place over a distance across the membrane interface.

Propeptide binding allows VKDPs to be specifically recognized by VKGC while excluding other Glu-containing proteins as substrates. In the Glu-rich region, the binding of each c-Glu is specific (Fig. 4e), yet its flanking residues can vary considerably (Extended Data Fig. 7), such that propeptide attachment even to non-natural Glu-containing sequences triggers γ-carboxylation53. Given that HL9 also constitutes part of the c-Glu binding site, the conformational change explains the increased binding affinity of Glu-containing substrates upon propeptide binding54.

Catalysis of γ-carboxylation needs to overcome a difficult chemistry, as deprotonation of the γ-carbon of glutamate residues requires a superbase with a pKa of ~25–28, which can be generated through a base-amplification mechanism6. Our structures and mutational analyses reveals that D263 facilitates the deprotonation of K218, which in turn deprotonates KH249. The resulting KH− is subsequently oxygenated to generate the OH− superbase, which deprotonates the γ-carbon to form a carbanion (Fig. 5e). Chemical modeling experiments and quantum chemical analyses show that KH− oxygenation provides ample amount of energy for this process50,55. In principle, energy input is required for carboxylation reactions because CO2 is a low-energy molecule56. Through the sequential deprotonation that enables KH− oxygenation, VKGC generates the high-energy γ-carbanion intermediate necessary for CO2 addition.

The coupling of epoxidation and carboxylation must bridge the physical separation between the two reaction centers (Fig. 5c), a challenge elegantly resolved by employing OH− as a diffusive superbase. The distance from where OH− is generated to the γ-proton is ~4–5 Å (Fig. 5a, e), which is relatively short for OH− (1.1 Å diameter) to diffuse. The OH− is confined within a sealed hydrophobic tunnel (~2 Å diameter) that connects the reactions centers and defines the diffusion path (Fig. 5b). Surrounding this tunnel, M401 and M402 are vital to carboxylation activity (Fig. 5f) because they contain electron-rich sulfur groups that repel OH⁻, likely facilitating its attack on the γ-proton. The subsequent CO2 addition to γ-carbanion, i.e., carboxylation, is tightly coupled to epoxidation in the native enzyme57. However, decoupling occurs with smaller residue substitutions at the hydrophobic tunnel that cause leakage and OH− quenching (Fig. 5f, g). The decoupling occurs also with a limited CO2 supply57, or H160A and Y395A mutations outside the tunnel (Figs. 4f and 5g). H160 may facilitate CO2 addition43 and, together with Y395, could stabilize the carbanion intermediate58. The carbanion may tautomerize with the carboxyl group to generate an enolate (Fig. 5e). Thus, VKGC likely conforms to the general mechanisms of carboxylases (or decarboxylases), which generate and stabilize an enolate as the nucleophile and accommodate a CO2 molecule nearby for the reaction56.

The large chamber in VKGC structure regulates the processivity of VKDPs. In naturally occurring VKDPs, Glu residues from positions +6 to +40 are carboxylated, whereas those at +1 to +5 or beyond +40 remain unmodified by VKGC. This specificity is due to the propeptide binding at the top of the large chamber of VKGC, requiring a minimum of 4–5 AA to reach the carboxylation site at the membrane interface (Fig. 4a). Conversely, the ~40 AA limitation may arise from steric overcrowding for longer polypeptides in the chamber, which may also exclude nonnatural Glu-containing substrates when a VKDP Prop-Glu is bound (Extended Data Fig. 4a, b).

Overall, the structural findings provide a framework that integrates half century of extensive biochemical data59,60 into a coherent molecular picture, showing how VKGC couples epoxidation with carboxylation, manages the specificity of the propeptide and the promiscuity of the tethered Glu-rich region, and allosterically regulates multiple substrates for sequential reactions. Reflecting this complexity, VKCFD1 mutations are clustered in several distinct structural regions, likely influencing various catalytic stages (Extended Data Fig. 5). The high-resolution structures lay the groundwork for the development of therapeutic strategies to selectively tackle hemostatic and non-hemostatic disorders associated with γ-carboxylation defects.

Cryo-EM data processing, model building and refinement

Cryo-EM data were processed using cryoSPARC v4.0.367, and an exemplified procedure for VKGC-TMG2 fusion is outlined in Extended Data Fig. 3c. Briefly, a total of 6226 dose-fractionated movies were subjected to Patch motion correction using MotionCor268 and Patch CTF estimation using CTFFIND-4.1.1369, with data downsampled by 3/4 (0.885333 Å/pixel after downsampling). Images with the defocus values outside of −0.6 to −2.8 μm or CTF fit resolutions worse than 5 Å were excluded from further steps. A total of 3,747,388 particles were picked using the Blob picker followed by Template picker in cryoSPARC. Junk particles were removed through two rounds of 2D classifications. A total of 1,262,119 particles from the good 2D classes were used for Ab-initio reconstruction of four maps. The initial models were set as the starting references for heterogeneous refinement (3D classification). Two rounds of heterogeneous refinement produced 171,392 particles from the good class. The good 3D class was then subjected to further non-uniform and CTF refinements to generate the final map with a 3.34 Å resolution. Map resolutions were determined by gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) at 0.143 between the two half-maps. Local resolution variation was estimated from the two half-maps in cryoSPARC. The density maps were sharpened by B factors. For the dataset of VKGC-FIX fusion protein in the partially carboxylated state, unsharpened map or blurring with a positive B factor showed improved density in the Gla-rich region.

Mass spectrometry detection of γ-carboxylation

HEK 293S GnTI− cells expressing the TMG2-BRIL-VKGC fusion protein were grown in Freestyle medium containing 10 μM warfarin to block endogenous γ-carboxylation. After quenching the reaction with 10 μL of 10 M NaOH, the samples were processed using a filter aided sample preparation (FASP) method80. The quenched sample and 200 μL of 8 M urea were loaded onto a Microcon centrifugal filter (Millipore Sigma, 30 kDa cutoff) pre-washed with 100 mM [NH4]HCO3. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C, followed by three washes with urea through repeated centrifugation.
