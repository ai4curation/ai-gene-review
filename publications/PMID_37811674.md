## Full Text

Abstract

Excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance is carefully maintained by the nervous system. The neurotransmitter GABA has been reported to be co‐released with its sole precursor, the neurotransmitter glutamate. The genetic and circuitry mechanisms to establish the balance between GABAergic and glutamatergic signaling have not been fully elucidated. Caenorhabditis elegans DVB is an excitatory GABAergic motoneuron that drives the expulsion step in the defecation motor program. We show here that in addition to UNC‐47, the vesicular GABA transporter, DVB also expresses EAT‐4, a vesicular glutamate transporter. UBR‐1, a conserved ubiquitin ligase, regulates DVB activity by suppressing a bidirectional inhibitory glutamate signaling. Loss of UBR‐1 impairs DVB Ca 2+ activity and expulsion frequency. These impairments are fully compensated by the knockdown of EAT‐4 in DVB. Further, glutamate‐gated chloride channels GLC‐3 and GLC‐2/4 receive DVB's glutamate signals to inhibit DVB and enteric muscle activity, respectively. These results implicate an intrinsic cellular mechanism that promotes the inherent asymmetric neural activity. We propose that elevated glutamate in ubr‐1 mutants, being the cause of the E/I shift, potentially contributes to Johanson Blizzard syndrome.

Keywords: co‐release, defecation, E/I balance, glutamate metabolism, ubr‐1

Subject Categories: Neuroscience

This study reveals an unexpected role and mechanism of the E3 ubiquitin ligase UBR‐1 in the balance between excitatory GABA and inhibitory glutamate transmission in the C. elegans defecation motor circuit.

Introduction

A neural network maintains an equilibrium between excitatory (E) and inhibitory (I) synaptic signaling (Yizhar et al , 2011 ). Disruptions to the E/I balance, whether due to increased or decreased levels of inhibitory signaling, appear to be a recurring theme in neurodevelopmental and neurological disorders (Rubenstein & Merzenich, 2003 ). Understanding the cellular processes that affect the E/I balance is critical to our understanding of neural homeostasis and its associated disorders.

The ubiquitin‐proteasome system plays a pivotal role in the spatial and temporal regulation of protein metabolism, impacting various aspects of cellular processes (Bachmair et al , 1986 ; Gardner et al , 2005 ; Haglund & Dikic, 2005 ; Varshavsky, 2014 ). A vital component of the systems is the substrate‐recognition factor, the ubiquitin ligases (E3s; Hershko et al , 1983 ). UBR1 is one such E3 ligase, and loss‐of‐function mutations in human UBR1 lead to Johanson Blizzard syndrome (JBS), a rare autosomal recessive disorder with broad developmental and neurological symptoms, including abnormal facial appearance, exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, and varying degrees of mental retardation (Johanson & Blizzard, 1971 ; Daentl et al , 1979 ). Pathophysiology of JBS has remained unclear, and there is currently no causal treatment available (Sukalo et al , 2014 ). Yeast and mouse loss‐of‐function models for human UBR1's closest homologs do not exhibit JBS‐like syndromes, or phenotypes that provide insights into the underlying cellular defects associated with JBS (Varshavsky, 1996 ; Balogh et al , 2002 ).

Considerable attention has been devoted to identifying UBR1 substrates. Some critical substrates include the cohesion complex subunit SCC1, the transcriptional activator Msn4 and the hydroxyaspartate dehydratase Sry1 in yeast (Rao et al , 2001 ; Kim et al , 2014 ); the pluripotency factor LIN‐28 in Caenorhabditis elegans (Weaver et al , 2017 ); the GTPase activators RGS4/5 (Hu et al , 2005 ; Lee et al , 2011 ), the breast cancer‐associated tumor suppressor BRCA1 (Xu et al , 2012 ), and the PTEN induced putative kinase 1 PINK1 in mammalian cells (Yamano & Youle, 2013 ). However, it remains unclear whether these substrates share functional conservation across species and how their dys‐regulation might relate to the various pathological features of JBS (Choi et al , 2010 ; Matta‐Camacho et al , 2010 ).

An alternative and complementary approach to address JBS is through the characterization of genetic modifiers of phenotypes exhibited by an UBR1 animal model. This approach is physiologically relevant as it can reveal dys‐regulated signaling pathways in the absence of UBR1, along with genetic suppressors of altered behaviors (Kelly & Stanley, 2001 ).

Caenorhabditis elegans offers a suitable experimental platform for such investigations (Chalfie et al , 1985 ; Kawano et al , 2011 ; Petrash et al , 2013 ; Stawicki et al , 2013 ; Lim et al , 2016 ; Gao et al , 2018 ; Huang et al , 2019 ). C. elegans has a single homolog of the UBR family protein UBR‐1 (Fig EV1A ), which contains all main conserved functional domains and is consistently expressed in muscles and neurons (Chitturi et al , 2018 ). Previously, we demonstrated that the loss of UBR‐1 disrupts glutamate homeostasis, leading to elevated glutamate levels (Chitturi et al , 2018 ). One behavioral consequence of elevated glutamate is stiff body bending during backward movement, resulting from the simultaneous activation of the execution motor neurons (Chitturi et al , 2018 ).

Figure EV1. The C. elegans UBR‐1 orthologs and structure.

Glutamate is an abundant and essential metabolite (Kelly & Stanley, 2001 ) and serves as a predominant excitatory neurotransmitter. Being the sole precursor of γ‐amino butyric acid (GABA), an inhibitory neurotransmitter (Costa et al , 1979 ), glutamate, has been reported to be coexist with GABA in both glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons (Beltrán & Gutiérrez, 2012 ; Vaaga et al , 2014 ; Root et al , 2018 ). In a rat model of depression, treatment with an antidepressant leads to reduced GABA/glutamate‐mediated synaptic responses and increased GABA signaling after (Shabel et al , 2014 ). Whether elevated glutamate level in C. elegans ubr‐1 mutant results in an imbalanced GABA/glutamate signaling remains an intriguing and unexplored question.

Caenorhabditis elegans DVB neuron exhibits rhythmic activity, contracting enteric muscles through an excitatory GABAergic signaling (Beg & Jorgensen, 2003 ; Branicky & Hekimi, 2006 ; Wang et al , 2013 ). In this study, we show that loss of UBR‐1 impairs rhythmic expulsion, a motor behavior controlled by DVB. We find that DVB expresses not only the vesicular GABA transporter (vGAT) UNC‐47 but also the vesicular glutamate transporter (vGluT) EAT‐4. In ubr‐1 mutants, calcium (Ca 2+ ) activity of the DVB neuron is decreased. The removal of EAT‐4 from DVB fully rescues the expulsion defect in ubr‐1 mutants and restores the Ca 2+ activity of the DVB neuron. Additionally, removing either of the inhibitory glutamate‐gated chloride channels (GluCl), GLC‐3 in DVB and GLC‐2/GLC‐4 in intestinal muscles also rescue the expulsion defects. Lastly, we demonstrate that exogenous glutamate perfusion led to an instantaneous inhibition of the Ca 2+ activity of DVB, an effect that is attenuated by low extracellular chloride and the glc‐3 mutation. UBR‐1, by regulating the level of glutamate, maintains an inherent imbalance of excitatory and inhibitory signaling in the DVB neuron, specifically, by suppressing inhibitory glutamatergic signaling at a neuron that primarily functions through excitatory GABAergic signaling.

Results

ubr‐1 loss‐of‐function mutants exhibit reduced expulsion in the defecation motor program

ubr‐1 mutants exhibit multiple motor defects. In addition to reduced body bending (Chitturi et al , 2018 ), they also exhibit defect in defecation, a rhythmic motor program that occurs every 45–50 s.

The defecation motor program comprises a stereotypically ordered, three‐step motor sequence: the posterior body contraction (pBoc), the anterior body contraction (aBoc), and the final expulsion step (Exp; Fig 1A ). We found that while the pBoc and aBoc steps in ubr‐1 remained unaffected by ubr‐1 mutations (pBoc 13.6 ± 0.2/10 min, aBoc 13.6 ± 0.2/10 min in ubr‐1 ; pBoc 13.23 ± 0.17/10 min, aBoc 13.23 ± 0.17/10 min in wild type; Fig 1B ), the expulsion frequency in ubr‐1(hp684) loss‐of‐function mutants (7.55 ± 0.34/10 min) was significantly reduced compared to wild‐type animals (12.9 ± 0.21/10 min; Fig 1B ). Importantly, this deficiency was fully rescued by restored expression of a UBR‐1 genomic fragment under its endogenous promoter (P ubr‐1 ; Fig 1B and C ). These observations suggest that the ubr‐1 gene is specifically required for the expulsion step.

Figure 1. ubr‐1 regulates the defecation Exp step.

The hp684 mutant allele leads to a nonsense truncation of the last 194 amino acids of UBR‐1(Q1864X; Fig EV1B ), which raises concerns of the residual protein function. We further analyzed the expulsion rate in three other ubr‐1 alleles that miss one or more critical conserved domains (Fig EV1B ): hp821 (E34X), which contains an N‐terminal stop codon mutant that predicts the loss of all domains; hp821 hp833 (E34X, E1315X), which contains an additional premature internal stop codon in the RING finger domain; and hp865 , which lacks the entire RING finger domain by replacing it with an SL2‐NLS::GFP (Chitturi et al , 2018 ). All these alleles exhibited a similar degree of expulsion defects as the hp684 allele ( hp684 7.55 ± 0.34/10 min; hp821 6.8 ± 0.36/10 min; hp821hp833 7.6 ± 0.47/10 min; hp865 8 ± 0.33/10 min). To briefly summarize these changes, we used “Exp/Cycle” in the following quantifications (WT 0.98 ± 0.01; hp684 0.55 ± 0.02; hp821 0.49 ± 0.03; hp821hp833 0.53 ± 0.03; hp865 0.57 ± 0.03; Wang et al , 2013 ). Importantly, all these alleles selectively regulated the expulsion frequency without affecting the pBoc and aBoc steps (Figs 1D , and EV1C and D ); the UBR‐1 genomic fragment successfully rescued expulsion frequency in all alleles (Figs 1D , and EV1C and D ).

These results demonstrate that the ~50% reduction in expulsion results from a complete loss of functional UBR‐1 protein. It is worth noting that we also found that the expression of the human ortholog of UBR1 restored the reduced expulsion in ubr‐1 loss‐of‐function mutants (Fig 1D ), indicating functional conservation. We analyzed the hp684 allele in most follow‐up experiments.

ubr‐1 promotes the expulsion motor step through the GABAergic neuron that contracts enteric muscles

UBR‐1 expression has been observed consistently in neurons and muscles (Kwon et al , 2001 ; Hwang et al , 2011 ; Chitturi et al , 2018 ). To pinpoint the tissue requirement for expulsion, we used tissue‐specific promoters to restore UBR‐1 expression in ubr‐1 mutants.

The expulsion process is driven by the contraction of enteric muscles, facilitated by two classes of excitatory GABAergic neurons DVB and AVL (Fig 1A ; Thomas, 1990 ; McLntire et al , 1993b ; Branicky & Hekimi, 2006 ). We found that pan‐neuronal expression of UBR‐1, but not a pan‐muscle expression, fully restored the expulsion frequency (Fig 1E and F ). Furthermore, restoring UBR‐1 in the DVB, AVL and D‐class GABAergic neurons (D‐MNs, P unc‐47 , 0.97 ± 0.01 exp/cycle) or in the DVB, AVL and RIS neurons (P lim‐6 (int3) , 0.94 ± 0.02 exp/cycle; Hobert et al , 1999 ) also completely restored the defecation frequency in ubr‐1 mutants (Figs 1E and F , and EV2A ). Specifically restoring UBR‐1 expression solely in the DVB neuron (P flp‐10 , 0.92 ± 0.03 exp/cycle; Choi et al , 2021 ), also rescued the expulsion defect of ubr‐1 mutants. In contrast, the expression of UBR‐1 in RIS neurons (P flp‐11 , 0.61 ± 0.01 exp/cycle; Turek et al , 2016 ), did not rescued the expulsion defect (Figs 1E and F , and EV2B ), similar to restoring UBR‐1 expression in either D‐class neurons (P unc‐25s , 0.57 ± 0.06 exp/cycle; Jin et al , 1999 ), cholinergic neurons (P acr‐2 , 0.54 ± 0.04 exp/cycle) or interneurons (P glr‐1 , 0.49 ± 0.05 exp/cycle). Therefore, it is likely that the expulsion defect in the ubr‐1 mutant results from functional deficiencies in DVB and AVL, the GABAergic neurons responsible for mediating enteric muscle contractions. This observation is consistent with the strong and consistent expression of the ubr‐1 gene in DVB and AVL neurons, as indicated by both the translational reporter (Fig EV2C ) and the transcriptional reporter (Figs 1G and EV2D ).

Figure EV2. Expression pattern of ubr‐1 in AVL and DVB.

Collectively, UBR‐1 promotes the expulsion motor step through GABAergic neurons that contract enteric muscles during the defecation process.

UBR‐1 is required for DVB neuron's oscillated calcium activity

Previous studies have revealed that the DVB neuron exhibits periodic Ca 2+ activities that are tightly correlated with the expulsion behavior (Wang et al , 2013 ; Jiang et al , 2022 ). We asked whether the reduced expulsion in ubr‐1 mutants is associated with changes in DVB activity.

Using a genetic calcium sensor GCaMP6s (in green) for DVB with a calcium‐insensitive wCherry (red) as reference (Figs 2A and EV3A ), we examined Ca 2+ activity in the DVB neuron. We observed robust oscillatory Ca 2+ transients in loosely attached wild‐type animals (Fig EV3B and C , Materials and Methods ), similar to those observed in free‐moving animals (Wang et al , 2013 ). These Ca 2+ transients exhibited the same frequency and amplitude in both dual‐channel (GCaMP6s/wCherry, 5.4 ± 0.65 Hz/180 s) and single‐channel (GCaMP6s, 4.63 ± 0.17 Hz/180 s) Ca 2+ imaging (Figs 2B and EV3D ). For simplicity, subsequent experiments were performed using the single‐channel approach. We found that each epoch of DVB's Ca 2+ transient was accompanied by an expulsion step (Movie EV1 ). Furthermore, the expulsion action and DVB Ca 2+ oscillation were simultaneously abolished in glued nlp‐40 mutants, which lack an instructive neuropeptide from the intestine that delivers temporal information to the DVB neuron (Wang et al , 2013 ). This suggests that the DVB Ca 2+ dynamics are dependent on the NLP‐40‐initiated pacemaker signal.

Figure 2. ubr‐1 regulates DVB Ca 2+ activity.

Figure EV3. Dual‐channel calcium imaging of DVB neuron.

In ubr‐1 mutants, DVB neuron exhibited a reduced frequency (3.46 ± 0.21 Hz/180 s; Fig 2B and C ) and a decrease in the peak amplitude (Fig 2B and D ) of the Ca 2+ oscillatory transients. The morphological development of the DVB neuron was similar between the wild type and ubr‐1 animals (Fig EV4A ). Therefore, the loss of UBR‐1 compromises DVB neuronal activity.

Figure EV4. ubr‐1 does not affect the morphology of DVB and enteric muscles, as well as the expression of eat‐4 .

Consistent with the behavioral observations, the restored expression of UBR‐1 in DVB, AVL, and D‐MNs (P unc‐47 ) or in DVB (P flp‐10 ), but not in D‐MNs (P unc‐25s ), fully restored the frequency and peak amplitude of DVB's Ca 2+ oscillatory transients (Fig 2B–D ). These results support the notion that UBR‐1 regulates DVB activity cell autonomously.

Although AVL's activity was not directly examined, the known gap junctional connection between AVL and DVB supports the idea that AVL activity should be co‐regulated by UBR‐1 (Bhattacharya et al , 2019 ; Choi et al , 2021 ).

ubr‐1 mutant's expulsion defect is not due to insufficient GABAergic signaling

DVB is a GABAergic motor neuron (McLntire et al , 1993b ). It releases GABA, which activates EXP‐1, a GABA‐gated cation channel expressed by the enteric muscles to initiate muscle contraction (Beg & Jorgensen, 2003 ). The most straightforward implication of the reduced expulsion and DVB Ca 2+ activity in ubr‐1 mutants is a defective GABAergic signaling the system, due to either reduced GABA release and/or GABA receptor EXP‐1.

To explore these possibilities, we investigated whether the expulsion frequency of ubr‐1 mutants could be restored by the application of exogenous GABA. Consistent with previous report (McLntire et al , 1993a ), the addition of GABA to the culture media significantly increased the expulsion frequency of unc‐25 , a mutant that lacks the enzyme to convert glutamate to GABA (Fig EV4B ; Jin et al , 1999 ). However, the expulsion defect observed in ubr‐1 mutants did not improve with exogenous GABA application (Fig EV4B ).

We further compared the endogenous GABA levels in synchronized wild‐type and ubr‐1 mutants using high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Surprisingly, and in line with our observation of exogenous GABA application, the GABA levels not only did not decrease but instead showed a moderate increase in ubr‐1 mutants (Fig EV4C ). This increase of GABA levels aligns with the metabolism consequences of elevated glutamate in ubr‐1 mutants (Chitturi et al , 2018 ). Lastly, the localization and expression density of the postsynaptic GABA receptor EXP‐1 showed no obvious changes in ubr‐1 mutants (Fig EV4D ).

In conclusion, the reduced defecation frequency or DVB neuronal activity of the ubr‐1 mutant cannot be attributed to insufficient excitatory GABA signaling.

Preventing glutamate release in ubr‐1 mutants restores the defecation frequency

Does UBR‐1 affect DVB activity independently of GABA signaling? Glutamate, GABA's sole precursor (Miller et al , 1978 ), is elevated in ubr‐1 mutants (Chitturi et al , 2018 ). We hypothesized that increased glutamate signaling might contribute to ubr‐1 's defecation defects.

To investigate this hypothesis, we assessed the effect of blocking glutamate release in the ubr‐1 mutant background by removing EAT‐4, the vesicular glutamate transport (Lee et al , 1999 ). Remarkably, the removal of EAT‐4 robustly restored ubr‐1 's expulsion frequency ( ubr‐1; eat‐4 , 12 ± 0.30/10 min; Fig 3A and B ). Conversely, overexpression of EAT‐4 in ubr‐1 mutants (P unc‐47 ::EAT‐4) worsened the defecation defect (Fig EV4E ). These findings suggest that glutamate signaling may directly regulate expulsion, which was unexpected given that previous studies did not reveal synaptic connections from glutamatergic neurons to enteric muscles. We found that a transcriptional reporter of eat‐4 showed moderate but consistent expression in DVB and AVL (Fig 3C ). This suggests the possibility of dual transmission of glutamate and GABA, as observed in other systems (Root et al , 2018 ), from these enteric muscle‐innervating, excitatory GABAergic neurons.

Figure 3. eat‐4 suppresses ubr‐1 expulsion defect.

Further supporting the possibility that increased glutamate signaling in the defecation circuit might be responsible for ubr‐1' s reduced defecation, restoring EAT‐4 expression in DVB, AVL, and D‐class GABAergic neurons (P unc‐47 , 0.90 ± 0.03 exp/cycle) or in DVB, AVL, and RIS neurons (P lim‐6 (int3) , 0.54 ± 0.02 exp/cycle) or in single DVB neuron (P flp‐10 , 0.51 ± 0.03 exp/cycle) in ubr‐1; eat‐4 mutants reverted the expulsion frequency back to that of ubr‐1 mutants (0.55 ± 0.02 exp/cycle; Fig 3B ). Reversion was not observed when we drove EAT‐4 expression in RIS neuron (P flp‐11 , 0.88 ± 0.05 exp/cycle), D‐class neurons (P unc‐25s , 0.92 ± 0.03 exp/cycle), cholinergic neurons (P acr‐2s , 0.94 ± 0.02 exp/cycle), or premotor interneurons (P glr‐1 , 0.93 ± 0.04 exp/cycle). Lastly, when we knocked down eat‐4 expression in DVB and AVL ( Materials and Methods ) in ubr‐1 mutants, expulsion frequency significantly increased (0.72 ± 0.04 exp/cycle; Fig 3B ). These results establish a direct role of vesicular glutamate transporter EAT‐4 in the defecation circuit.

Recapitulating the genetic interactions at the behavioral level, removing eat‐4 in ubr‐1 mutant background also led to increased DVB calcium activity (Fig 3D–F ). Restoring EAT‐4 in DVB, AVL, and D, or in DVB alone, but not in D in ubr‐1; eat‐4 mutants, also reverted DVB's calcium activity to that of ubr‐1 mutants (Fig 3D–F ). Thus, neuronal activity could also be compensated by the removal of VGLUT in these GABAergic motor neurons. Consistent with behavioral defect, overexpression of EAT‐4 in ubr‐1 mutants (P unc‐47 ::EAT‐4) further decreased DVB calcium activity (Fig EV4F and G ). We observed no significant difference in eat‐4 transcription and EAT‐4 abundance levels between ubr‐1 and and wild‐type animals (Fig EV4H–J ), suggesting that eat‐4 is not regulated by UBR‐1.

Neither the expulsion frequency nor DVB activity was changed between eat‐4 loss of function mutants and wild‐type animals (Fig 3B, E and F ). The pBoc frequency in eat‐4 , or eat‐4; ubr‐1 mutants, was not altered, indicating that the DMP cycle intervals are normal in these mutants (Fig EV4K ). This implicates a low basal glutamatergic signaling in the defecation circuit, which may play a modulatory role.

Inhibitory neuronal glutamatergic signaling contributes to ubr‐1 mutant's reduced expulsion

Glutamatergic signaling from DVB should activate glutamate receptors in the defecation circuit. C. elegans genome encodes four classes of glutamate receptors, including the excitatory classes (the ionotropic AMPA and NMDA‐type), the metabotropic classes (mGluRs), and the inhibitory classes (the glutamate‐gated chloride channels, GluCls; Brockie & Maricq, 2006 ; Dillon et al , 2006 ).

To determine which glutamate receptors underlie glutamatergic signaling in the defecation circuit, we began by examining their expression patterns. Excitatory glutamate receptors have been reported to be expressed in the nervous system, but none have been reported in the expulsion circuit (Appendix Table S1 ; Brockie et al , 2001 ; Greer et al , 2008 ; Jeong & Paik, 2017 ; Katz et al , 2019 ). The expression pattern of inhibitory GluCls was not as comprehensively described (Holden‐Dye & Walker, 2014 ). We generated transcriptional reporters of all six GluCls and found that one, the glc‐3 reporter, exhibited strong expression in DVB (Figs 4A and EV5 ).

Figure 4. Glutamate‐gated chloride channel GLC‐3 regulate UBR‐1‐mediated expulsion.

Figure EV5. Expression pattern of glutamate‐gated chloride channels in the tail.

The co‐presence of the glutamate release machinery (EAT‐4) and the inhibitory glutamate receptor (GLC‐3) in DVB implies an auto‐inhibitory regulation. To test this possibility, we examined the effect of removing GLC‐3. In ubr‐1; glc‐3 mutants, the expulsion frequency (0.87 ± 0.03 exp/cycle), as the case for ubr‐1; eat‐4 , was significantly rescued (Fig 4B and C ). Furthermore, restoring GLC‐3 expression, either pan‐neuronally or in DVB and AVL but not in D, reverted the expulsion frequency of ubr‐1; glc‐3 to that of ubr‐1 mutants (Fig 4B and C ). The calcium activity of DVB in these mutants fully recapitulated the behavioral effects of the corresponding genotypes (Fig 4D–F ).

Similar to eat‐4 mutants, we did not find significant differences in pBoc frequency, expulsion frequency, or DVB activity between glc‐3 mutants and wild‐type animals (Figs 4C and EV4K ). These results implicate that glutamate signaling from DVB negatively regulates its activity, an effect that is likely amplified by elevated glutamate levels in ubr‐1 mutants.

Inhibitory glutamatergic signaling from DVB contributes to ubr‐1' s expulsion defect

We also observed strong expression of the transcription reporters for glc‐2 and glc‐4 in the muscles of the defecation circuit, including the enteric and anal depressor muscles, responsible for expulsion (Figs 5A and B , and EV5 ; Branicky & Hekimi, 2006 ). We examined whether these muscles also contributed to the expulsion defect of ubr‐1 mutants.

Figure 5. Intestinal muscular GLC‐2/4 are required for UBR‐1‐mediated expulsion.

The functional loss of either glc‐2 or glc‐4 in ubr‐1 mutants led to an increase of their expulsion frequency ( ubr‐1; glc‐2 0.75 ± 0.03 exp/cycle; ubr‐1; glc‐4 0.83 ± 0.02 exp/cycle; Fig 5A–C ). Reintroducing GLC‐2 or GLC‐4 to the enteric muscles in ubr‐1 glc‐2 or ubr‐1; glc‐4 mutants restored their expulsion frequencies to those of ubr‐1 mutants (Fig 4B and C ). These results implicate that inhibitory glutamatergic signaling from DVB to the enteric muscles contributes to the expulsion defect observed in ubr‐1 mutants.

To directly assess the impact of GLC‐2 and GLC‐4 on expulsion, we examined the calcium activity in the enteric muscles (Fig 5D ). In wild‐type animals, enteric muscles exhibited robust, oscillating calcium signals closely associated with expulsion (Fig 5D and E , and Movie EV2 ). However, in ubr‐1 mutants, the enteric muscles showed a significant reduction in the frequency and peak amplitude of calcium signals, resembling the reduced DVB activity (Fig 5E ). This decrease in enteric muscle activity correlated with reduced expulsion frequency in ubr‐1 mutants. Notably, enteric muscles showed normal shape and comparable basal GCaMP intensity between wild type and ubr‐1 animals (Fig EV4A ).

Consistent with the behavioral changes, enteric muscle calcium signals in ubr‐1; glc‐2 mutants exhibited an increase compared to ubr‐1 mutants (Fig 5E–G ), and this effect was reversed when we restored the expression of GLC‐2 in enteric muscles (Fig 5E–G ). We observed similar genetic interactions between ubr‐1 and glc‐4 (Fig 5E–G ). These results suggest that GLC‐2 and GLC‐4 may form a heteromeric GluCl receptor, negatively regulating the activity of enteric muscles.

Similar to eat‐4 and glc‐3 mutants, we did not find changes in pBoc frequency in glc‐2 , ubr‐1; glc‐2 or glc‐4 and ubr‐1; glc‐4 compared to wild type (Fig EV4K ), although the expulsion frequency was partially restored when compared ubr‐1; glc‐2 to glc‐2 , or ubr‐1; glc‐4 to glc‐4 animals (Fig 4C ). These results indicate that an elevated inhibitory glutamate signaling between the DVB motor neuron and enteric muscles, in response to increased glutamate release in ubr‐1 mutants, contributes to the reduced expulsion observed in mutant animals.

Glutamate perfusion potently inhibits the defecation circuit

A global elevation of glutamate levels was observed in ubr‐1 mutants (Chitturi et al , 2018 ). However, whether this elevation leads to an increase in the functional neurotransmitter within the defecation circuit has not been demonstrated directly.

To investigate this, we initially performed glutamate perfusion to simulate the effect of excessive glutamate. When animals were exposed to glutamate, there was a significant reduction in calcium activity in the DVB neuron (Fig 6A ). Quantitatively, at a concentration of 5.9 mM, exogenous glutamate exposure resulted in a decrease in the frequency (Fig 6B ) and peak amplitude (Fig 6C ) of calcium signals from DVB. Importantly, this glutamate treatment did not lead to inhibition in glc‐3 mutants (Fig 6B and C ), supporting the notion that exogenous glutamate inhibits DVB activity through GLC‐3. Similarly, glutamate exposure reduced the activity of enteric muscles, causing a modestly reduced frequency (Fig 6D and E ) and a significantly reduction in amplitude (Fig 6F ) of DVB's calcium transient. Notably, the inhibition of enteric muscle induced by glutamate was not observed in glc‐2 ; glc‐4 double mutants (Fig 6E and F ), which also exhibit significant suppression of ubr‐1 . Together, these findings support to the hypothesis that increased glutamate could lead to heightened inhibitory glutamate signaling within the defection circuit.

Figure 6. Glutamate inhibits DVB neuron and enteric muscles through the activation of GLC‐3 and GLC‐2/GLC‐4.

To further investigate the role of glutamate as a signaling molecule within the defection circuit, we administered glutamate directly onto the DVB neuron in a dissected C. elegans preparation (Fig 7A and Materials and Methods ). In wild‐type animals, DVB exhibited robust oscillatory calcium signals in a bath solution with high chloride (167 mM; Materials and Methods ). Perfusion of 1 mM glutamate led to an immediate and potent attenuation of DVB's calcium signals (Fig 7B and C ). This attenuation was reversed upon washing out the glutamate with of a glutamate‐free solution, indicating that glutamate directly inhibits DVB. Importantly, the inhibitory effect of glutamate was abolished in low chloride (17 mM; Fig 7B and C ), indicating that glutamate activates a chloride‐dependent inhibitory conductance in DVB. In glc‐3 mutants, DVB's calcium signals were unaffected by glutamate puff (Fig 7B and C ). These results strongly support the existence of an auto‐inhibitory glutamate signaling pathway in DVB through GLC‐3.

Figure 7. Glutamate directly inhibits dissected DVB neuron.

Furthermore, GLC‐3 forms a glutamate‐gated chloride‐dependent conductance, which we successfully reconstituted in the HEK293T cells. In these cells, 1 mM glutamate activated a GLC‐3‐dependent inward current at −60 mV in the bath solution with high chloride concentration (150.6 mM; Fig 7D ). This glutamate‐activated and GLC‐3‐dependent conductance was significantly reduced in low extracellular chloride conditions (10.6 mM; Fig 7E ).

These results collectively demonstrate that the glutamate acts as an inhibitory signaling molecule that negatively regulates the activity of the defecation circuit.

Discussion

Our study sheds light on the role of the UBR‐1 E3 ligase in regulating the balance of excitatory and inhibitory signaling strength within the defecation motor circuit. This particular circuit is characterized by its excitatory GABAergic nature, accompanied by a lower level of inhibitory glutamatergic signaling. This signaling network comprises the GABAergic motor neuron DVB, which also releases glutamate. It involves auto‐inhibitory signaling within DVB and the inhibition of enteric muscles via glutamate‐gated chloride channels. In ubr‐1 mutants, the elevated glutamate levels lead to an increase in inhibitory signaling, resulting in reduced activity within the defecation circuit and, consequently, impaired expulsion.

A functional model for UBR‐1‐mediated regulation of E/I balance at the defecation circuit

We propose a functional model of the defecation circuit and UBR‐1's effect (Synopsis). Within these GABAergic neurons, there is dual signaling: excitatory GABAergic and inhibitory glutamatergic signaling, with GABAergic signaling predominantly driving the circuit's activity. UBR‐1 plays a critical role in maintaining this signaling imbalance. When UBR‐1 malfunctions, it leads to an increase in glutamate levels, resulting in elevated glutamate upload and release. Consequently, there is an augmentation of inhibitory glutamatergic signaling. This increase hampers the functional output of the excitatory GABAergic signaling, ultimately reducing the frequency of expulsion.

Mechanistically, GABAergic signaling stimulates the defecation circuit by facilitating GABA release via DVB and activating the enteric muscles through EXP‐1. Conversely, glutamatergic signaling inhibits the defecation circuit promoting auto‐inhibition in DVB via GLC‐3 and inhibiting enteric muscles through GLC‐2/4. By controlling the glutamate levels, UBR‐1 regulates the relative strength of excitatory GABAergic and inhibitory glutamatergic signaling, thereby influencing DVB activity and the final output of the defecation motor program, expulsion.

The potential role of dual signaling: a robust motor program with flexibility

The defecation motor program exhibits a highly stereotypic nature, characterized by a robust short‐term rhythm (Thomas, 1990 ). The underlying signaling pathway has been thoroughly characterized (McLntire et al , 1993a , 1993b ; Dal Santo et al , 1999 ; Branicky & Hekimi, 2006 ; Teramoto & Iwasaki, 2006 ; Mahoney et al , 2008 ; Nehrke et al , 2008 ; Wang et al , 2013 ; Choi et al , 2021 ; Jiang et al , 2022 ). This motor program can be reset by food content and mechanical stimulation (Thomas, 1990 ; Liu & Thomas, 1994 ), thereby allowing for modulation. Our study unveils one circuit mechanism responsible for this modulation.

DVB plays a pivotal role in driving excitatory GABAergic signaling (McLntire et al , 1993b ), which, in turn, triggers periodic enteric muscle contractions (Beg & Jorgensen, 2003 ). Its co‐release of glutamate, activating inhibitory signaling, serves a modulatory function, fine‐tuning the rhythm output. In contrast to the sensorimotor circuit, which achieves flexibility by coordinating excitatory and inhibitory signaling through different neurotransmitters originating from distinct neurons (Zhen & Samuel, 2015 ) or via different receptors for the same neurotransmitter (Sato et al , 2021 ), DVB employs a dual‐neurotransmitter system with opposite effects, using both glutamate and GABA to regulate this stereotyped motor function. This arrangement enables the compact defecation motor circuit to adapt to changing conditions. The strong dominance of excitatory GABAergic signaling over low‐level inhibitory glutamatergic signaling in this dual system is likely crucial for maintaining the robustness of the defecation motor program while allowing for flexibility.

Co‐release of GABA and glutamate

In the mammalian brain, neurons have been shown to produce and release multiple neurotransmitters. Inhibitory GABA and excitatory glutamate have been reported to coexist and be co‐released from the same synapses in the central nervous system (Root et al , 2014 , 2018 ; Shabel et al , 2014 ; Barker et al , 2017 ; Kim et al , 2022 ). The GABA/glutamate co‐release phenomenon undergoes alterations in animal models of depression and addiction (Shabel et al , 2014 ; Meye et al , 2016 ), highlighting its functional and behavioral relevance.

GABA can also be an excitatory neurotransmitter in the immature brain (Ben‐Ari, 2002 ; Owens & Kriegstein, 2002 ). Na + ‐dependent GABA depolarizations have also been observed in the adult nervous system, such as in the stomatogastric ganglion of the crab and the accessory olfactory bulb of the rat (Goldmakher & Moss, 2000 ; Swensen et al , 2000 ; Lu & Trussell, 2001 ; Chavas & Marty, 2003 ; Gulledge & Stuart, 2003 ). Additionally, inhibitory effects of glutamate through mGluRs have been reported (Pinheiro & Mulle, 2008 ).

Our study presents a novel example of the co‐release of excitatory GABA and inhibitory glutamate. This unique functional mode not only involves the same neuron but also occurs at the same synapse, allowing for self‐modulate of a highly stereotypic rhythmic behavior. A bidirectional glutamate‐dependent inhibition system at this excitatory GABA synapse may enhance the sensitivity and fidelity of this synapse to external modulators.

UBR‐1 regulates E/I signaling imbalance in the dual‐release neuron

In ubr‐1 mutants, the activity and functional output of the DVB neuron are impaired. These defects are not attributed to reduced GABA release or a development effect of ubr‐1 . Our studies unequivocally demonstrate that the critical source of this defect is elevated glutamatergic signaling. As we previously reported, we found that the whole‐animal glutamate level is elevated in ubr‐1 mutants (Chitturi et al , 2018 ). Here, we provide evidence that in the DVB neuron, which exhibits a low level of inhibitory glutamatergic signaling, this elevated glutamate level results in increased inhibition of the defecation circuit activity.

While direct demonstration of neurotransmitter co‐release is challenging, multiple lines of experimental evidence strongly support this concept. The vesicular glutamate transporter and the vesicular GABA transporter are co‐expressed within the same neuron. Knocking down of EAT‐4 in DVB fully rescues the defecation defects observed in ubr‐1 mutants. Thus, UBR‐1 effectively regulates the signaling strength between excitatory GABA and inhibitory glutamate transmission by modulating glutamate content.

Glutamate homeostasis and implications for the JBS

We have identified the consequences of altered synaptic E/I balance in the absence of functional UBR‐1 protein, which arises from disrupted glutamate homeostasis. Elevated glutamate, if it proves to be a widespread cellular consequence of UBR1 dysfunction, holds intriguing and significant implications for the underlying cause of JBS .

In the nervous system, deviations from the normal E/I balance have been observed in various disorders, including autism, Rett syndrome, mood disorders, and fragile X syndrome (Eichler & Meier, 2008 ; Gatto & Broadie, 2010 ; Zhang & Sun, 2011 ; Marín, 2012 ). Given the disturbance of glutamate homeostasis, alterations in the relative E/I signaling could potentially contribute to neural development and functional impairment in the JBS patients. From this perspective, UBR1 might represent a potential regulator of the GABA/glutamate signaling balance, as well as glutamate metabolism.

Materials and Methods

C. elegans strains and transgenic lines

Caenorhabditis elegans was grown at 22°C on the Nematode Growth Medium (NGM) plates with Escherichia coli OP50 as a food source (Brenner, 1974 ) Wild‐type animals are Bristol N2. ubr‐1(hp684) was isolated from the EMS screen, and other ubr‐1 alleles were generated using the CRISPR‐Cas9 system (Chitturi et al , 2018 ). The other genetic mutants were obtained from the CGC (Caenorhabditis Genetics Center). The transgenic worms were created by microinjection according to standard protocols. Target DNA plasmids (~50 ng/μl) were injected together with co‐injections marker P myo‐2 ::RFP or P myo‐3 ::RFP at concentrations of 5–10 ng/μl. Typically, one transgenic line was tested in most of our rescue' experiments except the glc‐3 behavioral rescue experiment, in which two lines of P glc‐3 ::GLC‐3 were examined. All strains used in this study are listed in Appendix Table S2 .

Defecation behavior assays

Defecation behavior was measured on NGM plates under standard conditions as previously described (Thomas, 1990 ). All behavioral tests were performed at 20–22°C. Worms were synchronized and well‐fed young hermaphrodites adults were transferred to fresh NGM plates with OP50 for behavior assay during freely moving state. The defecation phenotypes were scored under a Zeiss V16 microscope after 16–20 h. Each animal was quantified in 10 min after the first pBoc step pass using the Etho program software (Liu & Thomas, 1994 ). Due to the aBoc step was difficult to distinguish between anterior body wall muscle contraction and pumping, we simplified the observation on the pBoc and the Exp steps (Iwasaki & Thomas, 1997 ). Exp/cycle was calculated as the ratio of the number of Exp/pBoc during a 10‐min recording (Wang et al , 2013 ). The assay with GABA in ubr‐1 and unc‐25 mutants was performed as previously described (McLntire et al , 1993a ).

Molecular biology

All expression and rescue plasmids were generated via the multisite gateway system (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Magnani et al , 2006 ). Three entry clones (Slot1, Slot2, and Slot3) corresponding to promoter, target gene, and fluorescent marker gene were recombined into pDEST™ R4‐R3 Vector II via LR reaction, and then expression constructs were obtained (Chen et al , 2022 ). The genes ubr‐1 , eat‐4 , glc‐2 , glc‐3 , and glc‐4 were cloned from wide‐type genomic DNA. The ubr‐1 minigene plasmid contained a sequence of unc‐54‐3′UTR . The high fidelity polymerase platinum HiFi Taq (Invitrogen) was used to amplify the cDNA sequence in the construction of the minigene. Plasmid sequence fidelity was verified by sequencing and enzyme digestion. The promoter lengths of P ubr‐1 , P eat‐4 , P glc‐2 , P glc‐3 , and P glc‐4 were 1.6, 5.6, 2.0, 2.0, and 3.0 kb, respectively. All detailed information about plasmids and primers information are listed in Appendix Tables S3 and S4 .

For cell‐specific knockout experiments, we generate a loxp flanked (floxed) allele of eat‐4 minigene: P eat‐4 ::loxP::EAT‐4minigene(stop)::loxP::GFP (P eat‐4 ::EAT‐4 flox/flox ) that fully reverts the expulsion frequency of ubr‐1; eat‐4 to ubr‐1 mutant. A Cre pilot strain was generated whose Cre recombinase is expressed in DVB neuron using the P unc‐47 promoter. Cre recognized loxp sequences and caused a site‐specific deletion of eat‐4 DNA between two loxp sites.

Fluorescence microscopy

The confocal images were obtained with a laser scanning confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus) using 40× objectives (numerical aperture = 0.95). The neurons and muscles labeled with GFP or RFP have been imaged with excitation wavelength laser at 488 or 561 nm respectively. The worms were immobilized with 2.5 mM levamisole (Sigma‐Aldrich) in M9 buffer. The images were processed and analyzed with ImageJ (National Institutes of Health).

In vivo calcium imaging

The two integrated strains hpIs468 (P unc‐47 ::GCaMP6s::wCherry) and hpIs582 (P exp‐1 ::GCaMP6s::wCherry) were used for calcium imaging of DVB neuron and enteric muscle. Both strains exhibit normal defecation interval and Exp step. Young hermaphrodite adults were glued dorsally and imaged with a 60× water objective (numerical aperture = 1.0). In particular, animals are loosely immobilized with WormGlu (Histoacryl Blue, Braun, Germany) to a sylgard‐coated cover glass covered with bath solution (Sylgard 184, Dowcorning, USA) but leave the tail free in the recording solution. To keep the worms' regular pumping and defecation activities, a small amount of OP50 (~100 μl, OD 600 = 1) was mixed into the recording solution, which consists of (in mM): NaCl 150; KCl 5; CaCl 2 5; MgCl 2 1; glucose 10; sucrose 5; HEPES 15, pH 7.3 with NaOH, ~330 mOsm.

Fluorescence images were acquired with excitation wavelength LED at 470 nm and a digital sCMOS camera at 100 ms per frame for 3 min. Data was collected from HCImage (Hamamatsu) and analyzed by Image Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, Inc., Rockville, MD, USA) and Image J (National Institutes of Health). For single‐channel Ca 2+ imaging, the GCaMP6s fluorescence intensity of the region of interest (ROI) was defined as F , and the background intensity near the ROI was defined as F 0 . The true neuronal calcium fluorescence signal was obtained by subtracting the background signal from the ROI. Δ F / F 0 = ( F − F 0 )/ F 0 was plotted over time as a fluorescence variation curve. For dual‐channel Ca 2+ imaging, with 470 nm and 590 nm excitation (LED, BioLED Light Source Control Module 9‐24VDC), the emitted green (GCaMP6s) and red (wCherry) fluorescence were simultaneously collected under a wide‐field microscope (Nikon LV‐TV), which equipped with a dichroic beamsplitter (W‐VIEW GEMINI, Japan), a sCMOS digital camera (Hamamatsu ORCA‐Flash 4.0V2) and a 60× water objective (Nikon, Japan, numerical aperture = 1.0). And the ratiometric quantitation (Δ R / R 0 ) was used to analyze the fluorescence change, in which R means the ratio of GFP/RFP (GCaMP6s/wCherry) and R 0 means the minimal R , Δ R indicates ( R − R 0 ).

For extrinsic glutamate perfusion experiment in intact animals, glued worms were pretreated in the bath solution with 5.9 mM glutamate for 5 min, and then were imaged for another 3 min with 5.9 mM glutamate. For extrinsic glutamate perfusion experiment in dissected animals, DVB neuron was exposed by tail dissection as described for electrophysiological recording. To keep the circuit integrity, the dissection was performed just on the dorsal side in the tail near DVB soma. A glass capillary (2–6 MΩ) was used to punctured the epidermis, and a small cut (5–10 μm width) was made to limit the possible damage or contents. Under this condition, almost all preparations exhibited regular rhythmic DVB Ca 2+ activity (Fig 7B ). Dissected DVB Ca 2+ transient was recorded by the perfusion of 1 mM glutamate for ~60 s and washing out by glutamate‐free bath solution.

HEK293T expression and electrophysiology

GLC‐3 expression in HEK293T cells: GLC‐3 cDNA was flanked between SmaI and KpnI sites by PCR from cDNA of C. elegans and cloned into the expression vector pEGP‐N1. HEK293T cells were cultured in the DMEM medium with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% antibiotics (penicillin/streptomycin) at 5% CO 2 , and 37°C. After 24 h of transfection of the plasmid with the liposome ExFect2000 (Vazyme, China), cells were patched using 4–6 MΩ resistant borosilicate pipettes (1B100F‐4, World Precision Instruments, USA). Pipettes were pulled by micropipette puller P‐1000 (Sutter, USA), and fire‐polished by microforge MF‐830 (Narishige, Japan). Membrane currents and I – V curve were recorded and plotted in the whole‐cell configuration by pulse software with the EPC‐9 amplifier (HEKA, Germany) and processed with the Igor Pro (WaveMetrics, USA) and Clampfit 10 software (Axon Instruments, Molecular Devices, USA). Membrane currents were recorded at −60 mV, and I – V curve was recorded at a holding potential from −100 to +100 mV with 10 mV step voltages. Data were digitized at 10–20 kHz and filtered at 2.6 kHz. The pipette solution contained (in mM): KCl 140; MgCl 2 1; EGTA 10; HEPES 10; Na 2 ATP 4; pH 7.3 with KOH, ~300 mOsm. The bath solution consisted of (in mM): NaCl 140; KCl 3.6; CaCl 2 2.5; MgCl 2 1; pH 7.3 with NaOH, ~310 mOsm. For low extracellular Cl − recording, the bath solution contained (in mM): Na‐gluconate 140; KCl 3.6; CaCl 2 2.5; MgCl 2 1; pH 7.3 with NaOH, ~310 mOsm. Chemicals were obtained from Sigma unless stated otherwise. Experiments were performed at room temperature (20–22°C).

Statistical analysis and display

All defecation behavior data was displayed as bar graphs by GraphPad Prisim 8 (GraphPad Software Inc.). Calcium imaging data was mainly plotted using Matlab (MathWorks) for heat maps, Igor Pro (WaveMetrics) for curve graph, and GraphPad for scatter plots, with each point representing a single worm test. The error bars represent the standard errors of the mean (SEM). An unpaired student's t ‐test was used to analyze differences and calculate P ‐values when the comparison was limited to two groups. When multiple groups (more than two groups) of data were compared, ordinary one‐way or two‐way ANOVA analysis was performed for statistical analysis. Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to analyze amplitude cumulative fraction. The P ‐values are indicated as follows: ns, no significance, * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001, **** P < 0.0001.
