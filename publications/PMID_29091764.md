Skeletal muscle regeneration requires resident muscle stem cells, termed
satellite cells (SCs). SCs are largely quiescent during homeostasis yet become
activated upon injury to supply myonuclei and self-renewed SCs. Molecular
mechanisms underlying the competence of SCs to proliferate and self-renew in
response to injury remain unclear. Here, we show that CREB activity establishes
proliferative potential during SC quiescence. SCs with inhibited CREB activity
remain quiescent and positioned in their niche, but upon injury, they cannot
enter or maintain a proliferative state for expansion and self-renewal. We
demonstrate mechanistically that Mpp7 is a CREB target and its functional
mediator. MPP7 loss affects the level and sub-cellular localization of AMOT and
YAP1 in quiescent SCs. Furthermore, MPP7 and AMOT are required for YAP1 nuclear
accumulation, and the three are individually required for a proliferative state
in myoblasts. We propose that the CREB-MPP7-AMOT-YAP1 axis establishes the
competence of quiescent SCs to expand and self-renew, thereby preserving stem
cell function.

Introduction

A central property of stem cells is the capacity to generate daughter cells that self-renew and differentiate into specialized cell types (Weissman, 2000). The resident skeletal muscle stem cells, satellite cells (SCs) (Mauro, 1961), are largely quiescent and can re-enter proliferative cycles after injury to self-renew and generate new muscle cells (Brack and Rando, 2012). SC quiescence is actively maintained by microRNAs, chromatin modifiers, and signaling molecules, among others (Cheung et al., 2012; Crist et al., 2012; Boonsanay et al., 2016; Yamaguchi et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2016). Disruption of a single quiescence regulator can trigger SCs to re-enter the cell cycle without injury, suggesting that quiescent SCs are maintained in a competent state for proliferation. The molecular nature of this proliferation competent state is poorly understood.

Cell polarity has been implicated in cell division as well as in quiescence, of various somatic stem cells (Florian and Geiger, 2010). Quiescent SCs display asymmetrical distribution of α7/β1 integrin at the basement membrane (basal side) and M-cadherin at the sarcolemma (apical side) (Irintchev et al., 1994; Burkin and Kaufman, 1999). (β1-integrin is required for SC quiescence and sustained SC proliferation during regeneration (Rozo et al., 2016). M-cadherin mutant mice have no muscle regeneration defects, possibly due to compensation by other cadherins (Hollnagel et al, 2002). Targeting these basal and apical membrane proteins to their perspective domains depends on polarity regulators Par1b and Par3/Par6/PKCλ, respectively. The roles of Par1b and Par3 in SC quiescence have not been reported. However, in activated SCs in vitro, Par1b regulates asymmetric division (Dumont et al., 2015), while Par3/PKCλ, promote progenitor transit amplification (Troy et al., 2012). Meanwhile, Scrib, a basolateral regulator in epithelial cells (Su et al., 2012), is only expressed in activated SCs and critical for both myoblast proliferation and differentiation (Ono et al., 2015).

A major signaling pathway that regulates proliferation of polarized cells is the Hippo pathway (Pan, 2010). The most downstream effector of this pathway is the Yap/Tead transcription complex that activates pro-proliferation genes. Activation of the core Hippo pathway kinases Mst1/2 and Lats1/2 leads to phosphorylation and cytoplasmic retention/degradation of Yap, thereby inhibiting proliferation (Pan, 2010). Importantly, Yap can promote proliferation and inhibit differentiation of myoblasts (Judson et al., 2012; Tremblay et al., 2014). Yap also plays a role in fate determination in pre-implantation embryos, where it is sequestered by Angiomotin (Amot) and Amot-like2 (Amotl2) to the membrane domain of the pluripotent inner cell mass, but enters the nucleus in the trophectoderm lineage (Hirate et al., 2013; Leung and Zernicka-Goetz, 2013). Depending on cellular contexts, Yap function can either be inhibited or facilitated by Amot (Chan et al., 2011; Zhao et al., 2011; Yi et al., 2013; Zheng et al., 2009). Among the many protein complexes containing Amot, a complex containing Amot, Amotl1 and Amotl2, and Mpp7 (a homolog of the polarity regulator Stardust; Hong et al., 2001; Katoh and Katoh, 2004), has been described but not functionally characterized (Wells et al., 2006). Whether and how this Amot-containing complex regulates Yap activity in the myoblast is unknown.

Previous work by us and others has shown that the CREB family of transcription factors, consisting of CREB, CREM and ATF1, control muscle development, growth, and survival. Phosphorylation of CREB at Ser133 and of CREM and ATF1 at analogous serines (herein collectively referred to as pCREB) initiates recruitment of cofactors and transcriptional machinery for gene activation (Mayr and Montminy, 2001). Their transcriptional activity can therefore be inferred from their phosphorylation state. Our previous study showed that inhibition of CREB family by a highly selective pan-dominant negative, A-CREB (Ahn et al., 1998; Vinson et al., 2002), abolishes myogenic gene induction in mouse embryos (Chen et al., 2005). In adult muscle, CREB acts directly to prevent muscle fiber death (Berdeaux et al., 2007). Indirectly, it acts in M2 macrophages to promote muscle repair (Ruffell et al., 2009). Mice expressing an activation-sensitive form of CREB (CREB-YP) showed increased cell proliferation after muscle injury, but whether those proliferating cells were SCs was not determined (Stewart et al., 2011). Lastly, SCs of CREB-YP mice during homeostasis and regeneration were not examined. As extensive as these collective studies are, the autonomous role of CREB family in adult SCs remains unclear.

Here we provide evidence that the activity of the CREB family is present in quiescent SCs, which endows them the competence to commence proliferation and self-renewal for regeneration. We identified Mpp7 as a target and functional mediator of CREB, and showed that Mpp7 and Amot family members drive nuclear Yap localization and function. This regulatory axis sheds light on how CREB confers its function through Yap in the SC, which likely extends to other biological systems.

ResultsCREB activity declines during SC differentiationTo understand how CREB family operates in adult myogenesis, we examined their activity in the SC lineage by probing for pCREB signal using immunofluorescence (IF). In uninjured tibialis anterior (TA) muscle, pCREB was detected in 97% of Pax7-expressing (Pax7+) SCs (Figure S1A). We next followed pCREB pattern in YFP lineage-marked myofiber-associated SCs from tamoxifen (TAM) treated Pax7CE/+RosaYFP/YFP (YFP) mice (Figure S1B; Lepper et al., 2009). Immediately after harvest, 98% of SCs possessed high pCREB levels (Figure S1C), in accordance with in vivo data. Fluorescence intensity of pCREB in myonuclei was used as a baseline to determine relative pCREB levels in SCs (see Experimental Procedures). SCs with high pCREB levels declined in number the next 2 days (d) in culture (Figure S1C). We also assessed pCREB in SC-derived primary myoblasts, in combination with myogenic markers, Pax7, MyoD, Myog, and MHC, which represent stem, progenitor, myocyte, and differentiated muscle cells, respectively. Many Pax7+ and MyoD+ cells had high levels of pCREB, while most Myog+ and MHC+ cells had low to undetectable levels of pCREB (Figure S1D). Thus, CREB activity is high in quiescent and cultured SCs and myoblasts, but low in differentiated cells.CREB activity is not critical for adult SC quiescenceTo determine the role of CREB family in adult SCs, we used a Rosa26A-CREB allele (Lopez and Fan, 2013) for Cre-dependent expression of A-CREB to inactivate the CREB family (Ahn et al., 1998; Riccio et al., 1999; Herzig et al., 2001). An IRES-directed nuclear GFP (nGFP) exists in this allele to follow A-CREB expression (Figures 1A and S1B). After TAM application, nGFP expression was found in 95% of SCs in Pax7CE/+; Rosa26A-CREB/A-CREB (A-CREB) mice, similar to cell-marking efficiency in control YFP mice (Figures S1E and S1F). SCs expressing A-CREB (A-CREB SCs) did not show altered Pax7+ cell number immediately nor over the long-term (Figures S1E and S1F). Furthermore, they maintained quiescence and niche association as indicated by normal β1-integrin pattern, absence of EdU incorporation, and sub-laminal position at 21 d postTAM regimen (Figures S2A – S2E). Thus, CREB activity is not required to maintain the quiescent SC population.CREB activity is required for SC-mediated regenerationBecause Pax7+ and MyoD+ cells showed high levels of pCREB signal, we examined whether CREB have a role in regeneration. At 5 and 10 d post cardiotoxin (CTX)-induced injury (dpi) to the TA muscle (Figure 1A), A-CREB mice showed significant reductions in regenerated myofiber number and diameter relative to control YFP mice (Figures 1B, 1C and 1E). By 35 dpi, regenerated fibers were no longer detectable (Figures S2F – S2H), consistent with CREB's role in maintaining mature myofiber survival (Berdeaux et al., 2007). When we assessed the Pax7+ population at 5 and 10 dpi, A-CREB mice possessed 90% fewer Pax7+ cells compared to controls (Figures 1D and 1E). No Pax7+ cells were observed in damaged areas at 35 dpi (Figures S2G and S2I). These results demonstrate an essential role for CREB in muscle regeneration.CREB activity is necessary for SC self-renewalThe depletion of Pax7+ cells after injury in A-CREB mice suggests defects in SC proliferation and/or self-renewal. To determine this, we monitored proliferation by daily EdU incorporation in lineage-labeled cells during early regeneration. A reduction in total and EdU+ lineage marked A-CREB cells were found, compared to control YFP cells (Figures 2A and 2B). Significantly larger fractions of A-CREB cells expressed Myog at early time points, suggestive of their precocious differentiation (Figure 2C).It is possible that the poor regenerative environment secondarily affected A-CREB SC proliferation. To circumvent this, we examined A-CREB and control YFP SCs in single myofiber cultures, where they contact intact wild type (WT) myofibers. Most control SCs expanded to form multi-cell clusters, but most A-CREB SCs remain as single cells after 3 d (Figure 2D). Supporting a precocious differentiation phenotype in vivo, A-CREB SCs expressed Myog as early as 1 d in culture, while control SCs did not (Figure 2E). To determine whether the minority of A-CREB SCs that divided once (Figure S3A) were defective in maintaining Pax7, we compared cell fates in control and A-CREB SC doublets (Figure 2F). Eighty percent of A-CREB SC doublets were both Pax7-, compared to 14% in controls. Both A-CREB and control doublets express MyoD at similar frequencies.
