## Full Text

Abstract

Hes6 is a member of the hairy-enhancer of split homolog (Hes) family of transcription factors and interacts with other Hes family genes. During development, Hes genes are expressed in neural stem cells and progenitor cells. However, the role of Hes6 in adult hippocampal neurogenesis remains unclear. We therefore investigated the effects of Hes6 on adult hippocampal neurogenesis, by comparing Hes6 knockout and wild-type mice. To this end, we immunostained for markers of neural stem cells and progenitor cells (nestin), proliferating cells (Ki67), post-mitotic neuroblasts and immature neurons (doublecortin, DCX), mature neuronal cells (NeuN), and astrocyte (S100β). We also injected 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU) to trace the fate of mitotic cells. Nestin- and Ki67-positive proliferating cells did now show any significant differences between wild and knockout groups. Hes6 knockout negatively affects neuroblast differentiation based on DCX immunohistochemistry. On the contrary, the ratio of the BrdU and NeuN double-positive cells did not show any significance, even though it was slightly higher in the knockout group. These results suggest that Hes6 is involved in the regulation of neuroblast differentiation during adult neurogenesis, but does not influence integration into mature neurons.

Keywords: Hes6 , Neuronal differentiation, Hippocampus, Adult neurogenesis

Introduction

Although adult neurogenesis is restricted to the subgranular zone of the hippocampal dentate gyrus, it continues throughout life (Ming and Song 2005 ). During development, however, embryonic neuroepithelial cells become neural stem cells in the primordium of the central nervous system and mature into neurons and glial cells (Temple 2001 ; Zhang et al. 2001 ). Although the exact mechanism of neurogenesis from neural stem cells to neurons is not clear, work thus far has shown that in neural stem cells, several genes involved in proliferation and neuronal differentiation are turned on and off in a coordinated manner. Among these genes, the hairy-enhancer of split homolog ( Hes ) genes, consisting of several subtypes, are translated and function as nuclear effectors of Notch signaling and as transcription cofactors (Kageyama and Ohtsuka 1999 ). Hes family members are classified as helix-loop-helix (HLH) proteins (Sasai et al. 1992 ). The function of the Hes1 and Hes5 genes has been well studied using knockout mice, and Hes1 , Hes3 , and Hes5 triple knockout mice have also been generated (Hatakeyama et al. 2004 ). During brain development, inactivation of these genes accelerated neuronal differentiation, but premature differentiation resulted in the depletion of neural stem cells (Hatakeyama et al. 2004 ; Hirata et al. 2000 ). In contrast, overexpression of Hes1 inhibited neurogenesis and promoted astrocyte formation (Wu et al. 2003 ).

Another subtype, Hes6 , is expressed during nervous system development in mouse and Xenopus (Koyano-Nakagawa et al. 2000 ; Pissarra et al. 2000 ). Hes6 differs from the other family members in that it promotes neuronal or myogenic differentiation (Bae et al. 2000 ; Cossins et al. 2002 ; Frior and Henrique 2005 ; Vilas-Boas and Henrique 2010 ). Moreover, unlike the other subtypes of Hes genes, Hes6 is not regulated by Notch signaling (Bae et al. 2000 ; Frior and Henrique 2005 ; Katoh and Katoh 2007 ). Proneuronal genes such as Mash1 , Math3 , and Ngn2 induce Hes6 expression (Kageyama et al. 2005 ), and their mutation blocks neuronal differentiation (Fode et al. 1998 ; Guillemot et al. 1993 ; Ma et al. 1998 ). An upstream promoter region of Hes6 in mice, rats, and humans contains binding sites for Ngn1/3 and NeuroD1 (Lee et al. 2012 ). Hes6 can also regulate the transcription of proneuronal factors, including Mash1 , Ngn2 , Nts , and NeuroD1 (Vias et al. 2008 ). Although Hes6 is known to be essential for developmental neurogenesis (Bae et al. 2000 ; Koyano-Nakagawa et al. 2000 ; Murai et al. 2011 ; Vilas-Boas and Henrique 2010 ), its effect on adult hippocampal neurogenesis has not been established. Hes6 mRNA has been found in many areas of the developing brain, but in the adult brain it has only been detected in the hippocampal dentate gyrus (Eun et al. 2010 ). To our best knowledge, the study about the expression of HES6 protein in the adult mice brain is reported only by Gene Expression Nervous System Atlas (GenSAT) at Rockefeller University. Although the differentiation potential of adult hippocampal neural stem cells is not identical to that of embryonic stem cells, both can generate neuronal cells using a similar signaling pathway (Lukaszewicz et al. 2010 ). We therefore hypothesized that Hes6 functions during adult neurogenesis, particularly in neuronal differentiation.

We investigated the effect of Hes6 on adult hippocampal neurogenesis in vivo using knockout and wild-type mice.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals

Genetically engineered mice have been used to investigate the function and role of certain gene in the context of human disease (Kim and Shin 2010 ). Non-inducible Hes6 model mice background is C57BL6/J, and Hes6 mutant mice were generated by insertion of tau - lacZ indicator gene to replace the endogenous gene (Koyano-Nakagawa et al. 2000 ). Male and female Hes6 heterozygous mice were endowed from Ewha Womans University (Professor Jaesang Kim, Seoul, South Korea) and mated each other to get homozygous male Hes6 wild and knockout mice. The animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free condition under adequate temperature (23 °C) and humidity (60 %) control with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle and were fed tap water and Purina 5008 diet ad libitum. The procedures for handling and caring of animals follow the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by Institute of Laboratory Animal Resources, U.S.A., 1996, and the experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Seoul National University (approval no. SNU-120321-4). All of the experiments were conducted in a manner to minimize the number of animals utilized and the suffering caused by the procedures used in this study.

Genotyping of Hes6 Gene

Genotype of Hes6 gene herein was determined with the process described in the following. For the effects of Hes6 knockout induced on an adult hippocampal neurogenesis, Hes6 wild and knockout mice were used ( n = 15 in each group). DNA was isolated from tail as follows. Polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were prepared by cutting collected tail tissue into small piece and adding Direct-PCR reagent (VIAGEN Biotech Inc., Los Angeles, CA, USA) with proteinase K solution (Sigma, St Louis, MO). And it was incubated overnight at 55 °C in a dry oven. The tail lysate was centrifuged and used for PCR. PCR was performed with ExTaq polymerase (TAKARA Co., Japan) using primers CAC CTC GCT GAG TAG TTT GCAC (forward), TCA CGG TCA GCT CCA GAA CCTC (reverse), and GTC TTC CAG GTT GGA CGT GTC G (Tau). PCR was performed at 57 °C annealing temperature and 30 cycles.

BrdU Administration

At 6 weeks of age, to examine a labeling of the newly generated cells in the dentate gyrus, all the animals ( n = 7 in each group) received the intraperitoneal injection of 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) at a dosage of 50 mg/kg in saline twice daily for 3 consecutive days. They were then sacrificed 4 weeks after the final day of a BrdU treatment.

Check for Body Weight

Body weight was measured on Monday morning of first week and at the end of the experiment.

Tissue Processing

Four weeks after the last BrdU injection, 10-week-old mice ( n = 7 in each group) were anesthetized with 30 mg/kg Zoletil 50 ® (Virbac, Carros, France) and perfused transcardially with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) followed by 4 % paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4). Additionally, to rule out the effect of Hes6 knockout on postnatal hippocampus, we sacrificed Hes6 wild and knockout mice at 21st postnatal day (PD21) ( n = 5 in each group). The brains were removed and postfixed in the same fixative for 12 h. PD21 brain tissues were dehydrated with graded concentrations of alcohol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Three-μm-thick sections were serially cut using a microtome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and were mounted onto silane-coated slides (Muto-glass, Tokyo, Japan). For immunohistochemical staining, brain tissues were cryoprotected by infiltration with 30 % sucrose overnight. Thirty-µm-thick brain sections were serially cut in the coronal plane using a cryostat (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Brain sections were selected between 1.46 and 2.46 mm posterior to the bregma for each animal with reference to a mouse atlas (Franklin and Paxinos 1997 ). Above area is corresponding to the dorsal hippocampus, and we used only dorsal hippocampus for the different role of the newly generated cells in dorsal and ventral hippocampus (Fanselow and Dong 2010 ). Six sections spaced 180 μm apart from each other were collected in six-well plates containing PBS and stored in storage solution until further processing.

Immunohistochemistry for β-Galactosidase, NeuN, Nestin, Ki67, and DCX

To examine the effect of Hes6 knockout on neuronal development in PD21 hippocampus, paraffin-embedded sections were used for NeuN (a marker for mature neuron) immunohistochemistry. The sections were deparaffinized and hydrated with graded concentrations of alcohol. Then the sections were placed in 400-mL jars filled with citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and heated in a microwave oven (Optiquick Compact, Moulinex) operating at a frequency of 2.45 GHz and 800 W power setting. After three heating cycles of 5 min each, slides were allowed to cool at room temperature and were washed in PBS. Free-floating sections were carefully processed under the same conditions to obtain accurate data for immunohistochemistry. From each well, selected sections (every sixth section, 5 sections per mouse) spaced 180 μm apart from each other were sequentially treated with 0.3 % hydrogen peroxide (H 2 O 2 ) in 0.1 M PBS and 10 % normal goat or rabbit serum in 0.1 M PBS. Then, 5 sections per mouse were incubated with diluted chicken anti-β-galactosidase antibody (1:300; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), mouse anti-NeuN antibody (1:1,000; Chemicon, Temecula, CA, USA), mouse anti-nestin antibody (1:200; Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), rabbit anti-Ki67 antibody (1:500; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), or goat anti-doublecortin (DCX) antibody (1:50; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) overnight at room temperature. After the reaction with primary antibody, sections were subsequently exposed to biotinylated goat anti-chicken IgG, horse anti-mouse IgG, goat anti-rabbit IgG, or rabbit anti-goat IgG (diluted 1:200; Vector Labs., Burlingame, CA, USA) and streptavidin peroxidase complex (diluted 1:200, Vector Labs.). Then, the sections were visualized by reaction with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Double Immunofluorescence

For BrdU immunostaining, DNA denaturation was conducted. Briefly, 5 sections per animal were incubated in 2N HCl and in boric acid and thereafter the sections were incubated in the mixture of rat anti-BrdU antibody (1:200, BioSource International, Camarillo, CA, USA) and mouse anti-NeuN antibody (1:500, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) or rabbit anti-S100β antibody (1:200, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) for 24 h at 4 °C. After washing 5 times for 7 min with 0.01 M PBS, the sections were then incubated in a mixture of both FITC-conjugated anti-rat IgG (1:200; Vector Labs.) and Texas red conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:500; Vector Labs.) or Texas red conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:500; Vector Labs.) for 2 h at room temperature. The immunoreactions were observed under the BX51 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a digital camera (DP71, Olympus) connected to a PC monitor.

Quantification of Histology

To elucidate the effects of Hes6 on cell proliferation, neuroblast differentiation, and neuronal integration, the corresponding areas of the dentate gyrus were measured from every sixth section (5 sections) 180 μm apart per animal. For quantitative analysis for cell counting, the number of Ki67-, DCX-, S100β-, BrdU-, or BrdU/NeuN-immunoreactive cells in the dentate gyrus of dorsal hippocampus was roughly counted using an image analysis system equipped with a computer-based CCD apoptosis in a p53-dependent man camera (Optimas 6.5 software, CyberMetrics, Scottsdale, AZ, USA).

The region of interest (ROI) in the dentate gyrus was analyzed using an image analysis system. Images were calibrated into an array of 512 × 512 pixels corresponding to a dentate gyrus (×100 primary magnification). Pixel resolution was 256 gray levels. The intensity of nestin and DCX immunoreactivity were evaluated by means of a relative optical density (ROD), which was obtained after transforming the mean gray level using the formula: ROD = log (256/mean gray level). ROD of the background was determined in unlabeled portions using NIH Image 1.59 software (ImageJ) and the value was subtracted for correction, yielding high ROD values in the presence of preserved structures and low. The ROD ratio of was calibrated as a percentage.

Western Blot

To confirm the effects of Hes6 knockout on the expression level of nestin in hippocampus, 10-week-old mice ( n = 3 in each group) were sacrificed by decapitation after deep anesthesia. Brains were rapidly removed and hippocampi were dissected out with a surgical blade. Hippocampi were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further processing. Tissues were homogenized in 50 mM PBS (pH 7.4) containing 0.1 mM ethylene glycol bis(2-aminoethyl ether)- N , N , N ′, N ′ tetraacetic acid (EGTA) (pH 8.0), 0.2 % Nonidet P-40, 10 mM ethylenediamine-tetraacetic acid (EDTA) (pH 8.0), 15 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 100 mM β-glycerophosphate, 50 mM NaF, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM sodium orthovanadate, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT). After centrifugation, the protein level was determined in the supernatants using a Micro BCA protein assay kit using bovine serum albumin as the standard (Pierce Chemical, Rockford, IL, USA). Aliquots containing 50 μg of total protein were boiled in a loading buffer that contained 150 mM Tris (pH 6.8), 3 mM DTT, 6 % SDS, 0.3 % bromophenol blue, and 30 % glycerol. The aliquots were then loaded onto a polyacrylamide gel. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred from the gel to nitrocellulose transfer membranes (Pall Corp., East Hills, NY, USA). To reduce background staining, the membranes were incubated with 5 % non-fat dry milk in PBS containing 0.1 % Tween 20 for 45 min, followed by incubation with mouse anti-nestin antibody (1:10,000), peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse IgG, and an enhanced luminol-based chemiluminescent (ECL) kit (Pierce Chemical). The expression level of nestin from Western blot was determined by the densitometric analysis as the quantification of ROD of each band using NIH Image 1.59 software.

Statistical Analysis

Data represent the means of experiments performed for each experimental area. Differences among the means were evaluated by a one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) in order to elucidate differences between wild and Hes6 knockout groups. A p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Genotyping of the Hes6 Gene, Changes in Body Weight, and Detection of β-Galactosidase

We confirmed the genotype of the Hes6 gene in order to distinguish homozygous wild-type mice, homozygous knockout mice, and heterozygotes. A 0.4-kb band was observed in for the homozygous wild group, a 0.3-kb band in the homozygous Hes6 knockout group, and both 0.4- and 0.3-kb bands in the heterozygotes (Fig. 1 a). Body weight at 6- and 10-week-old group tended to be higher in wild group compared to knockout group, but the difference was not significant between groups (Fig. 1 b). In order to detect the expression of tau - lacZ reporter gene, we conducted immunostaining of β-galactosidase in the hippocampus of the 10-week-old groups. β-Galactosidase-immunoreactive cells were observed only in the knockout group, and they reside in the subgranular zone of the hippocampal dentate gyrus (Fig. 1 c).

Fig. 1.

Effects of Hes6 Knockout on PD21 Neuronal Development in Hippocampus

We compared the effects of Hes6 gene knockout on postnatal hippocampal neuronal development by immunostaining mature neuronal marker NeuN, in order to investigate whether the difference in adult neurogenesis is derived from the developmental brain defect caused by Hes6 knockout in postnatal period. As previous studies which reported no significant phenotypic difference between wild and knockout (Koyano-Nakagawa et al. 2000 ; Qian et al. 2006 ), we also could not distinguish the brain of knockout mice from that of wild in the aspect of hippocampal neuronal development at PD21 (Fig. 2 a, b).

Fig. 2.

Effects of Hes6 Knockout on Neural Stem Cells and Progenitor Cells

In both wild and knockout groups, nestin-immunoreactive cells and fibers were detected in the subgranular zone and granule cell layer of the dentate gyrus (Fig. 3 a, b). Instead of counting the number of cells and fibers, we compared nestin immunoreactivity by measuring ROD. There was no statistical significance between the groups in the nestin immunoreactivity (Fig. 3 c). Additionally, the protein level of nestin was not different between wild and knockout groups (Fig. 3 d).

Fig. 3.

Effects of Hes6 Knockout on Cell Proliferation

In all groups, clustered Ki67-positive nuclei were detected mainly in the subgranular zone of the dentate gyrus (Fig. 4 a, b). The number of Ki67-positive nuclei per section in dorsal hippocampus was similar between the wild and knockout groups with no statistically significant differences. The average number of Ki67-immunoreactive nuclei was 18.95 per section in the wild group and was 16.5 per section in the knockout group (Fig. 4 c).

Fig. 4.

Effects of Hes6 Knockout on Neuroblast Differentiation

In wild group, DCX-immunoreactive neuroblasts and immature neurons had dendrites and some were well developed (Fig. 5 a, b). In the dorsal hippocampus, the average number of DCX-immunoreactive neuroblasts and immature neurons in the wild group was 177.77 per section (Fig. 5 e). DCX-immunoreactive neuroblasts and immature neurons in the knockout group were present only sparsely in the dentate gyrus (Fig. 5 c, d). The average number of DCX-immunoreactive neuroblasts and immature neurons in the knockout group significantly decreased in the dentate gyrus compared to that of the wild group with 133.54 (Fig. 5 e). In this group, DCX-immunoreactive dendrites were poorly developed in the knockout group compared to the wild group. DCX immunoreactivity in the knockout group was only 70.88 % of that in the wild group (Fig. 5 f).

Fig. 5.

Effects of Hes6 Knockout on Integration into Mature Astrocytes

Four weeks after BrdU treatment, we additionally assessed whether BrdU-immunoreactive cells developed into the mature astrocytes. We choose S100β as a marker of mature astrocyte to rule out the GFAP-immunoreactive neural stem cells. In both groups, S100β-immunoreactive cells were abundantly detected in the dentate gyrus (Fig. 6 b, e). There is no statistical difference in the number of S100β-immunoreactive cells between the wild and knockout groups (113.25 % of that in the wild group) (Fig. 8 e). Unlike the case of double staining of BrdU and NeuN, we could not detect BrdU + –S100β + -immunoreactive cells in the dentate gyrus of both groups (Fig. 6 c, f).

Fig. 6.

Fig. 8.

Effects of Hes6 Knockout on Integration into Mature Neurons

Four weeks after BrdU treatment, BrdU-immunoreactive cells were assessed in the dentate gyrus of the wild and knockout groups because DCX is known to be exclusively expressed in immature neurons from 1 to 28 days of cell age (Brown et al. 2003 ). BrdU-immunoreactive cells were primarily distributed in the granule cell layer (Fig. 7 a, d). In the dorsal hippocampus, the average number of BrdU-immunoreactive cells was 16.68 per section in the wild group and was 14.36 per section in the knockout group with no statistically significant difference (Fig. 8 a). To elucidate whether newly generated BrdU-immunoreactive cells were incorporated into mature granule cells, double immunofluorescence staining was performed. The number of BrdU + –NeuN + -immunoreactive cells in the wild group was 8.84 per section and was 8.68 per section in the knockout group (Figs. 7 a–f, 8 b). The proportion of BrdU-immunoreactive cells that were colocalized with NeuN was 53.64 % in the wild group and 63.41 % in the knockout group (118.22 % of the wild group) (Fig. 8 c). NeuN-immunoreactive mature neurons were detected throughout the dentate gyrus, especially in the granule cell layer (Fig. 7 b, e). In the knockout group, NeuN-immunoreactive mature neurons were similar in number and preserved well in the dentate gyrus (Fig. 8 d).

Discussion

In our study, we evaluated that the Hes6 influences on an adult hippocampal neurogenesis using the Hes6 knockout mice. With neurogenic marker proteins, we observed neural stem cells, cell proliferation, neuroblast differentiation, and neuronal integration in the hippocampal dentate gyrus 4 weeks after BrdU injection.

Nestin revealed that knockout of Hes6 did not markedly affect pool of neural stem cells and progenitor cells. However, Ki67-immunoreactive proliferating cells were slightly decreased with no statistical significance in the dentate gyrus of knockout group. To our knowledge, unlike the regulation of neural stem cells by Hes1 , Hes3 , and Hes5 (Hatakeyama et al. 2004 ; Hirata et al. 2000 ), the effects of Hes6 knockout on neural stem cells were not demonstrated, and we observed that the neural stem cell pools in the subgranular zone of the dentate gyrus were not significantly different between the wild and knockout groups. Reports have differed regarding the effects of Hes6 on cell proliferation. In an in vitro study, Hes6 was shown to act as a regulator of the cell cycle and to repress cell proliferation (Eun et al. 2008 ). However, in other in vitro studies, Hes6 was observed to enhance cancer cell proliferation in breast and brain tumorigenesis (Haapa-Paananen et al. 2012 ; Hartman et al. 2009 ). Hes6 is indirectly related to Hes1 , which functions to increase cell proliferation by sustaining the undifferentiated state (Ishibashi et al. 1994 ; Ohtsuka et al. 2001 ). Although we did not examine the interaction between the two molecules in the present study, Hes6 and Hes1 mRNA are known from previous work to oscillate in an anti-phasic manner during the 24 h of circadian cycle (Lee et al. 2012 ).

Next, we employed DCX immunohistochemical staining in the dentate gyrus of adult hippocampus to observe the effects of Hes6 on neuroblast differentiation. DCX is a microtubule-associated protein expressed in migrating neuroblasts or immature neurons (Brown et al. 2003 ; Yi et al. 2010 ; Nam et al. 2014 ). Above all, the number of DCX-immunoreactive neuroblast and immature neuron was significantly decreased in the Hes6 knockout group compared to age-matched wild-type littermates. Also, both cellular density and dendritic complexity in DCX-immunoreactive neuroblasts and immature neuron were reduced in the Hes6 knockout group compared to age-matched wild-type controls. This result is consistent with that of a previous study, which demonstrated that either Hes6 knockdown using siRNA in vitro cell cultures or Hes6 knockout during the developmental period impaired neuronal differentiation (Jhas et al. 2006 ). Importantly, our study also demonstrated participation of Hes6 in neuronal differentiation during adult hippocampal neurogenesis. Also previous studies reported that Hes6 gene is expressed in both neuronal precursors and cells in a stage of neuronal differentiation (Koyano-Nakagawa et al. 2000 ; Bae et al. 2000 ). Although the mechanism of Hes6 action in neuronal differentiation during the adult neurogenesis remains unclear, linoleic acid-mediated enhancement of neuronal differentiation was suggested to be mediated by Hes6 (Okui et al. 2011 ). Additionally, activated endothelial cells isolated from ischemic brain were found to promote neuronal differentiation by elevating levels of Hes6 mRNA (Teng et al. 2008 ).

In the present study, we confirmed the fates of proliferating cells into astrocytes and mature neurons in the dentate gyrus. S100β-immunoreactive astrocytes were slightly increased in the dentate gyrus of hippocampus in the knockout group, although we could not find any statistical significance between wild and knockout groups. During adult neurogenesis, neural stem cells are determined to choose either neuronal or glial cell fate, but the list of participated genes and the mechanism are not clearly defined (Hodge et al. 2012 ; Klempin et al. 2012 ; Ro et al. 2013 ). Among those genes, Hes6 is known to participate in neuronal differentiation and also repressed the differentiation into astrocytes in cell culture (Jhas et al. 2006 ). We suggest that Hes6 may partially be involved in astrocyte differentiation in adult hippocampus; however, only Hes6 knockout is not enough to show significant difference. Our in vivo observations suggest that more complex mechanism may be involved in the regulation of neural stem cell’s differentiation into astrocytes. Also, the fact that neural stem cells divide asymmetrically and only small portion of BrdU-incorporated cells differentiate into astrocytes (Encinas et al. 2011 ) can be thought as an another reason of no significant difference in astrocyte differentiation.

We additionally carried out double staining for BrdU and NeuN, to investigate whether Hes6 knockout modulates the integration into mature neurons in the hippocampal dentate gyrus. The number of BrdU and NeuN double immunoreactive cells was not significantly different between wild and knockout mice. Also, we did not observe any significant difference in the BrdU-immunoreactive cells and NeuN-immunoreactive mature neurons. As a result, the ratio of BrdU + –NeuN + - versus BrdU + -immunoreactive cells was statistically insignificant between the Hes6 wild and knockout groups. These results suggest that Hes6 knockout selectively reduces the neuroblast differentiation, without affecting the survival of the matured BrdU-incorporated cells in the dentate gyrus. Additionally, the relative number of the NeuN-immunoreactive mature neuron was not significantly different between the wild and knockout groups. For the reason of an unnoticeable difference, we suggest that Hes6 may accelerate neuronal differentiation without significant effect on neuronal maturation in vivo or other proneuronal factors can compensate the effect of Hes6 gene knockout. In the other aspect, dentate granule cells persist until 11 months after their generation, the small portion of the granule cell was continuously replaced by the cells from adult neurogenesis (Dayer et al. 2003 ; Kempermann et al. 2003 ), and the period of DCX expression is relatively short compared to the NeuN (Brown et al. 2003 ). So the difference between the wild and knockout can be unnoticeable.

We did not elucidate the mechanism of Hes6 action in proneuronal differentiation and anti-gliogenesis, but other studies have shown that Hes6 antagonizes the transcription of Hes1 and Hes5 or their repressive action on proneuronal genes (Bae et al. 2000 ; Frior and Henrique 2005 ; Jhas et al. 2006 ; Vilas-Boas and Henrique 2010 ). In addition, both HES6 and nuclear factor-κB mutually inhibit one another, and their balance prevents premature neuronal differentiation and maintains progenitor pool during periods of brain development (Methot et al. 2013 ). However, overexpression of Hes6 induced neuronal apoptosis in a p53-dependent manner (Eun et al. 2010 ).

From the results presented here, we suggest that Hes6 knockout does not significantly affect the neural stem cells' pool and their proliferation. Only neuroblast differentiation was significantly impaired by Hes6 knockout without showing any significant losses in mature neuron. Taken together, our results demonstrate that Hes6 is a modulating factor of neuroblast differentiation during adult neurogenesis in the hippocampal dentate gyrus.
