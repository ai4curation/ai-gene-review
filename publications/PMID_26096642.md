Summary

In maize ( Zea mays ), the mitogen‐activated protein kinase ZmMPK5 has been shown to be involved in abscisic acid (ABA)‐induced antioxidant defence and to enhance the tolerance of plants to drought, salt stress and oxidative stress. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms are poorly understood. Here, using ZmMPK5 as bait in yeast two‐hybrid screening, a protein interacting with ZmMPK5 named ZmABA2, which belongs to a member of the short‐chain dehydrogenase/reductase family, was identified. Pull‐down assay and bimolecular fluorescence complementation analysis and co‐immunoprecipitation test confirmed that ZmMPK5 interacts with ZmABA2 in vitro and in vivo . Phosphorylation of Ser173 in ZmABA2 by ZmMPK5 was shown to increase the activity of ZmABA2 and the protein stability. Various abiotic stimuli induced the expression of ZmABA2 in leaves of maize plants. Pharmacological, biochemical and molecular biology and genetic analyses showed that both ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 coordinately regulate the content of ABA. Overexpression of ZmABA2 in tobacco plants was found to elevate the content of ABA, regulate seed germination and root growth under drought and salt stress and enhance the tolerance of tobacco plants to drought and salt stress. These results suggest that ZmABA2 is a direct target of ZmMPK5 and is involved in ABA biosynthesis and functions.

Keywords: abiotic stress, abscisic acid biosynthesis, maize, mitogen‐activated protein kinase, protein interaction, short‐chain dehydrogenase/reductase

Introduction

Abscisic acid (ABA) is a classic phytohormone that plays important roles in various aspects of plant growth and development, including seed development, dormancy, germination, vegetative growth and environmental stress responses. In higher plants, ABA is synthesized from an ‘indirect’ pathway through the cleavage of a C40 carotenoid precursor, followed by a two‐step conversion of the intermediate xanthoxin to ABA via ABA‐aldehyde (Nambara and Marion‐Poll, 2005 ; Xiong and Zhu, 2003 ; Xu et al ., 2013 ). Under drought and salt stress, ABA accumulates in plant cells, stimulates stomatal closure and regulates the expression of many genes, thus increasing the plant's capacity to cope with stress conditions (Cutler et al ., 2010 ; Fujita et al ., 2011 ; Hubbard et al ., 2010 ; Umezawa et al ., 2010 ). Protein kinases, such as SNF1‐related protein kinase 2s (SnRK2s), mitogen‐activated protein kinases (MAPKs), calcium‐dependent protein kinases and Ca 2+ /CaM‐dependent protein kinase (CCaMK), have been shown to play important roles in ABA signalling (Cutler et al ., 2010 ; Danquah et al ., 2014 ; Ding et al ., 2013 ; Fujii and Zhu, 2012 ; Hubbard et al ., 2010 ; Liu, 2012 ; Ma et al ., 2012 ; Shi et al ., 2012 , 2014 ; Umezawa et al ., 2010 ).

MAPK cascades have been shown to play a central role in the process, whereby extracellular stimuli are transduced into intracellular responses in plant cells (Colcombet and Hirt, 2008 ; Rodriguez et al ., 2010 ). MAPK and immediate upstream activators, MAPK kinase (MAPKK) and MAPKK kinase, constitute a functionally interlinked MAPK cascade. In the genomes of Arabidopsis, rice and maize, there are 20, 17 and 19 MAPK genes, respectively (Liu et al ., 2013b ; MAPK Group, 2002 ; Reyna and Yang, 2006 ). In Arabidopsis, AtMPK3 , AtMPK6 , AtMPK9 and AtMPK12 have been shown to be involved in ABA signalling (Danquah et al ., 2014 ; Gudesblat et al ., 2007 ; Jammes et al ., 2009 ; Liu, 2012 ; Lu et al ., 2002 ; Xing et al ., 2008 ). Overexpression of AtMPK3 increased ABA sensitivity in ABA‐induced postgermination arrest of growth (Lu et al ., 2002 ), and guard cell‐specific antisense inhibition of AtMPK3 expression displayed partial insensitivity to ABA in inhibition of stomatal opening (Gudesblat et al ., 2007 ). atmpk6 mutant blocked and overexpressing AtMPK6 enhanced ABA‐dependent expression of catalase 1 ( CAT1 ) and H 2 O 2 production (Xing et al ., 2008 ). AtMPK6 was also shown to be involved in ABA inhibition of seed germination and in drought tolerance. Double mutants of atmpk9 / atmpk12 exhibited enhanced transpirational water loss and ABA and H 2 O 2 insensitivity in both stomatal opening and closure, suggesting that MPK9 and MPK12 are positive regulators of ABA signalling in guard cells (Jammes et al ., 2009 ). In rice, the expression of OsMPK1 , OsMPK4 , OsMPK5 , OsMPK7 and OsMPK14 was induced by ABA treatment (Liu, 2012 ; Shi et al ., 2014 ; Xiong and Yang, 2003 ; Zhang et al ., 2012 , 2014b ). OsMPK5 was shown to positively regulate drought, salt and cold tolerance (Xiong and Yang, 2003 ). OsMPK1 and OsMPK5 are required for ABA‐induced antioxidant defence (Shi et al ., 2014 ; Zhang et al ., 2012 ). Moreover, ABA inhibition of root growth was impaired in the mutant of osmpk1 , suggesting that disruption of OsMPK1 reduces ABA sensitivity in root growth of rice seedlings (Shi et al ., 2014 ). In maize, ZmMPK3, ZmMPK5 and ZmMPK7 were also shown to be involved in ABA‐induced antioxidant defence and to enhance the tolerance of plants to drought, salt stress and oxidative stress (Ding et al ., 2009 ; Lin et al ., 2009 ; Wang et al ., 2010a ; Zhang et al ., 2006 , 2010 , 2014a ; Zong et al ., 2009 ). These results indicate that MAPKs play important roles in ABA signalling, including antioxidant defence, guard cell signalling, seed germination and root growth, and stress tolerance.

Although AtMPK6 in Arabidopsis and the homologues in maize and rice, ZmMPK5 and OsMPK1, have been shown to be involved in ABA‐induced antioxidant defence, the mechanisms by which how ABA‐induced activation of these MAPKs regulates the antioxidant defence in ABA signalling remain to be determined. Identifying the components in the downstream of these MAPKs is very important for the understanding of the roles of these MAPKs in ABA signalling. In this study, using the full length of ZmMPK5 as bait to screen a maize leaf cDNA library, a protein named ZmABA2, which belongs to a member of the short‐chain dehydrogenase/reductase (SDR) family and has high similarity with rice OsABA2 and Arabidopsis AtABA2, was identified. Previous studies have shown that AtABA2 and OsABA2 are involved in ABA biosynthesis (Cheng et al ., 2002 ; Endo et al ., 2014 ; Gonzales‐Guzman et al ., 2002 ). Evidence is provided to show that phosphorylation of Ser173 in ZmABA2 by ZmMPK5 affects ZmABA2 activity and protein stability and that both ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 coordinately regulate the biosynthesis of ABA. Moreover, it is suggested that overexpression of ZmABA2 in tobacco regulates seed germination and root growth under drought and salt stress and enhances the tolerance of plants to drought and salt stress.

Results

Characterization of a ZmMPK5‐associated protein

To isolate interacting proteins of ZmMPK5 in maize leaves, full‐length ZmMPK5 expressed as a fusion protein with the GAL4 DNA‐binding domain (GAL4‐DBD) was used as bait in a yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) screen of a maize leaf cDNA library fused to the GAL4 activation domain (GAL4‐AD). Approximately one million transformants were screened, and several independent cDNA clones encoding a protein designated ZmABA2 were identified (data not shown). To validate the interaction between ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 in yeast, the interaction of ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 was assessed by growth in the absence of histidine in the presence of 50 m m 3‐aminotriazole and by β‐galactosidase assays. The results showed that ZmMPK5 interacts with ZmABA2 in yeast (Figure 1 a).

Figure 1.

Using 5ˊ rapid amplification of cDNA ends, a full‐length cDNA of ZmABA2 (GenBank accession no. EU965612 ) was identified. The ZmABA2 cDNA sequence was 1090 bp in length and had an open reading frame (ORF) of 852 nucleotides, encoding a protein of 283 amino acids with a predicted molecular mass of approximately 29.3 kDa and a pI of 5.75. Based on a database search, ZmABA2 is a member of the SDR family. Like AtABA2 in Arabidopsis, ZmABA2 contains six conserved motifs, including an NAD/NADH binding site (TGXXXGXG) in motif I and a catalytic motif (YXXS/T/AK) in motif V (Figure S1a). These six motifs have been well‐characterized in the members of the SDR superfamily across microorganisms, animals and plants (Cheng et al ., 2002 ; Hwang et al ., 2012 ).

Our phylogenetic analysis showed that the deduced amino acid sequence of ZmABA2 was most similar to Oryza sativa OsABA2 (76% similarity), followed by Zingiber zerumbet ZSD1 (68% similarity) (Figure S1b). The similarities of ZmABA2 with Solanum tuberosum StABA2, Citrus sinensis CsABA2 and Arabidopsis thaliana AtABA2 were 64%, 59% and 58%, respectively.

Validation of the interaction between ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 in vitro and in vivo

To confirm the interaction between ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 in vitro , glutathione S‐transferase (GST) pull‐down assay was performed. The full‐length ZmABA2 was expressed with GST tag in Escherichia coli , and the GST‐ZmABA2 recombinant proteins were purified on glutathione‐Sepharose 4B. ZmMPK5 was expressed in maize protoplasts as a Myc tag recombinant protein. ZmMPK5‐Myc was incubated with bead‐bound GST‐ZmABA2 fusion protein or GST alone. Figure 1 b shows that the recombinant protein ZmMPK5‐Myc interacts with GST prey only but not with GST alone.

To investigate the interaction of ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 in vivo , the co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assay and the bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay were conducted. For Co‐IP test, the total proteins extracted from the cotransfected protoplasts were immunoprecipitated with anti‐Myc antibody to conjugated protein A agarose. Immunoblot analysis of the agarose using anti‐Flag antibody revealed an interaction between ZmMPK5‐Myc and ZmABA2‐Flag (Figure 1 c). For BiFC assay, the coding regions of ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 were cloned into the plasmids pSPYNE and pSPYCE to generate the constructs pSPYNE‐ZmMPK5 and pSPYCE‐ZmABA2, respectively. The constructs were transiently expressed in onion epidermis cells to test the interaction of ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2. Strong yellow fluorescence was observed in the nucleus and in the cytosol (Figure 1 d), suggesting that ZmMPK5 can interact with ZmABA2 in these subcellular sites.

Ser173 of ZmABA2 is a phosphorylation site and its modification affects ZmABA2 activity and stability

To investigate whether ZmMPK5 can phosphorylate ZmABA2 in vitro , immunocomplex kinase activity assay was performed using recombinant His‐ZmABA2 as substrate. As shown in Figure 2 a, a strong phosphorylation band was detected, suggesting that ZmABA2 is a phosphorylation substrate of ZmMPK5 in vitro .

Figure 2.

To identify which amino acid residue is required for ZmMPK5 phosphorylation, KinasePhos 2.0 (Wong et al ., 2007 ) was used to search for putative MAPK phosphorylation sites. Five Ser residues (Ser6, Ser7, Ser11, Ser173 and Ser 199) were predicted as potential ZmMPK5 phosphorylation sites according to the common Ser/Tyr kinases phosphorylated motifs (Figure S2). Recombinant ZmABA2 proteins were generated by mutating the putative Ser (S) residues to Ala (A) by site‐directed mutagenesis and were used as substrates for ZmMPK5 phosphorylation assay. As shown in Figure 2 b, ZmMPK5 could almost not phosphorylate the S173A mutant, whereas the other mutants could still be phosphorylated, suggesting that Ser173 in ZmABA2 is a ZmMPK5 phosphorylation site.

Previous studies have shown that phosphorylation can enhance enzymatic activity or alter protein stability (Liu and Zhang, 2004 ; Wang et al ., 2010b ; Yoo et al ., 2008 ). To determine whether phosphorylation of Ser173 affects ZmABA2 activity, purified ZmABA2‐GST and ZmABA2S173A‐GST proteins were used to catalyse the NAD‐dependent oxidation of xanthoxin, and ZmMPK5 was added before reaction. Experimental results showed that phosphorylation of Ser173 significantly increased the activity of ZmABA2 (Figure 2 c). To determine the effect of phosphorylation on the protein stability, ZmABA2 and ZmABA2S173A were introduced into maize protoplasts, and the stability of ZmABA2 and ZmABA2S173A proteins was monitored after cycloheximide (CHX) treatment in the transfected protoplasts. After 4‐h treatment, ZmABA2 protein was still stable, but ZmABA2S173A protein exhibited a marked degradation (Figure 2 d).

The expression of ZmABA2 in response to various abiotic stimuli

To investigate whether the expression of ZmABA2 is regulated by various abiotic stimuli, the maize plants were treated with polyethylene glycol (PEG), NaCl, cold, wound, ABA, H 2 O 2 , ethephon (ETH) and brassinosteroid (BR), and the expression of ZmABA2 in the treated leaves was analysed by real‐time quantitative PCR. Experimental results showed that all these treatments induced a rapid increase in the expression of Zm ABA2 (Figure 3 a–d). A significant increase in the expression of ZmABA2 was observed within 15 min after the treatments of H 2 O 2 (Figure 3 a), wound (Figure 3 c) and PEG (Figure 3 d); or within 30 min after the treatments of ABA (Figure 3 a), BR and ETH (Figure 3 b) and NaCl (Figure 3 d); or within 120 min after cold treatment (Figure 3 c). The maximum increase in the expression of ZmABA2 occurred at 15 min (H 2 O 2 and PEG) or 30 min (BR and wound) or 120 min (ETH, cold and NaCl) or 240 min (ABA) after these treatments.

Figure 3.

ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 are involved in ABA biosynthesis

To investigate whether ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 are involved in ABA biosynthesis, four experiments were conducted. First, maize seedlings were pretreated with the MAPKK inhibitors 2‐(2‐amino‐3‐methoxyphenyl)‐4H‐1‐benzopyran‐4‐one (PD98059) and 1,4‐diamino‐2,3‐dicyano‐1,4‐bis(o‐aminophenyl‐mercapto) butadiene (U0126) and then exposed to PEG and NaCl treatments. Pretreatments with these inhibitors significantly inhibited the increases induced by PEG and NaCl in the content of ABA (Figure 4 a), suggesting that MAPK pathway is involved in the regulation of ABA content under stress conditions. Second, the mutant of osmpk1 was used. OsMPK1, which is a homologue of ZmMPK5 in rice, was shown to interact with OsABA2, a homologue of ZmABA2, tested by Y2H and BiFC (Figure S3), and to phosphorylate OsABA2 in vitro (Figure S4a), and the mutation of osmpk1 decreased the stability of OsABA2 protein (Figure S4b). Moreover, ZmMPK5 and OsMPK1 phosphorylated ZmABA2/OsABA2 each other, although the phosphorylations of OsABA2 by ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 by OsMPK1 were weaker (Figure S4a). These results suggest that ZmMPK5 and OsMPK1 are functioning in a similar manner. Mutation of osmpk1 blocked the increases in the content of ABA induced by PEG and NaCl (Figure 4 b), indicating that OsMPK1 is required for the stress‐induced increase in the content of ABA in rice. Third, a transient gene expression analysis and a transient RNA interference (RNAi) test in protoplasts, which have been proven to be applicable tools for functional analysis of plant genes (Cao et al ., 2014 ; Ding et al ., 2013 ; Ma et al ., 2012 ; Shi et al ., 2012 , 2014 ; Wen et al ., 2015 ; Yoo et al ., 2007 ; Zhai et al ., 2009 ; Zhang et al ., 2012 , 2014b ), were used. Protoplast transfections with ZmMPK5‐Myc and ZmABA2‐Flag plasmids caused a significant increase in the expression of ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 , respectively, but transfections in protoplasts with in vitro ‐synthesized double‐stranded RNAs against ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 resulted in a substantial suppression in the expression of ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 , respectively (Figure S5). Transient expression of ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 in protoplasts resulted in significant increases in the content of ABA, when compared with that in the control protoplasts, but RNAi‐mediated silencing of ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 decreased the content of ABA (Figure 4 c). Further, in the protoplasts with transiently silenced ZmABA2 , the transient expression of ZmMPK5 only induced a slight increase in the content of ABA, compared with the control (Figure 4 c). The transient expression of ZmABA2 in the protoplasts with transiently silenced ZmMPK5 had a similar effect on the content of ABA. These results suggest that both ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 coordinately regulate the biosynthesis of ABA. Finally, the role of Ser173 phosphorylation in ZmABA2 in the regulation of ABA content was analysed. Expression of ZmABA2S173A in protoplasts exhibited a significant decrease in the content of ABA, compared with that in the protoplasts transiently expressing ZmABA2 (Figure 4 d). Further, co‐expression of ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 in protoplasts significantly increased the content of ABA, but the content of ABA was reduced in the protoplasts transiently co‐expressing ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2S173A . These results suggest that the phosphorylation of Ser173 in ZmABA2 mediates the regulation of ABA content.

Figure 4.

Overexpression of ZmABA2 in tobacco regulates seed germination and root growth under drought and salt stress

To examine the role of ZmABA2 in the regulation of seed germination and root growth under drought and salt stress, the transgenic tobacco plants expressing ZmABA2 ( ZmABA2 ‐OE) were generated. The expression of ZmABA2 in ZmABA2 ‐OE was confirmed by semi‐quantitative RT‐PCR (Figure S6). Under the control conditions, there was no obvious difference in seed germination between ZmABA2 ‐OE seeds and wild‐type (WT) seeds (Figure 5 a). Under drought induced by PEG treatment (20% PEG; simulation of drought stress) and salt stress by NaCl treatment (100 m m NaCl), the germination of both ZmABA2 ‐OE seeds and WT seeds was inhibited, and ZmABA2 ‐OE seeds were relatively sensitive to drought and salt stress than the WT (Figure 5 a). The germination rates of ZmABA2 ‐OE seeds were significantly lower than those of WT seeds under drought (Figure 5 b) and salt stress (Figure 5 c).

Figure 5.

To test whether overexpression of ZmABA2 affects root growth of seedlings under drought and salt stress, 5‐d‐old seedlings of ZmABA2 ‐OE and WT were treated with 30% PEG and 200 m m NaCl for 12 days, and the length of primary root was measured. Under the control conditions, there was no significant difference in root lengths between ZmABA2 ‐OE plants and WT plants (Figure 5 d, e). Drought and salt stress significantly inhibited root growth in both ZmABA2 ‐OE and WT seedlings, in which the roots of ZmABA2 ‐OE seedlings were relatively insensitive to these stresses compared with those of WT (Figure 5 d,e). These results suggest that the overexpression of ZmABA2 maintains root growth of seedlings under drought and salt stress.

Overexpression of ZmABA2 enhances the tolerance of tobacco plants to drought and salt stress

To investigate the role of ZmABA2 in the tolerance of plants to drought and salt stress, 4‐week‐old seedlings of ZmABA2 ‐OE and WT were treated with 20% PEG and 200 m m NaCl, and the content of malondialdehyde (MDA) and the percentage of electrolyte leakage, which are indicators of membrane damage, were analysed. Overexpression of ZmABA2 significantly increased the content of ABA under the control conditions and further increased the content of ABA under drought and salt stress (Figure 6 a). Drought and salt stress induced significant increases in the content of MDA (Figure 6 b) and the percentage of electrolyte leakage (Figure 6 c) in both ZmABA2 ‐OE and WT plants. However, the increases in the content of MDA and the percentage of electrolyte leakage were less in the leaves of ZmABA2 ‐OE plants exposed to drought and salt stress than those in WT plants (Figure 6 b,c).

Figure 6.

To further determine the function of ZmABA2 in the tolerance of stresses, 4‐week‐old seedlings of ZmABA2 ‐OE and WT grown on soil pots were treated by withholding water for 14 days or by supplying with 400 m m NaCl for 14 days. Under the control conditions, the size of ZmABA2 ‐OE plants was smaller than that of WT plants (Figure 6 d,e). After the treatments of drought and salt stress, WT seedlings showed severe chlorosis or wilting phenotypes, whereas ZmABA2 ‐OE plants showed a relatively insensitive phenotype compared with WT plants (Figure 6 d). Under drought and salt stress, ZmABA2 ‐OE plants had heavier fresh weight (Figure 6 e) and higher relative water content (RWC) than those of WT plants (Figure 6 f). These results indicate that ZmABA2 is involved in the tolerance of tobacco plants to drought and salt stress.

Discussion

MAPKs have been shown to be involved in ABA signalling, including antioxidant defence, guard cell signalling, seed germination and root growth, and stress tolerance (Danquah et al ., 2014 ; Liu, 2012 ). Arabidopsis AtMPK6, one of the best characterized MAPKs, and its homologues in maize and rice, ZmMPK5 and OsMPK1, have been shown to be required for ABA‐induced antioxidant defence (Ding et al ., 2009 ; Lin et al ., 2009 ; Shi et al ., 2014 ; Xing et al ., 2008 ; Zhang et al ., 2012 ). However, it is unknown how ABA‐induced activation of these MAPKs regulates the antioxidant defence in ABA signalling. Previous studies have identified and characterized some substrates of AtMPK6, including 1‐aminocyclopropane‐1‐carboxylic acid synthase‐6 (ACS6; Joo et al ., 2008 ; Liu and Zhang, 2004 ), ethylene‐insensitive 3 (EIN3; Yoo et al ., 2008 ), ethylene response factor ERF104 (Bethke et al ., 2009 ), nitrate reductase isoform NIA2 (Wang et al ., 2010b ), C2H2‐type zinc finger proteins ZAT10 (Nguyen et al ., 2012 ) and ZAT6 (Liu et al ., 2013a ), and MYB transcription factor AtMYB41 (Hoang et al ., 2012 ). ACS6, EIN3 and ERF104 have been shown to be involved in ethylene biosynthesis or signalling (Bethke et al ., 2009 ; Joo et al ., 2008 ; Liu and Zhang, 2004 ; Yoo et al ., 2008 ). NIA2 phosphorylation by AtMPK6 is required for NO production and signal transduction in response to H 2 O 2 during Arabidopsis root development (Wang et al ., 2010b ). The phosphorylation of AtMYB41 and ZAT6 by AtMPK6 was shown to be required for seed germination and root growth under salt stress (Hoang et al ., 2012 ; Liu et al ., 2013a ). Using protein microarray methods, very large numbers of putative AtMPK6 substrates, including transcription factors, transcription regulators, splicing factors, receptors, histones and others, were suggested (Feilner et al ., 2005 ; Popescu et al ., 2009 ). In rice, using Y2H system and pull‐down, Co‐IP, BiFC and kinase activity assay, 41 putative interacting proteins of OsMPK1 were identified (Singh et al ., 2012 ). However, it is not clear whether these substrates are involved in ABA‐mediated cellular and physiological processes in plants. In this study, using ZmMPK5 as bait in Y2H screening, a new ZmMPK5‐interacting protein, ZmABA2, was identified in yeast (Figure 1 a). Previous studies have shown that AtABA2 and OsABA2 are involved in ABA biosynthesis, in which they catalyse the conversion of xanthoxin to abscisic aldehyde in the cytosol (Cheng et al ., 2002 ; Endo et al ., 2014 ; Gonzales‐Guzman et al ., 2002 ). Pull‐down, Co‐IP and BiFC confirmed that ZmMPK5 interacts with ZmABA2 in vitro and in vivo (Figure 1 b–d). Ser173 of ZmABA2 was shown to be a phosphorylation site and its modifications affect ZmABA2 activity and stability (Figure 2 b–d). Pharmacological, biochemical and molecular biology and genetic analyses further showed that both ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 are required for ABA biosynthesis (Figure 4 a–d). Overexpression of ZmABA2 in tobacco increased the content of ABA in stress and nonstressed leaves of tobacco plants (Figure 5 a), also inhibited seed germination and maintained root growth of seedlings under drought and salt stress (Figure 5 b–e) and enhanced the tolerance of tobacco plants to drought and salt stress (Figure 6 ). In Arabidopsis, it was shown that aba2‐11 seeds were more tolerant of salt stress and osmotic stress at germination than WT seedlings, and aba2‐11 plants were more sensitive to salt or osmotic stress at later stages of development (Gonzales‐Guzman et al ., 2002 ). AtABA2 ‐overexpressing transgenic plants exhibited more tolerance to salt stress than WT (Lin et al ., 2007 ). Moreover, a genetic approach also reveals that ABA promotes primary root elongation in maize under water stress (Sharp, 2002 ). Taken together, our results clearly indicate that ZmABA2 is a direct target of ZmMPK5 and is involved in ABA biosynthesis and functions (Figure 7 ).

Figure 7.

Under the normal conditions, however, there were no obvious differences in seed germination (Figure 5 a) and root growth of seedlings (Figure 5 d,e) between ZmABA2 ‐OE and WT. In a previous study, it was also shown that AtABA2 overexpression lines did not exhibit significant differences in seed germination after cold pretreatment and primary root length under normal conditions, compared with those in WT (Lin et al ., 2007 ). Moreover, the overexpression lines exhibited no apparent phenotypic differences in aerial structures from the WT. These results seem to be contradictory to the traditional idea that ABA usually inhibits growth when applied to well‐watered plants. An explanation from Lin et al . ( 2007 ) for the contradiction is that the basal levels of ABA are essential for maintaining primary root elongation under normal growth conditions. Besides, it is also possible that the increases in the levels of ABA in these transgenic lines [a 44.7% increase from Lin et al . ( 2007 ) and a twofold increase in this study] may be not enough for the inhibition of seed germination and root growth. However, due to a relatively higher level of ABA in ZmABA2 ‐OE plants, after 6 weeks of growth, ZmABA2 ‐OE plants had a smaller size than the WT (Figure 6 d).

The ABA biosynthetic pathway in higher plants has been largely established. In Arabidopsis, genes encoding the enzymes for most steps of the ABA biosynthesis pathway, including AtABA1 , AtABA4 , AtNCED3 , AtABA3 , AtABA2 and AtAAO3 , have been identified using ABA‐deficient mutants (Nambara and Marion‐Poll, 2005 ; Xiong and Zhu, 2003 ; Xu et al ., 2013 ). ABA biosynthesis is regulated both by internal developmental cues as well as by environmental stresses. Previous studies indicated that most of the genes involved in the biosynthesis of ABA, such as AtABA1 , AtNCED3 , AtABA3 and AtAAO3 , are up‐regulated by dehydration, salt stress and ABA treatment (Barrero et al ., 2006 ; Lin et al ., 2007 ; Nambara and Marion‐Poll, 2005 ; Xiong and Zhu, 2003 ; Xu et al ., 2013 ). In contrast, AtABA2 is expressed constitutively at a relatively low level and is not induced by short‐term drought or ABA treatment (Cheng et al ., 2002 ; Gonzales‐Guzman et al ., 2002 ). In rice, the levels of OsABA2 mRNA did not significantly change in response to dehydration and ABA treatment (Endo et al ., 2014 ). However, prolonged drought and salt treatments led to a relatively stronger induction of AtABA2 promoter activity and detectable induction of the AtABA2 transcript (Lin et al ., 2007 ). A recent study also showed the expression of AtABA2 was significantly up‐regulated by the treatments of ABA and bamboo mosaic virus (Alazem et al ., 2014 ). Moreover, the expression of pepper CaMNR1 , an ortholog of AtABA2 , was strongly induced by drought, ABA, SA and ethylene treatment, in which the treatments of drought, ABA and ethylene induced a significant increase in the expression of CaMNR1 at 1 h after treatments (Choi et al ., 2008 ). These results suggest that the orthologs of AtABA2 in different plants may have different expression profiles in response to drought, salt stress and ABA treatment. In this study, the expression of ZmABA2 in the leaves of maize plants was rapidly induced by PEG, NaCl, cold, wound, ABA, H 2 O 2 , ETH and BR (Figure 3 ). A significant increase in the expression of ZmABA2 was observed within 15–30 min after these treatments except for cold, which induced the expression of ZmABA2 after 2‐h treatment. These results suggest that like other ABA biosynthetic genes, ZmABA2 is also regulated by abiotic stresses. In addition, this study also showed that ZmABA2 is a phosphorylation substrate of ZmMPK5, suggesting that a post‐translational regulation is involved in controlling ZmABA2 activity.

Experimental procedures

Plant materials and treatments

Seeds of maize ( Zea mays L. cv. Nongda 108) were sown under the conditions as described previously (Ding et al ., 2013 ). For protoplast isolation, maize plants were grown at 25 °C under dark conditions. When the second leaves were fully expanded, they were collected and used for investigations.

For plant hormone and H 2 O 2 treatments, maize seedlings were placed in the beakers wrapped with aluminium foil containing 100 μ m ABA, 10 n μ BR, 1 m m ETH and 10 m m H 2 O 2 solutions, respectively. For drought and salt stress, maize seedlings were treated with 10% (w/v) PEG 6000 (simulation of drought stress) and 250 m m NaCl. For cold treatment, the plants were placed in a growth cabinet at 4 °C. For wound treatment, the leaves were cut with sterile scissors. To study the effects of inhibitors, the plants were pretreated with 100 μ m PD98059 or 10 μ m U0126 for 4 h and then exposed to PEG and NaCl treatments. After treatments of maize plants, the second leaves were sampled and immediately frozen under liquid N 2 for further analysis.

Y2H screening and assay

Y2H analyses were performed using the ProQuest™ Two‐Hybrid System with Gateway Technology according to the manufacturer's instructions (Invitrogen: California). Full‐length ZmMPK5 fused to the GAL4‐DBD was expressed from pDEST32‐ZmMPK5 in Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain MaV203 (Invitrogen: California). The prey cDNA library from maize seedlings was prepared in the prey plasmid pDEST22 by LR recombination in MaV203 following CloneMiner™ cDNA Library Construction Kit with Gateway Technology (Invitrogen: California) and was screened on synthetic dropout medium containing 50 m m 3‐amino‐1,2,4‐triazole (Sigma: Shanghai, China). The pDEST22 plasmids encoding putative ZmMPK5‐interacting proteins were isolated and retested for interaction. Inserts of confirmed interactors were sequenced to ensure in‐frame coding sequence with the GAL4‐AD.

For validation of the results obtained from the above Y2H screening, ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 coding regions were amplified by PCR and cloned into pDEST32 (pDEST32‐ZmMPK5) as bait and pDEST22 (pDEST22‐ZmABA2) as prey, using the ProQuest ™ Two‐Hybrid System with Gateway Technology (Invitrogen: California). Yeast transformation and downstream analysis were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Double transformants were selected on media lacking leucine and tryptophan; bait‐only or prey‐only controls were transformed with empty pDEST32 or pDEST22. Interactions were assessed by growth in the absence of histidine in the presence of 50 m m 3‐aminotriazole and by β‐galactosidase assays.

Pull‐down assay

The full‐length ZmABA2 was PCR amplified and cloned into pGEX‐4T‐1 vector (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech: HongKong, China) using the BamHI and EcoRI sites for GST fusion. Expression of GST‐ZmABA2 recombinant proteins in Escherichia coli (BL21) was induced by adding isopropyl‐1‐thio‐b‐D‐galactopyranoside to a final concentration of 0.5 m m at 28 °C for 6 h. GST‐ZmABA2 recombinant proteins were purified on glutathione‐Sepharose 4B (Amersham Biosciences: HongKong, China) according to the suppliers' instructions. Protein expression was checked by loading protein onto 12% SDS–polyacrylamide gels and staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R250. Meanwhile, maize protoplasts were transfected with Myc‐tagged ZmMPK5 construct. After incubation for 16 h, harvested protoplasts were lysed on ice for 30 min by adding lysis buffer as described previously (Zhang et al ., 2006 ), and the supernatants were collected by centrifugation. Pull‐down assays were carried out according to a standard protocol as described by Singh et al . ( 2012 ).

Co‐IP assay

Maize protoplasts were transiently transfected or cotransfected with ZmMPK5‐Myc and ZmABA2‐Flag constructs, and after incubation for 16 h, protoplasts were harvested and homogenized in Co‐IP buffer as described previously (Zhang et al ., 2006 ). After incubation on ice for 30 min, the protoplasts were lysed and centrifuged at 12 000 g at 4 °C. The supernatants were precleared with protein A agarose before incubation with anti‐Myc antibody overnight at 4 °C. The antibody–protein immunocomplexes were collected by adding protein A agarose beads and were washed with immunoprecipitation buffer. The pellet (immunocomplexes with beads) was resuspended in 4×SDS‐PAGE loading buffer. Eluted proteins were analysed by immunoblotting using anti‐Flag antibody.

BiFC assay

To test in vivo interactions, the coding regions of ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 were amplified by PCR with primers that contained appropriate restriction sites, and the amplified fragments were inserted into the plasmids pSPYNE and pSPYCE to form pSPYNE‐ZmMPK5 and pSPYCE‐ZmABA2 constructs, respectively. Epidermal cells from onion were transfected with the following combinations of plasmids: pSPYNE‐ZmMPK5 and pSPYCE‐ZmABA2, pSPYNE‐ZmMPK5 and pSPYCE, and pSPYNE and pSPYCE‐ZmABA2. Onion tissues were prepared, and DNA preparation and biolistic bombardment (Biolistic‐PDS‐1000/He Particle Delivery System; Bio‐Rad: California) were performed as described previously (Lee et al ., 2008 ). YFP fluorescence in transformed onion tissues was detected at 12–20 h postbombardment, using a confocal microscope (TCS‐SP2; Leica, Bensheim, Germany). All figures show representative images from three independent experiments.

Site‐directed mutagenesis

To mutate ZmABA2, the Multi‐Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies: California) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions. The DNA oligonucleotides used in mutagenesis were synthesized, and their sequences are listed in Table S1. After mutagenesis, all of the mutated plasmids were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Assay of ABA2 activity

Enzyme assays contained 100 m m K 2 HPO 4 , pH 7.2, 100 μ m NAD, 100 μ m xanthoxin (Xan) and 10 μL of purified ZmABA2 protein or ZmABA2S173A protein (10 ng/μL). Xan was prepared from (R)‐4‐hydroxy‐β‐cyclogerinol using a known procedure (Kuba et al ., 2008 ). Assays were incubated for the 30 min at 25 °C in a total volume of 1 mL and stopped by adding 10 μL of 0.1 m m HCl. The rate of reduction of NAD to NADH was followed by scoring the increase in A340.

Vector construction

Coding regions of ZmMPK5 and ZmABA2 , flanked with C‐terminal Myc and Flag epitope sequences, respectively, were amplified by PCR and cloned into pXZP008 vector, resulting the constructs of ZmMPK5‐Myc and ZmABA2‐Flag. The PCR primer sequences for adding the C‐terminal Myc or Flag epitope tagging were as shown in Table S1. The resulting constructs ZmMPK5‐Myc and ZmABA2‐Flag were then transfected into maize protoplasts for further analysis.

In vitro synthesis of double‐stranded (ds) RNAs

dsRNAs were produced by in vitro transcription of a PCR generated DNA template using the primer pairs shown in Table S1 containing the T7 promoter sequence on both the 5′ and 3′ ends. The PCR products were purified and used for in vitro synthesis of dsRNAs of ZmABA2 and ZmMPK5 using the RiboMAX™ Large Scale RNA Production System‐T7 (Promega: Madison) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The purity and concentration of synthesized dsRNAs were checked by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometer.

Transfection with constructs or dsRNAs

Protoplast isolation and transfection with constructs or dsRNAs were performed according to the method described by Cao et al . ( 2014 ). For transfection, 1 mL of maize protoplasts (usually 5–8 × 10 5 cells/mL) were transfected with 150 μg of ZmMPK5‐Myc, ZmABA2‐Flag fusion constructs (pXZP008 vector as control) or dsRNAs (dsMPK5, dsABA2) (H 2 O as control) using a PEG‐calcium‐mediated method.

Expression and purification of His‐ZmABA2

Full‐length ZmABA2 was cloned into BamHI / HindIII ‐digested pET‐28a (+) vector (Novagen: Billerica, MA) to generate a His tagged ZmABA2 construct. Fusion proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli strain BL21 (DE3) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Protein expression was induced by adding isopropyl 1‐thio‐β‐D‐galactopyranoside to a final concentration of 0.1 m m at 25 °C for 6 h. The bacteria were collected by centrifugation at 5000 g for 15 min, resuspended in lysis–equilibration buffer (50 m m NaH 2 PO 4 , 300 m m NaCl, pH 8.0), sonicated and centrifuged at 12 000 g at 4 °C for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was used for protein purification with High Affinity Ni‐NTA Resin (Genscript: Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Immunoprecipitation kinase activity assay

Protein was extracted from maize leaves or protoplasts as described previously (Zhang et al ., 2006 ). Protein content was determined according to the method of Bradford ( 1976 ) with bovine serum albumin as standard. For immunoprecipitation kinase assay, protein extracts from maize leaves (200 μg) or from protoplasts (50 μg) were incubated with anti‐ZmMPK5 antibody (5 μg) in an immunoprecipitation buffer as described previously (Zhang et al ., 2006 ). Kinase activity in the immunocomplex was determined by an in‐gel kinase assay using myelin basic protein or purified His‐ZmABA2 fusion protein as substrates as described previously (Zhang et al ., 2006 ).

Isolation of total RNA and real‐time quantitative RT‐PCR expression analysis

Total RNA was isolated from maize leaves or protoplasts using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa Bio Inc.: Qingdao, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNase treatment was included in the isolation step using RNase‐free DNase (TaKaRa Bio Inc.: Qingdao, China). Approximately 2 μg of total RNA was reverse‐transcribed using oligo d(T)16 primer and M‐MLV reverse transcriptase (TaKaRa Bio Inc.: Qingdao, China).

Real‐time quantitative reverse transcription PCR was performed on a 7500 real‐time PCR system (Aplied Biosystems Inc.) using SYBR Premix Ex Taq ™ (TaKaRa Bio Inc.: Qingdao, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was amplified by PCR using the primers shown in Table S1. To standardize the results, the amplification of β ‐actin was determined and used as the internal standard. The data were normalized to the amplification of a maize β ‐actin gene.

Determination of ABA content

Leaves (0.5 g) or protoplasts (about 10 7 cells) were collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen and then finely ground, followed by extraction with 3 mL of methanol containing 19% (v/v) water and 1% (v/v) acetic acid at 4 °C for 12 h. After centrifugation (8000g, 4 °C, 10 min), the supernatant was collected and passed through a C‐18 (500 mg) SPE cartridge, which was preconditioned with 6 mL of water, 6 mL of methanol and 8 mL of methanol containing 19% (v/v) water and 1% (v/v) acetic acid. The solution was then extracted by 3 mL of ethyl acetate three times. A total of 9 mL of the extract was dried under nitrogen gas and reconstituted in 0.2 mL of mobile phase (methanol:1% acetic acid, 45 : 55). Samples were injected on a reverse‐phase C18 column with the flow rate of 0.8 mL/min after filtration through a 0.45‐mm membrane filter. Elution from the column was monitored with an absorbance detector at a wavelength of 254 nm. Quantification was obtained by comparing the peak areas with those of known amounts of ABA (Sigma: Shanghai, China).

Generation of ZmABA2 transgenic tobacco

To obtain the transgenic plants with overexpression of ZmABA2 , the full‐length ORF of ZmABA2 was inserted into the plant binary vector GV3101 to construct GV3101‐ ZFP36 . Then the ZmABA2 gene under the control of the Cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter was transformed into tobacco ( Nicotiana tabacum ) by the Agrobacterium ‐mediated transformation method (Zhang et al ., 2007 ).

Phenotype analysis

For seed germination assay, the seeds of WT and homozygous T2 transgenic tobacco were placed on solid Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with or without 20% PEG and 100 m m NaCl. The seeds were incubated at 4 °C for 2 days and then transferred to a growth chamber (16 h light/8 h dark; 200 μmol/m 2 /s light intensity; 25 °C) to germinate. After 5 days of incubation as described above, the germination rate of seeds that had germinated and developed fully green expanded cotyledons was determined. For the analysis of root growth, 5‐day‐old seedlings of transgenic and WT plants grown in the normal conditions were treated with 30% PEG and 200 m m NaCl for 12 days, and the length of primary root was measured. For the tolerance analysis to PEG and NaCl, 4‐week‐old seedlings of transgenic and WT plants were treated with 20% PEG and 200 m m NaCl, and the contents of ABA and MDA and the percentage of electrolyte leakage were determined. For the soil experiments, 4‐week‐old seedlings of transgenic and WT plants grown on soil pots were treated by withholding water for 14 days or by supplying with 400 m m NaCl for 14 days. The phenotype of seedlings was photographed, and the shoot fresh weight and the RWC were measured.

The content of MDA was determined as described by Hodges et al . ( 1999 ). The percentage leakage of electrolyte was determined as described by Jiang and Zhang ( 2001 ). RWC was determined as described by Jiang and Zhang ( 2002 ).
