Oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) is a key metabolic process that couples redox
energy to ATP production. While some core OXPHOS complex subunits are found
across all domains of life, many have diverged or expanded across evolution-as
seen in the protozoan pathogen Acanthamoeba castellanii. By integrating
cryo-electron microscopy of unenriched mitochondrial lysate with mass
spectrometry proteomics, we resolved the structures of endogenous mitochondrial
ATP synthase (complex V), Hsp60, and respiratory complex III from Acanthamoeba.
We capture Acanthamoeba ATP synthase in an IF1-inhibited state and reveal how
Acanthamoeba-specific subunits and extensions stabilize the molecular machine,
which includes a β subunit extension that interfaces with the peripheral stalk.
Additionally, we characterize an active malate dehydrogenase (MDH) dimer
structurally integrated within the ATP synthase peripheral stalk, thus revealing
a direct protein tether between OXPHOS and the tricarboxylic acid cycle.
Together, these findings provide structural insight into lineage-specific
adaptations in Acanthamoeba that may tune protozoan metabolism.

INTRODUCTION

The mitochondrion is the site of several critical bioenergetic functions, including ATP synthesis by oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA)1,2. OXPHOS complexes I–IV in the electron transport chain (ETC) couple redox reactions to generate an electrochemical gradient across the inner membrane. Translocation of protons from the intermembrane space (IMS) back to the mitochondrial matrix through ATP synthase (also known as complex V), catalyzes the formation of ATP from ADP3. OXPHOS and the TCA cycle are directly coupled—NADH is a product of the TCA cycle and substrate of complex I and complex II (succinate dehydrogenase, SDH) is a shared component of both processes4. Yet, the full extent to which the TCA cycle and OXPHOS are intertwined across eukaryotes has not been fully investigated5.

ATP synthase is essential for aerobic life across all domains of biology, with homologs found in bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotic organelles such as mitochondria and chloroplasts3,6–8. ATP synthase consists of two functional regions: the ATP synthesizing F1 catalytic head9–11, containing three alternating α and β subunits, and a membrane-embedded Fo domain that translocates protons across the membrane7,11–14. The F1 and Fo regions are connected by central and peripheral stalks, coupling rotary motion driven by proton transport to ATP synthesis.11 Mitochondrial ATP synthases assemble into oligomers with the dimeric form seen as the minimal functional unit3.

While the core subunits α, β, γ, ε, δ, c, ATP6, b, d, and OSCP are highly conserved, ATP synthases have diversified across organisms through the acquisition of lineage-specific subunits that tune regulation, stability, and oligomerization15–18. Many of these additional proteins contribute to the formation of dimers and higher-ordered oligomers of mitochondrial ATP synthases19–21. Due to the limited structural diversity captured to date, the functional and evolutionary significance of lineage-specific ATP synthase subunits remains poorly understood. While structures have been resolved from mammalian, fungal, and a few protozoan systems, many branches of the eukaryotic tree of life are unexplored15,22–29.

One structurally unexplored branch is the Amoebozoa eukaryotic supergroup, an evolutionary “cousin” clade to the Opisthokonta supergroup, which includes animals and fungi (Figure 1A). Both amoebozoans and opisthokonts are also members of the taxonomic supergroup Amorphea (Figure 1A)30–32,32–34. The extent to which ATP synthase composition, assembly, and mechanism is conserved within Amorphea is underexplored. Acanthamoeba castellanii is a free-living pathogenic amoebozoan that causes amoebic keratitis and fatal brain infections in humans35. Its mitochondria have been previously reported to exhibit ETC features not seen in humans, such as an alternative oxidase (AOX)36–41. Through our MitoCarta Tree of Life (MitoTOL) project, we redefined the entire proteome (via new genome reannotation in Stefely et al, co-submitted42), generated a high-quality mitochondrial proteome of Acanthamoeba castellanii (a resource called the AcMitoCarta), and cataloged conserved and lineage-specific subunits for OXPHOS complexes I–V using multiple mass-spectrometry approaches (Stefely et al co-submitted43). Previous work has also identified OXPHOS subunits using BN-PAGE-MS44, yet, the molecular architecture of these OXPHOS machines remains unknown. In particular, it is unknown how conserved and lineage-specific ATP synthase subunits are assembled in this lineage and whether amoebozoans possess distinct structural adaptations.

One notable observation from BN-PAGE-MS that has been previously reported44 and again seen43, is a malate dehydrogenase (MDH) migrating with other ATP synthase subunits. This is surprising because MDH is an integral component of the TCA cycle whereas ATP synthase is part of a separate bioenergetic process, OXPHOS45,46. Whether the MDH is integrated into ATP synthase or simply co-migrates as a part of another structure is unknown. To address this, we sought to solve the structure of ATP synthase from Acanthamoeba. Structures of mammalian, fungal, and protist (including Trypanosoma, Toxoplasma, and Polytomella) ATP synthases have revealed not only how lineage-specific subunits and features are assembled into the full complexes, but also provide mechanistic insight into their contribution to stabilization and function15,22–29.

Here we present cryo-EM structures of multiple endogenous A. castellanii mitochondrial protein complexes from mitochondrial lysate. We report the structures of ATP synthase, mitochondrial Hsp60 and respiratory complex III. The structure of ATP synthase reveals lineage-specific biology, extensions, and organization of subunits including an MDH integrated into the peripheral stalk. This finding reveals a structural link between OXPHOS and the TCA cycle in A. castellanii mitochondria. We find that integration of MDH into ATP synthase via an Acanthamoeba-specific scaffolding protein increases the working substrate concentration range of this TCA enzyme. This report of a metabolic TCA enzyme physically integrated into an ATP synthase expands our view of the functional and structural integration of OXPHOS and the TCA cycle in eukaryotes. Together, with comparative analyses of other mitochondrial ATP synthases, this work provides new insight into the evolution of ATP synthase assembly and oligomerization.

RESULTSDetermining cryo-EM structures of complexes from mitochondrial lysateTo determine the structure of mitochondrial complexes from Acanthamoeba castellanii, we imaged native mitochondrial lysate using an enrichment- and purification-free approach to circumvent the limited genetic tools and scalability of the pathogen35. Mitochondria were isolated from Acanthamoeba trophozoites using differential centrifugation and gently lysed in a 0.5% n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM) buffer. Solubilized mitochondrial membranes were then vitrified on cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) grids (Figure 1B). This approach did not involve purification of or enrichment of a specific target47–51, but rather direct imaging of a solubilized membrane mixture. Similar purification free methods have recently been used for structure determination of ribosomes52, which are highly abundant. We collected 68,290 movies and manually picked 53,882 particles for single particle analysis (SPA) cryo-EM (Figure S1A). From these particles, we reconstructed maps of the ATP synthase dimer (11,338 particles), mitochondrial Hsp60 (1,154 particles), and OXPHOS complex III (5,208 particles) (Figure 1C and S1B–C).Initial ab initio reconstruction of all manually picked particles resulted in two classes; one consistent with the size and shape of an ATP synthase dimer (14,917 particles) and another with Hsp60 (7,597 particles) (Figure S1B)24,28,53–55. The set of particles consistent with ATP synthase were filtered down to 11,338 particles through three-dimensional (3D) classification resulting in an initial 7–10 Å 3D refined map. Density for one monomer was weaker, likely reflecting flexibility between monomers or damage at the air-water interface (Figure 1C)53. Optimization of the image processing workflow—including masking and recentering on individual ATP synthase monomers or the dimer interface within each ATP synthase dimer and performing local refinements—improved maps of the overall ATP synthase monomer (4.2 Å), F1 region (3.8 Å), OSCP region (4.3 Å), and transmembrane dimer interface (4.1 Å) (Figure 1D,E, S1B, S2A–D,G–I, and Table 1). A composite map of the ATP synthase monomer was generated by combining the local maps of the F1 and OSCP regions, with that of the ATP synthase monomer (Figure 1D). Together with the locally refined dimer interface, these maps reveal the first structure of an ATP synthase from the Amoebozoa eukaryotic superfamily (Figure 1C–E).For the Hsp60 class, the set of particles was filtered by several rounds of two-dimensional (2D) and 3D classification resulting in a 3.9 Å map of Hsp60 (final set of 1,154 particles) (Figure S1B, and S2E,J and Table 1). Additionally, careful re-examination of all particles in the dataset revealed another class that resembled OXPHOS complex III (Figure S1B, blue boxed class)56. Subsequent template-based picking approaches followed by several rounds of 2D and 3D classification, and 3D refinement resulted in a 3.9 Å resolution map of a complex III dimer (CIII2) from 5,208 particles (Figure S1C, S2F,K and Table 1).Integrated approaches to build atomic modelsTo overcome the limited particle number due to the enrichment- and purification-free nature of our structure determination approach, we utilized sequence agnostic model building by ModelAngelo57 and used HMMER58 to search the generated Hidden Markov model (HMM) profiles against the Acanthamoeba proteome42 to verify the identity of the maps we obtained (Figure S4). ModelAngelo built partial peptides into regions of the ATP synthase maps with sufficient resolution (F1 domain, OSCP region, and Fo dimer region) (Figure 1D,E and S4A–C). All hits were mitochondrial proteins and resulted in the assignment of ATP synthase subunits α, β, γ, OSCP, d, f, 6, b, 8, e, g, and i/j as well as the Acanthamoeba-specific ATP synthase subunits – ACANB14707 and mitochondrial MDH (AcMito-MDH) (Figure 1D,E,F and S4A–C)43,59,60. The remainder of the subunits were manually built into the map using COOT informed by previously solved structures of homologs or AlphaFold3 predictions58,59. While the majority of a dimeric AlphaFold3 model of AcMito-MDH agreed with the density of the map, the N-terminal region differed, as indicated by the ModelAngelo build and was manually rebuilt using COOT (Figure S4A–C)57,62.After subunits identified and placed by ModelAngelo were manually built to completion, there remained unaccounted regions of the map. Previous BN-PAGE complexome data identified additional components, subunits c, δ and ε, IF1, and ACANB04460 (Figure 1F, S3A,B), that co-migrated with ATP synthase subunits43,44, which were verified by IP-MS analyses (reported in Stefely et al., co-submitted)43. While AlphaFold3 failed to predict the entire structure of ATP synthase, predictions of the rotor (c-ring and subunits δ and ε, and part of the γ subunit) and ACANB04460 with other components of the peripheral stalk resulted in models that agreed with the unassigned density in the cryo-EM map and were manually placed and fitted (Figure S4F). Within the F1 domain we observed a helical density between one α/β pair. Based on previously solved structures of eukaryotic ATP synthases and consistent detection by multiple IP-MS-method experiments43, we assigned this density to the inhibitory factor, IF1 (Figure S3B)22,28,63. The rest of the map where helices were visible, but unassigned, was manually built using COOT (Figure 1D, gray).Model building for the other two structures obtained from the dataset followed more standard workflows. For the mitochondrial Hsp60 map, the mitochondrial Hsp60 (ACANB13112) was the top hit for all peptides built. The rest of the map was slightly disordered, but a few helices in the apical domain could be built using an AlphaFold361 model of the monomer as a guide in COOT62 (Figure S1B). This structure closely reflects the structure of the human mitochondrial Hsp6054,64. In the CIII2 electron density map, ModelAngelo57 and HMMER58 identified, placed, and built eight CIII subunits de novo—UQCRC2, UQCR11, UQCRB, UQCRQ, MPP-β, CYC1 and MT-CYB (Figure S4E and S5A)57. The remaining unassigned density agreed with the placement of UQCR10 and UCRH from an AlphaFold361 prediction of the subunits identified from complexome analysis43,44. The Rieske subunit was the only subunit identified that could not be built into the cryo-EM map (Figure S5A). While previous studies have identified the composition of complex III in Acanthamoeba44, this is the first structure confirming a unique dimerization arrangement, a heterotetramer of UQCRC2 and MPP-β (Figure S5A,B)56,65,66. Phylogenetic analysis across the tree of life (Chen et al, co-submitted67) predicts that an ancestral duplication gave rise to MPP-α and UQCRC2 paralogs in amorpheans and that the MPP-β and UQCRC1 paralogs in opisthokonts may have arisen independently in animals and fungi (Figure S5C). This evolutionary history is consistent with the distinctive subunit composition observed in the AcCIII2 structure. Taken together, these observations on Acanthamoeba Hsp60 and CIII demonstrate the discovery potential of imaging wild-type mitochondrial lysate.Overall structure of the amoebozoan ATP synthase dimerDue to their prominence in the micrographs, dimers of ATP synthase dominated our particle picks (Figure S1A). The overall AcATP synthase structure reveals a pyramid shaped head or F1 region, made of three α and three β subunits surrounding two long helices from the γ subunit. The peripheral stalks of each monomer are offset and the dimer angle between the two monomers is 70° (Figure S6), indicating that the AcATP synthase dimer is a Type IV dimer3. We observe a distinct “hat” region with large density sitting above the OSCP subunit that is distinct from previous ATP synthase structures.Structure of the catalytic F1 domainAtomic models built by focused local refinement of a single monomer within the dimer reveal a monomeric architecture that resembles ATP synthase structures from other organisms including mammals, fungi, Trypanosoma, Toxoplasma, and other protists22,24,28. All core subunits were built as well as three Acanthamoeba-specific subunits in the peripheral stalk—ACANB04460, ACANB14707, and AcMito-MDH. ACANB04460 makes contact with subunits d, b, and 8, suggesting this additional subunit contributes to the stabilization of the peripheral stalk. ACANB14707 and AcMito-MDH sit above OSCP, creating the “hat” noted earlier no obvious interaction with the F1 catalytic domain or the Fo rotor.As in other ATP synthases, the F1 domain contains three copies each of the α and β subunits, labeled as αE/βE, αDP/βDP, and αTP/βTP following established nomenclature22,24,29,63. The nucleotide occupancy in the F1 domain is consistent with an inhibited state. In the cryo-EM map, all nucleotide binding sites are visible (Figure 2A,B). Each of the non-catalytic α subunits nucleotide binding pockets in the are occupied by an ATP molecule while the occupancy of the three catalytic β subunits varies (Figure 2A,B). The nucleotide binding pocket in βE is empty and adopts an open conformation whereas βDP and βTP are both occupied by an ADP molecule and adopt a closed conformation (RMSD value of 1.5 Å, Figure 2C). Density consistent with a Mg2+ ion is also observed in the βTP binding site, but typically this pocket is occupied by an ATP molecule. Despite the observation of an ADP molecule in both βTP and βDP, there are subtle differences between the two active sites—including a repositioning of two arginines, one in the β subunit (Arg375) and one in the neighboring α subunit (Arg204) (Figure 2D). αDP-Arg204 (gray) reaches into the binding site, coordinating with the γ-phosphate in the ADP molecule in the βDP active site. βTP-Arg375 (gray) comes down closer to the βTP active site, occupying the position αDP-Arg204 (blue) occupies in the βDP active site (Figure 2D). These differences may underlie phosphate binding and ATP synthesis. The movement of the two arginines may accommodate the binding of a phosphate ion in the βTP active site.A similar occupancy of the F1 domain was observed in four structures of human ATP synthase where it was postulated that these structures reflected an inhibited state or perhaps even represents the reverse activity of the synthase (hydrolysis of ATP to ADP)63. In our case, the nucleotide occupancy likely reflects the inhibited state caused by the binding of the inhibitory factor, IF1 (Figure 2E).In other organisms, IF1 binds to the F1 domain via the insertion of a C-terminal helix in the βDP and αDP interface, but the mode of N-terminal binding varies22,28. In mammals, the N-terminus forms a short helix that extends into the F1 domain interacting with the coiled-coil of the γ subunit, preventing its rotation22,68. In contrast, in the algae Euglena gracilis, IF1 lacks an N-terminal helix entirely and an N-terminal loop extends in the opposite direction, no longer contacting the γ subunit28. The binding mode of AcIF1 more closely reflects that of mammalian IF1. While only the C-terminal helix could be confidently built into the map, an overlay with the mammalian IF1 reveals there is density in our cryo-EM map consistent with the N-terminal helix from SsIF1 (magenta), suggesting that AcIF1 (orange) binds to the F1 domain in a similar manner (Figure 2E).While AcIF1 binds to the F1 domain in a manner similar to mammals, the lack of a pH sensing histidine suggests that although the inhibitory mechanism may be conserved, the mode of regulation is distinct (Figure 2F, purple box and S7)68–70. This divergence is further supported by O2 perturbation profiling by Stefely et al, co-submitted43 (Figure 2G and S3C). While IF1 overexpression has been suggested to regulate ATP synthase activity in anoxic mammalian cancer cells71, we observe the opposite in Acanthamoeba–IF1 levels decrease relative to other ATP synthase subunits under anoxic conditions (<0.1% O2). This suggests a different mode of O2-dependent regulation of IF1 expression in Acanthamoeba.The structure also sheds light on lineage-specific extensions to core subunits that were apparent from sequence, but whose functional significance remained unclear44. In Acanthamoeba, the β subunit has a long C-terminal extension (118 residues) that forms two helices that wrap around a neighboring α subunit and, in one case, contacts the peripheral stalk (Figure 3A and S6). The additional contact of one of the β subunit extensions with the peripheral stalk likely reflects a different evolutionary solution to stabilization of the F1 domain to generate ATP. This extension, while present in other amoebozoan β subunits (D. discoideum: 58 residues), the Acanthamoeba extension is by far the longest (Figure S8). In more distant organisms additional subunits sit up against the α subunits at a similar interface, but in a different manner (Figure 3B,C)17,24,28,29. In Trypanosoma brucei the lineage-specific subunit, p18, shields most of the matrix exposed surface of the α subunit, burying a total of 26 nm2.24 In comparison, Acanthamoeba, the β subunit extension shields a smaller portion, burying only 13 nm2 (Figure 3B,C). This highlights two distinct strategies for F1 stabilization – subunit accretion in T. brucei versus domain extension of an existing core subunit in Acanthamoeba and other amoebozoans – underscoring ATP synthase adaptation throughout evolution, potentially reflecting differences in matrix composition or energy demands across species.Structure of the membrane embedded Fo domain.Similarly to the F1 domain, most of the Fo subunits and the proton-driven mechanism are conserved. Protons enter through the pore formed at the interface between subunit 6 and the c-ring where each c subunit binds to a proton via a conserved glutamate (Glu53 in humans and Glu63 in Acanthamoeba) (Figure S9A)13,72,73. The cryo-EM structure reveals the Acanthamoeba c-ring stoichiometry (c9) differs from yeast (c10), mammals (c8), and Trypanosoma (c10)12–14,24,68,74. The stoichiometry of the c-ring is indicative of the efficiency of the synthase, i.e. the number of protons translocated across the membrane for every three ATP molecules generated. In Acanthamoeba, we hypothesize that ATP synthase transports three H+ for every ATP molecule, whereas in mammals the ratio is 2.7 H+/ATP3,12. Several inhibitory drugs, including bedaquiline (BDQ), block proton transport targeting the conserved glutamate in subunit c72,73. While the glutamate is conserved in Acanthamoeba, the surrounding residues fail to create the negative binding pocket necessary for BDQ binding, suggesting BDQ would be ineffective against Acanthamoeba ATP synthase (Figure 9B)72,73,75.Inspection of transmembrane proteins flanking the c-rotor reveals a different packing of the transmembrane segments (TMS) compared to previously solved structures24. Similar to mammalian subunit b, the Acanthamoeba subunit b traverses the membrane twice via an N-terminal helix (bH1) and a long helix (bH2) that helps form the peripheral stalk (Figure 4A, mint green), but the positioning of this helix differs. In Acanthamoeba ATP synthase, bH1 packs closer to bH2 compared to that in pig ATP synthase, by 12.9 Å, which allows for subunits g and e to pack closer to the c-ring than in mammals (Figure 4A,B). In Trypanosoma ATP synthase, while subunit b possesses a single transmembrane segment (TMS), an additional subunit ATPEG4 sits between the TMSs of subunit b and f (Figure 4A, yellow/green). This organization of the helices in T. brucei ATP synthase, results in a similar TMS packing to that of mammals. In Acanthamoeba, the TMS of all core subunits pack tightly together, resulting in a compact ATP synthase monomer in the transmembrane region that is also reflected in the dimerization interface.Acanthamoeba-specific dimer interfaceThe tight packing of the TMS within the monomeric Fo domain is reflected in the dimer interface. Mediated by transmembrane subunits e and g, AcATP synthase dimers form a conserved ancestral interface, which has also been observed in Trypanosoma and mammalian ATP synthases (Figure 4C)24. In Acanthamoeba, the ATP synthase dimer interface is predominantly mediated by subunit e, which forms a central ‘x’, while subunit g flanks the interface, running parallel to the membrane surface and then inserts itself between the matrix facing side of the ‘x’ (Figure 4D).Strikingly, the conserved GXXXG motif present in subunits e and g in other species that allow for close interactions between their TMSs is absent in Acanthamoeba subunit g (Figure 4D, orange)24. Instead, the contact between each subunit e is mediated by hydrophobic interactions, resulting in a tighter interaction between the two in Acanthamoeba (Figure 4D). The absence of close contacts between subunits e and g via the GXXXG motifs demonstrates that this sequence is not sufficient to predict this ancestral dimerization interface, but rather one of several binding modes for achieving oligomerization. This alternative binding mode of the e subunits may be related to the unusual origin of subunit e in Acanthamoeba, which we predict is the product of a recent horizontal gene transfer from prokaryotes that replaced the canonical e subunit found in opisthokonts (Figure S10). A similar occupancy of the F1 domain was observed in four structures of human ATP synthase where it was postulated that these structures reflected an inhibited state or perhaps even represents the reverse activity of the synthase (hydrolysis of ATP to ADP)63. In our case, the nucleotide occupancy likely reflects the inhibited state caused by the binding of the inhibitory factor, IF1 (Figure 2E).

In other organisms, IF1 binds to the F1 domain via the insertion of a C-terminal helix in the βDP and αDP interface, but the mode of N-terminal binding varies22,28. In mammals, the N-terminus forms a short helix that extends into the F1 domain interacting with the coiled-coil of the γ subunit, preventing its rotation22,68. In contrast, in the algae Euglena gracilis, IF1 lacks an N-terminal helix entirely and an N-terminal loop extends in the opposite direction, no longer contacting the γ subunit28. The binding mode of AcIF1 more closely reflects that of mammalian IF1. While only the C-terminal helix could be confidently built into the map, an overlay with the mammalian IF1 reveals there is density in our cryo-EM map consistent with the N-terminal helix from SsIF1 (magenta), suggesting that AcIF1 (orange) binds to the F1 domain in a similar manner (Figure 2E).

While AcIF1 binds to the F1 domain in a manner similar to mammals, the lack of a pH sensing histidine suggests that although the inhibitory mechanism may be conserved, the mode of regulation is distinct (Figure 2F, purple box and S7)68–70. This divergence is further supported by O2 perturbation profiling by Stefely et al, co-submitted43 (Figure 2G and S3C). While IF1 overexpression has been suggested to regulate ATP synthase activity in anoxic mammalian cancer cells71, we observe the opposite in Acanthamoeba–IF1 levels decrease relative to other ATP synthase subunits under anoxic conditions (<0.1% O2). This suggests a different mode of O2-dependent regulation of IF1 expression in Acanthamoeba.

The structure also sheds light on lineage-specific extensions to core subunits that were apparent from sequence, but whose functional significance remained unclear44. In Acanthamoeba, the β subunit has a long C-terminal extension (118 residues) that forms two helices that wrap around a neighboring α subunit and, in one case, contacts the peripheral stalk (Figure 3A and S6). The additional contact of one of the β subunit extensions with the peripheral stalk likely reflects a different evolutionary solution to stabilization of the F1 domain to generate ATP. This extension, while present in other amoebozoan β subunits (D. discoideum: 58 residues), the Acanthamoeba extension is by far the longest (Figure S8). In more distant organisms additional subunits sit up against the α subunits at a similar interface, but in a different manner (Figure 3B,C)17,24,28,29. In Trypanosoma brucei the lineage-specific subunit, p18, shields most of the matrix exposed surface of the α subunit, burying a total of 26 nm2.24 In comparison, Acanthamoeba, the β subunit extension shields a smaller portion, burying only 13 nm2 (Figure 3B,C). This highlights two distinct strategies for F1 stabilization – subunit accretion in T. brucei versus domain extension of an existing core subunit in Acanthamoeba and other amoebozoans – underscoring ATP synthase adaptation throughout evolution, potentially reflecting differences in matrix composition or energy demands across species.

DISCUSSIONIn this study, we present the first high-resolution structure of an ATP synthase from the Amoebozoa eukaryotic supergroup, an important evolutionary “cousin” outgroup to Opisthokonta (the supergroup that includes humans and yeast). By integrating cryo-EM, proteomics, and computational modeling, we characterized lineage-specific subunits and extensions to conserved subunits in A. castellanii ATP synthase. A notable Acanthamoeba-specific feature was an MDH dimer integrated into each ATP synthase monomer, revealing a physical connection between OXPHOS and the TCA cycle (Figure 6). Together these findings fill an important phylogenetic gap in understanding mitochondrial ATP synthase structure and evolution.The combination of cryo-EM, unbiased model building, and proteomics was critical for building an atomic model of ATP synthase. The overall architecture of AcATP synthase is strikingly similar to that of other eukaryotes, with a conserved F1 catalytic head, Fo proton channel, and connecting stalks11,17,22,24,26,28,29,63,68. The well-resolved AcATP synthase F1 domain revealed an inhibited state resembling the inhibited human ATP synthase. Yet despite this shared core, our analyses reveal numerous Acanthamoeba-specific features. Three Acanthamoeba-specific subunits were identified and placed into the peripheral stalk: ACANB04460, MASA subunit, and AcMito-MDH. The identification and placement of multiple novel Acanthamoeba-specific proteins in AcATP synthase directly from the electron density map emphasizes the advantages of structure determination from native sources and highlights the structural diversity of OXPHOS complexes in eukaryotes.A recent report also identified a weak link between MDH and ATP synthase in mammals that could only be detected by cross-linking MS82, highlighting the broader relevance of this link and the need for a tractable system to investigate its potential regulatory roles and biological relevance. The Acanthamoeba ATP synthase-MDH complex is an ideal system for such studies as it is a stable assembly that can be purified and manipulated through mutations to individual subunits. The cryo-EM structure reveals that the ATP synthase:MDH interaction in Acanthamoeba is facilitated by both a lineage-specific scaffolding subunit and three additional N-terminal helices in each AcMito-MDH monomer. These Acanthamoeba-specific adaptations mediate the stable assembly of two enzymes from two distinct metabolic pathways–OXPHOS and the TCA cycle (Figure 6). Here we lay the foundation for future work by purifying the complex from Acanthamoeba and demonstrating that AcMito-MDH remains active within it, and that ATP synthase substrates can differentially modulate MDH activity.Acanthamoeba mitochondria show unusual features of malate metabolism. The glyoxylate shunt, an anabolic pathway that bypasses many TCA enzymes converting acetyl-CoA to succinate for carbohydrate synthesis83, is mitochondrially localized in Acanthamoeba, rather than in specialized peroxisomes (fungal and plants) or the cytosol (bacterial)43,59. Interestingly, its two core enzymes, isocitrate lyase and malate synthase are fused as a single polypeptide (MS-ICL). This raises the possibility that in the amoebozoan, the regulatory effects we observe in MDH through integration into ATP synthase impact the glyoxylate shunt as well or instead of the TCA cycle. Future work investigating the MDH:ATP synthase interaction under different carbon sources will help clarify this. Nevertheless, the co-occurrence of an MDH integrated into an ATP synthase in Acanthamoeba alongside a mitochondrial glyoxylate shunt (Figure 6)43,59 underscores the primacy of malate metabolism in Acanthamoeba mitochondria.In addition to lineage-specific subunits, we also visualize key features of core subunits. In this study, we present the first high-resolution structure of an ATP synthase from the Amoebozoa eukaryotic supergroup, an important evolutionary “cousin” outgroup to Opisthokonta (the supergroup that includes humans and yeast). By integrating cryo-EM, proteomics, and computational modeling, we characterized lineage-specific subunits and extensions to conserved subunits in A. castellanii ATP synthase. A notable Acanthamoeba-specific feature was an MDH dimer integrated into each ATP synthase monomer, revealing a physical connection between OXPHOS and the TCA cycle (Figure 6). Together these findings fill an important phylogenetic gap in understanding mitochondrial ATP synthase structure and evolution.

The combination of cryo-EM, unbiased model building, and proteomics was critical for building an atomic model of ATP synthase. The overall architecture of AcATP synthase is strikingly similar to that of other eukaryotes, with a conserved F1 catalytic head, Fo proton channel, and connecting stalks11,17,22,24,26,28,29,63,68. The well-resolved AcATP synthase F1 domain revealed an inhibited state resembling the inhibited human ATP synthase. Yet despite this shared core, our analyses reveal numerous Acanthamoeba-specific features. Three Acanthamoeba-specific subunits were identified and placed into the peripheral stalk: ACANB04460, MASA subunit, and AcMito-MDH. The identification and placement of multiple novel Acanthamoeba-specific proteins in AcATP synthase directly from the electron density map emphasizes the advantages of structure determination from native sources and highlights the structural diversity of OXPHOS complexes in eukaryotes.

A recent report also identified a weak link between MDH and ATP synthase in mammals that could only be detected by cross-linking MS82, highlighting the broader relevance of this link and the need for a tractable system to investigate its potential regulatory roles and biological relevance. The Acanthamoeba ATP synthase-MDH complex is an ideal system for such studies as it is a stable assembly that can be purified and manipulated through mutations to individual subunits. The cryo-EM structure reveals that the ATP synthase:MDH interaction in Acanthamoeba is facilitated by both a lineage-specific scaffolding subunit and three additional N-terminal helices in each AcMito-MDH monomer. These Acanthamoeba-specific adaptations mediate the stable assembly of two enzymes from two distinct metabolic pathways–OXPHOS and the TCA cycle (Figure 6). Here we lay the foundation for future work by purifying the complex from Acanthamoeba and demonstrating that AcMito-MDH remains active within it, and that ATP synthase substrates can differentially modulate MDH activity.

Acanthamoeba mitochondria show unusual features of malate metabolism. The glyoxylate shunt, an anabolic pathway that bypasses many TCA enzymes converting acetyl-CoA to succinate for carbohydrate synthesis83, is mitochondrially localized in Acanthamoeba, rather than in specialized peroxisomes (fungal and plants) or the cytosol (bacterial)43,59. Interestingly, its two core enzymes, isocitrate lyase and malate synthase are fused as a single polypeptide (MS-ICL). This raises the possibility that in the amoebozoan, the regulatory effects we observe in MDH through integration into ATP synthase impact the glyoxylate shunt as well or instead of the TCA cycle. Future work investigating the MDH:ATP synthase interaction under different carbon sources will help clarify this. Nevertheless, the co-occurrence of an MDH integrated into an ATP synthase in Acanthamoeba alongside a mitochondrial glyoxylate shunt (Figure 6)43,59 underscores the primacy of malate metabolism in Acanthamoeba mitochondria.

In addition to lineage-specific subunits, we also visualize key features of core subunits. In amoebozoans there is a C-terminal extension on the β subunit that folds into two helices and wraps around a neighboring α subunit. In some other Type IV ATP synthases this outcome is achieved through subunit accretion instead of a modification to a core subunit24,84. The C-terminal extensions of one of the β subunits interfaces with the peripheral stalk resulting in a distinct mode of F1 stabilization not observed in other organisms like mammals and Polytomella—where the N-terminus of an α subunit integrates into the peripheral stalk11,17,22,29,63,68. Together, these features highlight how extension of a core subunit can serve as an alternative evolutionary solution to shielding the α subunit and stabilizing the F1 region.

The Acanthamoeba ATP synthase dimer retains an ancestral dimerization interface while exhibiting a different mode of dimerization. Rather than the possessing GXXXG motifs in the e and g subunits that facilitate TMS packing in other organisms, a hydrophobic interface on the lumen facing side of the membrane drives dimerization in Acanthamoeba. This interface results in a tighter packing between two ATP synthase monomers and demonstrates how alternative structural solutions can achieve a similar dimerization interfaces22,24. The observation of the ancestral dimerization interface in AcATP synthase, together with phylogenetic evidence that amoebozoans are a closer neighbor to opisthokonts than more diverged protists (Trypanosoma, Polytomella, Euglena)32,34,85–87, support the notion that the canonical dimer observed in mammals and fungi is Opisthokonta-specific.

Our integrative approach demonstrates the power of combining cryo-EM on native mixtures, proteomics, and computational tools to characterize architectural specialization in protein complexes from a diverged protozoan pathogen. Similar strategies may be used to expand the structural understanding across the eukaryotic tree of life, ultimately structurally linking molecular evolution to eukaryotic diversity.

While it is a technical feat to solve the structure from a small number of starting particles (~15,000), the limited particle count from our dataset constrained us to only capturing the inhibited IF1 bound state. As no other structures of Acanthamoeba ATP synthase exist and prediction algorithms fail to predict the entire complex, we have no active states available for comparison.
