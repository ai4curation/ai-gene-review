mRNA nuclear export is an essential biological process that is mediated
primarily by nuclear export factor 1 (NXF1). NXF1 exerts its mRNA transport
activity by binding to adaptor proteins. In an effort to identify novel NXF1
adaptors involved in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) development, we discovered
that C7orf25, a previously uncharacterized protein named
proliferation-associated NXF1 adaptor (PANAD), interacts with NXF1 in the
nucleus. Specifically, the R122/E125/G133/D139 residues of PANAD bound to the
Q20/K22/K23 residues in the RBD of NXF1. This binding disrupted the
intramolecular interaction between the RBD and NTF2L domains of NXF1, thereby
enhancing the association of NXF1 with target mRNAs. The mRNAs whose nuclear
export was impaired upon PANAD knockdown were highly enriched in cell
proliferation pathways. Moreover, PANAD directly bound to CDK6 mRNA. Gain- and
loss-of-function analyses in cellular and mouse models revealed that PANAD
accelerated G1/S transition and tumor cell proliferation, promoted colony
formation and hepatoma xenograft growth, and acted through interactions with
NXF1 to increase the nuclear export and translation of mRNAs encoding
proliferation stimulators, including CDK6. PANAD and CDK6 were both upregulated
in HCC tissues and were positively correlated. Furthermore, PANAD-overexpressing
hepatoma cells exhibited increased sensitivity to the CDK4/6 inhibitor
palbociclib, whereas PANAD-silenced cells exhibited reduced sensitivity. These
findings identify PANAD as a novel NXF1 adaptor; delineate its critical
regulatory roles in mRNA nuclear export, G1/S transition and tumor development;
and highlight its potential as both a therapeutic target and a predictive
biomarker for CDK4/6-targeted therapy.

Introduction

The nuclear export of messenger RNA (mRNA) represents an evolutionarily conserved post-transcriptional regulatory process that involves the transport of mature mRNAs from the nucleus to the cytoplasm for translation.1,2 This process is mediated mainly by nuclear export factor 1 (NXF1).1,3,4 The N-terminal arginine-rich RNA-binding domain (RBD) mediates RNA binding of NXF1, while the NTF2-like (NTF2L) and ubiquitin-associated (UBA) domains facilitate its interaction with nucleoporins.5,6 NXF1 relies on adaptors for effective RNA binding.3,7 In the absence of an adaptor, an intramolecular interaction occurs between the RBD and NTF2L domains of NXF1, thus inhibiting the RNA-binding activity of NXF1.8 Adaptors can associate with NXF1 and disrupt this intramolecular interaction, enabling NXF1 to stably bind mRNA and initiate mRNA nuclear export.8,9 Currently, two types of NXF1 adaptors have been identified: transcription-export (TREX) complex-related adaptors, including ALYREF and UIF, and the serine/arginine-rich (SR) protein family, such as SRSF1, SRSF3, and SRSF7.7,10–16 Given the importance of NXF1 adaptors in mRNA nuclear export, identifying additional NXF1 adaptors will provide new insights into the regulatory mechanisms of mRNA nuclear export and their roles in physiological and pathological processes.

Stringent regulation of mRNA nuclear export is critical for maintaining normal cellular homeostasis, and its dysregulation is implicated in the pathogenesis of diverse diseases.1 Emerging evidence indicates that core regulators of NXF1-mediated mRNA export are frequently dysregulated across multiple tumor types.1,17–24 For example, mutations in NXF1 have been identified in chronic lymphocytic leukemia.19 Components of the THO subcomplex within the TREX complex, such as THOC1, THOC2, and THOC5, are upregulated in breast cancers.20,21 Furthermore, ALYREF expression is increased in multiple tumor types, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).22,23 In clear cell renal cell carcinoma, elevated SRSF3 expression in tumor-infiltrating T cells promotes the nuclear export of PD-1 mRNA, thereby impairing the antitumor function of T cells in mouse models.24 These observations suggest that deregulation of NXF1-mediated mRNA nuclear export may play a critical role in tumor development. Although posttranscriptional dysregulation is a common feature of human tumors, yet the specific effects and underlying mechanisms of mRNA nuclear export in tumor development remain poorly understood.

Uncontrolled cell proliferation is a defining hallmark of malignant tumors, primarily stemming from deregulation of the cell cycle, particularly defects at the G1/S checkpoint.25 This checkpoint acts as a master regulator governing S-phase entry and cell division, it is therefore subject to stringent regulation. Specifically, growth signals drive the transcription of cyclin D, which activates cyclin-dependent kinases 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) to phosphorylate and inactivate RB, thus abrogating the binding of RB to E2F and releasing E2F activity. Derepressed E2F then elicits the transcription of S-phase genes, including cyclin E; the cyclin E-CDK2 complex triggers RB hyperphosphorylation, fully inactivating RB and generating a positive feedback loop that irreversibly commits cells to the G1/S transition.26 Abnormal expression or activation of G1/S transition regulators is prevalent in human cancers, fueling accelerated cell cycle progression and uncontrolled proliferation.27 In HCC patients, aberrations in G1/S regulatory pathways are frequently observed, encompassing amplification of cyclin D1 and its transactivator (e.g., c‑Myc), as well as deletion or inactivation of RB1 and CDK inhibitor.28 These lesions collectively disrupt G1/S control, driving malignant proliferation of HCC cells. Palbociclib, an FDA-approved CDK4/6 inhibitor for breast cancer,29 is under investigation for HCC treatment. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that palbociclib effectively inhibits HCC cell proliferation in vitro, reduces tumor burden in mouse models with spontaneous hepatoma and patient‑derived HCC xenografts, potentiates the therapeutic response to sorafenib, and exhibits no obvious toxicity, suggesting its potential value in anti-HCC therapy.30

HCC is an aggressive malignancy with high mortality rate because of the lack of effective treatment options.31–35 Given the pivotal role of post-transcriptional regulation in cellular function, elucidating the aberrant mRNA nuclear export in HCC cells may not only provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms of HCC development but also facilitate the development of targeted and effective anti-HCC strategies. However, NXF1 is intractable for direct targeting because its essential role in global mRNA export, which precludes tumor-specific inhibition without collateral damage to normal cells.36 Instead, identifying dysregulated adaptors that selectively govern HCC-related mRNA export may enable the design of targeted therapies that evade systemic toxicity. PANAD levels were associated with HCC patient survival and tumor cell sensitivity to CDK4/6 inhibitors. These findings identify the function and regulatory mechanism of PANAD in mRNA nuclear export and tumor growth, offering a potential therapeutic target and predictive biomarker for cancer therapy.

Subsequent bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) analysis, which enables direct visualization of protein interactions in living cells, was performed using cells coexpressing PANAD tagged with N-terminal YFP (YFPn-PANAD) and NXF1 tagged with C-terminal YFP (YFPc-NXF1). Strong YFP fluorescence signals, which are indicative of protein‒protein interactions, were specifically localized within the Lamin B1-demarcated nuclear boundaries, confirming that PANAD and NXF1 colocalize and interact in the nucleus (Fig. 1h; Supplementary Fig. 4b). To quantitatively and dynamically assess their interaction, acceptor bleaching fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assays were performed using the FRET pair of either mEGFP-PANAD or unfused mEGFP as the donor and mCherry-NXF1 as the acceptor. Acceptor photobleaching led to a significant increase in donor fluorescence intensity in cells expressing mEGFP-PANAD/mCherry-NXF1, with a robust FRET efficiency of 12.94 ± 0.42%. In contrast, control cells expressing mEGFP/mCherry-NXF1 exhibited negligible FRET efficiency (<2.0%) (Fig. 1i). These results confirm the specific, close-range (< 10 nm) physical association between the PANAD and NXF1.

Collectively, these results suggest specific, dynamic, and nuclear interactions between PANAD and NXF1.

Disruption of the intramolecular interaction between the RBD and NTF2L domains of NXF1 is required for NXF1 to bind mRNAs.8 In vitro binding assays revealed that PANAD attenuated this intramolecular interaction (Fig. 3d, lane 3). BiFC assays revealed strong fluorescence in cells coexpressing YFPn-NTF2L and RBD-YFPc (Fig. 3e, upper panel, “Ctrl” group), confirming the intramolecular RBD-NTF2L interaction. Notably, this fluorescence signal was diminished by wild-type PANAD or PANAD-mut2 (Fig. 3e, lower panel; bars 2 and 4), both of which interacted with NXF1 (Fig. 3c). However, the signal was unaffected by PANAD-mut1 (Fig. 3e, lower panel; bar 3), which is deficient in NXF1 binding (Fig. 3c). Subsequent RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays revealed that overexpression of wild-type PANAD or PANAD-mut2, but not PANAD-mut1, increased NXF1 binding to all nine proliferation-stimulating mRNAs (Fig. 3f). These results indicate that PANAD interacts with NXF1 to disrupt the intramolecular interaction of NXF1, thereby promoting NXF1 binding to mRNAs.

To determine whether PANAD also interacts with proliferation-stimulating mRNAs, we performed RIP assays in cells overexpressing PANAD-Flag. The results revealed that compared with the IgG control, all nine mRNAs were enriched in the anti-Flag immunoprecipitates (Fig. 3g), suggesting an interaction between PANAD and these mRNAs. To determine whether these interactions involve direct RNA binding, CDK6 was selected as a representative candidate for detailed analysis. RIP assays demonstrated that the anti-Flag antibody specifically precipitated the 1–3 knt fragment of CDK6-3’UTR but not other regions of the CDK6 mRNA (Fig. 3g; Supplementary Fig. 8a). In vitro RNA pulldown assays using a purified GST-PANAD fusion protein confirmed that PANAD specifically bound to the proximal 1–3 knt region of the CDK6-3’UTR but not to the distal 4–6 knt sequence (Supplementary Fig. 8b), indicating direct binding of PANAD to CDK6 mRNA.

We then investigated whether PANAD and NXF1 mutually regulate each other. Notably, PANAD had no effect on either NXF1 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 9a–b) or its subcellular localization (Supplementary Fig. 9c–d). However, NXF1 silencing decreased both the cytoplasmic PANAD mRNA ratio (Supplementary Fig. 10a) and the cellular PANAD protein level (Supplementary Fig. 10b). RIP assays further confirmed the direct binding of NXF1 to PANAD mRNA (Supplementary Fig. 10c). This interaction was significantly strengthened by the overexpression of PANAD or PANAD-mut2 but not PANAD-mut1 (Supplementary Fig. 10d), suggesting that PANAD promotes the recruitment of its own mRNA to NXF1. Moreover, exogenous PANAD expression increased endogenous PANAD protein levels, an effect that was abolished upon NXF1 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 10e). These findings suggest the presence of a PANAD-NXF1 positive feedback loop: PANAD enhances NXF1-mediated nuclear export and translation of its own mRNA, and the resulting increase in endogenous PANAD further amplifies this effect in an NXF1-dependent manner.

In this study, we identified PANAD as a novel pro-oncogenic adaptor of NXF1, which promoted G1/S transition and tumor growth by enhancing the mRNA nuclear export of cell proliferation stimulators.

Our findings demonstrate that PANAD functions by directly interacting with NXF1, disrupting the intramolecular interaction of NXF1 and facilitating the nuclear export of target mRNAs. Canonical NXF1 adaptors are typically classified into two main categories: ALYREF and UIF, which depend on additional cofactors to facilitate NXF1–mRNA association,8,14,39 and SR family proteins (e.g., SRSF1 and SRSF3), which harbor conserved RNA recognition motifs (RRMs) for RNA binding and arginine/serine-rich (RS-rich) domains for protein‒protein interactions.40,41 In vitro binding assays revealed that PANAD alone was sufficient to unlock the intramolecular interaction of NXF1, suggesting that it does not rely on cofactors to enable binding of NXF1 to mRNAs. Collectively, these findings establish PANAD as a novel NXF1 adaptor with properties uniquely distinct from known adaptors, thereby expanding our understanding of the diversity and regulatory complexity of NXF1-dependent mRNA export. Interestingly, previous studies have reported that ALYREF and SR proteins bind the RBD-RRM domains of NXF1, and the PANAD-binding sites we identified also reside within the RBD domain of NXF1. Given this overlapping binding region, PANAD may compete with the ALYREF and SR proteins for NXF1 interaction, a hypothesis that warrants further experimental validation in future studies.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism of the PANAD-NXF1 interaction, we integrated structural predictions with biochemical and functional validation. Mutations at key predicted binding sites (PANAD-R122/E125/G133/D139 and NXF1-Q20/K22/K23) abolished the interaction, whereas control mutations had no effect. FRET assays further validated a specific, close-range physical association between PANAD and NXF1. RIP assays revealed that wild-type PANAD and PANAD-mut2, but not PANAD-mut1, enhanced NXF1 binding to its target mRNAs. Notably, RNA pulldown assays demonstrated that PANAD directly interacted with target mRNAs such as CDK6. These findings suggest that PANAD employs a dual regulatory mechanism: it disrupts the intramolecular interaction of NXF1 to shift it to an active conformation while simultaneously binding directly to target mRNAs to promote their recruitment to NXF1. These findings provide new insights into the mechanisms of mRNA export. Although the current data support the role of PANAD in unlocking the RBD-NTF2L interface through direct binding, future structural studies by cryo-electron microscopy or nuclear magnetic resonance are warranted to fully validate this mechanism.

Notably, NXF1 mediates the nuclear export of most mRNAs.42–45 Our integrated analysis of the RNA-seq data of PANAD-silenced cells and public NXF1 iCLIP-seq data revealed that PANAD regulated a specific subset of NXF1-bound transcripts that were enriched in proliferation-related pathways as well as other cancer-relevant biological processes, indicating that PANAD may selectively regulate oncogenic mRNAs. To gain critical mechanistic insight, identifying the RNA motif recognized by PANAD is essential. Future studies employing PANAD iCLIP-seq will precisely map the consensus sequence of PANAD-interacting RNAs and pinpoint direct targets of PANAD, which is crucial for clarifying the molecular basis of the target selectivity of PANAD.

To date, the specific roles of NXF1 or its adaptors in regulating the G1/S transition remain unexplored. We demonstrated that PANAD facilitated NXF1 binding to mRNAs encoding proliferation stimulators, including CDK6, thereby promoting the nuclear export and translation of these mRNAs. This cascade accelerated the G1/S transition and subsequent cell division. Importantly, silencing NXF1 attenuated the functions of PANAD, and mutations in the amino acid residues of PANAD that mediated the NXF1-PANAD interaction abolished the functions of PANAD. These findings reveal a novel mechanism through which NXF1 and its adaptor regulate G1/S transition, highlighting the crucial regulatory role of mRNA nuclear export during cell cycle control and tumor development.

CDK6 is a key driver of the G1/S transition. It is frequently upregulated in cancer cells, which may result from various mechanisms, including genomic amplification, abnormal transcriptional activation, dysregulation of posttranscriptional regulation and altered protein stability.46–51 Here, we demonstrated that upregulation of PANAD in tumor tissues represents a novel mechanism underlying CDK6 upregulation in cancer, establishing a functional PANAD-CDK6 regulatory axis that promotes tumor proliferation.

Given the significant function of PANAD in promoting tumor growth, it represents a potential therapeutic target in cancer. While both NXF1 and PANAD are upregulated in multiple tumor types, including HCC, targeting NXF1, a broadly essential nuclear exporter, may lead to widespread toxicity in normal cells.36 In contrast, inhibiting PANAD may offer a novel and potentially less toxic anticancer strategy, as it selectively regulates oncogenic mRNAs rather than global mRNA export. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for the potential of PANAD as a therapeutic target and as a predictive biomarker for CDK4/6-targeted therapy.
