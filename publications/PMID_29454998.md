The mitochondrial proteome comprises 1000 to 1500 proteins, in addition to
proteins for which the mitochondrial localization is uncertain. About 800
diseases have been linked with mutations in mitochondrial proteins. We devised a
cell survival assay for assessing the mitochondrial localization in a
high-throughput format. This protocol allows us to assess the mitochondrial
localization of proteins and their mutants, and to identify drugs and nutrients
that modulate the mitochondrial targeting of proteins. The assay works equally
well for proteins directed to the outer mitochondrial membrane, inner
mitochondrial membrane mitochondrial and mitochondrial matrix, as demonstrated
by assessing the mitochondrial targeting of the following proteins: carnitine
palmitoyl transferase 1 (consensus sequence and R123C mutant), acetyl-CoA
carboxylase 2, uncoupling protein 1 and holocarboxylase synthetase. Our screen
may be useful for linking the mitochondrial proteome with rare diseases and for
devising drug- and nutrition-based strategies for altering the mitochondrial
targeting of proteins.

The mitochondrial proteome comprises 1,000 to 1,500 proteins [1] (http://locate.imb.uq.edu.au), not counting several hundred proteins for which the mitochondrial localization is uncertain (http://www.mitoproteome.org). Possible impacts, both adverse and beneficial, of altering the mitochondrial targeting of proteins through xenobiotic and nutritional factors are poorly understood. The tissue specificity of the mitochondrial proteome is largely unknown and, along the same lines, the mitochondrial localization and function in mitochondrial biology are unknown for a meaningful number of proteins. Likewise, the extent to which mutations affect mitochondrial localization is unknown for the vast majority of proteins. The identification of mitochondrial proteins is further complicated by the low abundance of many mitochondrial proteins, the potential impact of developmental stages, and because no single targeting sequence directs the mitochondrial import of proteins [1, 2].

We have developed a cell death-based assay in a high-throughput format to position investigators for answering many of the questions raised above, and tested the assay with a number of proteins to demonstrate feasibility. Briefly, the protocol is based on transfecting human cells with plasmids coding for putative mitochondrial proteins fused to the cell death peptide KLAKLAKKLAKLAK [3]. The peptide KLAKLAKKLAKLAK disrupts the mitochondrial membrane potential and causes cell death when brought in close physical proximity to the mitochondrial membrane through the fused protein. Cell survival is assessed using the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) assay [4].

The following proteins were included in our analysis in order to have a broad representation of proteins of uncertain mitochondrial localization, proteins localizing in distinct mitochondrial compartments, proteins with key roles in fatty acid metabolism, protein mutants, and proteins implicated in rare diseases. (a) The protein biotin ligase holocarboxylase synthetase (HLCS) is a protein of uncertain mitochondrial localization. HLCS is not listed in databases of mitochondrial proteins (http://locate.imb.uq.edu.au
http://www.mitoproteome.org) [5], yet it catalyzes the covalent binding of the coenzyme biotin to the enzymes pyruvate carboxylase (PC), 3-methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase, and propionyl-CoA carboxylase [6], which reside in the mitochondrial matrix. Western blots of mitochondrial HLCS are inconclusive [7]. Mutations on HLCS gene may lead to a condition determined as multiple carboxylase deficiency (MCD) [8]. MCD patients generally exhibit low activities of all five biotin-dependent carboxylases and the incidence of HCLS deficiency is less than 1 of 100 000 live births in Japan [9]. (b) Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1) and acetyl-CoA carboxylase 2 (ACC2) localize to the outer mitochondrial membrane and play essential roles as positive and negative regulators, respectively, of the mitochondrial import of fatty acids [10, 11]; due to their role in the regulation of fatty acid metabolism they are targets for the treatment of the metabolic syndrome [12]. When HEK293 cells were transfected with ACC2-KLAK and cultured with chemically pure compounds representing the three major polyphenol classes in Hamamelis, 3-chlorogenic acid caused a 54% increase in cell survival compared with the vehicle control (Fig. 5a); in a similar way, quercetin-3-rutinoside (rutin) and gallic acid caused increases (38 and 28%, respectively) in HEK293 cell survival. Despite all compounds offering significant increase in cell survival, we chose 3-chlorogenic acid for further analysis due to numerically higher potential. The survival-enhancing effects were dose dependent for 3-chlorogenic acid (Fig. 5b). Theoretically, it is possible that these compounds activate cell survival pathways rather than inhibit the targeting of ACC2-KLAKLAKKLAKLAK to the mitochondrial membrane. To determine whether the observed effects were caused by altered mitochondrial binding of ACC2, HEK293 cells were transfected with plasmid ACC2 (Fig. 1b) and mitochondrial targeting was assessed by tracking the GFP tag by confocal microscopy. When cells were treated with an aqueous extract of 3-chlorogenic acid (10 μM, final concentration), the GFP signal associated with mitochondria decreased compared with vehicle controls, evidenced by the loss of yellow signal in chlorogenic acid-treated cells compared with controls in the merged images (Fig. 5c,d).

Application to identifying mitochondrial proteins and its regulation by small molecules

Next, the assay was applied to assessing the mitochondrial localization of human proteins in HEK293 cells. Rapid cell death was evident when cells were transfected with KLAKLAKKLAKLAK fusion constructs of CPT1, ACC2, UCP1, and HLCS, judged by MTT assay (Table 1). Mitochondrial targeting was not impaired for the CPT1R123C mutant, indicating that the activity loss is not related to mitochondrial localization. Transfection efficiency was not significantly different among the various constructs, judged by the expression of GFP mRNA (Supplementary Table 1).

KLAKLAKKLAKLAK fusion constructs are useful for detecting effects of nutrients and drugs on the mitochondrial targeting of proteins, as illustrated by the following examples. (a) We determined whether biotin availability affects the mitochondrial localization of the protein biotin ligase HLCS. The mitochondrial localization of HLCS increased moderately by about 10% in biotin-supplemented HEK293 cells compared with biotin-sufficient cells, evidenced by a decrease in cell growth judged by MTT assay (abs. at 562 nm): 0.93±0.07 vs. 1.03±0.06 (Supplementary Fig. 3). Efficacy of biotin depletion and supplementation was confirmed by assessing the abundance of biotinylated holocarboxylases, which is a well-established marker for biotin nutrition in cell cultures and humans (Supplementary Fig. 4) [20, 21].

Such studies are not feasible when using an approach that involves plasmids with constitutively active promoters such as those described above, because KLAKLAKKLAKLAK fusion proteins kill cells within about 24 h (Fig. 2 and 3); the above assays are intended for use in high-throughput screens that do not require lengthy treatment of cells. GFP-tagged ACC2-KLAK was subcloned into a tetracycline-inducible plasmid (pcDNA5-FRT/TO-ACC2,GFP-KLAKTet-On) and stably integrated into the FRT locus in the HEK293-Flp/TRex cell genome (Invitrogen); these cells also express the tetracycline repressor. DiscussionWe have developed a new protocol for the identification of mitochondrial proteins and the assessment of xenobiotic and nutritional factors and mutations that alter the mitochondrial localization of proteins. The protocol was validated by testing five known and putative mitochondrial proteins and identifies proteins regardless of the mitochondrial compartment they are associated with. Our cell death assay has considerable strengths when compared with traditional protocols for mitochondrial proteins such as immunocytochemistry and western blot analysis. First, the assay is suited for high-throughput screens. The entire screen can be carried out in one single plate, using 96-well or 384-well plate formats, without requiring the transfer of samples to other plates or washing plates between steps. There is no need for using costly reagents such as antibodies or specialized equipment which renders the assay an economical approach to studies of the mitochondrial proteome and its regulation. Second, the assay yields semi-quantitative results because it is coupled to the MTT assay or similar assays. Note that similarly powerful protocols have been published by Ting and co-workers and present a viable alternative to the KLAK-based protocol [26, 27].

We envision numerous meaningful applications for this assay. Examples include the (a) identification of food-borne or synthetic molecules that mask the mitochondrial anchoring motif in the ACC2 N-terminus [11], thereby increasing the mitochondrial uptake of fatty acids for subsequent β-oxidation and causing a lean phenotype [11, 28, 29]; (b) identification of the entire mitochondrial proteome in distinct tissues or cell lines; (c) identification of mutations and polymorphisms that alter the mitochondrial localization of proteins; and (d) drug screens for inhibiting the localization of γ-secretase subunits (presenilins 1 and 2, nicastrin, APH-1, and PEN-2) in the mitochondria-associated membranes space to combat Alzheimer's disease [30-32]..

The KLAK-based analysis of mitochondrial proteins is not a viable choice for identifying the localization of proteins in distinct mitochondrial compartments. For example, the assay produced an equally strong signal for proteins that reside in the inner (UCP1) and outer (ACC2, CPT1) membrane. This being said, the assay is a mere screening tool for the overall mitochondrial targeting of proteins, identification of mitochondrial localization signals, and discovery of compounds that inhibit mitochondrial targeting of proteins.

There also is the possibility that overexpression of proteins might cause aberrant localization of proteins. We titrated the amount of HLCS protein in an attempt to provide a point reference for users of mitochondrial targeting assays. Note that cells stably transfected with ACC2-KLAK behave similar to transiently transduced cells, and that the level of expression typically is considerably small in stable transformations compared with transient transformations.

In its current form, the assay does not provide a firm cut-off, i.e., an absolute or percent change in absorbance in the MTT assay, when screening for compounds that alter mitochondrial localization of ACC2. These cut-offs need to be defined by users, depending on the experimental conditions and objectives of their studies. Investigators are cautioned that some compounds might affect cell proliferation and death and are advised to assess any such effects in the absence of KLAK plasmids.

In retrospect, we would advise users of this assay to use a mitochondrial stain other than CMXRos mitotracker, because of its dependence on mitochondrial integrity. We would expect dyes that do not depend on membrane potential produce a much stronger counterstain than what we achieved by using CMXRos.

Note that Tet-On protocols are of limited use when seeking to culturing cells stably transformed with KLAKLAKKLAKLAK fusion proteins. The cumulative number of cells of ACC2,GFP-KLAKTet-On cells was about one order of magnitude smaller than control cells over a 15-day period. The low productivity of ACC2,GFP-KLAKTet-On cell cultures is due to a leaky expression of the Tet-On system used in this study and the strong death signal of KLAKLAKKLAKLAK. This observation is consistent with the leaky expression of Tet-On system reported in previous studies [33, 34]. The application of KLAKLAKKLAKLAK-based fusion constructs in the spatio-temporal analysis of mitochondrial proteins will have to await the creation of protocols that prevent transgene expression in solvent controls.
