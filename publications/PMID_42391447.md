ABSTRACT

Temozolomide (TMZ) is still the first‐line drug for glioblastoma (GBM) treatment though tumor cell resistance remains a major challenge. TMZ administration may induce cellular senescence (CSEN), which exerts a dual regulatory effect on tumor progression, and evidence has suggested that CSEN is closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction. Acylglycerol kinase (AGK), a mitochondrial membrane kinase, has been reported to participate in regulating mitochondrial function and the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Here, we aimed to investigate the role of AGK in GBM progression and TMZ resistance, assessed whether CSEN mediates these effects, and investigated the therapeutic potential of senolytic agents. Firstly, the analysis of TCGA data revealed that the increased expression of AGK was correlated with a poor prognosis of GBM patients. Secondly, through cell experiments of AGK‐knockdown and overexpression, we found that AGK suppression inhibited the proliferation of tumor cells while paradoxically promoting TMZ resistance. Mechanistically, AGK suppression amplified TMZ‐induced CSEN through increasing mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) and decreasing membrane potential. Conversely, Mito‐TEMPO, a mtROS scavenger, and FOXO4‐DRI, a senolytic agent, both enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of TMZ by eliminating these senescent cells via apoptosis. Furthermore, the clinical analysis linked AGK levels, CSEN, and prognosis of GBM patients treated with TMZ. In conclusion, our results establish senescence induction as a novel mechanism for AGK‐mediated TMZ sensitization, suggesting that co‐targeting AGK and CSEN represents a promising strategy to enhance TMZ therapy.

Keywords: acylglycerol kinase, cellular senescence, glioblastoma, mitochondrial ROS, Temozolomide

1. Introduction

According to the latest WHO diagnostic criteria for central nervous system tumors, glioblastoma (GBM) is a neuroepithelium‐derived glioma classified as WHO grade IV, which is recognized as the most common and aggressive malignant primary brain tumor in adults [ 1 ]. At present, the standard treatment regimen for GBM involves maximal safe surgical resection followed by radiotherapy combined with temozolomide (TMZ) and adjuvant TMZ chemotherapy [ 2 , 3 ]. TMZ is a lipophilic DNA alkylating agent that crosses the blood‐brain barrier and exerts anti‐tumor effects by inducing apoptosis [ 4 , 5 ]. Despite the advances in surgical techniques, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy in the past decade, almost all GBM tumors eventually recur locally due to their profound intratumoral heterogeneity [ 6 ]. Among these challenges, the development of TMZ resistance constitutes a major obstacle in GBM management, underscoring the urgent need to elucidate the potential mechanism of TMZ resistance in GBM.

Recent studies demonstrate that TMZ can induce GBM cellular senescence (CSEN) accompanied by impaired DNA repair, a phenomenon termed therapy‐induced senescence [ 7 , 8 ]. Furthermore, Beltzig et al. report that TMZ‐induced O 6 ‑methylguanine lesions predominantly trigger CSEN, which occurs at levels fourfold higher than apoptosis, and depends on DNA double‐strand break and ROS production [ 9 ]. CSEN is a permanent cell cycle arrest at G1/S phase and mediated through p21 CIP1 and/or p16 INK4A pathways, primarily characterized by the senescence‐associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [ 10 ]. Although CSEN acts as a tumor‐suppressive barrier by halting proliferation and enabling immune clearance, its persistent accumulation after therapy may paradoxically promote tumor progression and immune suppression via the secretion of proinflammatory factors such as IL‐1, IL‐6, and IL‐8 [ 11 , 12 ]. Moreover, other components of SASP, including growth factors, chemokines, and proteases, can stimulate angiogenesis and induce epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) [ 13 ]. Notably, senescence‐targeting therapeutic strategies, such as CDK4/CDK6 inhibitors and senolytic agents, are currently being evaluated in GBM clinical trials as promising treatment approaches [ 7 , 14 ]. However, their clinical efficacy remains inconclusive to date.

In cancer, CSEN can be triggered by multiple stressors, including DNA damage, oncogene activation, and radiation/chemotherapy [ 15 , 16 ]. In addition, mitochondrial dysfunction serves as a key physiological trigger due to its role in ROS production, oxidative stress, and energy deficits [ 17 , 18 ]. Acylglycerol kinase (AGK), a critical component of the translocase of inner mitochondrial membrane 22 complex, contributes to maintain mitochondrial structure and function, and mainly participates in catalyzing the production of lysophosphatidic acid and phosphatidic acid from monoacylglycerol and diacylglycerol [ 19 ]. AGK mutations cause Sengers syndrome (manifesting hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, skeletal myopathy, cataracts, and lactic acidosis through impaired mitochondrial function) [ 20 ]. Recently, multiple studies have established AGK as an oncogene that drives tumor progression across cancer types. Aberrant AGK expression has been observed in oral squamous cell carcinoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, breast cancer, and prostate cancer, where the elevated AGK promotes tumor proliferation and progression through PI3K/Akt signaling, G1‐S phase regulation, and EGFR pathway modulation [ 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 ]. Our previous studies have established AGK as a key regulator in both tumor and immune contexts, and demonstrated that AGK impairs Venetoclax mediated sensitization of diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma by promoting PTEN phosphorylation, which activates PI3K/AKT pathway, suppresses FOXO1 nuclear localization, and upregulates BCL‐2 expression [ 25 ]. In addition, our recent studies revealed that AGK deficiency activates cGAS‐STING signaling pathway and type I interferon response through mitochondrial damage‐induced cytoplasmic mtDNA accumulation in tumor‐associated macrophages, thereby enhancing T cell‐mediated anti‐tumor immunity [ 26 ]. Given the pivotal role of AGK in mitochondrial regulation and tumor progression, it may provide critical insights for improving TMZ chemotherapy efficacy in GBM to elucidate its function in GBM progression and TMZ resistance, and clarify the underlying mechanisms.

In this study, we employed database analysis, tumor tissues of patients, and U251 and LN299 cell models with AGK‐knockdown or overexpression to investigate the role of AGK in GBM progression and TMZ sensitivity. The results demonstrated that AGK promotes GBM proliferation, invasion, and migration. However, AGK significantly affected negatively the sensitivity of GBM to TMZ. Mechanistically, AGK knockout amplified TMZ‐induced mitochondrial damage and mtROS production in GBM cells and, subsequently, promoted CSEN and the secretion of SASP factors, leading to TMZ resistance. Further intervention with mito‐TEMPO reversing the production of mtROS or FOXO4‐DRI, a senolytic agent promoting the apoptosis of senescent cells, synergistically enhanced the anti‐tumor efficacy of TMZ. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of AGK/CSEN targeting combined with TMZ as a novel combinatorial strategy for GBM treatment.

2. Methods and Materials

2.1. Analysis of Data From TCGA

RNA sequencing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were obtained via https://toil.xenahubs.net . TCGA‐GBM, TCGA‐LGG, and TCGA‐Normal Brain datasets were analyzed using R (v4.2.1). Boxplots were generated by ggplot2 package (v3.4.3). Raw sequencing reads were aligned using STAR aligner and normalized as fragments per kilobase million (FPKM). The samples were stratified into AGK‐low and AGK‐high expression groups based on the median AGK level. Kaplan‐Meier survival curves (OS, DSS, DFI) and time‐dependent ROC analysis were performed using R survival packages.

2.2. Cell Culture

Human GBM cell lines (U251 and LN299) and HEK293T cells from ATCC were cultured in RPMI DMEM (Gibco, C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% FBS (Cellmax, SA211.02) and 1% penicillin streptomycin (Gibco, 15240062) at 37°C with 5% CO 2 .

2.3. Generating Stable Cell Lines

GBM cell lines with AGK knockdown and overexpression were established by constructing lentiviral shRNA. AGK shRNA were cloned into pLKO.1 vector. Specific sequences were as follows:

shAGK1 (TRCN0000153242): 5′‐CCGGGCCCTTCCATTTCTCTTCTTTCTCGAG AAAGAAGAGAAATGGAAGGGCTTTTTTG;

shAGK2 (TR CN0000153828): 5′‐CCGGCCTCAACTGTACTTGGAGAAACTCG AGTTTCTCCAAGTACAGTTGAGGTTTTTTG;

Full‐length cDNA encoding AGK was cloned into Murine Stem Cell Virus‐IRES‐Thy1.1 (MSCV‐IRES‐Thy1.1, RV) vector. AGK knockdown and overexpression lentiviral shRNA constructs, pMD2.G (Addgene, #12259) and psPAX2 (Addgene, #12260) were transfected into HEK293T cells of 70% confluency at a 3:1:2 ratio using Lipofectinmin 2000, and the supernatants containing viral particles were collected after 48 h and 72 h, respectively. U251 and LN299 cells were incubated with specific lentiviral particles for 6 h, and then repeated the transduction on the next day. Stable cell lines were selected with 8 μg/mL puromycin for at least 14 days, and the efficiency of AGK knockdown and overexpression were verified.

2.4. qRT‐PCR

Cells were harvested and the total RNA was extracted using M5 universal RNA mini kit (Mei5bio, MF036), and then reverse transcribed to cDNA (TSINGKE, TSE203) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The mRNA expression levels were analyzed using Bio‐Rad SYBR Green intercalated fluorophore system (Applied Biosystems). Data were normalized to the expression of GAPDH using ΔΔ CT. The upstream and downstream primers were listed in Table S1 .

2.5. Immunoblotting

Each group of GBM cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer and equal amounts of proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE electrophoresis, and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. After the non‐specific binding sites were blocked, the blots were probed with the anti‐AGK (Abcam, ab137616), anti‐P16 (CST, #18769), anti‐P21 (CST, #2947), anti‐P53 (CST, #2524), anti‐phospho‐AKT(T308) (CST, #2965), anti‐ phosphor‐AKT (S473) (CST, #4060) and anti‐β‐Actin (CST, #4970) antibodies overnight at 4°C. Finally, the membrane was incubated with a horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibody for 1.5 h, followed by ECL detection (Bio‐Rad) and analysis by ImageJ software.

2.6. Cell Proliferation and Viability Assay

Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8, KeyGEN BioTECH, KGA9310) was used to detect the cell proliferation and viability of GBM cells in shNC, shAGK, or AGK#1 group. Briefly, 5 × 10 3 GBM cells were plated onto 96‐well plates, and the cell proliferation was assessed on the 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 days, respectively. In addition, the above cells were treated with varying amounts of TMZ (SL PHARM, Beijing, #H20110153), and the cell viability was detected after 72 h. 10 μL CCK‐8 solution was added in per 100 µl culture medium and incubated for 1–2 h, and the absorbance at 450 nm were detected. The IC50 concentrations of TMZ were determined by logarithmic (inhibitor) vs normalized responses using GraphPad Prism 9.

2.7. Cell Invasion Assay

Cell invasion assay was performed using 12‐well transwell insert. Each group cells were harvested for 24 h and seeded into the upper chambers where 60 μL Matrigel (1 mg/mL, Corning, 354277) was added in advance and cultured in serum‐free medium. DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber of 12‐well transwell insert. The control group, TMZ treatment group, TMZ combined with Mito‐TEMPO (Sigma‐Aldrich, Shanghai, #1334850‐99‐5) or FOXO4‐DRI (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, #HY‐P4157) were established, and the invasive cells were stained with crystal violet and counted to assess the invasion ability.

2.8. Migration Assay

5 × 10 6 cells of each group were seeded on a 12‐well plate, and the inserts were removed after the cells adherence and concentration were more than 95%. The control group, TMZ treatment group, TMZ combined with Mito‐TEMPO or FOXO4‐DRI group were established, and the cell migratory capacity was monitored at 0, 24, and 48 h after stimulation. The percentage of wound areas of different sections was analyzed by Image J.

2.9. Flow Cytometry

1 × 10 6 GBM cells with shNC, shAGK, and AGK#1 were respectively treated with varying amounts of TMZ or TMZ combined with FOXO4‐DRI for 48 h. Each group cells were stained with propidium iodide and FITC conjugated Annexin V (Biolegend, 640906) for 15 min, and the percentage of early and late apoptotic cells was determined on HighFlux flow cytometer (Gaugene) and analyzed using FlowJo software. The cells in the above groups were stained with MitoTracker Green (Invitrogen, M7514) and MitoTrack Deep Red (Invitrogen, M22426 ) at 37°C for 15 min, and the proportion of MitoTracker Deep Red positive cells in MitoTracker Green positive cells was detected by HighFlux flow cytometer to reflect the loss of mitochondrial potential. MitoSOX Red (Invitrogen, M36008 ) was used to detect the ROS generation status in the mitochondria. The cells in each group were also stained with MitoSOX Red at 37°C for 15 min, and the MFI of ROS in each group was detected by HighFlux flow cytometer.

2.10. RNA‐Sequencing Analysis

shNC, shNC+TMZ, shAGK1 and shAGK1+TMZ U251 cells were cultured in same situation, and the total RNA was isolated by TRIzol (Invitrogen, 15596026), then subjected to RNA‐sequencing using Illumina Nextseq. 6000. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with a cutoff of log2 (fold change) > 0.5 using the R package limma. The key genes and pathways were identified by R language using Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, and GSEA.

2.11. ATP Assay

ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0026) was used to detect the levels of ATP. The equal amount of cells in the above groups were lysed by lysis buffer, and a standard curve was formed within the range of 0.1–10 μM. 100 μL ATP detection reagent was added in 12‐well plate, 20 μL cell lysate and standard were added in above ATP detection reagent, and the relative light units were detected and analyzed by luminometer.

2.12. Immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissue of GBM patients was isolated, fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin and cut into serial tissue sections, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Immunohistochemistry was performed with antibodies against AGK (Abcam, ab137616). The percentage of positive cells in different sections was determined by Image J.

2.13. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 9. Statistical significance was determined by log‐rank test, spearman correlation analysis, unpaired Student's t ‐test or for variances by one‐way ANOVA or two‐way ANOVA. No statistically significant differences were considered when p values were more than 0.05. Data was presented as Means ± SEM.

3. Results

3.1. High AGK Expression Was Correlated With Poor Prognosis and Promoted GBM Cell Proliferation

We firstly analyzed the mRNA expression level of AGK and survival data from TCGA database. The results revealed significant upregulation of AGK in multiple tumor types, including GBM (Figure S1A ; Figure 1A ). Kaplan‐Meier analysis demonstrated that patients with high AGK expression exhibited poor overall survival (OS, Figure 1B ), disease‐specific survival (DSS, Figure S1B ), and disease‐free interval (DFI, Figure S1C ). Time‐dependent ROC curves in TCGA cohort showed that the AUC values of OS predicted by AGK was 0.676 (1‐year), 0.730 (3‐year), and 0.769 (5‐year) (Figure 1C ), respectively, indicating our prognostic model had robust discriminatory power.

Figure 1.

We also established stable models with AGK‐knockdown (shAGK1, shAGK2) and overexpression (AGK#1) in U251 and LN299 cell lines. QRT‐PCR and western blot confirmed significantly reduced AGK mRNA/protein levels in knockdown groups and elevated expression in AGK#1 cells compared with scramble shRNA controls (shNC; Figure S1D–E , Figure 1D–E ). CCK‐8 assays revealed that AGK knockdown markedly inhibited the proliferation of GBM cell, while AGK overexpression enhanced the proliferative capacity (Figure 1F–G ). Cell line experiments further confirmed that AGK suppression weakened the GBM progression and inhibited tumor cell proliferation.

3.2. AGK Knockdown Reduced the Sensitivity of GBM Cell Lines to TMZ

To determine whether AGK modulates TMZ sensitivity, we assessed the viability of AGK‐modified GBM cells across different TMZ concentrations. The results demonstrated that the resistance of cells with AGK knockdown to TMZ was significantly enhanced compared with the corresponding control cells, with IC50 values rising from 209.8 μM (shNC) to 586.1 μM (shAGK1) and 627.9 μM (shAGK2) (Figure 2A ). Conversely, the cells with AGK overexpression were more sensitive than control cells, with IC50 values lowering from 279.1 μM (shNC) to 135.5 μM in AGK#1 U251 cells (Figure 2B ). A similar trend was observed in LN299 cells (Figure S2A,B ), which indicated that AGK supports the cytotoxicity of alkylating drug.

Figure 2.

We further evaluated the impact of AGK on the malignant phenotype. Transwell assays revealed TMZ concentration‐dependent inhibition of U251 cell invasion, which was partially reversed by AGK knockdown. Conversely, the cells with AGK‐overexpression exhibited an even lower invasive capacity (Figure 2C,D ). The wound‐healing assay also demonstrated that AGK suppression severely reversed the TMZ‐induced migratory inhibition in U251 cells, while overexpression of AGK further suppressed migration (Figure 2E,F ). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that AGK negatively regulates the inhibitory effect of TMZ on proliferation, invasion and migration of U251 cells.

3.3. AGK Negatively Regulates TMZ‐Induced CSEN in GBM Cells

To investigate the underlying mechanism of AGK‐modulated sensitization of GBM cells to TMZ, RNA sequencing (Illumina platform) was performed in AGK‐knockdown and control U251 cells with/without TMZ treatment. Heatmap and volcano plot analyses revealed significant upregulation of the genes associated with cell migration, CSEN, and EMT in AGK knockdown cells after TMZ treatment (Figure S3A,B ). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis identified enriched cytokine‐cytokine receptor interactions and cell adhesion molecule pathways (Figure S3C ). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) corroborated these findings, showing AGK knockdown suppressed the proliferation/mitotic cell cycle gene sets while upregulating CSEN‐associated IL6 expression, without altering apoptosis‐related pathways (Figure S3D ).

The evaluation of CSEN and EMT pathway demonstrated that AGK suppression significantly enhanced the expression of senescence markers, but did not alter that of EMT markers in U251 cells (Figure S4A,B ). Flow cytometry revealed that AGK knockdown increased G0/G1 phase arrest induced by TMZ (Figure 3A,B ). SA‐β‐gal staining confirmed that TMZ dose‐dependently induced CSEN, which was exacerbated by AGK depletion but attenuated by AGK overexpression (Figure 3C,D ). Western blotting showed that AGK knockdown amplified the upregulation of senescence‐associated proteins (P16, P21 and P53) driven by TMZ, while AGK overexpression suppressed the expression of these markers. These data established that AGK suppression exacerbates TMZ‐induced CSEN in GBM.

Figure 3.

3.4. AGK Suppression Exacerbates TMZ‐Induced Senescence via Promoting Mitochondrial Dysfunction

Next, we investigated how AGK suppression promotes TMZ‐induced CSEN. It was reported that mitochondrial dysfunction, including increased production of oxygen free radicals and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, is an important cause and consequence of CSEN [ 27 ]. We quantified mtROS levels using MitoSOX Red staining. Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence revealed that AGK depletion amplified the accumulation of mitochondrial ROS induced by TMZ (Figure 4A,B ; Figure S5A ). Concurrently, AGK knockdown reduced intracellular ATP levels (Figure S5B ). Mitochondrial membrane potential assessment also demonstrated that AGK suppression exacerbates TMZ‐induced mitochondrial dysfunction using MitoTracker probes (MitoTracker Deep Red + /MitoTracker Green + ) (Figure S5C,D ).

Figure 4.

Notably, Mito‐TEMPO, a mitochondrial targeted ROS scavenger and mimic that specifically scavenges superoxide dismutase, attenuated the senescence phenotypes. Moreover, the increased β‐galactosidase activity was abolished by Mito‐TEMPO (Figure S5E ; Figure 4C,D ). Western blot analysis further confirmed that Mito‐TEMPO suppressed the expression of P16, P21, and P53 in U251 cells with AGK knockdown under TMZ treatment (Figure 4E,F ). These findings demonstrated that AGK suppression amplifies TMZ‐induced senescence through mtROS overproduction.

3.5. FOXO4‐DRI Sensitizes GBM to Tmz By Enhancing Apoptosis of Senescent Cells Induced by AGK Suppression

Considering the major role of CSEN in regulating TMZ sensitivity of GBM cells, we tested the efficacy of FOXO4‐DRI, which was previously reported to be senolytic through inducing apoptosis of senescent cells by blocking the interaction between FOXO4 and p53. The results of SA‐β‐gal staining and flow cytometry confirmed that FOXO4‐DRI remarkably reduced CSEN (Figure 5A,B ) and enhanced TMZ‐induced apoptosis, particularly in AGK‐knockdown cells (Figure 5C,D ).

Figure 5.

We next assessed the effects of TMZ combined with Mito‐TEMPO or FOXO4‐DRI in shNC and shAGK U251 cells, and found that Mito‐TEMPO attenuated AGK suppression‐induced migration and invasion by mitigating CSEN under TMZ treatment. In addition, FOXO4‐DRI further suppressed the migration and invasion of U251 cells, with an amplified effect in shAGK cells (Figure 5E–H ). In conclusion, these findings demonstrated that CSEN promoted by mtROS mediates increase of TMZ sensitivity regulated by AGK, and FOXO4‐DRI represents a promising combinatorial strategy for TMZ‐resistant GBM through targeted clearance of senescent cells.

3.6. High AGGK Expression Predicts Enhanced TMZ Sensitivity in GBM Patients

To evaluate AGK as a predictive biomarker of TMZ response in GBM, we performed immunohistochemical staining of AGK in 82 cases of GBM treated with TMZ‐based chemotherapy. The results demonstrated that AGK exhibit predominant cytoplasmic localization and was significantly upregulated in GBM compared with normal brain tissues (Figure 6A ). Stratification into low/high AGK expression groups revealed significant differences in protein abundance (Figure 6B,C ). Consistent with in vitro findings, patients with high AGK expression demonstrated improved OS during TMZ treatment (Figure 6D ). Notably, senescence markers mediated the prognosis of AGK, with significant inverse correlations between AGK and p21 or p16 genes expression (Figure 6E,F ). However, there was no significant correlation between the expression of AGK and the senescent related cytokine IL‐6 (Figure 6G ). Most importantly, these clinical data confirmed that the increase of AGK expression correlates with TMZ sensitivity and attenuated senescence in GBM, underscoring its therapeutic relevance.

4. Discussion

The current standard of care of GBM includes maximal surgical resection, followed by radio‐chemotherapy with the DNA‐methylating compound TMZ. Clinical studies have shown that resistance of most GBM to TMZ is associated with a high level of DNA repair protein O 6 ‐methylguanine‐DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) [ 28 , 29 ]. However, even for patients with negative or low expression level of MGMT, TMZ inevitably leads to treatment failure [ 30 ]. Thus, the specific mechanism still needs to be further clarified.

Our study reveals AGK as a novel regulator of therapeutic response independent of the MGMT status. Clinical and functional analysis revealed the context‐dependent role of AGK in GBM. In untreated GBM, high AGK expression promoted their proliferation and correlated with poor prognosis. In contrast, following TMZ treatment, high AGK expression was associated with improved survival and enhanced TMZ sensitivity, as it suppresses therapy‐induced senescence. This paradox, where AGK knockdown inhibits proliferation yet promotes TMZ resistance, is resolved by its ability to amplify mitochondrial damage and mtROS accumulation, thereby driving a fate of pro‐senescent cell.

CSEN, characterized by irreversible cell cycle arrest and SASP secretion, exhibits a dual role in cancer progression [ 31 ]. Therapy‐induced senescence from genotoxic agents like TMZ causes a therapeutic dilemma as initially growth suppression is induced but senescent cells may promote tumor recurrence through immunosuppression and pro‐inflammatory microenvironments [ 32 , 33 , 34 ]. Our findings position AGK as a critical modulator of this balance, and its knockdown amplifies TMZ‐induced CSEN via mitochondrial dysfunction (Figure 4 ). Mechanistically, AGK knockdown disrupts mitochondrial membrane potential, and increases the production of mtROS, which is a known driving factor of CSEN [ 35 , 36 ]. As a key switch, AGK‐mitochondrial axis tilts the balance between senescence and apoptosis under TMZ toxicity. Preserving mitochondrial function via AGK sustains a pro‐apoptotic state and improves TMZ efficacy, whereas its loss triggers a CSEN‐dependent survival pathway leading to chemoresistance. This model provides a rationale for targeting mitochondrial homeostasis or eliminating senescent cells as a strategy to overcome TMZ resistance in GBM.

In principle, targeting senescent cells through either elimination (senolytic therapy) or suppression of the SASP (senomorphic therapy, e.g., via inhibition of NF‐κB, mTOR, or p38 pathways) represents a promising therapeutic strategy for tumors [ 37 ]. Senolytics primarily target anti‐apoptotic pathways in senescent cells, with multiple agents demonstrating preclinical efficacy in cancer therapy [ 38 ]. For instance, panobinostat, a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, induces death of senescent cell in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and head/neck squamous cell carcinoma treated with cisplatin or paclitaxel [ 39 ]. Similarly, navitoclax and ABT‐737 (BCL‐2 family inhibitors targeting BCL‐2, BCL‐XL, and BCL‐W) selectively eliminate the senescent cells of ovarian and breast cancer in PARP inhibitor‐treated mouse xenografts or radiation‐damaged lung tissues [ 40 , 41 ]. FOXO4‐DRI, a peptide disrupting the interactions between FOXO4 and p53, promotes apoptosis of senescent cells in a model of NSCLC treated by radiotherapy both in vitro and in vivo [ 42 ]. In GBM, preclinical studies showed that chemotherapy‐induced senescent cells evade senescence via p53/BCL‐XL inhibition, and systemic navitoclax treatment prolongs the survival time of GBM xenografts by clearing senescent cells [ 43 ]. Our findings align with these reports: FOXO4‐DRI synergizes with TMZ, particularly in AGK suppressed cells, by overcoming senescence‐associated apoptosis resistance. The aforementioned effects of FOXO4 DRI offer a novel potential option for the selection of clinical GBM‐specific senolytic agents. This aligns with the emerging strategies of combining pro‐senescence therapies with subsequent senolytic clearance ‐ the “one‐two punch” approach.

CSEN represents a stress response induced by intrinsic and extrinsic insults, including oncogenic activation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, irradiation, or chemotherapeutic agents [ 44 ]. The regulatory mechanism of SASP secretion includes DNA damage response, mitochondrial dysfunction, p38 MAP kinase activity, cGAS/STING, mTOR, or NOTCH signaling pathways [ 17 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 ]. The AMPK/mTOR axis further contributes to senescence regulation, and enhanced AMPK phosphorylation or reduced mTOR phosphorylation may relieve the process of CSEN [ 49 ]. As a conserved energy sensor responsive to AMP/ADP: ATP ratios, AMPK maintains metabolic homeostasis through elevating AMP/ADP levels and activates AMPK signaling to restore energy balance [ 50 ]. Investigating the role of AMPK/mTOR in CSEN, we observed that AGK knockdown significantly suppressed AMPKα phosphorylation, while it did not affect PTEN and AKT phosphorylation (data not shown). This AMPK inhibition likely synergizes with the accumulation of mtROS to mediate the anti‐senescent effects of AGK though mechanisms that require further exploration.

Although this study provides novel insights into the role of AGK in modulating TMZ resistance through mitochondrial homeostasis and CSEN, several limitations warrant cautious interpretation of the findings. First, the precise mechanism of AGK suppressing the production of mtROS in GBM remains unclear. Second, the impact of AGK‐modulated senescence on tumor‐immune interactions (e.g., tumor‐associated macrophage recruitment) requires validation in in vivo model. Third, the functional contribution of specific SASP components (e.g., IL‐6 or IL‐8) to TMZ resistance necessitates systematic proteomic characterization. Finally, the critical translational questions, including the blood‐brain barrier penetration efficiency of FOXO4‐DRI and the optimal combination of drug delivery schemes need preclinical pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic evaluation.

In conclusion, our findings establish AGK as a mitochondrial rheostat controlling TMZ sensitivity through CSEN regulation related to mitochondrial damaged. The combinatorial strategy of AGK inhibition (pro‐senescence) followed by FOXO4‐DRI (senolytic clearance) presents a promising therapeutic axis for TMZ‐resistant GBM. Future work should address in more detail the mechanism involved and the interaction with the microenvironment to optimize for clinical translation of this approach.
