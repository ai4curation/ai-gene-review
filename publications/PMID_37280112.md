## Full Text

Abstract

Background

Mitochondrial hyperpolarization achieved by the elevation of mitochondrial quality control (MQC) activity is a hallmark of glioblastoma (GBM). Therefore, targeting the MQC process to disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis should be a promising approach for GBM therapy.

Methods

We used 2-photon fluorescence microscopy, Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting, and confocal microscopy with specific fluorescent dyes to detect the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and mitochondrial structures. Mitophagic flux was measured with mKeima.

Results

MP31, a phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) uORF-translated and mitochondria-localized micropeptide, disrupted the MQC process and inhibited GBM tumorigenesis. Re-expression of MP31 in patient-derived GBM cells induced MMP loss to trigger mitochondrial fission but blocked mitophagic flux, leading to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria in cells, followed by reactive oxygen species production and DNA damage. Mechanistically, MP31 inhibited lysosome function and blocked lysosome fusion with mitophagosomes by competing with V-ATPase A1 for lactate dehydrogenase B (LDHB) binding to induce lysosomal alkalinization. Furthermore, MP31 enhanced the sensitivity of GBM cells to TMZ by suppressing protective mitophay in vitro and in vivo, but showed no side effects on normal human astrocytes or microglia cells (MG).

Conclusions

MP31 disrupts cancerous mitochondrial homeostasis and sensitizes GBM cells to current chemotherapy, without inducing toxicity in normal human astrocytes and MG. MP31 is a promising candidate for GBM treatment.

Keywords: GBM, mitophagy, MP31, MQC, V-ATPase A1

Key Points.

Glioblastoma (GBM) maintains an elevated mitochondrial membrane potential by upregulating mitochondrial quality control (MQC) activity.

MP31 suppresses GBM cells mitophagy by competitively binding LDHB with V-ATPase A1.

MP31 disrupts the GBM cells MQC process and enhances temozolomide sensitivity.

Importance of the Study.

We found that elevated mitochondrial quality control (MQC) activity underlies the sustained hyperpolarized mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) in glioblastoma (GBM) tissues compared with normal tissues. A PTEN uORF-translated micropeptide MP31 inhibited MQC activity by inducing lysosomal alkalinization and blocking lysosome fusion with autophagosomes. We have demonstrated here for the first time that MP31 function as a tumor suppressor by targeting MQC and enhancing the sensitivity of GBM cells to temozolomide in vitro and in vivo. Our results not only explain how GBM cells sustain a relatively high MMP but also provide an ideal candidate for the future clinical treatment of GBM.

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive central nervous system tumor with inevitable rapid recurrence, poor overall survival, and limited clinical therapeutic options. 1 Radiation and temozolomide (TMZ)-based chemotherapy after surgery are the standard clinical therapies for GBM, but the median survival time remains approximately 12–15 months regardless of any treatment. 2 New treatments and therapeutics for GBM are desperately needed. 3

Upstream open reading frames (uORFs) are often cis regulators of downstream main-coding ORFs and are located in the 5’UTRs of eukaryotic mRNAs. 4 Recently, uORFs were suggested to be translated to novel functional uPeptides. 5 , 6 We previously reported that the PTEN uORF-translated mitochondrial micropeptide MP31, whose expression is commonly lost in GBM cells, can function as a tumor suppressor in GBM by limiting lactate oxidation. MP31 is the first bioactive micropeptide identified to have a function independent of its host gene, PTEN. 5 Studies of uPeptides are rare, and more attention should be paid to these fields.

Mitochondrial fission and subsequent mitophagy constitute the key steps of MQC. 7 Many cancers, including GBM, exhibit higher mitochondrial fission activity and upregulated mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). 8 , 9 However, the role of mitochondrial fission in cancer is context-dependent. 9–11 Mitophagy is a process for the selective elimination of damaged and dysfunctional mitochondria, cut off by mitochondrial fission, when cells undergo stressed or adverse conditions. 12 , 13 In mammalian cells, mitophagy is regulated mainly by 3 pathways. 14–18 The best-studied mitophagy pathway is the PINK1/Parkin pathway. PINK1 is degraded in healthy mitochondria and stabilized on the outer mitochondrial membrane when mitochondria are depolarized or damaged. Following recruitment of the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin, impaired mitochondria undergo autophagic degradation. 19 Mitophagy may be a double-edged sword in cancer. As a key step in MQC, mitophagy removes damaged mitochondria in an effective and timely manner, which may result in drug resistance. 20–23 On the other hand, excessive mitophagy activity can lead to reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, apoptosis, and mitophagic cell death in cancer. 24 Nevertheless, it is generally believed that blocking chemical- or stress-induced mitophagic activity is detrimental to GBM cells, and it is therefore a promising strategy for improving the therapeutic efficacy of chemical compounds in GBM. 21

Here, we uncovered that GBM tissues maintain higher MQC activity to sustain higher MMP than paired normal tissues. Re-expression of MP31 in GBM cells induced MMP loss and tumor inhibition in vitro and in vivo by inducing lysosomal alkalinization and blocking lysosome fusion with autophagosomes, which suppressed mitophagic flux to induce ROS production and DNA damage accumulation. Furthermore, MP31 enhanced the sensitivity of GBM cells to TMZ by suppressing protective mitophagy both in vitro and in vivo but showed no additional side effects on normal human astrocytes (NHAs) or MG.

Materials and Methods

Cell Lines and Cell Culture

Patient-derived primary MES28 and GSC23 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with B-27, EGF, and FGF. HEK-293T cells (ATCC, CRL-11268) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. HMC3 cells (ATCC, CRL-3304) were cultured in MEM supplemented with 10% FBS. NHA cells (Lonza, CC-2565) were cultured using the AGM Bullet Kit™ (Lonza, CC-3186).

Human Samples

All paired GBM samples were acquired from the Department of Neurosurgery of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University with informed consent. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (application ID: 2020-322).

Animal Experiments

Four-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were implanted intracranially with 10 000 patient-derived primary GBM cells. Daily intraperitoneal injections of TMZ (20 mg/kg) were administered beginning on day 5 for 15 days after implantation. All experiments were approved by the IEC of Clinical Research and Animal Trials of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (application no. [2021]588).

Stable Cell Line Generation

MES28 and GSC23 cells were infected with the indicated lentivirus and selected with puromycin. Cell lines with stable MP31 KO were generated as described previously. 5

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay

Cells and tissues were incubated with 100 nM TMRM or 100 nM MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos (MT Red; Invitrogen, M7512) for 30 minutes and imaged with a 2-photon laser-scanning microscope (LEICA, TCS SP8 DIVE) for fresh tissue and confocal microscopy (Zeiss, LSM 880) for cells.

Immunofluorescence (IF) Analysis

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeated with 0.25% Triton X-100, blocked with 3% BSA in PBS, and stained with appropriate primary and secondary antibodies.

Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting

Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) was performed with an Invitrogen Attune NxT Flow Cytometer. For TMRM and MitoTracker Red staining, positive populations were gated against unstained cells. For mitochondrial function analysis, the population of cells with dysfunctional mitochondria was gated by MitoTracker Green high and MitoTracker Deep Red low staining.

Mitochondrial Morphology

Mitochondrial morphology was assayed by transmission electron microscopy or IF staining with MitoTracker Deep Red or MitoTracker Green.

Assessment of Lysosomal Function

Lysosomal function was mainly assessed according to the red/green ratio of acridine orange (AO) and the Em 440 nm/Em 540 nm ratio of LysoSensor Yellow/Blue.

Immunoprecipitation (IP)

Cells were lysed in co-IP RIPA buffer and incubated with the indicated primary antibodies at 4°C on a rotator overnight and with protein A/G agarose for 2 hours at room temperature. The collected agarose–protein complexes were centrifuged, washed, and subjected to SDS‒PAGE.

Reactive Oxygen Species Assays

Cellular and mitochondrial ROS were detected by staining with 5 μM DCFH-DA or 2 μM MitoSOX for 30 minutes and 2 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 for 10 minutes and then assessed by fluorescence microscopy.

Neurosphere Formation Assay

Equal amounts of cells from the indicated groups were plated on 96-well plates for 7 days after plating. Relative sphere sizes were calculated accordingly.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

A 1.0 T small animal MRI scanner (Aspect M3, Aspect Imaging) was used to acquire axial T1-weighted images of tumor-bearing mice after intraperitoneal injection of Gd-DTPA (Bayer Healthcare).

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

The normalized fluorescence intensity of the microscopic images was quantified by ImageJ. The FACS data were analyzed using FlowJo. Statistical tests were conducted using GraphPad Prism. The data are presented as the mean ± SD of 3 independent experiments. P < .05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. For comparisons of parametric data from paired GBM samples, the paired 2-tailed Student’s t -test was used. For others, the unpaired 2-tailed Student’s t -test or one-way ANOVA was used. Overall survival curves were assessed with the Kaplan − Meier method and compared by log-rank test.

More detailed methods are described in Supplementary Material.

GBM Maintains Elevated MMP by Upregulating MQC Activity

Previous reports showed that freshly isolated GBM tissues had higher MMP than noncancerous brain tissues. 8 We found that in a 40-patient cohort, MMP was greatly elevated in freshly isolated GBM tissues compared with paired normal tissues, as indicated by representative images from 2-photon fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry cell sorting (FACS) of the MMP indicator TMRM/MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos (MT Red) ( Figures 1A , B , Supplementary Figure S1A, B ). The MMP was also elevated in patient-derived MES28 and GSC23 GBM cells compared with NHAs ( Figures 1C , Supplementary Figure S1C, D ). MMP plays a key role in MQC and mitochondrial homeostasis through selective elimination of dysfunctional mitochondria and is also a driving force for the transport of ions and proteins that are necessary for healthy mitochondrial function. 25 Thus, we speculated that GBM tissues would exhibit MQC activity distinct from that of paired normal tissues. Indeed, representative immunofluorescence (IF) analysis of the mitochondrial fission marker p-DRP1(S616) 13 indicated that mitochondrial fission activity was greatly activated in GBM tissues and in patient-derived GBM cells compared with paired normal tissues and NHAs, respectively 9 ( Figure 1D , E ). The elevation of p-DRP1(S616) and FIS1, a peripheral mitochondria fission marker, in GBM tissue and in patient-derived GBM cells was also confirmed by immunoblotting (IB) ( Supplementary Figure S1E, F ). Under the governance of MQC, damaged mitochondria cleaved by mitochondrial fission are then degraded by mitophagy. Damaged mitochondria are labeled by PINK1 and Parkin on the outer membrane, recognized by SQSTM1, and encircled by phagophores to form mitophagosomes, whose formation requires the participation of ATG proteins. The mitophagosome then fuses with the lysosome, an acidic organelle containing a wide variety of acid-sensitive hydrolytic enzymes, and the outer membrane, marked by LAMP1, 26 to form the mitolysosome, where the damaged mitochondrion is degraded and recycled 27 ( Figure 1F ). We next evaluated the mitophagy markers and found that mitophagy activity in both GBM tissues and patient-derived primary GBM cells was greatly enhanced compared to that in paired normal brain tissues and NHAs, respectively, as indicated by the upregulated expression of Parkin, PINK1, ATG5, and LAMP1, the classic markers of different stages in mitophagy ( Figure 1G ). The above results clearly showed that MQC activity in GBM is elevated to maintain a higher MMP.

MP31 Induced MMP Loss to Promote Mitochondrial Fission

MP31, usually lost in GBM ( Supplementary Figure S2A, S2B ), is a mitochondria-located micropeptide encoded by the 5’UTR of PTEN, as we previously reported. 5 Re-expression of MP31 in patient-derived primary MES28 and GSC23 GBM cells led to lower MMP than that measured in control cells, as indicated by representative IF images TMRM ( Figure 2A upper and middle, 2B left and middle). NHAs with MP31 knockout (KO) exhibited elevated MMP compared with wild-type (wt) NHAs ( Figure 2A lower, 2B right). A plot of TMRM FACS also showed that patient-derived primary MES28 and GSC23 GBM cells with MP31 re-expression displayed reduced TMRM fluorescence intensity ( Supplementary Figure S2C, S2D , left and middle). In contrast, MP31 KO NHAs exhibited elevated TMRM MRM fluorescence intensity compared with wt NHAs ( Supplementary Figure S2C, S2D , right). In addition, re-expression of MP31 in NHA-KO cells downregulated the MMP and rescued the phenotype of elevated MMP by MP31-KO, as indicated by representative images and a plot of TMRM FACS ( Figure S2E-H ). Given the mitochondrial localization of MP31, we tested the role of MP31 in regulating mitochondrial activity by using 2 mitochondrial-specific dyes, MitoTracker Deep Red (active mitochondria) and MitoTracker Green (total mitochondria). FACS plots and representative IF images of MP31-re-expressing MES28 and GSC23 cells stained with MitoTracker Deep Red and MitoTracker Green showed reduced active mitochondria (MT Deep Red) compared with those in control cells ( Figure 2C upper and middle, S2I left and middle). In contrast, MP31 KO NHA cells exhibited elevated mitochondrial activity compared with wt NHA cells ( Figure 2C lower, S2I right). These data indicated that re-expression of MP31 induced MMP loss and suppressed mitochondrial activity in patient-derived primary GBM cells.

Figure 2.

To explore whether MP31-induced MMP loss may engage in feedback regulation of mitochondrial dynamics, we next performed IF in patient-derived primary GBM cells re-expressing MP31-GFP or control vector. MitoTracker Deep Red staining showed that patient-derived primary GBM cells stably expressing MP31-GFP exhibited shorter, more fragmented, and more swollen mitochondria than control cells, supporting the hypothesis that re-expression of MP31 in patient-derived primary GBM cells may trigger mitochondrial fission ( Figure 2D ). Mitochondrial shape was classified into 3 categories: Tubular, intermediate, and fragmented. 28 We calculated the proportion of each category in MES28 and GSC23 cells stably expressing MP31 and found that MP31 re-expressing GBM cells displayed more fragmented and less tubular mitochondria than the corresponding control cells ( Figure 2E ). IB showed elevated expression of the mitochondrial fission marker p-DRP1(S616) in MP31 re-expressing MES28 and GSC23 cells and repressed expression of p-DRP1(S616) in MP31 KO NHAs compared with control MES28 and GSC23 cells or wt NHAs ( Figure 2F ). The above results indicated that MP31-induced MMP loss triggered mitochondrial fission.

MP31 Blocked Mitophagic Flux in GBM Cells

To sustain mitochondrial homeostasis in the case of MMP loss, cancer cells cleave inactive mitochondria by mitochondrial fission for mitophagic degradation. We next explored mitophagy activity in patient-derived primary GBM cells re-expressing MP31. First, transmission electron microscopy was used to monitor the morphology of mitochondria, and we found that patient-derived primary GBM cells re-expressing MP31 exhibited short, fragmented, and swollen mitochondria ( Supplementary Figure S2J left and middle). However, MP31 KO NHA cells exhibited relatively longer mitochondria than wt NHA cells ( Supplementary Figure S2J right). Damaged mitochondria are engulfed by lysosomes for degradation in control GBM cells. Notably, most of the damaged mitochondria in GBM cells re-expressing MP31 were not engulfed by lysosomes for mitophagic degradation ( Supplementary Figure S2J ). Second, mKeima, a mitochondrion-located and pH-sensitive fluorescent protein, was introduced to study mitophagic flux. In a neutral environment, a short excitation wavelength (440 nm) is predominant, whereas, in an acidic environment, a long wavelength (586 nm) is predominant. As shown in Figure 3A and 3B , the red/green fluorescence ratio was slightly downregulated in patient-derived primary MES28 and GSC23 cells stably expressing MP31. Moreover, CCCP, which induces mitophagy, did not enhance the red (550 nm)/green (440 nm) fluorescence ratio upon the expression of MP31 in MES28 and GSC23 GBM cells. These data suggested that MP31 suppressed both basal and CCCP-induced mitophagic flux in patient-derived primary GBM cells. In contrast, the red (550 nm)/green (440 nm) fluorescence ratio was increased in MP31 KO NHA cells compared with wt NHA cells, with further elevation when CCCP was added ( Supplementary Figure S3A and S3B ). As mitophagy is a selective type of autophagy, MP31-re-expressing patient-derived primary GBM cells also showed blocked autophagic flux, as indicated by an enhanced LC3B-II/LC3B-I ratio and elevated expression of the autophagy substrate SQSTM1 ( Supplementary Figure S3C ).

Figure 3.

The successful progression of mitophagic flux includes mitophagosome formation, fusion of mitophagosomes with lysosomes and degradation. 19 We next proceeded to clarify the precise stage in which MP31 participated in blocking mitophagic flux. IB of the mitophagy markers PINK1 and Parkin in subcellular fractions indicated that MP31 induced PINK1 and Parkin accumulation in the mitochondrial fraction of MES28 and GSC23 cells ( Figure 3C ). Notably, NHA cells with MP31 KO also showed enhanced PINK1 and Parkin expression in the mitochondrial fraction compared with wt NHA cells ( Supplementary Figure S3D ). The reason for these contradictory results may be as follows: For GBM cells, the elevated expression of PINK1 and Parkin was the consequence of mitophagy initiation triggered by MP31-induced MMP loss; for NHA cells, the elevated expression of PINK1 and Parkin resulted directly from MP31 KO-induced increases in MMP and MQC activity. These data suggested that the role of MP31in blocking mitophagic flux does not act at the step of mitophagy initiation.

Therefore, we next tested the role of MP31 in later steps. Coimmunofluorescence of the mitochondrial marker TOMM20 and the autophagy marker LC3B was performed to monitor the fusion of mitochondria with autophagosomes. MES28 and GSC23 cells with MP31 re-expression showed greater colocalization of mitochondria with the autophagosome than the control cells, indicating that MP31 enhanced the engulfment of damaged mitochondria by autophagosomes ( Supplementary Figure S3E ). However, when coimmunofluorescence analysis of the mitochondrial marker TOMM20 and lysosome marker LAMP1 was performed to monitor the fusion of mitophagosomes and lysosomes, we found that MES28 and GSC23 patient-derived primary GBM cells with stable MP31 expression showed less colocalization of mitochondria and lysosomes ( Figure 3D ). Pearson’s correlation analysis of LAMP1 with TOMM20 also suggested that MP31 suppressed the fusion of mitochondria with lysosomes ( Figure 3E ). In contrast, upon CCCP treatment, both the colocalization of mitochondria with autophagosomes and the fusion of mitophagosomes with lysosomes were enhanced in MP31 KO NHA cells compared with wt NHA cells ( Supplementary Figure S3F-H ). These results indicated that MP31 blocks the fusion of mitophagosomes with lysosomes.

MP31 Suppressed Lysosome Function and Induced Lysosomal Alkalinization by Competitively Binding LDHB With V-ATPase A1

As lysosomes contain a wide variety of acid-sensitive hydrolytic enzymes, the normal function of lysosomes is pH-dependent. 29 Lysosome activity was measured with AO and the pH-sensitive probe LysoSensor Yellow/Blue DND-160. AO shows green fluorescence when bound to DNA and red fluorescence when present in acidic compartments, including lysosomes; thus, it can be used as an indicator of lysosome activity. 30 The downregulated AO red/AO green ratio indicated that the lysosome pH was elevated in MES28 and GSC23 patient-derived primary GBM cells with stable MP31 expression ( Figure 4A , 4B ), and MP31 KO NHAs showed reduced lysosome pH in both the presence and absence of the lysosome activity inhibitor bafilomycin A1 ( Figure 4C , 4D ). These results suggested that MP31 also inhibits lysosome activity. We also noticed that the 440 nm to 540 nm emission fluorescence ratio of LysoSensor Yellow/Blue DND-160 was upregulated in MES28 and GSC23 cells with stable MP31 expression compared with the control cells ( Figure 4E left and middle), which was additional evidence to support the elevation of lysosome pH and suppression of lysosome activity. In contrast, the 440 nm to 540 nm emission fluorescence ratio and lysosomal pH were slightly decreased in MP31 KO NHAs ( Figure 4E right). Collectively, the above results indicated that MP31 could block mitophagic flux by inducing lysosomal alkalinization and blocking the fusion of lysosomes with mitophagosomes.

Figure 4.

To investigate the precise molecular mechanism by which MP31 suppressed mitophagy, deep RNA sequencing of MP31 re-expressing and controlling GBM cells was performed. However, none of the genes with altered expression had previously been reported as key regulators that govern the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes or lysosome acidification. We also tested the protein expression of key regulators governing the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes by IB, and none of the selected proteins exhibited a difference in expression between patient-derived primary MES28 and GSC23 cells with MP31 re-expression ( Supplementary Figure S4A ). These results indicated that MP31 did not directly inhibit the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes. Lysosomal alkalinization can induce lysosomal dysfunction, which has been reported to result in the disturbance of autophagosome–lysosome fusion and a blockade of mitophagic flux. 29 Thus, MP31 may induce lysosomal dysfunction and ultimately suppress mitophagosome–lysosome fusion.

Lysosome acidification is critical for the cleavage and activation of the cysteine protease cathepsins family (CTSB and CTSD). We tested the maturation of cathepsins B and D and their activity in patient-derived GBM cells and NHAs. MP31-induced lysosome pH elevation suppressed the cleavage of pro-CTSB and pro-CTSD into mature CTSB and CTSD, which eventually inhibited the enzyme activity of CTSB and CTSD in patient-derived GBM cells compared with control cells ( Figure 4F left and middle). Conversely, MP31 KO NHA cells exhibited increased cleavage of pro-CTSB and pro-CTSD into mature CTSB and CTSD and enhanced enzyme activity of CTSB and CTSD ( Figure 4F right). Furthermore, a CTSB activity assay also showed that MP31 could negatively regulate CTSB activity in patient-derived GBM cells ( Figure 4G ). V-ATPase, a ubiquitously expressed multisubunit enzyme that is present in endomembrane organelles such as lysosomes, is necessary for protein sorting, zymogen activation, receptor-mediated endocytosis, and synaptic vesicle proton gradient generation. 31 V-ATPase translocates 2 protons for each ATP hydrolyzed and is the major contributor to lysosomal acidification. 32 Therefore, we next performed IB to detect the protein level of V-ATPase A1. V-ATPase A1 protein levels were not changed in patient-derived primary MES28 and GSC23 GBM cells with MP31 re-expression or in MP31 KO NHAs compared with the control cells ( Figure 4H upper). We reported that MP31 restricted lactate oxidation in GBM cells by interacting with mitochondrial LDHB. 5 LDHB also yields protons that fuel lysosomal V-ATPase A1 by lactate oxidation and promote V-ATPase-dependent lysosomal acidification, facilitated by a close interaction between LDHB and V-ATPase A1 at the lysosomal surface. 33 Given that total LDHB levels were also not changed, we speculated that MP31 competes with V-ATPase A1 for LDHB binding to induce lysosome alkalinization. Immunoprecipitation (IP) with a V-ATPase A1 antibody pulled down less LDHB protein in patient-derived GBM cells with MP31 re-expression than in control cells ( Figure 4H ). On the other hand, the V-ATPase A1 antibody precipitated more LDHB in the MP31 KO NHA cells than in the wt NHA cells. The same results were obtained when using LDHB antibody for IP ( Figure 4H ). The above data suggested that mitochondrial MP31 could inhibit lysosomal activity by competing with V-ATPase A1 for LDHB binding and reducing protons to fuel lysosomal V-ATPase A1 by restricting lactate oxidation.

MP31 Induced ROS Accumulation and DNA Damage in Patient-Derived Primary GBM Cells

MP31 blocks mitophagic flux; thus, impaired mitochondria may accumulate in cells and induce ROS production. The ROS probe DCFH-DA was used to monitor the ROS level in cells by IF and FACS assays. MES28 and GSC23 cells with MP31 re-expression exhibited enhanced DCF fluorescence intensity compared with control cells, as indicated by representative IF images ( Figure 5A , 5B ). However, in MP31-re-expressing NHAs and HMC3 cells (a microglia cell line), no obvious change in DCF intensity was observed, CCCP treated cells were used as positive control of ROS induction ( Figure 5C , 5D , Supplementary Figure S5A, S5B ). As MP31 is a mitochondrion-localized micropeptide, we wondered whether it may also induce mitochondria-specific ROS. Mitochondrial ROS were detected by using the specific probe MitoSOX Red, a mitochondrial superoxide indicator designed for the highly selective detection of superoxide in the mitochondria. As shown in Supplementary Figure S5C and S5D , MP31 also induced mitochondrial ROS accumulation in patient-derived primary MES28 and GSC23 GBM cells. However, MP31 cannot induce mitochondrial ROS in NHAs or HMC3 cells ( Supplementary Figure S5E-H ). These different effects of MP31 in patient-derived GBM cells and normal cell lines suggest that the toxicity of MP31 could only be restricted to GBM cells. The reason may lie in that the blockade of MQC activity in normal cell lines were saturated by endogenous MP31.

Figure 5.

Additionally, autophagy plays important roles in DNA damage repair by recycling key proteins involved in the processing of DNA damage repair and in providing metabolic precursors for the generation of ATP, which are employed in several steps of DNA repair. 34 We next validated the role of MP31 in the DNA damage response. Representative IF images of MP31-re-expressing MES28 and GSC23 cells revealed a significant increase in dots composed of the DNA damage response marker γ-H2Ax in the nucleus ( Figure 5E , F ). The upregulation of γ-H2Ax was further validated by IB in modified patient-derived GBM cells ( Figure 5G ). In contrast, overexpression of MP31 in NHAs or HA1800 cells did not obviously alter DNA damage in the presence or absence of TMZ, as indicated by IF and IB of the DNA damage response marker γ-H2Ax ( Figure 5H-J and Supplementary Figure S5I-K ). These data implied that MP31 treatment did not enhance the toxicity of TMZ in normal astrocytes or microglia cells

MP31 Enhanced the Sensitivity of Patient-Derived Primary GBM Cells to TMZ In Vitro and In Vivo

TMZ is a first-line oral chemotherapeutic agent for GBM treatment, but TMZ resistance induced by protective autophagy and mitophagy is commonly observed. 35 Preclinical studies have shown that the pharmacological inhibition of autophagy by hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) enhances the therapeutic effect of TMZ. 36–38 Here, we compared the effects of MP31, TMZ, and their combination on GBM. We found that TMZ treatment markedly induced protective autophagy, as evidenced by enhanced LC3B lipidation (LC3B II/LCB I ratio) and reduced levels of the autophagy substrate SQSTM1 in patient-derived primary GBM cells ( Supplementary Figure S6A ). However, basal and TMZ-induced autophagic activity was markedly attenuated by MP31 re-expression ( Supplementary Figure S6A ). Neurosphere formation assays revealed that MP31 repressed the ability of patient-derived GBM cells to form spheres and significantly increased the sensitivity of patient-derived GBM cells to 200 µM or 400 µM TMZ ( Figure 6A , 6B ). These results supported the synergistic inhibitory effect of the combination of MP31 and TMZ on proliferation in patient-derived GBM cells in vitro. Next, we examined the regulatory effect of MP31 on TMZ treatment in patient-derived GBM cell xenografts. Patient-derived MES28 and GSC23 cells stably expressing a control vector or MP31 were implanted into the striatum of nude mice by stereotactic intracranial injection, and the implanted mice were then treated with TMZ (20 mg/kg) or DMSO for 15 consecutive days beginning at day 5 post-implantation ( Figure 6C ). MP31 re-expression also enhanced the sensitivity of the patient-derived GBM cells to TMZ in vivo, as indicated by representative MRI and H&E images of brain tissues ( Figure 6D ). Additionally, MP31 re-expression significantly prolonged the survival of nude mice ( Figure 6E ). Next, mouse brain tumor tissues were immunohistochemically stained for Parkin, PINK1, γ-H2Ax, and SQSTM1. Parkin, PINK1, and γ-H2Ax staining were readily induced in the TMZ or MP31 group and significantly upregulated in the MP31 plus TMZ group, while SQSTM1 staining was downregulated in the TMZ-treated group but upregulated in the MP31 group and significantly upregulated in the MP31 plus TMZ group ( Figure 6F ). Collectively, our findings revealed that MP31 inhibited mitophagic flux and enhanced the sensitivity of patient-derived GBM cells to TMZ in vitro and in vivo, highlighting the future clinical potential of MP31.

Discussion

In this study, we describe a PTEN uORF-translated and mitochondrion-located micropeptide, MP31, whose expression is often lost in GBM and disrupts the MQC process by blocking mitophagic flux. Specifically, MP31 blocks mitophagic flux by inducing lysosomal alkalinization and suppressing its fusion with autophagosomes. MP31-mediated MQC blockade induced ROS production and DNA damage accumulation and enhanced TMZ sensitivity in GBM in vitro and in vivo. Our results revealed that in addition to limiting lactate oxidation, MP31 could also function as a tumor suppressor by suppressing mitophagic flux and disrupting the MQC process in GBM.

Cancer cells have hyperpolarized MMP compared with normal cells, which makes MMP an ideal selective target for cancer treatment. 8 Some researchers have suggested that the difference in MMP between cancer and normal cells may be caused by a difference in the metabolic preferences of the cells. 39 However, the precise mechanism underlying this difference remains unknown. 39 , 40 We found that GBM elevated MQC activity to maintain a higher MMP. Therefore, targeting MQC activity to break MPP homeostasis should be a promising approach for GBM therapy.

MQC involves the cooperation of mitochondrial fission and mitophagy to sustain mitochondrial homeostasis. The role of mitochondrial fission in cancer is controversial and should be considered differently in different contexts. For example, in triple-negative breast cancer, mitochondrial fission inhibits signaling pathways associated with cancer progression and metastasis. 41 In liver cancer, mitochondrial fission can enhance stemness and tumor-initiating capability. 42 In glioma, inhibition of mitochondrial fission activity resulted in the loss of stemness and suppressed tumor-formation capacity. 9 Here, we found that MP31-induced MMP loss can trigger mitochondrial fission but suppress GBM progression. We speculated that MMP loss induced by MP31 re-expression caused excessive mitochondrial fission but blocked mitophagic flux, which disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis and was detrimental to GBM cells. We previously reported that MP31 suppressed lactate oxidation in GBM by binding LDHB. 5 Others have reported that lactate can support lysosomal acidification and autophagy in cancer and that LDHB can oxidize lactate and yield protons to fuel lysosomal V-ATPase A1. 33 Additionally, the inhibition of internal acidification in acidic organelles disturbs autophagosome–lysosome fusion, which blocks autophagic flux. 29 Collectively, our results indicated that MP31 inhibited lysosome function and blocked lysosome fusion with mitophagosomes by competing with V-ATPase A1 for LDHB binding to induce lysosomal alkalinization. These results indicate that MP31 has dual functions in disrupting mitochondrial homeostasis. On the one hand, MP31 re-expression-induced MMP loss produced more damaged mitochondria in GBM cells; on the other hand, MP31-induced lysosomal alkalinization blocked mitochondrial degradation and recycling. Under these conditions, the excess damaged mitochondria in GBM cells could not be degraded, due to the diminished mitophagy process, and instead accumulated, which was detrimental to GBM progression. This made MP31 more effective than other single-target agents in targeting MQC.

Radiation therapy concomitant with TMZ followed by maintenance TMZ has been established as the standard care for patients with newly diagnosed GBM. 2 In glioma cells, autophagy is an adaptive response to both radiation and TMZ, 43 suggesting that targeting autophagy could have therapeutic benefits. Blocking basal or chemically induced mitophagic flux by the chemical compound bafilomycin A1 or CQ could also significantly enhance the sensitivity of cancer cells to chemotherapy or radiotherapy. 44 Preclinical studies have shown that pharmacological inhibition of autophagy by HCQ enhanced the therapeutic effect of TMZ. 36 However, in a phase I/II trial, autophagy inhibition was not consistently achieved in patients treated with low doses of HCQ, and no significant improvement in overall survival was observed; dose-limiting toxicity prevented escalation to higher doses of HCQ. 45 Additionally, HCQ failed to improve survival compared to radiotherapy alone in elderly patients with GBM in a randomized phase II clinical trial due to an excess of deaths. 46 Therefore, safer and more specific agents are needed for targeted mitophagic flux treatment for GBM in the clinic. MP31 is an endogenously expressed micropeptide that can cross the blood‒brain barrier, and it is detrimental to patient-derived primary GBM cells but shows no toxicity to NHAs. Moreover, MP31 enhanced the chemosensitivity of GBM to TMZ in vitro and in vivo by suppressing chemical adaptive autophagy. Thus, MP31 is a promising candidate for GBM treatment clinically in terms of both safety and efficacy. A limitation of this study is that we only used female mice for all animal experiments in this manuscript for animal manipulation consistency and result stability considerations. However, as GBM seems to have a preponderance in males, we should repeat the animal studies in male mice in the future.

In summary, we uncovered that elevated MQC activity underlies the sustained hyperpolarization of MMP in GBM tissues compared with normal tissues. We have demonstrated here for the first time that MP31 can function as a tumor suppressor by targeting MQC and enhancing the sensitivity of GBM cells to TMZ in vitro and in vivo. Our results not only explain how GBM cells sustain a relatively high MMP but also provide an ideal new nontoxic candidate for future GBM clinical trials.
