Lipoprotein lipase (LPL) carries out the lipolytic processing of
triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRL) along the luminal surface of capillaries.
LPL activity is regulated by the angiopoietin-like proteins (ANGPTL3, ANGPTL4,
ANGPTL8), which control the delivery of TRL-derived lipid nutrients to tissues
in a temporal and spatial fashion. This regulation of LPL mediates the
partitioning of lipid delivery to adipose tissue and striated muscle according
to nutritional status. A complex between ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL8 (ANGPTL3/8)
inhibits LPL activity in oxidative tissues, but its mode of action has remained
unknown. Here, we used biophysical techniques to define how ANGPTL3/8 and
ANGPTL3 interact with LPL and how they drive LPL inactivation. We demonstrate,
by mass photometry, that ANGPTL3/8 is a heterotrimer with a 2:1 ANGPTL3:ANGPTL8
stoichiometry and that ANGPTL3 is a homotrimer. Hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass
spectrometry (HDX-MS) studies revealed that ANGPTL3/8 and ANGPTL3 use the
proximal portion of their N-terminal α-helices to interact with sequences
surrounding the catalytic pocket in LPL. That binding event triggers unfolding
of LPL's α/β-hydrolase domain and irreversible loss of LPL catalytic activity.
The binding of LPL to its endothelial transporter protein (GPIHBP1) or to
heparan-sulfate proteoglycans protects LPL from unfolding and inactivation,
particularly against the unfolding triggered by ANGPTL3. Pulse-labeling HDX-MS
studies revealed that ANGPTL3/8 and ANGPTL3 catalyze LPL unfolding in an
ATP-independent fashion, which categorizes these LPL inhibitors as atypical
unfoldases. The catalytic nature of LPL unfolding by ANGPTL3/8 explains why low
plasma concentrations of ANGPTL3/8 are effective in inhibiting a molar excess of
LPL in capillaries.

## Full Text

Adapting energy metabolism to prolonged periods of famine with caloric deficits alternating with short periods of caloric surplus was fundamental for the survival of early humans. This adaptation included the evolution of a sophisticated system for regulating the intravascular processing of dietary lipids (1, 2). Key to this regulation is the dynamic partitioning and processing of the lipids in triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs) between storage and metabolic tissues (3–5). Energy-rich triglycerides in the core of TRLs are hydrolyzed along capillary endothelial surfaces by lipoprotein lipase (LPL). The free fatty acids (FFAs) are then taken up either by the heart or skeletal muscles to fuel oxidative metabolism or by adipocytes to replenish triglyceride stores after re-esterification.

In mammals, LPL is synthesized largely by myocytes and adipocytes and is then shuttled by dedicated transport proteins to its site of action in the lumen of immediately adjacent capillaries. Intracellular sorting of LPL from the trans-Golgi network to secretory vesicles is assisted by syndecan-1 (6), whereas trans-endothelial transport to the capillary lumen is mediated by glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored high-density lipoprotein–binding protein 1 (GPIHBP1) (7). Because LPL is metastable at body temperature with an apparent melting temperature (Tm) of only 34.8 °C (8), it requires stabilization throughout its itinerary. Conformational stability of LPL is conferred by stretches of polyanionic regions in both shuttling proteins—heparan sulfates in the case of syndecan-1 (6) and an intrinsically disordered N-terminal acidic tail in the case of GPIHBP1 (9, 10). The polyelectrolyte interactions between LPL and the disordered acidic tail in GPIHBP1 have important impacts on LPL function and intravascular lipolysis. First, those interactions increase the thermostability of LPL’s hydrolase domain by >20 °C (8, 11). Second, the interactions increase the association rate constant for LPL•GPIHBP1 interactions by 2,500-fold (9). Third, they greatly facilitate LPL transport across capillary endothelial cells by providing an electrostatic switch that dislodges LPL from heparan sulfate proteoglycans in the subendothelial spaces by competitive displacement (9, 12, 13). Fourth, the electrostatic interactions of LPL with the acidic domain of GPIHBP1 mitigate LPL inactivation by angiopoietin-like (ANGPTL) protein 4 (8, 14–16).

The nutrition-dependent partitioning of TRL-derived fatty acid delivery between adipose and oxidative tissues depends on tissue-specific regulation of LPL activity by a family of inhibitor proteins—ANGPTL4, ANGPTL3, and ANGPTL8 (1, 2, 5). Fasting triggers increased ANGPTL4 expression in adipose tissue (17), resulting in reduced amounts of LPL within capillaries, reduced TRL processing, and reduced fatty acid delivery to adipocytes. After refeeding, ANGPTL4 expression is reduced in adipose tissue while ANGPTL8 expression is increased in liver and adipose tissue, which increases TRL-derived fatty acid delivery to adipose tissue, thereby replenishing triglyceride stores. This metabolic switch between oxidative and storage lipid delivery depends on ANGPTL8, which exerts a dual and tissue-specific effect on LPL activity (18). In the postprandial state, LPL activity is restored in adipocytes due to an autocrine inhibition of the residual ANGPTL4 activity by ANGPTL8 binding—the resultant ANGPTL4/8 complex has decreased LPL-inhibitory activity compared to ANGPTL4 (19, 20). LPL activity in skeletal muscle and heart is attenuated in an endocrine fashion by the ANGPTL3/8 complex, which is produced by hepatocytes in response to feeding (19–21).

Previous studies investigating mechanism(s) underlying ANGPTL-mediated LPL inhibition focused largely on the inactivation of LPL by ANGPTL4. However, conflicting conclusions from those studies fostered uncertainty as to the actual mechanism of inhibition (10). One line of research, based on enzyme kinetics, proposed that ANGPTL4 is a reversible noncompetitive inhibitor of LPL (22, 23). Another line of research, based on biophysical studies, revealed a different modus operandi for ANGPTL4 inhibition. Unexpectedly, ANGPTL4 was found to catalyze the inhibition of LPL activity by lowering the thermostability of its α/β-hydrolase domain to less than 15 °C (8). This increase in LPL metastability results in irreversible unfolding and collapse of the catalytic pocket within LPL’s α/β-hydrolase domain, leading to a permanent inactivation of LPL activity (8, 14, 24–26).

In the current study, we use biophysical methods to explore how LPL activity is regulated by ANGPTL3/8 and ANGPTL3—the major endocrine inhibitors of LPL. Our studies reveal that ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL3/8 are atypical unfoldases that catalyze unfolding of LPL’s α/β-hydrolase domain. We also show that ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL3/8, when bound to LPL, are equipotent in their ability to induce unfolding of the α/β-hydrolase domain; however, inhibition of LPL activity was greater with ANGPTL3/8 at physiological LPL concentrations and this difference was exacerbated when LPL was tethered to polyanionic binding partners (GPIHBP1 or HSPGs). The latter observation explains why ANGPTL3/8—and not ANGPTL3—is the more physiologically relevant inhibitor of intravascular lipolysis in vivo (19–21, 27).

This relationship is supported by biochemical studies showing that ANGPTL3/8 inhibits LPL activity very efficiently—as efficiently as ANGPTL4, whereas ANGPTL3 is less active (19–21). There is no consensus, however, on the magnitude of the lower inhibitory activity of ANGPTL3. One study reported a mere twofold difference in the inhibitory potencies of the coiled-coil domains of ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL4 (both expressed in Escherichia coli) (23), while another study reported a 100-fold difference (20). Ultimately, these allosteric events trigger collapse of structural elements forming the catalytic pocket, leading to irreversible inactivation of LPL activity (8, 14, 15).Do ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL3/8 Compromise LPL Stability?We previously showed, with nanodifferential scanning fluorimetry (nano-DSF), that LPL metastability (Tm of 34.8 °C) results in spontaneous and irreversible unfolding of LPL’s α/β-hydrolase domain (8). Importantly, the Tm for the α/β-hydrolase domain decreases by >20 °C in the presence of ANGPTL4, while it increases by >20 °C in the presence GPIHBP1 (8). In the current study, we were unable to interpret changes in the Tm for LPL in the presence of ANGPTL3 or ANGPTL3/8 by nano-DSF. This shortcoming was attributed to large changes in fluorescence accompanying unfolding of the tryptophan-rich FLD in ANGPTL3 or ANGPTLT3/8, which at equimolar concentrations masked changes from LPL unfolding. To explore a possible mechanism for LPL inhibition entailing enzyme-catalyzed protein unfolding, we performed a set of pulse-labeling HDX-MS studies. Transitions of LPL into the unfolded state at 25 °C were assessed by bimodality in the isotope envelopes for two overlapping peptic peptides representing the β4–α4–β5 foldon (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). In the absence of inhibitors, spontaneous unfolding of LPL was much lower, reaching only 30% at the latest time points. Consistent with earlier studies (Figs. 1 F and 5 B), 12 µM GPIHBP1 dramatically delayed ANGPTL3- and ANGPTL3/8-driven LPL unfolding (Fig. 6B). In aggregate, these findings show that both ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL3/8 serve as catalysts for the unfolding of the α/β-hydrolase domain and by virtue of that property they can be characterized as atypical unfoldases that drive LPL inactivation by lowering the energy barrier for unfolding. An essential component of our research on LPL regulation by ANGPTL3, ANGPTL4, and ANGPTL3/8 has been analyzing the allosteric effects of those inhibitors on LPL metastability. To extract insights from HDX-MS studies regarding allostery and LPL unfolding, it was important for us to distinguish between deuterium uptake from noncorrelated exchange (local flexibility with unimodal isotope envelopes) and deuterium uptake from correlated exchange (global or local unfolding with bimodal isotope envelopes). By ignoring that distinction, earlier HDX-MS studies failed to recognize how inhibitor-induced allostery alters LPL stability (22, 42, 43). We found that ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL3/8—much like ANGPTL4 (8)—increase the flexibility of structures that anchor LPL’s lid and contribute to the catalytic pocket (α5 and β6–β7). That increased flexibility is accompanied by progressive unfolding of β4–α4–β5. We also present evidence showing that LPL unfolding is catalyzed by ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL3/8. In support of a catalytic mechanism, we showed that substoichiometric amounts of ANGPTL3 or ANGPTL3/8 rapidly unfold an entire population of LPL molecules (Figs. 5 and 6). This ANGPTL-catalyzed unfolding and inactivation of LPL is both time- and temperature-dependent, occurring ~2.5-fold faster at 37 °C than at 25 °C.

Based on these findings, we now classify ANGPTLs 3, 4, and 3/8 as atypical unfoldases that inactivate LPL by catalyzing unfolding of defined regions within its α/β-hydrolase domain. In contrast to archetypical AAA+ unfoldases, which linearize targeted protein substrates in an ATP-dependent process (44), unfolding of LPL by the ANGPTL proteins is region-specific and occurs in an ATP-independent process. We hypothesize that the intrinsic metastability of LPL is the main reason why this catalyzed unfolding can proceed without external energy consumption. Consistent with this proposition, we found that polyanionic binding partners (i.e., GPIHBP1’s acidic tail and HSPGs) that increase the thermostability of LPL inexorably slow the rate of ANGPTL-catalyzed LPL inactivation by heightening the activation barrier for unfolding.

In conclusion, our model, in which LPL is inhibited in a catalytic manner, not only explains how low amounts of ANGPTL3/8 can regulate a higher molar amount of LPL, but it also explains how APOA5 serves to regulate the catalytic unfolding process. Because ANGPT3/8 functions in a catalytic manner (akin to an enzyme) to promote LPL unfolding, the existence of APOA5 as an ANGPTL3/8 inhibitor represents an important regulatory mechanism.

Model for the regulation of LPL activity in the capillary lumen ANGPTL3/8. LPL is tethered to the luminal surface of capillary endothelial cells by binding to GPIHBP1 and HSPGs in the glycocalyx. In this polyanionic microenvironment (orange bar) LPL is stabilized and is less sensitive to inhibition by ANGPTL3 thus shifting its regulation toward ANGPTL3/8 inhibition. The unbalanced concentrations of LPL (8–16 nM) and its inhibitor ANGPTL3/8 (0.1 nM) call for an untraditional mechanism of inhibition—that of permanent LPL inhibition by catalyzed unfolding. Unbridled unfoldase activity of ANGPTL3/8 is controlled by its inhibitor APOA5.

An unexpected finding in our current studies is that ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL3/8 appeared to be equipotent in their unfoldase activities (at the relatively high protein concentrations used in HDX-MS and nano-DSF studies). That observation was puzzling because it seemed inconsistent with data indicating that ANGPTL3/8 (not ANGPTL3) plays a dominant role in regulating plasma triglyceride metabolism in vivo (18, 27–31). The absence of a strong correlation between the unfoldase activities of ANGPTL3 and ANGPTL3/8 in HDX-MS studies and their potencies in vivo likely relate to differences in their affinity for LPL. Binding studies with surface plasmon resonance (SI Appendix, Fig. S15) or NanoBiT (21) as reporter systems demonstrate a higher affinity of LPL for ANGPTL3/8. Furthermore, HDX-MS studies revealed greater protection of the LPL binding interface with ANGPTL3/8 than ANGPTL3, suggesting that the interaction of ANGPTL3/8 with LPL is stronger and has a longer lifetime (Figs. 2 B and C and 4 A).

Another factor contributing to the greater impact of ANGPTL3/8 on triglyceride metabolism in vivo is the greater capacity of ANGPTL3/8 than ANGPTL3 to bind and unfold LPL when it is attached to its polyanionic binding partners. Our studies demonstrated that ANGPTL3/8 is more potent than ANGPTL3 in catalyzing inactivation of both GPIHBP1-bound LPL and HSPG-bound LPL. We suspect that the greater sensitivity of GPIHBP1-bound and HSPG-bound LPL to ANGPTL3/8-catalyzed unfolding is physiologically significant. Recent studies have shown that as much as 30% of the LPL that is transported by GPIHBP1 into capillaries detaches from GPIHBP1 and is captured by the HSPG-rich glycocalyx lining of capillary endothelial cells (48). Thus, there are two pools of LPL attached to capillary endothelial cells by polyanionic binding partners, one bound to GPIHBP1 and another bound to HSPGs, and both are active in the lipolytic processing of TRLs (48). Our current studies revealed that these polyanion-bound LPL pools are more susceptible to ANGPTL3/8 than ANGPTL3 inhibition. Our biophysical observations explain why an inhibitory ANGPTL3/8-specific monoclonal antibody is so effective in preserving intracapillary LPL levels in vivo (49) and explain the remarkable potency of the monoclonal antibody in lowering plasma lipid levels in both mice and humans (35, 50).

Our current study raises intriguing questions about plasma triglyceride metabolism in vertebrate evolution. LPL-mediated intravascular triglyceride metabolism is an ancient process; LPL and key LPL regulators (APOC2, APOC3, APOA5, ANGPTL3, and ANGPTL4) are found in every vertebrate species, but GPIHBP1 expression is found only in mammals. ANGPTL8—and therefore the ANGPTL3/8 complex—is also found exclusively in mammals. In the future, it will be interesting to study whether lower vertebrates regulate intravascular LPL activity by catalyzing LPL unfolding, and if so, whether ANGPTL3 functions as the principal LPL unfoldase and APOA5 functions to regulate ANGPTL3 activity.
