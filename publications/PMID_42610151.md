Light sheet microscopy is a powerful tool for imaging live organisms. To enable
high-speed volumetric imaging, we have developed a light sheet system
incorporating an electrically tunable lens (ETL) capable of capturing volumes up
to 499 × 499 × 150 μm3 at 4 volumes per second with near diffraction-limited
resolution. The system employs sensorless adaptive optics to correct ETL induced
system aberrations, extending the usable field of view by fivefold. We apply
this system to image the propagation of seizures in zebrafish larvae and observe
that seizures originate in the posterior brain, propagate anteriorly toward the
optic tectum and gradually subside over tens of seconds.

Introduction

Rodent seizure models have advanced our understanding of epilepsy, yet the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood, and some forms of the disease remain resistant to treatment. In 2005, Baraban et al. demonstrated that seizures can be induced in zebrafish larvae using the convulsant agent pentylenetetrazole (PTZ), establishing a simple and accessible vertebrate model [1]. Zebrafish offer several advantages for neuroscience research, including small size, optical transparency at larval stages, and powerful genetic tools [2–4]. Light Sheet Fluorescence Microscopy (LSFM) has emerged as a powerful technique for imaging the zebrafish central nervous system (CNS), enabling high-resolution, time-efficient monitoring of neural activity across large brain volumes [5]. However, many studies remain limited to a single two-dimensional (2D) imaging plane, restricting the ability to observe global brain dynamics [4,6–8]. To extend these studies to three-dimensions, several LSFM approaches have been developed to enable fast volumetric imaging [9–11]. These systems typically use a galvo scanner to sweep the light sheet through the sample. To maintain focus, the imaging objective’s focal plane must be synchronized with the scanned light sheet. This can be achieved by various techniques, including piezo scanning of the imaging objective lens [12], remote focusing [13], extended depth of focus [9], and objective focal length tuning with an Electrically Tunable Lens (ETL) [14]. ETLs enable rapid focal adjustment without mechanical movement, offering a compact and cost-effective alternative to traditional focusing methods [14–17]. Fahrbach et al. were the first to apply an ETL for fast volumetric imaging, capturing the beating heart of a zebrafish [14]. However, when imaging with larger fields of view, extended scanning ranges, or higher numerical aperture objectives, the ETL introduces significant optical aberrations that can substantially limit volumetric imaging quality [18]. Oblique Plane Microscopy has also been used for volumetric imaging of the zebrafish CNS at moderate resolution and 1.5 to 3 volumes per second [19,20].

Previous studies used confocal microscopy for 2D seizure imaging and observed seizure propagation from anterior to posterior regions of the zebrafish CNS [6,21]. Similarly, we previously performed 2D seizure imaging, comparing wildtype zebrafish treated with PTZ to gad1b null mutants [8]. But 2D approaches alone do not provide a complete understanding of seizure dynamics. To date, the only report of fast volumetric seizure imaging is by de Vito et al. who used two-photon light sheet microscopy with an ETL to perform 3D imaging at 5 volumes per second and observed seizure propagation in the opposite direction, from posterior to anterior [22]. This work highlights the critical importance of 3D imaging for understanding the behavior of the CNS.

Adaptive Optics (AO) technology offers an effective solution to correct ETL-induced aberrations and improve image quality [23,24]. A deformable mirror (DM) or spatial light modulator (SLM) is used to correct phase distortions in the wavefront, restoring the point spread function (PSF) to close to its ideal shape. Optimal correction can be achieved via direct wavefront sensing (e.g. using a Shack-Hartmann wavefront sensor) or by sensorless iterative methods [25–27]. While direct sensing is complex in LSFM due to the additional hardware required and the lack of natural guidestars, extended-scene wavefront sensing offers an approach for sparsely labeled samples [28,29]. However, sensorless AO offers a simple, cost-effective solution without additional hardware or guidestars. Sensorless AO was first demonstrated in the detection path of LSFM by Bourgenot et al. [30]. We previously developed a light sheet microscopy system that integrates sensorless AO in the detection path for system aberration correction with an ETL for high-resolution large FOV volumetric imaging [18]. However, the system required approximately 1.75 seconds to acquire a single raw volume, with the majority of this delay stemming from frequent software commands required to move the focal plane. While faster acquisition can be achieved using continuous external sinusoidal signals, the approach to AO correction must be adapted to continuous sweeping of the axial range.

Here we present an improved LSFM system capable of volumetric imaging over a volume of
499×499×148μm3
 at a rate of 4 volumes per second with subcellular lateral resolution. The system incorporates sensorless AO for system aberration correction, thereby extending the field of view in both the lateral and axial directions. We applied this system to investigate three dimensional seizure propagation in 7 day-post-fertilization (dpf) zebrafish larvae expressing a GCaMP calcium indicator. Seizures were induced by exposing the larvae to PTZ and imaging was performed continuously over a 2.5 minute period.

However, in the fast volumetric imaging mode, continuous sweeping of the galvo and ETL is required which prevents the acquisition of multiple images at a single axial plane. Therefore, to achieve high-speed volumetric imaging with adaptive optics, we must be able to update the DM within a few milliseconds and synchronize the DM to the camera, galvo, and ETL. We measured a response time of the DM of about 1 millisecond by reflecting a laser beam off of the DM into a detector and periodically switching the DM between a flat and non-flat state. Because the DM lacks an external hardware trigger interface, we implement a hardware interrupt in Labview where a hardware trigger generates a software function call. This interrupt is triggered by the falling edge of the digital master signal generated by the DAQ, while the rising edge triggers the camera exposure. This mechanism ensures that the DM is updated with the new AO settings immediately prior to each camera exposure, enabling image acquisition with the correct DM settings. The dynamic, continuous scanning is applied both during image acquisition and determining the DM correction so that the ETL is always in thermal steady state. 2.4.AO correctionIn our previous work, multiple consecutive images were acquired at each axial position using static galvo and ETL signals [18]. However, in the fast volumetric imaging mode, continuous sweeping of the galvo and ETL is required which prevents the acquisition of multiple images at a single axial plane. Therefore, to achieve high-speed volumetric imaging with adaptive optics, we must be able to update the DM within a few milliseconds and synchronize the DM to the camera, galvo, and ETL. We measured a response time of the DM of about 1 millisecond by reflecting a laser beam off of the DM into a detector and periodically switching the DM between a flat and non-flat state. Because the DM lacks an external hardware trigger interface, we implement a hardware interrupt in Labview where a hardware trigger generates a software function call. This interrupt is triggered by the falling edge of the digital master signal generated by the DAQ, while the rising edge triggers the camera exposure. This mechanism ensures that the DM is updated with the new AO settings immediately prior to each camera exposure, enabling image acquisition with the correct DM settings. The dynamic, continuous scanning is applied both during image acquisition and determining the DM correction so that the ETL is always in thermal steady state. 4.ConclusionWe have demonstrated high-speed volumetric imaging of zebrafish larvae using light sheet fluorescence microscopy integrated with an ETL and sensorless AO. A dynamic calibration method enables imaging over a
499×499×148μm3
 volume at 4 volumes per second, achieving a 7-fold speed increase over our previous system [18]. To address the challenge of ETL-induced aberrations during the imaging with large FOV, extended scanning ranges, or high-NA objectives, we implemented the Mapped-AO correction scheme and demonstrated its effectiveness in 7 dpf zebrafish larvae. This approach led to a substantial improvement in both image quality and the usable field of view. We implemented a software-based interrupt for the DM in LabVIEW, triggered by the falling edge of a digital signal from the DAQ, while the rising edge of the same signal initiates camera exposure. This ensures that each image is acquired immediately after the DM is updated with the new AO settings, addressing the challenge of synchronizing DM control with high-speed imaging. We developed a procedure to obtain AO correction settings for all 40 axial planes simultaneously during continuous scanning. Currently this process takes approximately 2 to 3 minutes, depending on the number of iterations and algorithmic parameters, with most of the time spent on image readout and metric computation. To accelerate this step, the computations can be moved to a GPU.We applied this system to investigate three-dimensional seizure propagation at subcellular resolution in 7 dpf zebrafish larvae expressing the GCaMP calcium indicator. Seizures were induced by exposing the larvae to PTZ and imaging was conducted continuously over a 2.5 minute period. The results reveal that seizure activity originates in the posterior brain and propagates anteriorly toward the optic tecta and gradually subsides. This observation contrasts with the antero-posterior propagation pattern reported by Liu and Baraban [4], and instead agrees more closely with the findings of de Vito et al. which show a postero-anterior propagation of seizure activity [22]. We hope to acquire additional seizure datasets to enable a more comprehensive analysis of calcium wave propagation during seizure activity. Finally, volumetric imaging of seizure activity in gad1b null mutants would provide additional insight into GABAergic signaling in the developing zebrafish [8].
