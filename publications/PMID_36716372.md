The outer membrane (OM) is the defining feature of gram-negative bacteria and is
an essential organelle. Accordingly, OM assembly pathways and their essential
protein components are conserved throughout all gram-negative species.
Lipoprotein trafficking lies at the heart of OM assembly since it supplies
several different biogenesis machines with essential lipoproteins. The
Escherichia coli Lol trafficking pathway relies on an inner membrane LolCDE
transporter that transfers newly made lipoproteins to the chaperone LolA, which
rapidly traffics lipoproteins across the periplasm to LolB for insertion into
the OM. Strikingly, many gram-negative species (like Caulobacter vibrioides) do
not produce LolB, yet essential lipoproteins are still trafficked to the OM. How
the final step of trafficking occurs in these organisms has remained a
long-standing mystery. We demonstrate that LolA from C. vibrioides can
complement the deletion of both LolA and LolB in E. coli, revealing that this
protein possesses both chaperone and insertion activities. Moreover, we define
the region of C. vibrioides LolA that is responsible for its bifunctionality.
This knowledge enabled us to convert E. coli LolA into a similarly bifunctional
protein, capable of chaperone and insertion activities. We propose that a
bifunctional LolA eliminates the need for LolB. Our findings provide an
explanation for why some gram-negative species have retained an essential LolA
yet completely lack a dedicated LolB protein.

## Full Text

A two-membrane cell envelope architecture is the defining feature of all gram-negative bacteria like Escherichia coli. The outer membrane (OM) forms the cell’s surface and is an essential organelle. While the exact composition of the OM is unique to each bacterial species, the OM is generally an asymmetric lipid bilayer composed of phospholipids in the periplasmic leaflet and a surface-exposed leaflet of lipid A-derived glycolipids made of either lipo-polysaccharide (LPS) or lipo-oligosaccharide (LOS) (1). Within this bilayer reside transmembrane β-barrel proteins (termed OMPs) and lipid-anchored proteins (termed lipoproteins) whose globular domains face either the periplasm or are surface-exposed (2). The unique permeability properties of the OM generate an immense challenge for antibiotic penetration, and the biogenesis pathways that assemble the OM have proven to be a rich source of promising novel therapeutics (3, 4).

Several conserved cellular machines construct the OM: the β-barrel assembly machine (Bam), the lipopolysaccharide transport (Lpt) system, and the localization of lipoproteins (Lol) system (2). Bam folds OMPs into the OM (5), Lpt transports LPS molecules to the outer leaflet of the OM (6), and Lol traffics lipoproteins to the OM (7). Recent work also found a family of AsmA-like proteins in E. coli that likely make up the phospholipid transporter, allowing for the flow of phospholipids from the inner membrane (IM) to the OM (8–10). Since the OM is essential, proper function of these biogenesis machines is also essential, which makes them attractive targets for antibacterials. In particular, the Lol system is a hub for OM assembly, as Bam, Lpt, and Lol each require at least one essential lipoprotein component (BamD, LptE, and LolB, respectively) to function (11–13).

In Proteobacteria (the major gram-negative phylum), lipoproteins are typically secreted via the Sec translocon and then triacylated in the IM at an invariant cysteine (Fig. 1A) (14, 15). Mature lipoproteins can be retained in the IM, but most are targeted to the OM and require a trafficking pathway to cross the aqueous periplasm. The Lol pathway is the major trafficking route, and studies in E. coli have defined the system (Fig. 1A) (16). In the first step, the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter LolCDE extracts lipoproteins from the IM and delivers them to LolA, a periplasmic chaperone (17, 18). LolA shields the extremely hydrophobic acyl chains of lipoproteins from the unfavorable, aqueous environment of the periplasm (19, 20). LolA delivers lipoproteins to LolB, itself an OM lipoprotein, which inserts them into the OM bilayer (19, 21).

Due to the importance of lipoprotein trafficking, the Lol system is well conserved throughout Proteobacteria (Fig. 1B). The ABC transporter ATPase, LolD, is encoded in all species and is paired with either a transmembrane heterodimer of LolCE or a homodimer of LolF (22). The chaperone LolA is also universally conserved. However, despite the essentiality of lipoprotein insertion into the OM, LolB conservation is poor (Fig. 1B) (23). Many Proteobacteria, such as the α-Proteobacterial species Caulobacter vibrioides, encode LolA but appear to lack a LolB homolog. (Fig. 1B) (7, 24). Studies of wild-type E. coli show that loss of lolB is lethal because the major lipoprotein trafficking pathway is prematurely terminated (13). Furthermore, E. coli LolA appears to function solely with its LolB partner in lipoprotein trafficking. In cells that are genetically engineered to tolerate loss of lolB, the production of LolA when LolB is absent is detrimental for growth (or even lethal if LolA is overproduced), likely due to the accumulation of dead-end LolA-lipoprotein complexes (25). For distantly related bacteria, a phylogenetic puzzle has persisted. How do many Proteobacteria complete their Lol pathway without LolB? Moreover, since LolA seems to function exclusively with LolB in E. coli, why have these organisms retained an essential LolA despite lacking LolB?

In this study, we sought to address the discrepancy of LolB conservation. Examining the C. vibrioides LolA protein, we reveal that it is bifunctional, containing the activities of both LolA (chaperone) and LolB (OM insertase). We also identify the region of the protein responsible for bifunctionality, allowing us to convert E. coli LolA into a bifunctional protein that is sufficient for OM lipoprotein trafficking when lolB is deleted. Taken together, we offer an explanation for why lolB is absent from C. vibrioides and perhaps other Proteobacteria.

ResultsLolB Is Only Present in ɣ and β-Proteobacteria.Sequence homology searches fail to identify a lolB homolog outside of ɣ and β-Proteobacteria, as noted previously (7, 26) (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). In prior studies of another essential OM lipoprotein, LptE, sequence homology searches similarly struggled to identify clear homologs, but homologs could be found by analyzing the genetic synteny of the two essential genes that flank lptE in E. coli (27). The lolB gene in E. coli is also located between two essential genes, ispE (4-(cytidine 5′-diphospho)-2-C-methyl-D-erythritol kinase) and hemA (glutamyl-tRNA reductase) (Fig. 1C). We examined synteny of these genes throughout Proteobacteria to determine whether highly sequence divergent lolB candidate genes could be identified.Many orders of ɣ-Proteobacteria share the E. coli gene organization (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Interestingly, we noted that species in several orders (e.g., Pseudomonadales, Xanthomonadales, among others) encode an additional gene between lolB and hemA; this gene encodes a protein with several tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) motifs. In β-Proteobacteria, the prs-ispE-lolB organization is preserved, but hemA homologs are located elsewhere on the chromosome (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Notably, a TPR protein-encoding gene is ubiquitously present upstream of lolB in β-Proteobacteria (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The strong synteny between ispE and lolB in both ɣ and β-Proteobacteria focused our search for possible distantly related lolB genes in other Proteobacterial orders. In ɑ-Proteobacteria, prs and ispE are separated, and no flanking gene with resemblance to lolB could be identified upstream of ispE. Strikingly, many ɑ-Proteobacteria still encode a TPR protein-gene upstream of ispE, reminiscent of ɣ and β-Proteobacterial gene organization, but conspicuously lack any candidate lolB gene (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The gene organization in δ and ε-Proteobacteria was highly divergent from other Proteobacteria, and we failed to find candidate lolB homologs adjacent to ispE (or hemA). Our analysis strengthens the concept that lolB is absent in α, δ, and ε-Proteobacteria. Indeed, the synteny between ispE and a TPR protein-encoding gene suggests that this organization may predate the emergence of lolB. The cviLolAeco Loop Is Required for Lipoprotein Insertion.

Our hypothesis was that a loop of cviLolAeco is the feature that enables its apparent bifunctionality, allowing lipoprotein insertion into the OM. Prior work established that mutating the LolB protruding loop leucine to an aspartate or deleting the loop leucine abrogates OM lipoprotein insertion function (30). We reasoned that such mutations of the loop leucine in cviLolAeco may have a similar effect. We expected that mutating the loop leucine of cviLolAeco would result in a protein that no longer has OM lipoprotein insertase activity but might still act as a lipoprotein chaperone. Hence, loop leucine mutations would still allow cviLolAeco to complement the deletion of the E. coli lolA chaperone but would no longer enable the deletion of the lolB insertase. Indeed, E. coli expressing either cviLolAeco(∆L119) or cviLolAeco(L119D) in which the loop leucine was deleted or mutated to an aspartate, respectively, could readily tolerate the deletion of lolA (Fig. 4 A and C) but could no longer tolerate the inactivation of lolB (Fig. 4B). This result confirmed that the chaperone activity of cviLolAeco is distinct from its OM lipoprotein insertion function and that the latter depends on an intact loop leucine.

DiscussionThe E. coli model has been a fruitful mainstay for deciphering the essential molecular mechanisms that are responsible for building the OM. At the same time, distantly related species have evolved unique OM assembly components. For example, the nonessential accessory BamBCE lipoproteins are not universally conserved, and α-Proteobacteria such as C. vibrioides evolved a unique BamF component to replace BamC (24). However, since the general composition of the OM is conserved, the OM assembly proteins that are essential in E. coli are universally conserved (and in many instances proved to be essential) throughout diverse Proteobacterial species and in other diderm phyla (24, 27, 39). The exception is LolB; no lolB gene can be identified outside of β and γ-Proteobacteria, either through sequence homology or genetic synteny analyses. How it is that many bacteria complete the final step of lipoprotein trafficking—inserting lipoproteins into the OM bilayer—has remained a mystery.The apparent production of LolB in only a subset of Proteobacteria and its complete absence from other Proteobacteria and diderm phyla strongly suggests that LolB evolved after β and γ-Proteobacteria diverged from α-Proteobacteria. E. coli LolA and LolB share limited sequence identity and they adopt similar structures, perhaps hinting that they may have arisen from an ancestral protein that contained features of both LolA and LolB (29). In this work, we made the striking observation that C. vibrioides LolA displays key structural features of both LolA and LolB, consistent with bifunctionality. Remarkably, we show that this protein displays both LolA chaperone activity and LolB insertion activity in vivo by complementing the deletion of either of these genes. Furthermore, we show that converting E. coli LolA into a bifunctional protein requires only a modest change to its sequence. We propose that in some species lacking a dedicated LolB protein, LolA may serve as a bifunctional protein to complete the lipoprotein trafficking pathway.A periplasmic protein with insertion activity is surprising, as insertion activity would not be restricted to the targeted membrane, potentially decreasing the accuracy of lipoprotein localization. An earlier study of E. coli LolB removed its lipid anchor to express a soluble periplasmic form (termed mLolB) (21). Periplasmic mLolB could receive lipoproteins from LolA but exhibited minimal membrane preference for its OM-targeted cargo; mLolB could insert lipoproteins into the OM or wrongly insert lipoproteins back into the IM (21). Mistargeted lipoproteins would reenter the Lol pathway until they reached the OM. Mistargeting is the likely explanation for why the ecoLolAloop bifunctional chimera is unable to complement ΔlolA in lpp+ cells, since this same ecoLolAloop protein can complement ΔlolA in lpp(ΔK58) cells, which produce just as much Lpp but, critically, in a form that is not lethally toxic when mislocalized to the IM (40).We could not detect insertase activity of ecoLolAloop in vitro, but the insertase activity of this protein was sufficient to support viability of Δlpp cells. This is in agreement with previous findings demonstrating that polar amino acid substitutions of the LolB loop leucine abolish insertase activity in vitro, and such lolB variants are not efficient enough to support viability of cells expressing the highly abundant lipoprotein, Lpp (30). However, these lolB mutants can complement ΔlolB in Δlpp cells. Since LolB is essential for viability in these cells, this clearly demonstrates that these mutants retain significant insertase activity. Importantly, both ecoLolA and ecoLolAloop worked efficiently in conjunction with LolB to facilitate membrane insertion of Pal in vitro. This demonstrates the greater efficiency of the LolA-LolB two-partner system compared to a soluble bifunctional enzyme. Furthermore, since mLolB can insert the lipoprotein Pal into membranes in vitro (30), this suggests that features of LolB beyond the loop region (which are not present in ecoLolAloop) are important for full activity.Our work demonstrates that the cviLolA leucine-tipped loop confers bifunctionality to this protein. Among LolB-lacking organisms, the sequence of this loop and the presence of a leucine is variable. Indeed, an earlier study of E. coli LolB suggests that many substitutions of the leucine tip result in functional insertase proteins (30). Hence, the rules governing insertase activity appear complex and are not even fully clear for E. coli LolB. Deciphering how these loops mediate insertase activity will require further work. With the discovery of a bifunctional LolA in C. vibrioides, we suggest that lolB may have emerged from a duplication of such a lolA gene, after which a process of gene specialization could have separated the chaperone and insertion activities into distinct genes. Placing a receptor in the OM and separating insertion activity from chaperone activity may offer a more efficient and, crucially, a more accurate trafficking pathway. This unidirectional pathway is one that may permit a broader range of lipoproteins to emerge. Enterobacteriaceae produce the abundant Lpp that must be efficiently trafficked to the OM and cannot reside in the IM due to lethal toxicities. Lpp attaches the OM to the peptidoglycan cell wall via covalent links. In distantly related bacteria, this function is entrusted to β-barrel OMPs, not lipoproteins (41–43). In addition, various OM lipoproteins in β and γ-Proteobacteria adopt transmembrane pore-forming structures (e.g., OprM in Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Wza in Enterobacteriaceae) (44, 45). Since a pore in the IM would destroy the proton motive force and would be lethal, the evolution of such lipoproteins might necessitate a highly efficient, OM-specific trafficking pathway.The ability of a LolA protein to function as an insertase is an interesting finding in the context of a key, long-standing paradox within the E. coli Lol pathway: to insert a lipoprotein into the OM, this pathway relies on a lipoprotein already in the OM. How such a pathway might have originated has never been clear. Now, with the observation of a bifunctional LolA that likely preceded the emergence of LolB, we suggest that such a LolA could deliver a lipoprotein with insertase activity (i.e., ancestral LolB) to the OM. Over time, the insertase activity could be localized and restricted to the OM lipoprotein. The redundancy of insertase activity between such LolA and ancestral LolB could have triggered the eventual loss of insertase activity in LolA over time. In confirming the apparent bifunctionality of a C. vibrioides LolA, we offer one explanation for how diderm bacteria can complete their lipoprotein trafficking pathway without requiring a LolB protein.

The apparent production of LolB in only a subset of Proteobacteria and its complete absence from other Proteobacteria and diderm phyla strongly suggests that LolB evolved after β and γ-Proteobacteria diverged from α-Proteobacteria. E. coli LolA and LolB share limited sequence identity and they adopt similar structures, perhaps hinting that they may have arisen from an ancestral protein that contained features of both LolA and LolB (29). In this work, we made the striking observation that C. vibrioides LolA displays key structural features of both LolA and LolB, consistent with bifunctionality. Remarkably, we show that this protein displays both LolA chaperone activity and LolB insertion activity in vivo by complementing the deletion of either of these genes. Furthermore, we show that converting E. coli LolA into a bifunctional protein requires only a modest change to its sequence. We propose that in some species lacking a dedicated LolB protein, LolA may serve as a bifunctional protein to complete the lipoprotein trafficking pathway.

A periplasmic protein with insertion activity is surprising, as insertion activity would not be restricted to the targeted membrane, potentially decreasing the accuracy of lipoprotein localization. An earlier study of E. coli LolB removed its lipid anchor to express a soluble periplasmic form (termed mLolB) (21). Periplasmic mLolB could receive lipoproteins from LolA but exhibited minimal membrane preference for its OM-targeted cargo; mLolB could insert lipoproteins into the OM or wrongly insert lipoproteins back into the IM (21). Mistargeted lipoproteins would reenter the Lol pathway until they reached the OM. Mistargeting is the likely explanation for why the ecoLolAloop bifunctional chimera is unable to complement ΔlolA in lpp+ cells, since this same ecoLolAloop protein can complement ΔlolA in lpp(ΔK58) cells, which produce just as much Lpp but, critically, in a form that is not lethally toxic when mislocalized to the IM (40).

We could not detect insertase activity of ecoLolAloop in vitro, but the insertase activity of this protein was sufficient to support viability of Δlpp cells. This is in agreement with previous findings demonstrating that polar amino acid substitutions of the LolB loop leucine abolish insertase activity in vitro, and such lolB variants are not efficient enough to support viability of cells expressing the highly abundant lipoprotein, Lpp (30). However, these lolB mutants can complement ΔlolB in Δlpp cells. Since LolB is essential for viability in these cells, this clearly demonstrates that these mutants retain significant insertase activity. Importantly, both ecoLolA and ecoLolAloop worked efficiently in conjunction with LolB to facilitate membrane insertion of Pal in vitro. This demonstrates the greater efficiency of the LolA-LolB two-partner system compared to a soluble bifunctional enzyme. Furthermore, since mLolB can insert the lipoprotein Pal into membranes in vitro (30), this suggests that features of LolB beyond the loop region (which are not present in ecoLolAloop) are important for full activity.

Our work demonstrates that the cviLolA leucine-tipped loop confers bifunctionality to this protein. Among LolB-lacking organisms, the sequence of this loop and the presence of a leucine is variable. Indeed, an earlier study of E. coli LolB suggests that many substitutions of the leucine tip result in functional insertase proteins (30). Hence, the rules governing insertase activity appear complex and are not even fully clear for E. coli LolB. Deciphering how these loops mediate insertase activity will require further work. With the discovery of a bifunctional LolA in C. vibrioides, we suggest that lolB may have emerged from a duplication of such a lolA gene, after which a process of gene specialization could have separated the chaperone and insertion activities into distinct genes. Placing a receptor in the OM and separating insertion activity from chaperone activity may offer a more efficient and, crucially, a more accurate trafficking pathway. This unidirectional pathway is one that may permit a broader range of lipoproteins to emerge. Enterobacteriaceae produce the abundant Lpp that must be efficiently trafficked to the OM and cannot reside in the IM due to lethal toxicities. Lpp attaches the OM to the peptidoglycan cell wall via covalent links. In distantly related bacteria, this function is entrusted to β-barrel OMPs, not lipoproteins (41–43). In addition, various OM lipoproteins in β and γ-Proteobacteria adopt transmembrane pore-forming structures (e.g., OprM in Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Wza in Enterobacteriaceae) (44, 45). Since a pore in the IM would destroy the proton motive force and would be lethal, the evolution of such lipoproteins might necessitate a highly efficient, OM-specific trafficking pathway.

The ability of a LolA protein to function as an insertase is an interesting finding in the context of a key, long-standing paradox within the E. coli Lol pathway: to insert a lipoprotein into the OM, this pathway relies on a lipoprotein already in the OM. How such a pathway might have originated has never been clear. Now, with the observation of a bifunctional LolA that likely preceded the emergence of LolB, we suggest that such a LolA could deliver a lipoprotein with insertase activity (i.e., ancestral LolB) to the OM. Over time, the insertase activity could be localized and restricted to the OM lipoprotein. The redundancy of insertase activity between such LolA and ancestral LolB could have triggered the eventual loss of insertase activity in LolA over time. In confirming the apparent bifunctionality of a C. vibrioides LolA, we offer one explanation for how diderm bacteria can complete their lipoprotein trafficking pathway without requiring a LolB protein.
