Cholera toxin (CT) produced by Vibrio cholerae causes the devastating diarrhea 
of cholera by catalyzing the ADP-ribosylation of the alpha subunit of the 
intestinal Gs protein (Gsalpha), leading to characteristic water and electrolyte 
losses. Mammalian cells contain ADP-ribosyltransferases similar to CT and an 
ADP-ribosyl(arginine)protein hydrolase (ADPRH), which cleaves the 
ADP-ribose-(arginine)protein bond, regenerating native protein and completing an 
ADP-ribosylation cycle. We hypothesized that ADPRH might counteract intoxication 
by reversing the ADP-ribosylation of Gsalpha. Effects of intoxication on murine 
ADPRH-/- cells were greater than those on wild-type cells and were significantly 
reduced by overexpression of wild-type ADPRH in ADPRH-/- cells, as evidenced by 
both ADP-ribose-arginine content and Gsalpha modification. Similarly, intestinal 
loops in the ADPRH-/- mouse were more sensitive than their wild-type 
counterparts to toxin effects on fluid accumulation, Gsalpha modification, and 
ADP-ribosylarginine content. Thus, CT-catalyzed ADP-ribosylation of cell 
proteins can be counteracted by ADPRH, which could function as a modifier gene 
in disease. Further, our study demonstrates that enzymatic cross talk exists 
between bacterial toxin ADP-ribosyltransferases and host ADP-ribosylation 
cycles. In disease, toxin-catalyzed ADP-ribosylation overwhelms this potential 
host defense system, resulting in persistence of ADP-ribosylation and 
intoxication of the cell.
