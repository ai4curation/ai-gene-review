Alleviating hepatic lipid deposition in aging laying hens is critical for
maintaining liver health and extending their productive lifespan. This study
aimed to identify potential indicators and elucidate the underlying mechanisms
associated with liver fat accumulation. Hepatic lipid ratio is a key indicator
of the lipid deposition in the liver. A total of 509 healthy G1 line hens at 66
weeks of age were assessed for hepatic lipid ratio and stratified into three
groups: low (L, <5%), medium (M, 5-10%), and high (H, >10%). The results
revealed that the correlation coefficients of liver triglyceride (TG) and total
cholesterol (TC) with the hepatic lipid ratio were 0.907 and 0.870,
respectively, indicating a strong and highly significant association. All three
metrics differed significantly among the three groups (P < 0.01), validating the
grouping approach. Compared with the L group, the M and H groups showed
significantly higher body weight, abdominal fat weight, abdominal fat index,
plasma TG, TC, and LDL-c (P < 0.05), while plasma HDL-C was significantly lower
in group H compared with groups L and M (P < 0.05). Abdominal skinfold thickness
differed significantly across all groups (P < 0.05). Transcriptomic analysis
revealed 115 up-regulated and 50 down-regulated genes in the H group compared
with the L group. We identified: four genes (PCSK9, ABCG8, G6PC2, FOLH1) were
associated with lipid metabolism; three (IL1RAPL1, TNIP2, HPSE2) with
inflammatory response; five (CCL3, CCL17, CCL20, SAMSN1, ICOS) with immune
response; and six (HBA1, HBAD, HBBA, CHAC1, CREG1, DDIT4) with antioxidant
function. KEGG pathway analysis highlighted enrichments in lipid
metabolism-related pathways such as "ABC transporters" and "Insulin secretion,"
as well as in the inflammatory response-related pathway "Cytokine-cytokine
receptor interaction." In conclusion, body weight, abdominal skinfold thickness,
plasma TG, TC, LDL-c, and HDL-c may serve as practical indicators for evaluating
hepatic lipid deposition in laying hens. Excessive lipid accumulation perturbs
hepatic metabolic homeostasis, triggering transcriptional reprogramming
associated with both inflammatory and oxidative stress responses, alongside
adaptive antioxidant defense and anti-inflammatory responses. However, further
studies are required to determine whether these changes reflect a bona fide
compensatory protective mechanism in the liver.

Introduction

The liver is the central organ for lipid metabolism in laying hens (Gloux, et al., 2019). It synthesizes most yolk constituents and plays a pivotal role in egg formation (Nys and Guyot, 2011). Consequently, maintaining optimal liver health and function is essential for sustaining efficient production of high-quality eggs (Han, et al., 2023). However, hepatic total cholesterol (TC) and triglyceride (TG) levels typically increase with age in laying hens, which adversely affects liver metabolism and function (Gu, et al., 2021). Excessive hepatic fat deposition is recognized as a key contributor to metabolic dysfunction and related pathologies, ultimately impairing growth and productive performance in animals (Chu, et al., 2024; Kuhla, et al., 2011; Peng, et al., 2019). Therefore, mitigating liver lipid accumulation in aging laying hens is crucial for preserving hepatic health and extending their productive lifespan.

In recent years, extensive research has focused on excessive hepatic lipid deposition in laying hens. For instance, Wang et al. established an early-stage fatty liver syndrome (FLS) model with liver injury by dexamethasone injection and reported that dietary betaine supplementation could prevent FLS, largely through modulating lipid metabolism and exerting antioxidant effects (Wang, et al., 2024). Similarly, Li et al. induced FLS using a high-energy, low-protein diet and demonstrated that the protective effect of genistein against lipid metabolic disorders may involve activation of the GPER-AMPK signaling pathway (Li, et al., 2023). In another study, Hu et al. suggested that targeting vitamin B2 and pantethine (a derivative of vitamin B5) could be an effective strategy for alleviating liver lipid deposition in aging laying hens fed a low-protein diet (Hu, et al., 2024). While these studies provide valuable insights into mitigating hepatic lipid accumulation, most are based on experimentally induced pathological models. Consequently, it remains unclear whether lipid metabolism indicators derived from such models accurately reflect liver fat deposition under practical production conditions. Moreover, the molecular mechanisms underlying spontaneous hepatic lipid deposition in late-phase laying hens during normal production have not been fully elucidated.

Hepatic lipid ratio is a key indicator of the lipid deposition in the liver. In this study, we measured hepatic lipid ratio in 509 healthy G1 line hens at 66 weeks of age and stratified them into three groups accordingly. We assessed variations in lipid metabolism parameters across these groups. Furthermore, we identified candidate genes and pathways in the liver associated with hepatic lipid ratio, providing a theoretical basis for mitigating excessive liver fat accumulation and improving production performance in late-phase laying hens.

Descriptive statistics and grouping of hepatic lipid ratioMedically, hepatic lipid ratio below 5% is generally considered normal. Based on clinical classifications of fatty liver severity, lipid levels exceeding 5% are defined as mild, above 10% as moderate, and above 25% as severe fatty liver. In the present study, the hepatic lipid ratio of the 509 laying hens examined ranged from 1.37% to 18.07%, with an average of 6.26% (Fig. 1A). ConclusionHepatic lipid deposition represents a complex physiological process that elicits a series of adaptive changes in the body. At the phenotypic level, it is characterized by increased body weight, enhanced abdominal fat deposition, greater abdominal skinfold thickness, elevated plasma levels of TG, TC, and LDL-c, along with reduced HDL-c. Although measuring these parameters could offer a simplified means to evaluate hepatic lipid deposition in flocks, specific and standardized methodological approaches require further exploration. At the molecular level, Excessive lipid accumulation perturbs hepatic metabolic homeostasis, triggering transcriptional reprogramming associated with both inflammatory and oxidative stress responses, alongside adaptive antioxidant defense and anti-inflammatory responses. However, further studies are required to determine whether these changes reflect a bona fide compensatory protective mechanism in the liver.
