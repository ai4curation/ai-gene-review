The balance between mitochondrial calcium (mCa2+) uptake and efflux is essential
for ATP production and cellular homeostasis. The mitochondrial sodium-calcium
exchanger, NCLX, is a critical route of mCa2+ efflux in excitable tissues, such
as the heart and brain, and animal models support NCLX as a promising
therapeutic target to limit pathogenic mCa2+ overload. However, the mechanisms
that regulate NCLX activity are largely unknown. Using proximity biotinylation
proteomic screening, we identify the mitochondrial inner membrane protein TMEM65
as an NCLX binding partner that enhances sodium (Na+)-dependent mCa2+ efflux.
Mechanistically, acute pharmacological NCLX inhibition or genetic deletion of
NCLX ablates the TMEM65-dependent increase in mCa2+ efflux, and loss-of-function
studies show that TMEM65 is required for Na+-dependent mCa2+ efflux. In line
with these findings, knockdown of Tmem65 in mice promotes mCa2+ overload in the
heart and skeletal muscle and impairs both cardiac and neuromuscular function.
Collectively, our results show that loss of TMEM65 function in excitable tissue
disrupts NCLX-dependent mCa2+ efflux, causing pathogenic mCa2+ overload, cell
death, and organ-level dysfunction. These findings demonstrate the essential
role of TMEM65 in regulating NCLX-dependent mCa2+ efflux and suggest modulation
of TMEM65 as a therapeutic strategy for a variety of diseases.

## Full Text

The mitochondrial sodium-calcium exchanger, NCLX, mediates the extrusion of calcium (Ca2+) from the mitochondrial matrix in exchange for sodium (Na+) and is the primary route for mitochondrial Ca2+ (mCa2+) efflux in excitable cells1,2. We recently demonstrated the requirement of NCLX in maintaining mCa2+ homeostasis and preventing pathogenic mCa2+ overload in cardiomyocytes3 and neurons4. We and others demonstrated the efficacy of NCLX overexpression to limit disease progression in models of cardiac injury and heart failure3,5, Alzheimer’s disease6, and cancer7. These results support the augmentation of NCLX activity as a promising therapeutic strategy for a wide range of diseases. However, a barrier to clinical translation is our limited understanding of the mechanisms regulating NCLX function. Knowing that plasma membrane Na+/Ca2+ exchangers structurally related to NCLX are regulated by allosteric interactions with other proteins8–10, we hypothesized that mapping the NCLX interactome would reveal regulators of function and provide new targets to modulate mCa2+ efflux11. Here we report the identification of TMEM65, an inner mitochondrial membrane protein, as a genetically confirmed regulator of NCLX-dependent mCa2+ efflux and demonstrate its critical role in preventing pathogenic mCa2+ overload in excitable tissues. Our report is supported by studies from two other groups, who independently obtained complementary results12,13.

Progress towards defining the regulation of NCLX activity has been stymied by many technical challenges that complicate the study of this protein. NCLX has a complex structure consisting of 13 hydrophobic transmembrane domains14 and it is embedded in the complex lipid environment of the inner mitochondrial membrane2. The lack of highly specific pharmacological modulators of its function11, and the inconsistent commercial availability of antibodies directed against it7,11,15, are major hurdles for the field. Therefore, we generated a fusion construct encoding human NCLX protein followed by a biotin ligase domain (BioID2) and C-terminal haemagglutinin (HA) tag (Fig. 1a) to interrogate the NCLX interactome. Expression of NCLX–BioID2–HA in intact cells and supplementation with biotin allows for biotinylation of lysine residues in proteins that come within a vicinity of fewer than 10 nm (refs. 16,17). Fractionation of human AC16 cardiomyocytes transiently transfected with NCLX–BioID2–HA confirmed its localization to mitochondria (Fig. 1b). Progressive digestion of mitochondria from these cells with increasing concentrations of trypsin indicated that despite increasing degradation of the full-length fusion protein, an approximately 30-kDa C-terminal BioID2–HA moiety was protected from proteolysis (Extended Data Fig. 1a). The addition of digitonin to permeabilize the inner mitochondrial membrane allowed for increased degradation of this moiety. After comparing the NCLX–BioID2–HA degradation pattern to that of the outer mitochondrial membrane protein TOM20, the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) protein MCU, and the mitochondrial matrix protein cyclophilin D (Extended Data Fig. 1a), we interpreted that NCLX–BioID2–HA properly localized to the IMM, with the C terminus positioned in the matrix (Fig. 1a). Blotting with streptavidin revealed the persistence of biotinylated proteins even at the highest concentration of trypsin; these were further degraded on addition of digitonin (Extended Data Fig. 1a), providing additional support that the C-terminal biotin ligase domain of NCLX–BioID2–HA was in the matrix. Biotinylated proteins from whole-cell lysates of AC16 cardiomyocytes transiently expressing NCLX–BioID2–HA, or control BioID2–HA alone (Fig. 1c), were identified using mass spectrometry (MS). Biotinylated proteins enriched twofold or more with NCLX–BioID2–HA compared to BioID2–HA in at least two out of three replicate experiments were cross-referenced to the Integrated Mitochondrial Protein Index (IMPI) to identify NCLX proximal mitochondrial proteins (Supplementary Table 1). This analysis identified TMEM65, a three-pass transmembrane protein of unknown molecular function that spans the IMM18, as a potential NCLX interactor in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 1d,e).

We prioritized TMEM65 for further analysis given a case report of mitochondrial encephalomyopathy in a patient with a homozygous null mutation in TMEM65 (ref. 19) and the resemblance of the resulting mitochondrial and organ-level dysfunction to that observed in human patients and mouse models with genetic mutations that perturb mitochondrial calcium homeostasis20–23. Immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 1b) and cellular fractionation (Fig. 1g) confirmed mitochondrial localization of exogenous MYC–Flag-tagged TMEM65 in AC16 cardiomyocytes. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) showed that mouse Tmem65 gene expression was enriched in striated muscle and other excitable tissues, including the brain (Fig. 1h). This was intriguing as NCLX protein expression and activity are high in skeletal muscle1,24, and NCLX activity is critical to mCa2+ efflux in the heart and the brain3,4. Analysis of gene expression across the heart, skeletal muscle and brain revealed a positive correlation between TMEM65 and NCLX (also known as SLC8B1) transcript expression in the mouse (Fig. 1i) and in humans (Fig. 1j), raising the possibility of a functional relationship between these proteins.

We next tested the effect of TMEM65 overexpression on mCa2+ flux in vitro. We generated clonal lines of AC16 cardiomyocytes with stable expression of C-terminal epitope-tagged human TMEM65 (TMEM65–MYC–Flag) (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 2a). There was a tendency for reduced NCLX expression in two out of three TMEM65 overexpression lines, although this did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 2b). Stable TMEM65 overexpression increased the inhibitory phosphorylation of pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) Ser293 (Extended Data Fig. 2g,h), indicating diminished steady-state matrix-Ca2+ content. This effect was consistent across TMEM65 overexpression lines; the variability in the degree to which PDH phosphorylation increased may reflect differences in secondary long-term remodelling of PDH regulation by PDH kinase or PDH phosphatase, as reported with chronic modulation of mCa2+ flux in vivo25. In permeabilized AC16 cardiomyocytes incubated with thapsigargin to inhibit sarcoplasmic and endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ uptake, stable TMEM65 overexpression enhanced the rate of mCa2+ efflux (Fig. 2b,c) as measured after acute inhibition of mCa2+ uptake with the mitochondrial calcium uniporter inhibitor, Ru360. TMEM65 overexpression also attenuated the initial mCa2+ uptake rate (Extended Data Fig. 2i) and net mCa2+ uptake before the addition of Ru360 (Fig. 2d), which is consistent with reports describing that mCa2+ uptake and efflux are ongoing processes that occur simultaneously3. Immunoblotting revealed variable upregulation of one or more mitochondrial calcium uniporter complex components in each of our TMEM65 overexpression lines (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 2c–f). This suggests that if anything, net uniporter function may be increased as a compensatory response to limit mCa2+ depletion in the face of TMEM65 overexpression, and that the diminished net mCa2+ uptake observed with TMEM65 overexpression is due to enhanced mCa2+ efflux rather than diminished uniporter function. Our finding that TMEM65 overexpression enhances net mCa2+ efflux specifically measured after the acute inhibition of the uniporter with Ru360 reinforces this conclusion. To verify that the effects on mCa2+ flux were not attributable to secondary compensatory effects of stable TMEM65 overexpression, we also assessed mCa2+ flux in permeabilized AC16 cells after acute adenoviral TMEM65 transduction (Extended Data Fig. 2j,k). Consistent with our findings in stable cell lines, acute TMEM65 overexpression enhanced the rate and extent of mCa2+ efflux (Extended Data Fig. 2l,m), and limited the extent of acute net mCa2+ uptake (Extended Data Fig. 2n).

Of note, acute treatment with the pharmacological NCLX inhibitor, CGP-37157, completely attenuated the TMEM65-dependent increase in mCa2+ efflux rate, reduction in net mCa2+ uptake, and reduction in mCa2+ uptake rate in AC16 cardiomyocytes (Fig. 2b–d and Extended Data Fig. 2i). This phenomenon, in which NCLX inhibition abrogated the TMEM65 enhancement of mCa2+ efflux, was conserved in another striated muscle cell type, mouse C2C12 skeletal myoblasts (Extended Data Fig. 2o–t). These findings indicate that TMEM65 functionally depends on NCLX activity to augment mCa2+ extrusion and support the hypothesis that TMEM65 functions as a positive modulator of NCLX activity.

Evaluation of KCl-evoked mCa2+ transients in intact AC16 cardiomyocytes likewise showed that stable overexpression of TMEM65 accelerated mCa2+ efflux and reduced net mCa2+ accumulation (Fig. 2e–g), without altering mCa2+ uptake (Fig. 2h,i). No changes in KCl-induced cytosolic Ca2+ transients were noted (Fig. 2j and Extended Data Fig. 2u–w). Finally, to further explore the potential NCLX dependence of TMEM65-sensitive mCa2+ efflux, we evaluated Na+-induced mCa2+ efflux in permeabilized cells. TMEM65 overexpression specifically accelerated mCa2+ efflux stimulated by the acute addition of Na+, and this effect was sensitive to NCLX inhibition (Fig. 2k,l). Together, these observations indicate that TMEM65 promotes Na+-sensitive mCa2+ efflux, likely through the mitochondrial Na+/Ca2+ exchanger NCLX.

To determine the requirement for TMEM65 in mitochondrial calcium efflux, we used CRISPR–Cas9 to disrupt the TMEM65 gene in AC16 cardiomyocytes (Fig. 2m). Immunoblotting confirmed loss of TMEM65 protein in clonal TMEM65−/− lines (Fig. 2n). NCLX protein expression was not substantially altered in TMEM65−/− cells (Fig. 2n and Extended Data Fig. 2x). Loss of TMEM65 decreased inhibitory phosphorylation of PDH (Ser293) (Fig. 2o,p), suggesting elevated matrix-Ca2+ content. Examination of mCa2+ exchange in permeabilized cells treated with thapsigargin showed that deletion of TMEM65 attenuated mCa2+ efflux (Fig. 2q–s). The disruption of mCa2+ efflux with loss of TMEM65 tended to enhance net mCa2+ uptake (Fig. 2t–u), further emphasizing that ongoing mCa2+ efflux occurs even during acute mCa2+ uptake3. Protein expression of components of the mitochondrial calcium uniporter complex was not significantly altered in TMEM65−/− cells (Fig. 2n and Extended Data Fig. 2y–b‘), although MCU and essential MCU regulator (EMRE) expression consistently trended downward. Together with our observations in cells overexpressing TMEM65, these findings argue against the possibility that TMEM65 enhances net mCa2+ efflux via direct modulatory effects on the uniporter that limit mCa2+ uptake. Finally, consistent with our observations in cells overexpressing TMEM65, loss of TMEM65 specifically abrogated Na+-induced mCa2+ efflux (Fig. 2v–w). Moreover, GCP-sensitive mCa2+ efflux was completely lost in the absence of TMEM65 (Fig. 2v–w). These data, our identification of TMEM65 in the NCLX interactome, and the resemblance of the in vitro phenotypes resulting from gain or loss of TMEM65 function to those resulting from gain or loss of NCLX function3, support the hypothesis that TMEM65 enhances mCa2+ efflux in an NCLX-dependent manner.

To examine more rigorously the functional interactions between TMEM65 and NCLX in mediating mCa2+ efflux, we evaluated mCa2+ transients in immortalized Nclxfl/fl (also known as Slc8b1fl/fl) mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) after acute deletion of NCLX by adenoviral delivery of Cre recombinase. Acute deletion of NCLX did not affect TMEM65 protein levels in these cells (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b). Adenoviral TMEM65 expression accelerated the initial decay of the mCa2+ transient (efflux rate) in control Nclxfl/fl cells, but not in Nclx−/− cells (Fig. 3a,b). We also observed a trend towards a reduced area-under-the-curve of the mCa2+ transient with Ad-TMEM65 in control cells; this effect was lost in Nclx−/− cells (Fig. 3c). These findings suggest that TMEM65 limits net matrix Ca2+ accumulation upon increased cytosolic Ca2+, but that this effect requires NCLX. Note that the change in efflux with Ad-TMEM65 was more modest in these experiments, probably because of more limited Na+-dependent mCa2+ efflux in fibroblasts in contrast to excitable cells. Yet, these results are consistent with TMEM65 enhancing mCa2+ efflux specifically through effects on NCLX.

We next sought to define the mechanism by which TMEM65 modulates NCLX-dependent mCa2+ efflux. Proximity ligation labelling of NCLX and Flag in AC16 cardiomyocytes expressing TMEM65–MYC–Flag (Fig. 3d,e) verified that NCLX and TMEM65 reside in close proximity of each other. Immunoprecipitation (IP) of endogenous TMEM65 from wild-type (WT) mouse heart mitochondria co-immunoprecipitated endogenous NCLX (Fig. 3f). This finding confirmed that NCLX and TMEM65 exist in a native macromolecular complex within the mitochondria, and not just as an artifact of experimental expression systems. To facilitate in situ investigative approaches for NCLX, we generated a Nclx-3×Flag knock-in mouse model, in which a C-terminal 3×Flag epitope tag was knocked into the last coding exon of Slc8b1 (Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 3c–e). Fast protein size-exclusion liquid chromatography (FPLC) of mitochondria isolated in nonreducing conditions from the hearts of Nclx-3×Flag knock-in mice showed that the distribution of protein complexes containing NCLX mirrored that of TMEM65, with the abundance of both proteins greatest in the ~160 kDa containing fraction (Fig. 3h and Extended Data Fig. 3f,g). This distribution pattern agrees with our co-IP results and is consistent with NCLX and TMEM65 existing in a common macromolecular complex.

To directly test this notion and better estimate the size of this NCLX–TMEM65 complex, we performed FPLC on nonreduced mitochondrial samples isolated from wild-type and TMEM65−/− AC16 cardiomyocytes, using a chromatography column with greater molecular mass resolution (Extended Data Fig. 3h,i). Under these conditions, NCLX from wild-type mitochondria was detected at a molecular mass greater than 100 kDa using immunoblotting (Fig. 3i). As mature mitochondrial human NCLX is ~61 kDa, this agrees with previous reports of NCLX forming SDS-resistant dimers1. The distribution of NCLX from wild-type mitochondria peaked around elution fractions 32–33 (~162–145 kDa) (Fig. 3i,j and Extended Data Fig. 3h,i). Genetic deletion of TMEM65 (~19 kDa in its mature mitochondrial form) resulted in a shift of the NCLX distribution towards fractions of lower molecular mass, with peak NCLX abundance in elution fraction 34 (~129 kDa) in the TMEM65−/− samples (Fig. 3i,j and Extended Data Fig. 3h,i). We were unable to further resolve this shift in the NCLX distribution at a sufficient molecular mass resolution to infer a precise stoichiometry between NCLX and TMEM65, and we cannot exclude the possibility that additional proteins may participate in this complex. Nevertheless, this reduction in NCLX complex size upon the loss of TMEM65 provides further evidence to suggest that these proteins physically interact.

We next performed mutagenesis of TMEM65 to clarify the mechanism(s) by which it promotes NCLX-dependent mCa2+ efflux. TMEM65 is annotated as its own protein domain in the InterPro (ref. no. IPR019537) and Pfam (ref. no. PF10507) databases and lacks other conserved protein domains. Therefore, we generated adenoviruses encoding epitope-tagged TMEM65 mutant constructs and expressed them in AC16 cardiomyocytes to elucidate which structural components of TMEM65 are critical for its function (Fig. 3k and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Protein expression and mitochondrial localization for all mutant TMEM65 constructs was assessed using immunoblotting and immunofluorescence (Extended Data Fig. 4b–d). All mutant constructs were confirmed to express at levels comparable to WT TMEM65 and to localize to the mitochondria, with the exception of the TMEM65(ΔTM3-C terminus) mutant, which had very low expression and was excluded from the functional analysis. Deletion of the soluble matrix domain of TMEM65 (TMEM65(Δmatrix domain)) had no effect on the ability of TMEM65 to enhance mCa2+ efflux, as measured in permeabilized AC16 cardiomyocytes (Fig. 3l,m). By contrast, deletion of the second and third transmembrane helices and C terminus of TMEM65 (TMEM65(ΔTM2-C terminus)) ablated its function in mCa2+ efflux; the mCa2+ efflux rate in cells expressing this construct was not significantly different from control cells expressing LacZ and was significantly lower than in cells expressing epitope-tagged wild-type TMEM65 (Fig. 3l,m). This indicates that the first transmembrane helix of TMEM65 is insufficient to enhance mCa2+ efflux and suggests that its second or third transmembrane helices are required for this function. Interestingly, multiple sequence alignment of human TMEM65 with human NCLX, human Na+/Ca2+ exchangers and human Na+/Ca2+/K+ exchangers revealed similarity between the second transmembrane helix of TMEM65 and these related proteins (Fig. 3n). TMEM65 residues Asn163 and Asp167 aligned with NCLX N467 and D471, respectively. These two residues in the second ɑ-domain of NCLX are thought to participate in Ca2+ ion coordination and mCa2+ exchange26,27. Residues corresponding to human TMEM65 Asn163 and Asp167 are also highly conserved in known and predicted orthologues across taxa ranging from vertebrates to green algae, bacteria and archaea, and bacterial viruses (Fig. 3o). Mutation of these two amino acids (Asn163Ala, Asp167Asn) to disrupt their side chain structure and reduce the net electronegative charge in this region of the protein ablated the ability of TMEM65 to increase mCa2+ efflux (Fig. 3k-m). This finding indicates that this portion of the second transmembrane helix of TMEM65 is critical for its function in mCa2+ efflux. The homology between these residues and known cation-binding residues in NCLX raises the intriguing possibility that TMEM65 may introduce a new Ca2+-regulated or even Ca2+-exchanging site within a NCLX–TMEM65 complex (Fig. 3p).

Disruption of NCLX-dependent mCa2+ efflux predisposes to catastrophic mCa2+ overload, cell death and organ-level dysfunction in excitable tissues like the heart and brain3,4. Based on our conclusion that TMEM65 promotes NCLX-dependent mCa2+ efflux, we reasoned that genetic disruption of Tmem65 expression should recapitulate these aspects of NCLX disruption in vivo. Indeed, adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9)-delivery of short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting Tmem65 to neonatal mice reduced TMEM65 expression and decreased inhibitory PDH (Ser293) phosphorylation in skeletal muscle in 24-week-old adult animals (Fig. 4a–d), indicating that reduced TMEM65 expression is sufficient to increase mCa2+ loading in intact tissue. Chronic Tmem65 knockdown reduced skeletal muscle NCLX protein expression (Fig. 4a,c), suggesting that NCLX relies on TMEM65 for long-term stability in this tissue. It is possible that this chronic effect on NCLX expression was more pronounced in adult muscle than in our TMEM65−/− cell lines because of inherent differences in NCLX protein turnover rates in fully differentiated tissue in vivo versus immortalized cell lines in vitro, or simply because of the longer duration of the in vivo experiments. Tmem65 knockdown produced a robust neuromuscular phenotype evident as early as 3 weeks of age. Tmem65 knockdown mice exhibited a waddling gait and abnormal posturing of the hind limbs when suspended by the tail (Fig. 4e), both classical signs of neuromuscular dysfunction. Ambulatory function was grossly disrupted in adulthood (Supplementary Videos 1 and 2), and Tmem65 knockdown mice displayed a diminished functional capacity to hold on to an inverted wire grid when assessed via a four-limb wire hang test (Fig. 4f). Skeletal muscle fibre size and muscle mass (Fig. 4g,h and Extended Data Fig. 5a,b) were reduced in Tmem65 knockdown animals. Furthermore, the percentage of centrally nucleated skeletal muscle fibres, an index of recent fibre death and regeneration, tended to increase with loss of TMEM65 (Fig. 4i and Extended Data Fig. 5a). We likewise noted reduced TMEM65 expression in the hearts of Tmem65 knockdown mice as early as 6 weeks of age (Fig. 4j,k). Protein expression of full-length, mature, mitochondrial mouse NCLX (~62 kDa) trended downward but was not significantly reduced in Tmem65 knockdown hearts at this early time point (Fig. 4j,l). However, PDH phosphorylation was reduced in Tmem65 knockdown hearts (Fig. 4j,m), which is consistent with increased mCa2+ loading. Cardiac function was diminished in Tmem65 knockdown mice (Fig. 4n and Extended Data Fig. 5c,d) and correlated pairwise with the loss of TMEM65 protein expression (Fig. 4o). Our observations in the skeletal muscle and heart suggest that reduced TMEM65 expression elicits pathogenic mCa2+ overload in vivo that increases cell death and striated muscle dysfunction.

To mechanistically investigate the relationship between TMEM65 expression, mCa2+ overload, and cell death, we challenged permeabilized AC16 cells with repeated boluses of Ca2+. Wild-type cells exhibited a precipitous drop in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) and spontaneous release of mCa2+ after the delivery of ~10 Ca2+ boluses, whereas TMEM65−/− cells depolarized and released mCa2+ with fewer Ca2+ boluses (Fig. 4p and Extended Data Fig. 5e). Tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM) staining of intact cells revealed that loss of TMEM65 slightly reduced resting ΔΨm, and that this could be rescued by acute incubation with the mitochondrial permeability transition pore inhibitor cyclosporin A (Fig. 4q). These findings suggest that similar to genetic loss of NCLX, loss of TMEM65 increases the susceptibility to ongoing mCa2+ overload and increased mitochondrial permeability transition pore activity that can compromise mitochondrial function and culminate in cell death.

In calcium retention capacity (CRC) assays, TMEM65 overexpression modestly reduced net mCa2+ uptake with each successive Ca2+ bolus and resulted in a gradual decline in ΔΨm. However, TMEM65 overexpression completely prevented the full depolarization of ΔΨm and the abrupt release of mCa2+ characteristic of mitochondrial permeability transition with the administration of up to 20 Ca2+ boluses (Fig. 4r and Extended Data Fig. 5f). This result bears a striking resemblance to that expected with enhanced, electrogenic (three Na+ in: one Ca2+ out) forward-mode NCLX activity. Finally, we assessed the pathophysiological relevance of increased TMEM65 expression by assessing its effects on thapsigargin-induced or ionomycin-induced cell death in AC16 cardiomyocytes as a model for cytosolic Ca2+ and subsequent mCa2+ overload. TMEM65 overexpression was sufficient to limit cell death in response to 24-h thapsigargin or ionomycin treatment (Fig. 4s,t), demonstrating the power of enhanced TMEM65 expression to limit pathogenic mCa2+ stress.

Therapeutically enhancing NCLX activity has emerged as a desirable goal for the treatment of pathologies driven by mCa2+ overload, permeability transition, and cell death. Our in vitro findings identified TMEM65 as a potent enhancer of mCa2+ efflux and indicate that it mediates this function via binding to its interacting partner, NCLX. The observations that TMEM65 overexpression increases and that TMEM65 knockout diminishes Na+-sensitive mCa2+ efflux support this conclusion. Moreover, genetic deletion of NCLX mitigates TMEM65-dependent mCa2+ efflux, indicating that TMEM65 enhances mCa2+ efflux via, rather than independent of, NCLX. Our identification of TMEM65 in the NCLX interactome and the ability for TMEM65 to co-immunoprecipitate NCLX support a model in which TMEM65 physically interacts with NCLX to regulate mCa2+ exchange. We identified a highly conserved site within the second transmembrane helix of TMEM65, consisting of residues N163 and D167, as critical for this function. As TMEM65 has no known enzymatic activity nor domains resembling kinases or other regulatory proteins, we propose that the effect of TMEM65 on NCLX-dependent mCa2+ efflux is mediated by direct binding between these two proteins.

The neuromuscular and cardiac dysfunction that develops with partial knockdown of Tmem65 in vivo demonstrates the importance of this protein in excitable tissues. The additional observation that reducing TMEM65 expression in skeletal muscle and heart tissue results in pathogenic mCa2+ overload reinforces the conclusion that TMEM65 has a fundamental role in maintaining physiological mCa2+ homeostasis in the intact organism. The reduced NCLX protein expression in Tmem65 knockdown gastrocnemius provides further evidence that these two proteins depend on each other in adult muscle tissue. Notably, homozygous null mutation of TMEM65 in humans causes a mitochondrial encephalomyopathy with neurological deficits, muscle weakness and impaired mitochondrial energetics19, all phenotypes that are consistent with pathological mCa2+ overload. Complete genetic loss of NCLX is embryonic lethal3, precluding study of its functional consequences in vivo. However, we propose that TMEM65 is an essential positive regulator of NCLX and that the human TMEM65−/− phenotype described previously19 and the effects of Tmem65 knockdown we report in this study result from impairment of NCLX-dependent mCa2+ efflux. Finally, our finding that TMEM65 overexpression in vitro is sufficient to limit mCa2+ overload-induced cell death highlights TMEM65 as a translational target for diseases driven by pathogenic mCa2+ accumulation and cell dropout.

Our results are in broad agreement with concurrent findings by our colleagues that TMEM65 mediates Na+-sensitive Ca2+ efflux, and that disruption of TMEM65 function causes deleterious mCa2+ overload12,13. We note several discrepancies between the in vivo phenotypes observed with partial Tmem65 knockdown in postnatal mice and those seen by the Glancy laboratory with constitutive deletion of Tmem65. Gross motor dysfunction was not apparent with skeletal muscle-specific Tmem65 deletion12, raising the possibility of off-target motor neuron involvement in our mice with Tmem65 KD, or differential compensatory responses within the muscle, possibly involving the neuromuscular junction, upon full versus partial loss of TMEM65. The Glancy group did not detect contractile dysfunction in the hearts of postnatal day 21 mice with global Tmem65 deletion12; our finding of a modest impairment in cardiac pump function in 6-week-old Tmem65 knockdown mice may reflect a gradually developing cardiac phenotype that takes longer than 3 weeks of postnatal development to manifest, or distinct compensatory mechanisms activated with loss of TMEM65 during embryonic development versus with partial postnatal knockdown. Such differences in our approaches and mouse age at the time of analysis, or differences in the muscle type examined, may also explain why we observed downregulation of NCLX in 24-week-old Tmem65 knockdown gastrocnemius muscle, whereas our colleagues did not observe altered NCLX expression in the soleus muscle of 8-week-old animals with skeletal-muscle-specific Tmem65 deletion. Future studies should clarify the reasons for these discrepancies and better specify the role(s) of TMEM65 in developing versus adult tissues.

Our discovery of TMEM65 as a genetically confirmed regulator of NCLX provides mechanistic insight into the endogenous modulation of mCa2+ efflux and opens an avenue for the design of strategies to modulate NCLX activity that have thus far eluded the field. We further demonstrate the physiological relevance of TMEM65 in mitigating deleterious mCa2+ overload in vivo and in vitro, and conclude that modulation of TMEM65 or its interaction with NCLX (or both) could be an innovative approach for the therapeutic control of mitochondrial calcium homeostasis in human disease.
