18S nonfunctional rRNA decay (NRD) detects and eliminates translationally
nonfunctional 18S rRNA. Although this process is critical for ribosome quality
control, the mechanisms underlying nonfunctional 18S rRNA turnover remain
elusive, particularly in mammals. Here, we show that mammalian 18S NRD initiates
through the integrated stress response (ISR) via GCN2. Nonfunctional 18S rRNA
induces translational arrest at start sites. Biochemical analyses demonstrate
that ISR activation limits translation initiation and attenuates collisions
between scanning 43S preinitiation complexes and stalled nonfunctional
ribosomes. The ISR promotes 18S NRD and 40S ribosomal protein turnover by
RNF10-mediated ubiquitination. Ultimately, RIOK3 binds the resulting
ubiquitinated 40S subunits and facilitates 18S rRNA decay. Overall, mammalian
18S NRD acts through GCN2, followed by ubiquitin-dependent 18S rRNA degradation
involving the ubiquitin E3 ligase RNF10 and the atypical protein kinase RIOK3.
These findings establish a dynamic feedback mechanism by which the
GCN2-RNF10-RIOK3 axis surveils ribosome functionality at the translation
initiation step.

INTRODUCTION

The ribosome is central to controlling gene expression and translating genomic information into functional proteins. This process is governed by the decoding center of the small ribosomal subunit and the peptidyl transferase center of the large ribosomal subunit1. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, rRNAs carrying mutations that render the ribosome nonfunctional by impacting the decoding center of 18S rRNA or the peptidyl transferase center of 25S rRNA are downregulated through a late-acting rRNA quality control pathway termed nonfunctional rRNA decay (NRD)2,3. NRD targets defective ribosomal subunits for degradation through specific pathways that depend on the site affected by the mutation: 18S NRD eliminates nonfunctional 18S rRNAs and 25S NRD degrades nonfunctional 25S rRNAs2,3. While NRD is identified in S. cerevisiae, the molecular mechanisms associated with the recognition and degradation of nonfunctional rRNA remain obscure. Additionally, since these quality control pathways have been exclusively studied in budding yeast2-9, they remain unexplored in mammals.

In response to diverse cellular stress conditions, cells have evolved quality control mechanisms to detect homeostatic perturbations and elicit appropriate cellular responses. One common response involves repressing protein synthesis through the phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) by kinases that respond specifically to distinct stresses, known as eIF2α kinases10. In mammals, there are four eIF2α kinases— GCN2, PKR, PERK, and HRI. Activation of each kinase initiates the integrated stress response (ISR), leading to reduction of global translation initiation and upregulation of selected genes, including the essential bZIP transcription factor ATF4, required for downstream stress response pathways to promote cellular recovery11. Recent studies show that GCN2 can also be activated by collided ribosomes with a vacant A site in the leading ribosome, independently of uncharged tRNAs 12,13. Moreover, the ribosomal P-stalk, composed of uL10 and two heterodimeric P1, and P2, is critical to GCN2 activation independently of uncharged tRNAs 13-16. While these studies highlight the pivotal role of GCN2 in monitoring the status of elongating ribosomes to maintain cellular homeostasis, the mechanism underlying GCN2 activation remains elusive.

When ribosomes stall, a triage process, the ribosome-associated quality control (RQC) pathway, is activated17-19. Upon prolonged stalling on mRNAs, trailing ribosomes collide with stalled ribosomes, resulting in the formation of a new interface between the two collided small ribosomal subunits20. The E3 ubiquitin ligase, ZNF598 (Hel2 in yeast), recognizes this interface and catalyzes site-specific ubiquitination of ribosomal proteins eS10 (RPS10) and uS10 (RPS20)21-25. This ubiquitination is critical for recruiting the factors responsible for cleaving the mRNA26 and for splitting the stalled ribosomes into subunits for recycling22,26,27. Resolving ribosome collisions is essential to avoid general stress responses, including the ISR and the ribotoxic stress response (RSR)28,29, as well as innate immune signaling30,31.

Moreover, ubiquitination of ribosomal proteins uS3 (RPS3) and uS5 (RPS2) has been associated with various cellular stressors that abolish translation32 and the degradation of 40S ribosomal proteins33,34. Specifically, the ubiquitin E3 ligase, RNF10, initiates hierarchical ubiquitination of ribosomal proteins uS3 and uS5 to target stalled elongating ribosomes and stalled 43S preinitiation complexes (PIC) for elimination33,34. Together, these previous studies indicate that uS3/uS5 ubiquitination targets 40S ribosomal subunits for destruction. However, the mechanisms governing this process remain unclear. Moreover, Mag2, the yeast homologue of RNF10, has been shown to play a critical role in recognizing nonfunctional 80S ribosomes stalled at initiation codons in a recent study 5. These intriguing observations suggest that 18S NRD may operate in mammals with RNF10 playing a potential role in this process.

Here, we developed an orthogonal human 18S rRNA expression system to characterize 18S NRD in mammalian cells. Fitness-based CRISPR genetic screens identified a negative-feedback mechanism that regulates translation initiation to facilitate the ubiquitination of ribosomal proteins and hence the turnover of nonfunctional 18S rRNA. Specifically, nonfunctional 18S rRNA leads to prolonged ribosome arrest at start codons. These decoding-incompetent ribosomes activate the ISR through GCN2 to reduce translation initiation, thereby preventing collisions between scanning 43S preinitiation complexes (PICs) and decoding-incompetent ribosomes. This negative-feedback loop ensures that RNF10 targets these decoding-incompetent ribosomes for destruction. Moreover, we identified the ubiquitin-binding atypical kinase RIOK3 that recognizes ubiquitinated 40S subunits and facilitates their turnover by initiating degradation originating in the 5′, central, and 3′ minor domains of 18S rRNA.

RESULTSAn orthogonal rRNA expression system to study human 18S NRDTo systematically explore how mammalian cells cope with nonfunctional 18S rRNA, we developed an orthogonal human 18S rRNA expression system in which 18S rDNA is flanked by a human 5′ external transcribed spacer (5′ ETS) and internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) to ensure proper 18S rRNA maturation35 (Figure 1A). At the tip of helix 9 near the 5′ end of the orthogonal 18S rRNA, we inserted either a tandem MS2 hairpin (dubbed 18S-tMS2) for ribosome isolation5 or a hybridization hairpin (dubbed 18S-H) for detection by Northern blotting35 (Figure S1A). In vivo SHAPE RNA structure probing confirmed that helix 9 and both hairpin insertions are properly folded (Figures 1B and S1B-S1E).mRNA decoding by the ribosome involves the coordination of the universally conserved monitoring bases— G626, A1824, and A1825 (human numbering; G530, A1492 and A1493 in E. coli) in the decoding center of the 40S ribosomal subunit (Figure S1F) to facilitate codon-anticodon interactions1,36-38. Mutations at the equivalent of human A1824 in the 18S rRNA trigger 18S NRD in S. cerevisiae2,3. While NRD is not observed in bacteria39,40, whether regulated turnover of nonfunctional 18S rRNA occurs in mammals has not yet been tested. To address this, we introduced a single nucleotide mutation in the decoding center of the orthogonal 18S rRNA—specifically, A1824C. Northern blotting and primer extension experiments revealed a ~2.5-fold reduction in 18S:A1824C rRNA levels compared to 18S:WT (Figures 1C-1D and S1G). Moreover, we tested the benign mutation (U630G), which is located close to the decoding center but plays no role in decoding2. The U630G mutant showed steady-state levels similar to 18S:WT. In contrast, 18S:A1824C rRNA was consistently downregulated across multiple human cell lines, including HEK293 and HaCaT (Figure S1H).To assess the decay kinetics of 18S:WT and 18S:A1824C rRNA, we engineered HeLa cells that constitutively express the orthogonal 18S rRNA constructs and determined the levels of 18S:WT and 18S:A1824C rRNA to be ~8% and ~3% of the endogenous 18S rRNA, respectively (Figure S1I). 18S rRNA levels were monitored over a 24-hour period after halting transcription by the Pol I inhibitor (CX-5461). 18S:A1824C rRNA indeed underwent faster turnover (t1/2= 5.6 ± 1.8 h) than 18S:WT which remained stable over the experimental timeframe (t1/2> 24 h) (Figure 1E). The long half-life of the control is consistent with the known stability of mature 18S rRNA41. These data indicate that 18S NRD occurs in mammalian cells and define a robust system for interrogating this process.Decoding-incompetent ribosomes are predominantly arrested at translation start sitesWe next asked whether nonfunctional 18S rRNA can be assembled into functional 40S subunits and actively translating ribosomes. Polysome profiling followed by primer extension revealed deep sedimentation of both 18S:WT and A1824C rRNA in the 40S, 80S, and polysome fractions (Figure S2A), indicating that 18S:A1824C rRNA is incorporated into fully mature 40S subunits and 80S ribosomes similar to budding yeast2. To understand how decoding-incompetent ribosomes impact global translation, we performed selective ribosome profiling (Figures S2B-S2C). To preserve the inserted tMS2 tag, nuclease P1 was used for sample preparation 42, which yields longer ribosome-protected footprints (Figure S2D). We observed strong enrichment of footprints from purified mutant ribosomes at start codons throughout the transcriptome including, for instance, HIST1H1C mRNA (Figure S2E). We calculated the average ribosome occupancies at the start codons of all transcripts and found that mutant ribosome footprints were ~7-fold enriched in this region compared to wild-type ribosome footprints (Figure 1F). Moreover, when computing a pause score for each start codon, the median pause score increased by ~3-fold in the mutant ribosomes compared to wild-type ribosomes (Figure 1G). In addition to the start codons of main ORFs, mutant ribosome footprints are also enriched (~4-fold) at the start codons of upstream ORFs (uORFs) (Figure 1H). Together, these results indicate that 18S:A1824C rRNA is incorporated into mature 80S ribosomes, resulting in decoding-incompetent ribosomes that stall, predominantly at start codons, across the transcriptome.Fitness-based CRISPR screens identify GCN2 as an NRD componentUpon integrating the orthogonal 18S rRNA into cells, we noted that cells expressing 18S:A1824C rRNA (dubbed 18S:A1824C cells) consistently showed reduced cell fitness compared to those expressing 18S:WT rRNA (dubbed 18S:WT cells) (Figure S3A). This decrease occurs despite the relatively low abundance of 18S:A1824C (Figure S1I). We hypothesized that the difference in growth rate is likely due, in part, to the presence of nonfunctional 18S rRNA. Therefore, we took advantage of this cell fitness phenotype and performed a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screen43 to systematically identify genes associated with the cellular response to nonfunctional 18S rRNA. 18S:WT and 18S:A1824C cells were transduced with a library of single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting known protein-coding genes (19,114 genes). After culturing each population for 10 doublings, we deep-sequenced the remaining sgRNAs at four time points (0, 5, 7, and 10 doublings) (Figure 2A).While overall trends identified at these time points were similar, depletion of essential genes was more pronounced at the later time points (Figure S3B), indicative of the effectiveness of Cas9-mediated gene editing. When comparing the changes in sgRNA abundance between 18S:WT and 18S:A1824C cells after 10 doublings, we focused on genes whose loss-of-function further impeded cell fitness. A total of 116 genes were significantly depleted in the 18S:A1824C population (Figure 2B), and gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed strong enrichment for genes implicated in translation, rRNA processing, and translation initiation (Figure S3C; Table S1). Genes involved in 18S rRNA processing and maturation, including SKIV2L2, RRP7, RRP9, and NOC4L44, were among those identified (Figures 2B and S3D). Moreover, the screen also identified HBS1L (Figure S3D), whose yeast homologue is an established 18S NRD factor4. Indeed, short hairpin RNA (shRNA)-mediated knockdown of HBS1L resulted in accumulation of 18S:A1824C rRNA (Figure S3E).In addition to these factors, the screen revealed several genes with no known 18S NRD-related function. One of the four mammalian eIF2α kinases— GCN2 emerged as the most prominent hit and its co-activator, GCN1, was also significantly de-enriched in 18S:A1824C cells (Figures 2B, and S3F-S3G; Tables S1 and S2). Because GCN2 has not been implicated previously in cellular responses to nonfunctional 18S rRNA, we chose to focus on this gene. The cell growth phenotypes were validated by knocking out GCN2 in 18S:WT and 18S:A1824C cells. As expected, loss of GCN2 exacerbated the moderate growth defects in 18S:A1824C cells though it had no discernible effect on 18S:WT cells (Figure S3A).GCN2 and its effectors are required for 18S NRDGiven the hypothesis that nonfunctional 18S rRNA reduces cell fitness and this effect is exacerbated by GCN2 knockout (KO) (Figure S3A), we predicted that GCN2 plays a role in 18S NRD. Strikingly, we observed a marked ~3-fold increase in 18S:A1824C rRNA levels in cells knocked down or knocked out for GCN2 (Figures 2C-2D and S4A) while 18S:WT rRNA levels were unaffected. Complementing GCN2KO cells with wild-type GCN2, but not a catalytically inactive mutant (Km), led to 18S:A1824C rRNA degradation (Figures 2E-2F), implying that GCN2 kinase activity is critical for 18S NRD. Consistently, knockdown of GCN1 also stabilized 18S:A1824C levels (Figures S4B-S4C), consistent with our CRISPR screen results (Figures 2B and S3D).Multiple lines of evidence argue that the ribosomal P-stalk, positioned near the A site, is indispensable for GCN2-mediated eIF2α phosphorylation 13-15, particularly during amino acid starvation-independent ribosome stalling13. Given that both GCN2 and GCN1 are essential for 18S NRD, we wondered whether the ribosomal P-stalk might also play a role in 18S NRD. Using CRISPR/Cas9, we ablated the ribosomal P-stalk genes RPLP0 and RPLP1, which encode uL10 and P1, respectively. While RPLP0 ablation increased 18S:A1824C rRNA to levels comparable to 18S:WT, RPLP1 ablation showed a less pronounced effect (Figures 2G-2H). This is likely because sgRNA-mediated editing deletes the C-terminal helical spine of uL10, leading to disintegration of the ribosomal P-stalk protrusion composed of uL10 C-terminus and two heterodimeric P1 and P2 proteins (Figure S4D). These data are consistent with previous structural, genetic, and biochemical evidence showing direct association/binding of the GCN complex with the P-stalk proteins14,15,45. Collectively, ablation of GCN2 kinase activity and co-factors required for GCN2 activation consistently reduces 18S NRD activity, indicating that eIF2α phosphorylation by GCN2 is critical for 18S NRD.Decoding-incompetent ribosomes trigger GCN2-dependent eIF2α phosphorylation without inducing ribosome collisionsGCN2 regulates global translation initiation by phosphorylating eIF2α at serine 51 in response to various cellular stressors46. In light of the ribosome-centric mechanisms describing GCN2 activation independently of uncharged-tRNA12,13,28,47, we asked whether decoding-incompetent ribosomes trigger eIF2α phosphorylation. Immunoblotting showed a ~2.5-fold increase in eIF2α phosphorylation levels and a strong concomitant induction of ATF4 in 18S:A1824C-transfected cells, indicating that the ISR is activated (Figures 3A-3B). Importantly, 18S:A1824C rRNA did not induce eIF2α phosphorylation and ATF4 expression in GCN2KO and GCN1KO cells (Figure 3A), consistent with recent findings that GCN1 is essential for GCN2-dependent eIF2α phosphorylation in an amino acid starvation-independent manner12,13.Given that collided ribosomes with a vacant A site in the leading ribosome activate GCN2 independently of uncharged tRNAs12,13, we first asked whether decoding-incompetent ribosomes contain a vacant A site. To this end, we turned to RNA structure probing by dimethyl sulfate (DMS), which probes the accessibility of the Watson-Crick base-pairing interface of adenosine and cytosine. Aminoacyl-tRNA binding to the ribosomal A site shields nucleotides A1824 and A1825 (A1492 and A1493 in E. coli) in the 18S rRNA from DMS modification48. Quantifying the accessibility of these nucleotides can thereby assess whether the A site is vacant. We found a ~27-fold increase in the mutation rate (i.e. more accessible) at position A1825 in decoding-incompetent ribosomes relative to wild-type ribosomes (Figures 3C and S4E). These data indicate that an inactivating mutation in the decoding center prevents aminoacyl-tRNA binding in the A site, as recently shown in yeast 6.Ribosome collisions activate the ISR through GCN2 and the RSR through mitogen-activated triple kinase (MAPKKK), ZAKα28,29. However, it seems unlikely that decoding-incompetent ribosomes arrested at start codons would result in such 80S-80S collisions, since subunit joining only occurs when the scanning 43S preinitiation complex (PIC) reaches a start codon49. Indeed, ZAKα phosphorylation was not detected in GCN2KO cells expressing 18S:A1824C rRNA compared to cells treated with an intermediate dose of anisomycin that induces ribosome collisions (Figure S4F). Moreover, p38 phosphorylation, the downstream amplified signal output of the ZAKα signaling cascade, was absent in the 18S:A1824C-expressing cells (Figure S4F). We further verified the absence of 80S-80S ribosome collisions by performing disome profiling in GCN2-ablated cells that express 18S:WT or A1824C rRNA. As expected, there was no apparent accumulation of disome footprints at the start codons in the metagene analysis (Figure S4G).Considering the binding capacity of GCN1/20 for the ribosome50, we hypothesized that GCN1/20 may be recruited to decoding-incompetent ribosomes. Purification of wild-type and decoding-incompetent ribosomes followed by immunoblotting showed that GCN1 preferentially binds decoding-incompetent ribosomes compared to wild-type ribosomes (Figure 3D). While we did not observe direct binding of GCN2 to decoding-incompetent ribosomes, this association may be transient and/or compromised by steps such as RNase treatment during sample preparation. Altogether, these results indicate that decoding-incompetent ribosomes stalled at initiation sites do not induce 80S-80S ribosome collisions, and yet, their vacant A sites are sufficient to induce GCN2-mediated eIF2α phosphorylation.GCN2 ensures 18S NRD by finetuning translation initiationOur data point to a model where decoding-incompetent ribosomes trigger GCN2-dependent eIF2α phosphorylation to reduce ribosome loading onto mRNAs, thereby attenuating translation initiation to facilitate 18S NRD. We therefore evaluated whether eIF2α phosphorylation could complement loss of GCN2 and promote 18S NRD by titrating a plasmid encoding phosphomimetic eIF2αS51D into GCN2KO cells expressing 18S:A1824C rRNA. Strikingly, intermediate, rather than high, levels of phosphomimetic eIF2αS51D complemented GCN2KO and promoted 18S NRD whereas titration of a plasmid encoding non-phosphorylatable eIF2αS51A had no effect (Figures 3E-3G). Protein synthesis, as measured by puromycin incorporation, decreased drastically in eIF2αS51D-expressing cells at the high end of the titration curve, reflecting the anticipated reduction in global translation initiation associated with eIF2α phosphorylation (Figure 3F). In contrast, titration of non-phosphorylatable eIF2αS51A resulted in no significant changes in protein synthesis even though the expression levels of eIF2αS51A were higher than those of eIF2αS51D. These results reveal a “V-shaped” dose-response curve of 18S NRD activity in response to eIF2αS51D levels (Figure 3G), suggesting that an optimal regime of translation initiation is critical for the turnover of nonfunctional 18S rRNA (Figure S4H). Altogether, our results suggest that upon sensing the vacant A site of arrested decoding-incompetent ribosomes, the GCN complex maintains an optimal level of translation initiation for 18S NRD through finetuning eIF2α phosphorylation.Loss of GCN2 exacerbates start codon arrest resulting in 43S-80S collisionseIF2α phosphorylation reduces translation initiation and subsequent ribosome loading onto mRNAs. If GCN2 promotes the degradation of 18S A1824C rRNA through eIF2α phosphorylation, we reasoned that GCN2 ablation would lead to an accumulation of decoding-incompetent ribosomes at start codons. Indeed, selective ribosome profiling with tMS2-tagged ribosomes showed ~2-fold enrichment of decoding-incompetent ribosomes at the start codons of main ORFs (Figures 4A-4B) and uORFs (Figure S5A) in GCN2KO compared to parental cells, while there is no discernible enrichment of wild-type ribosomes. Additionally, we readily observed a moderate increase in the start codon peak in GCN2-ablated cells expressing 18S:A1824C rRNA even without enriching tagged ribosomes (Figure S5B), indicative of a mild perturbation in the translational landscape by decoding-incompetent ribosomes despite their low abundance.Given that 80S-80S ribosome collisions do not occur at start codons (Figure S4G), we hypothesized that a scanning 43S PIC may collide with an arrested decoding-incompetent 80S ribosome, thereby dampening nonfunctional 18S rRNA turnover. To monitor 43S-80S collisions directly during initiation, we purified wild-type and decoding-incompetent ribosomes from parental and GCN2KO cells, and size-selected fragments 40-80 nt in length to include 43S-80S footprints42. Metagene analyses using the 5′ or 3′ ends of the purified wild-type ribosome footprints reveal no noticeable enrichment around start codons in either parental (Figure 4C) or GCN2KO cells (Figure 4D). In contrast, footprints from purified decoding-incompetent ribosomes showed strong enrichment at start codons in GCN2KO compared to parental cells (Figures 4E-4F). The distance between the 5′ and 3′ ends of the isolated footprints is ~19 codons (57 nt) (Figure 4F), corresponding to the footprint length protected by 43S-80S collision complexes as reported recently in budding yeast42. Curiously, we noted that there are 43S-80S footprints downstream of the start codon in GCN2KO cells (Figure 4F, asterisks), suggesting that a fraction of the 43S-80S collision complexes might be bound by proteins, thereby generating variable footprint lengths. Additionally, the presence or absence of uORF-containing51 transcripts had no impact on the pause amplitude at start codons (Figures S5C), suggesting that the observed initiation collisions are pervasive rather than a result of leaky scanning at the stop codons of uORFs in GCN2KO cells. Collectively, these results indicate that scanning 43S PICs collide with decoding-incompetent ribosomes and that GCN2 curbs translation initiation to alleviate such collisions during translation initiation.Ubiquitination of decoding-incompetent ribosome depends on GCN2-RNF10 axisWe next asked whether RNF10 is involved in mammalian 18S NRD. This idea was motivated by recent studies showing that RNF10 ubiquitinates uS3/uS5 to trigger the degradation of 40S subunits33,34 and that its yeast homolog Mag2 ubiquitinates uS3 to initiate 18S NRD in yeast5,6. Indeed, RNF10 knockdown stabilized 18S:A1824C rRNA by ~4-fold (Figures S6A-S6B). Purified decoding-incompetent ribosomes exhibited higher levels of mono-ubiquitinated uS3 compared to wild-type ribosomes, and RNF10 knockout abolished uS3 ubiquitination (Figure 5A). Given that harringtonine induces RNF10-mediated uS3/uS5 ubiquitination33,34, we asked whether prolonged harringtonine treatment triggers 18S rRNA decay. Polysome profiles of harringtonine-treated cells showed ~20% reduction in 40S subunits when normalized to 60S subunits. Moreover, this imbalance in subunit stoichiometry was abolished in RNF10KO cells (Figure S6C). These results confirmed the reported role of RNF10 in targeting stalled 80S ribosomes for degradation33,34. We next evaluated whether GCN2 impacts RNF10-dependent ubiquitination of decoding-incompetent ribosomes. Strikingly, GCN2 knockout strongly reduced uS3 ubiquitination (Figures 5B and S6D), phenocopying the observed effect in RNF10KO cells.Loss of either GCN2 or RNF10 diminished uS3 ubiquitination and 18S NRD activity, suggesting a genetic interaction between GCN2 and RNF10 in degrading 18S:A1824C rRNA (Figure S6E). To test this, we carried out a fitness-based CRISPR genetic interaction screen as described above using GCN2KO cells expressing 18S:WT or A1824C rRNA. GCN2 ablation further exacerbated the growth defects imparted by 18S:A1824C rRNA (Figure S3A). Thus, loss of a positive genetic interactor of GCN2 would buffer the growth defects, resulting in greater fitness than expected, whereas loss of a negative genetic interactor would worsen the growth defects, resulting in a more severe fitness impairment than anticipated 52-54 (Figure S6E). We further verified the absence of 80S-80S ribosome collisions by performing disome profiling in GCN2-ablated cells that express 18S:WT or A1824C rRNA. As expected, there was no apparent accumulation of disome footprints at the start codons in the metagene analysis (Figure S4G).

Considering the binding capacity of GCN1/20 for the ribosome50, we hypothesized that GCN1/20 may be recruited to decoding-incompetent ribosomes. Purification of wild-type and decoding-incompetent ribosomes followed by immunoblotting showed that GCN1 preferentially binds decoding-incompetent ribosomes compared to wild-type ribosomes (Figure 3D). While we did not observe direct binding of GCN2 to decoding-incompetent ribosomes, this association may be transient and/or compromised by steps such as RNase treatment during sample preparation. Altogether, these results indicate that decoding-incompetent ribosomes stalled at initiation sites do not induce 80S-80S ribosome collisions, and yet, their vacant A sites are sufficient to induce GCN2-mediated eIF2α phosphorylation.

GCN2 ensures 18S NRD by finetuning translation initiationOur data point to a model where decoding-incompetent ribosomes trigger GCN2-dependent eIF2α phosphorylation to reduce ribosome loading onto mRNAs, thereby attenuating translation initiation to facilitate 18S NRD. We therefore evaluated whether eIF2α phosphorylation could complement loss of GCN2 and promote 18S NRD by titrating a plasmid encoding phosphomimetic eIF2αS51D into GCN2KO cells expressing 18S:A1824C rRNA. Strikingly, intermediate, rather than high, levels of phosphomimetic eIF2αS51D complemented GCN2KO and promoted 18S NRD whereas titration of a plasmid encoding non-phosphorylatable eIF2αS51A had no effect (Figures 3E-3G). Protein synthesis, as measured by puromycin incorporation, decreased drastically in eIF2αS51D-expressing cells at the high end of the titration curve, reflecting the anticipated reduction in global translation initiation associated with eIF2α phosphorylation (Figure 3F). In contrast, titration of non-phosphorylatable eIF2αS51A resulted in no significant changes in protein synthesis even though the expression levels of eIF2αS51A were higher than those of eIF2αS51D. These results reveal a “V-shaped” dose-response curve of 18S NRD activity in response to eIF2αS51D levels (Figure 3G), suggesting that an optimal regime of translation initiation is critical for the turnover of nonfunctional 18S rRNA (Figure S4H). Altogether, our results suggest that upon sensing the vacant A site of arrested decoding-incompetent ribosomes, the GCN complex maintains an optimal level of translation initiation for 18S NRD through finetuning eIF2α phosphorylation.

Additionally, the presence or absence of uORF-containing51 transcripts had no impact on the pause amplitude at start codons (Figures S5C), suggesting that the observed initiation collisions are pervasive rather than a result of leaky scanning at the stop codons of uORFs in GCN2KO cells. Collectively, these results indicate that scanning 43S PICs collide with decoding-incompetent ribosomes and that GCN2 curbs translation initiation to alleviate such collisions during translation initiation.

DISCUSSIONOur work reveals a negative-feedback regulatory mechanism by which cells finetune translation initiation to surveil and eliminate defective ribosomes engaged in translation. Specifically, expression of the nonfunctional human 18S:A1824C rRNA results in decoding-incompetent ribosomes arrested at start codons. These stalled ribosomes effectively trigger three distinct molecular events— GCN2-dependent eIF2α phosphorylation, RNF10-mediated ubiquitination of the stalled ribosomes, and RIOK3-mediated 18S rRNA degradation, all of which are required to eliminate nonfunctional 18S rRNA.Arrested decoding-incompetent ribosomes activate GCN2, leading to eIF2α phosphorylation and a subsequent dampening of translation initiation, enabling RNF10 to detect and flag decoding-incompetent ribosomes. The inverse correlation between the elevated levels of 43S-80S collisions (Figure 4F) and the reduction in RNF10-mediated ubiquitination (Figure 5B) in GCN2KO cells can be explained by a model where initiation collisions between scanning 43S PICs and decoding-incompetent ribosomes are prohibitory for RNF10-mediated ubiquitination, thereby impeding 40S subunit degradation and nonfunctional 18S rRNA turnover. After subunit splitting, RIOK3 recognizes ubiquitinated 40S subunits and facilitates degradation originating from the 5′, central, and 3′ minor domains of the 18S rRNA, possibly through a combination of exo- and endo-ribonucleolytic cleavages (Figure 7E).Our findings indicate that intermediate levels of phosphomimetic eIF2αS51D, as opposed to high levels, effectively complement the loss of GCN2 in degrading nonfunctional 18S rRNA (Figures 3E-3G), suggesting that 18S NRD activity requires optimal levels of translation initiation (Figure S4H). This finding is supported by our initial fitness-based CRISPR screen where ablation of translation initiation factors and ribosomal proteins results in reduced growth of 18S:A1824C cells (Figure S3C; Table S1). We propose that such levels of translation initiation are maintained by GCN2-mediated eIF2α phosphorylation to prevent 43S-80S collisions. This negative-feedback loop enables RNF10 to effectively surveil stalled ribosomes (Figure S4H, green box). In the absence of the initiation brake mounted by GCN2, unchecked initiation results in 43S-80S collisions, allowing defective ribosomes engaged in translation to evade the quality surveillance (Figure S4H, red box). Lastly, high levels of phosphomimetic eIF2αS51D deplete the eIF2 ternary complex and reduce 43S PIC formation and translation initiation (Figure S4H, grey box). Therefore, decoding-incompetent ribosomes are not engaged in a “test run” to examine their functionality and do not undergo decay. Our model is parsimonious and easily reconciles our findings with an earlier observation that ongoing translation is required for 18S NRD in budding yeast3.In experiments aimed to address how decoding-incompetent ribosomes activate GCN2-dependent eIF2α phosphorylation, we found that the decoding-inactivating mutation (A1824C) renders the ribosomal A site empty (Figure 3C). Translational perturbations utilized in numerous previous studies to interrogate the mechanisms of GCN2 activation, including translation elongation inhibitors, alkylating agents that damage mRNAs, and depletion of tRNA or termination factor, lead to vacant A sites and ribosome collisions during elongation. Our work reveals a negative-feedback regulatory mechanism by which cells finetune translation initiation to surveil and eliminate defective ribosomes engaged in translation. Specifically, expression of the nonfunctional human 18S:A1824C rRNA results in decoding-incompetent ribosomes arrested at start codons. These stalled ribosomes effectively trigger three distinct molecular events— GCN2-dependent eIF2α phosphorylation, RNF10-mediated ubiquitination of the stalled ribosomes, and RIOK3-mediated 18S rRNA degradation, all of which are required to eliminate nonfunctional 18S rRNA.

Arrested decoding-incompetent ribosomes activate GCN2, leading to eIF2α phosphorylation and a subsequent dampening of translation initiation, enabling RNF10 to detect and flag decoding-incompetent ribosomes. The inverse correlation between the elevated levels of 43S-80S collisions (Figure 4F) and the reduction in RNF10-mediated ubiquitination (Figure 5B) in GCN2KO cells can be explained by a model where initiation collisions between scanning 43S PICs and decoding-incompetent ribosomes are prohibitory for RNF10-mediated ubiquitination, thereby impeding 40S subunit degradation and nonfunctional 18S rRNA turnover. After subunit splitting, RIOK3 recognizes ubiquitinated 40S subunits and facilitates degradation originating from the 5′, central, and 3′ minor domains of the 18S rRNA, possibly through a combination of exo- and endo-ribonucleolytic cleavages (Figure 7E).

Our findings indicate that intermediate levels of phosphomimetic eIF2αS51D, as opposed to high levels, effectively complement the loss of GCN2 in degrading nonfunctional 18S rRNA (Figures 3E-3G), suggesting that 18S NRD activity requires optimal levels of translation initiation (Figure S4H). This finding is supported by our initial fitness-based CRISPR screen where ablation of translation initiation factors and ribosomal proteins results in reduced growth of 18S:A1824C cells (Figure S3C; Table S1). We propose that such levels of translation initiation are maintained by GCN2-mediated eIF2α phosphorylation to prevent 43S-80S collisions. This negative-feedback loop enables RNF10 to effectively surveil stalled ribosomes (Figure S4H, green box). In the absence of the initiation brake mounted by GCN2, unchecked initiation results in 43S-80S collisions, allowing defective ribosomes engaged in translation to evade the quality surveillance (Figure S4H, red box). Lastly, high levels of phosphomimetic eIF2αS51D deplete the eIF2 ternary complex and reduce 43S PIC formation and translation initiation (Figure S4H, grey box). Therefore, decoding-incompetent ribosomes are not engaged in a “test run” to examine their functionality and do not undergo decay. Our model is parsimonious and easily reconciles our findings with an earlier observation that ongoing translation is required for 18S NRD in budding yeast3.

In experiments aimed to address how decoding-incompetent ribosomes activate GCN2-dependent eIF2α phosphorylation, we found that the decoding-inactivating mutation (A1824C) renders the ribosomal A site empty (Figure 3C). Translational perturbations utilized in numerous previous studies to interrogate the mechanisms of GCN2 activation, including translation elongation inhibitors, alkylating agents that damage mRNAs, and depletion of tRNA or termination factor, lead to vacant A sites and ribosome collisions during elongation. In contrast to those agonists, decoding-incompetent ribosomes are predominantly arrested at start codons, characterized by a vacant A site without inducing detectable 80S-80S ribosome collisions in the 5′ UTRs. Such prolonged ribosome arrest is sufficient to activate the GCN2-dependent ISR (Figure 3A), suggesting that ribosome collisions are not strictly required for GCN2 activation. Integrating our results with recent studies, we speculate that GCN1 is recruited to stalled monosomes, thereby signaling GCN2 to activate the ISR (Figure 7E). Future structural and biophysical studies of the relationship between the GCN complex and the ribosome (stalled vs. collided) will provide spatially and temporally resolved insights into how these regulatory mechanisms are coordinated.

While there is evidence indicating cap-tethered 43S PIC scanning limits ribosome loading and hence 43S-80S collisions at start codons62, it is possible that mRNA caps become available to eIF4E given the long half-life of 18S:A1824C rRNA (Figure 1E) and potentially prolonged ribosome arrest at start codons (Figure 1F). We implemented the recent improvement in ribosome profiling42 by using nuclease P1 to capture 43S-80S footprints at start sites induced by decoding-incompetent ribosomes (Figure 4F). Footprints protected by 43S-80S collision complexes (~57 nt in length) are readily discernible from typical disome footprints (~72 nt in length)42, and this distinction will enable future efforts to explore the potential role of initiation collisions in the regulation of protein synthesis.

Notably, our model where 43S-80S initiation collisions impede the ability of RNF10 to ubiquitinate uS3 (Figure 7E) aligns with a recent finding that Fap1, the yeast ubiquitin E3 ligase responsible for elongating the ubiquitin chain on uS3, selectively targets stalled ribosomes but not collided ribosomes for elimination6. While uS3 does not appear to be polyubiquitinated in mammalian cells (32-34 and Figure 5A), we speculate that RNF10 regulates the substrate specificity of the 18S NRD quality control pathway for stalled monosomes rather than collided ribosomes. Overall, our finding that RNF10 flags decoding-incompetent ribosomes for elimination confirms prior studies of RNF10 in mammals33,34 and Mag2 in yeast5,6. Given that both initiation and elongation inhibitors trigger uS3 ubiquitination33,34, further investigation will reveal how RNF10 surveils its biological targets and directs them toward degradation.

Here we also provide evidence that 18S rRNA decay intermediates bound by RIOK3 originate from the 5′, central, and 3′ minor domains (Figures S7D-S7F). RIOK1, one of three members of the RIO kinase family, promotes final maturation of 40S subunits by releasing biogenesis factors from pre-40S particle 63-65. Moreover, ribosomal protein eS26 can be released from mature yeast ribosomes in response to stress 66,67. Based on these precedents, we speculate that RIOK3 displaces ribosomal protein(s) through its kinase activity when bound to ubiquitinated 40S subunits, thereby permitting ribonucleolytic cleavages in the 5′, central, and 3′ minor domains of the 18S rRNA. We noted that the decay intermediates whose 3′ ends are mapped within the helix 44 of 18S rRNA (G1750 in Figure 7A and C1752 in Figure S7D) are reminiscent of the 16S rRNA degradation intermediates by the 3′ to 5′ exoribonuclease RNase R 68. While further characterization will require future studies, this observation suggests a conserved decay pathway for small subunit ribosomal RNA in both bacteria and mammals, potentially implicating RNase R homologs in 18S rRNA decay.

Such studies will help reveal the molecular mechanisms by which ribonucleases and other auxiliary factors disassemble 40S ribosomal subunits during 18S NRD. Our genetic screens identified genes implicated in various biological processes, including the ubiquitin proteasome system and mitochondrial translation, the roles of which in 18S NRD will require future in-depth characterization.

Limitations of the study

A detailed mechanistic understanding of how RIOK3 facilitates 18S rRNA decay through its kinase activity will require in vitro biochemical reconstitution and structural analysis of 18S NRD intermediates. Such studies will help reveal the molecular mechanisms by which ribonucleases and other auxiliary factors disassemble 40S ribosomal subunits during 18S NRD. Our genetic screens identified genes implicated in various biological processes, including the ubiquitin proteasome system and mitochondrial translation, the roles of which in 18S NRD will require future in-depth characterization.
