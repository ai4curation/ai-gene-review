Picornaviruses, including foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV), enterovirus 71
(EV71) and encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV), are important pathogens that cause
fever, herpes, and myocarditis in humans and animals. The interplay between
picornaviruses and their hosts remains enigmatic. Here we perform porcine
genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screens and identify methionine sulfoxide reductase B3
(MSRB3) as an essential factor for FMDV. MSRB3 deficiency inhibits FMDV
replication. Mechanistically, MSRB3 eliminates methionine oxidation of FMDV 3D
polymerase and stabilizes its expression. Further studies show that radical SAM
domain-containing protein 1 (RSAD1) catalyzes methionine oxidation of FMDV 3D
polymerase and promotes its aggregation and subsequent degradation through the
autophagy-lysosome pathway. Importantly, RSAD1-MSRB3-mediated redox modification
also affects the stability of 3D polymerases of EV71 and EMCV, and regulates
their infectivity and pathogenesis both in vitro and in vivo. Collectively, this
study corroborates that RSAD1-MSRB3-mediated redox cycling of 3D polymerase
plays a conserved function in modulating picornavirus infection, providing
insights into viral pathogenesis and broad-spectrum antiviral development.

Introduction

Foot-and-mouth disease is an acute and highly contagious disease caused by foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV)1. FMDV is a non-enveloped single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus that belongs to the genus Aphthovirus in the family Picornaviridae2. It has a broad host range and infects more than 70 cloven-hoofed animals, including swine, cattle, sheep, and various ruminants3. The high infectivity and frequent variation of FMDV make it spread worldwide, causing devastating economic losses and extensive international trade embargoes4. In addition to FMDV, picornaviruses also include enterovirus 71 (EV71), encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV), poliovirus, and coxsackievirus, which share similar genome organization, virion structure, and life cycle5–7. The picornavirus genome contains a single open reading frame (ORF) flanked by a 5’ untranslated region (5’UTR) and a polyadenylated 3’UTR5. The ORF encodes a large polyprotein that is processed into four structural proteins (VP1, VP2, VP3, and VP4) and eight nonstructural proteins (L, 2A, 2B, 2C, 3A, 3B, 3C, and 3D) by viral proteases8,9. The 3D protein functions as an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) and is indispensable for viral genome replication10–12.

Radical S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) enzymes, including radical SAM domain-containing protein 1 (RSAD1) and RSAD2, contain a conserved CxxxCxxC motif within the central SAM domain and participate in a variety of chemical reactions involving numerous cellular pathways13,14. During the catalytic reaction, three cysteine residues within the motif generally coordinate a [4Fe-4S] cluster, leading to the generation of a 5’-deoxyadenosyl (5’-dA) radical by homolytic cleavage of the S-C(5’) bond in SAM ligands15,16. As a potent oxidant, the 5’-dA radical initiates oxidative catalysis by extracting the target hydrogen atom from enzyme-bound substrates. The resulting substrate intermediates undergo complex rearrangement to form the products17,18. Increasing evidence indicates that methionine residues in substrate proteins are susceptible to oxidation, which commonly occurs during biological reactions in vivo19. RSAD2, also known as viperin, catalyzes methionine oxidation of DNA or RNA helicases of viruses (ORF44 of KSHV) and hosts (MCM7 and RIG-I), thereby affecting their protein stability and function20. Mammalian RSAD1 shares sequence homology with the bacterial protein HemW, which functions as a heme chaperone and mediates the insertion of heme into respiratory chain enzymes21. To date, the enzymatic activity and catalytic substrates of RSAD1 have not been determined.

Recently, methionine oxidation has been identified as an important post-translational modification in mammals20. Methionine oxidation is a reversible reaction in which the sulfur of methionine is oxidized, and the methionine is rapidly converted to methionine sulfoxide (MetO), catalyzed by 5’-dA radicals22,23. Methionine sulfoxide reductases (MSRs), including class A (MSRA) and class B (MSRB1, MSRB2 and MSRB3), catalyze the reduction of free and protein-based MetO back to methionine19,23. Therefore, MSRs are important enzymes that can repair oxidatively damaged proteins and are involved in various physiological processes. It has been reported that MSRB3 functions as an antioxidant enzyme and protects cells from oxidative stress24,25. Methionine, a sulfur-containing amino acid residue, serves as a functional redox switch that cycles between oxidation and reduction states to regulate distinct signaling outcomes26,27. During viral infection, oxidoreductase-mediated redox cycling by targeting viral proteins or host machinery is a constant tug-of-war between viruses and hosts23,28. Accumulating evidence has shown that viral infection may involve more sophisticated redox signaling mechanisms beyond indiscriminate oxidative damage29,30. So far, it is unclear how redox modification regulates the infection of picornaviruses.

In this study, we report that the radical SAM enzyme RSAD1 catalyzes methionine oxidation of FMDV-3D and facilitates its degradation, thereby impairing FMDV replication. Furthermore, the reductase MSRB3 eliminates RSAD1-mediated methionine oxidation of FMDV-3D and enhances its stability. Further studies show that RSAD1-MSRB3-mediated redox modification plays a conserved role in regulating the replication of EV71 and EMCV in cells and mice by affecting the stability of their 3D polymerases. Our findings broaden the understanding of picornavirus-host interactions.

To determine how RSAD1 mediates the degradation of FMDV-3D, we tested various inhibitors commonly used to study protein degradation. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4b, c, RSAD1 overexpression- or MSRB3 deficiency-induced FMDV-3D degradation could be mostly restored by the autophagy inhibitors 3-methyladenine (3-MA), chloroquine (CQ), and bafilomycin A1 (BafA1), as well as the lysosome inhibitor ammonium chloride (NH4Cl), but not the proteasome inhibitor MG132, indicating that RSAD1 promoted FMDV-3D degradation mainly through the autophagy-lysosome pathway. In mammals, three types of autophagy have been documented, including macroautophagy, microautophagy, and chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA)37. To determine whether CMA is implicated in FMDV-3D degradation, we downregulated the expression of HSPA8, a core component of the CMA machinery38, and found that HSPA8 knockdown had little effect on RSAD1-mediated degradation of FMDV-3D (Supplementary Fig. 4d). We next investigated the role of macroautophagy. Since ATG5 and ATG7 are two essential genes for mammalian macroautophagy39,40, we examined whether their depletion affects FMDV-3D degradation. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4e, f, downregulation of ATG5 or ATG7 impaired RSAD1-induced degradation of FMDV-3D. These results imply that FMDV-3D is degraded mainly through the macroautophagy pathway. Macroautophagy can deliver diverse substrates, such as damaged organelles, abnormal protein aggregates and invading pathogens, to autophagosomes for lysosomal degradation via selective autophagy receptors, including SQSTM1, CALCOCO2, TOLLIP, NBR1, OPTN, and BNIP3L41–44. Therefore, we attempted to screen potential autophagy receptors responsible for FMDV-3D degradation. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4g–i, knockdown of TOLLIP but not the other indicated autophagy receptors rescued FMDV-3D degradation induced by RSAD1 overexpression or MSRB3 deficiency. To further corroborate these findings, we generated TOLLIP-deficient PK-15 cells and found that RSAD1-mediated FMDV-3D degradation was almost completely abrogated upon TOLLIP knockout (Fig. 4c). These findings suggest that TOLLIP is required for RSAD1-induced degradation of FMDV-3D.

TOLLIP consists of an N-terminal TOM1-binding domain (TBD), a central conserved phospholipid-binding domain 2 (C2) harboring two tandem LC3-interacting regions (LIRs), and a C-terminal coupling of ubiquitin to endoplasmic reticulum degradation domain (CUE)45 (Fig. 4d). To explore the contribution of these domains to RSAD1-mediated degradation of FMDV-3D, we generated several mutants of TOLLIP, including TBDmut (D20A, F21A, R23A), C2mut (R78A, R123A), LIRmut-1 (W133A, T134A, H135A, I136A) and LIRmut-2 (W151A, Y152A, S153A, L154A), CUEmut-1 (M240A, F241A), and CUEmut-2 (L267A, L268A), which are defective in binding with TOM1, phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P), LC3 or ubiquitin chains, respectively46,47. As shown in Fig. 4e, reconstitution of TOLLIP-deficient cells with TOLLIP and its mutant TBDmut, but not the other indicated mutants, restored RSAD1-mediated degradation of FMDV-3D, indicating that the binding abilities of TOLLIP to PI3P, LC3 and ubiquitin are required for its function in modulating FMDV-3D degradation. Based on the above data, we first detected the effects of MSRB3 or RSAD1 on autophagy. As shown in Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 4j, MSRB3 deficiency or RSAD1 overexpression consistently enhanced FMDV-induced autophagy. Moreover, MSRB3 deficiency or RSAD1 overexpression promoted the colocalization of viral 3D protein with LC3 upon FMDV infection (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 4k), which was further confirmed by quantitative analysis of multiple images (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 4l). Collectively, these results indicate that RSAD1 is involved in TOLLIP-mediated autophagic degradation of FMDV-3D.

It has been reported that the cargo receptor TOLLIP is committed to the selective autophagy pathway by capturing ubiquitinated substrates through its CUE domain and anchoring LC3-enriched phagophore via its LIR motif for autophagy-lysosomal degradation of the substrates48,49. Since the CUE domain of TOLLIP is essential for RSAD1-mediated degradation of FMDV-3D (Fig. 4e), we examined the ubiquitination of FMDV-3D and found that RSAD1 but not RSAD1-C3A significantly enhanced FMDV-3D polyubiquitination (Fig. 4i). Consistently, TRIM25 deficiency impaired RSAD1-mediated polyubiquitination of FMDV-3D (Supplementary Fig. 5b). RSAD1-MSRB3-mediated redox modification plays a conserved function in regulating picornavirus infectionIn view of the fact that FMDV-3D polymerase shares structural and functional similarities with the 3D polymerases of other picornaviruses51, we asked whether the RSAD1-MSRB3-mediated redox modification mechanism is widely involved in picornavirus replication. To verify this hypothesis, we first examined the effects of MSRB3 and RSAD1 on the replication of two representative picornaviruses, EV71 and EMCV. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6a, b, MSRB3 overexpression significantly potentiated EV71 and EMCV replication, whereas MSRB3 deficiency notably inhibited EV71 and EMCV replication. Additionally, RSAD1 overexpression markedly impaired the replication of EV71 and EMCV, while RSAD1 knockout obviously promoted the replication of EV71 and EMCV (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). These results indicate that MSRB3 and RSAD1 also regulate EV71 and EMCV replication. Subsequently, we explored whether MSRB3 and RSAD1 modulate picornavirus replication relying on their reductase or oxidase activity. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6e, reconstitution of MSRB3-deficient cells with MSRB3 but not MSRB3-C82A restored EV71 and EMCV replication as determined by a comparable level of progeny virus titer with control cells. Similarly, reconstitution of RSAD1-deficient cells with RSAD1 but not RSAD1-C3A restored the inhibitory effects on EV71 and EMCV replication (Supplementary Fig. 6f). These data demonstrate that the enzymatic activities of MSRB3 and RSAD1 are indispensable for their regulation of EV71 and EMCV replication. We then asked whether MSRB3 and RSAD1 regulate EV71 and EMCV replication by targeting viral 3D proteins. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6g, h, MSRB3 and RSAD1 affected the protein levels of 3D but not VP1 of EV71 and EMCV. Furthermore, MSRB3 but not MSRB3-C82A prolonged the half-life of 3D proteins of EV71 and EMCV, which was further confirmed by quantitative analysis of immunoblot band intensities (Supplementary Fig. 6i, j). RSAD1-MSRB3-mediated redox modification plays a conserved function in regulating picornavirus infection

In view of the fact that FMDV-3D polymerase shares structural and functional similarities with the 3D polymerases of other picornaviruses51, we asked whether the RSAD1-MSRB3-mediated redox modification mechanism is widely involved in picornavirus replication. To verify this hypothesis, we first examined the effects of MSRB3 and RSAD1 on the replication of two representative picornaviruses, EV71 and EMCV. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6a, b, MSRB3 overexpression significantly potentiated EV71 and EMCV replication, whereas MSRB3 deficiency notably inhibited EV71 and EMCV replication. Additionally, RSAD1 overexpression markedly impaired the replication of EV71 and EMCV, while RSAD1 knockout obviously promoted the replication of EV71 and EMCV (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). These results indicate that MSRB3 and RSAD1 also regulate EV71 and EMCV replication. Subsequently, we explored whether MSRB3 and RSAD1 modulate picornavirus replication relying on their reductase or oxidase activity. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6e, reconstitution of MSRB3-deficient cells with MSRB3 but not MSRB3-C82A restored EV71 and EMCV replication as determined by a comparable level of progeny virus titer with control cells. Similarly, reconstitution of RSAD1-deficient cells with RSAD1 but not RSAD1-C3A restored the inhibitory effects on EV71 and EMCV replication (Supplementary Fig. 6f). These data demonstrate that the enzymatic activities of MSRB3 and RSAD1 are indispensable for their regulation of EV71 and EMCV replication. We then asked whether MSRB3 and RSAD1 regulate EV71 and EMCV replication by targeting viral 3D proteins. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6g, h, MSRB3 and RSAD1 affected the protein levels of 3D but not VP1 of EV71 and EMCV. Furthermore, MSRB3 but not MSRB3-C82A prolonged the half-life of 3D proteins of EV71 and EMCV, which was further confirmed by quantitative analysis of immunoblot band intensities (Supplementary Fig. 6i, j). These data indicate that MSRB3 and RSAD1 regulate EV71 and EMCV replication by targeting viral 3D polymerases and affecting their stability.

Considering that RSAD1 regulates EV71 and EMCV replication depending on its SAM domain, we wondered whether RSAD1 targets viral 3D polymerases for oxidative modification. DiscussionAs the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase of FMDV, 3D protein is indispensable for viral genome replication. Although the catalytic mechanism of 3D polymerase has been extensively characterized, how its protein stability is maintained during infection remains unclear. In this study, porcine genome-wide knockout screens identified MSRB3 as an essential host factor for FMDV infection. Gain- and loss-of-function analyses demonstrated that MSRB3 was required for efficient replication of different serotypes of FMDV. Further assays showed that MSRB3 eliminated the oxidative modification of FMDV-3D at M194 and M403, thereby prolonging its half-life. Consistently, MSRB3 deficiency markedly impaired FMDV pathogenesis in mice. Altogether, these findings indicate that MSRB3-mediated reductive modification plays an important function in enhancing the stability of viral 3D polymerase and regulating efficient replication of FMDV.Methionine residues in proteins are vulnerable to attack by some oxidases and are rapidly converted to methionine sulfoxides, resulting in protein misfolding and degradation52. Conversely, some reductases catalyze the reduction of methionine sulfoxide back to methionine to avoid excessive protein damage23,26. Therefore, we wondered about the underlying mechanisms of 3D polymerase undergoing methionine oxidation during FMDV infection. We first identified a radical SAM enzyme, RSAD1, that binds to FMDV-3D using silver staining combined with mass spectrometry. Several lines of evidence suggest that RSAD1-catalyzed methionine oxidation exerts a critical role in limiting FMDV-3D stability and subsequent viral replication. First, RSAD1 deficiency obviously promoted FMDV replication, and reconstitution of RSAD1 but not RSAD1-ΔSAM into RSAD1-deficient cells restored the inhibitory effect on FMDV replication. Second, RSAD1-catalyzed methionine oxidation of FMDV-3D at M194 and M403 sites and facilitated its degradation, and MSRB3 reversed the inhibitory effect of RSAD1 on FMDV-3D stability. Third, the oxidized FMDV-3D was prone to aggregation, followed by degradation through the autophagy-lysosome pathway mediated by the TRIM25-TOLLIP axis. Fourth, mutation of M194/403 residues in FMDV-3D or deficiency of RSAD1 potentiated FMDV pathogenesis in mice. As the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase of FMDV, 3D protein is indispensable for viral genome replication. Although the catalytic mechanism of 3D polymerase has been extensively characterized, how its protein stability is maintained during infection remains unclear. In this study, porcine genome-wide knockout screens identified MSRB3 as an essential host factor for FMDV infection. Gain- and loss-of-function analyses demonstrated that MSRB3 was required for efficient replication of different serotypes of FMDV. Further assays showed that MSRB3 eliminated the oxidative modification of FMDV-3D at M194 and M403, thereby prolonging its half-life. Consistently, MSRB3 deficiency markedly impaired FMDV pathogenesis in mice. Altogether, these findings indicate that MSRB3-mediated reductive modification plays an important function in enhancing the stability of viral 3D polymerase and regulating efficient replication of FMDV.

Methionine residues in proteins are vulnerable to attack by some oxidases and are rapidly converted to methionine sulfoxides, resulting in protein misfolding and degradation52. Conversely, some reductases catalyze the reduction of methionine sulfoxide back to methionine to avoid excessive protein damage23,26. Therefore, we wondered about the underlying mechanisms of 3D polymerase undergoing methionine oxidation during FMDV infection. We first identified a radical SAM enzyme, RSAD1, that binds to FMDV-3D using silver staining combined with mass spectrometry. Several lines of evidence suggest that RSAD1-catalyzed methionine oxidation exerts a critical role in limiting FMDV-3D stability and subsequent viral replication. First, RSAD1 deficiency obviously promoted FMDV replication, and reconstitution of RSAD1 but not RSAD1-ΔSAM into RSAD1-deficient cells restored the inhibitory effect on FMDV replication. Second, RSAD1-catalyzed methionine oxidation of FMDV-3D at M194 and M403 sites and facilitated its degradation, and MSRB3 reversed the inhibitory effect of RSAD1 on FMDV-3D stability. Third, the oxidized FMDV-3D was prone to aggregation, followed by degradation through the autophagy-lysosome pathway mediated by the TRIM25-TOLLIP axis. Fourth, mutation of M194/403 residues in FMDV-3D or deficiency of RSAD1 potentiated FMDV pathogenesis in mice. Importantly, RSAD1 knockout eliminated the replication difference between rFMDV-WT and rFMDV-mut, indicating that RSAD1-mediated methionine oxidation is the decisive factor for the pathogenicity alteration of the rFMDV-mut strain. Collectively, our study determines the enzymatic activity of RSAD1 and establishes RSAD1 as a previously unrecognized host restriction factor that suppresses FMDV replication, enriching the biological functions of radical SAM family members in regulating viral infection.

Interestingly, several oxidized residues within FMDV-3D protein, including M66, M150 and M170, were independent of RSAD1 and MSRB3 (Supplementary Data 2 and 5). We initially speculated that these oxidized methionine residues were catalyzed by RSAD2, another member of the radical SAM family, but RSAD2 was absent from the FMDV-3D interactome and did not affect FMDV-3D stability, suggesting the existence of other unknown redox mechanisms orchestrating 3D polymerase homeostasis. RSAD2, an interferon-inducible protein, restricts the replication of a broad spectrum of viruses depending on its SAM domain53,54. It harbors an N-terminal amphipathic α-helix that anchors it to the lipid droplet organelles55, which are frequently exploited by enveloped viruses during replication56,57. Closely related to this structural feature, RSAD2 has been reported to target the helicases of some enveloped DNA viruses, including KSHV, HCMV and MHV68, and catalyze methionine oxidation of KSHV helicase ORF4420. However, the currently identified catalytic substrates of RSAD2 are limited. In contrast, RSAD1 cannot be induced upon viral infection and does not contain a lipid droplet binding motif13. These observations suggest that radical SAM family members may have evolved specific substrate selectivity, which is determined by their subcellular localization and structural characteristics. Identification of additional substrates and regulatory partners of RSAD1 and RSAD2 will be an important direction for future studies.

Redox homeostasis is fundamental for maintaining normal physiological functions of cells23,60,61. However, excessive oxidation leads to protein aggregation and cell dysfunction52,62,63. Eight-week-old sex-matched wild-type and Msrb3−/− or Rsad1−/− mice were challenged intraperitoneally with FMDV (2.8 × 106 PFU per mouse) or EMCV (7 × 105 PFU per mouse), and 5-day-old wild-type and Msrb3−/− or Rsad1−/− suckling mice were injected subcutaneously with EV71 (2 × 107 PFU per mouse). A subset of mice were monitored daily throughout the experiment for clinical symptoms and survival. Mice were euthanized when they showed crawling difficulty and no response to touch stimuli. Another subset of mice were necropsied on day 3 after FMDV, EV71, or EMCV challenge, and the tissues were collected immediately for viral load determination and histopathological analysis.

Determination of viral loads in tissuesTissues from mice infected with FMDV, EV71, or EMCV were weighed and homogenized, and total RNA was extracted with RNAiso Plus reagent. One Step PrimeScript RT-PCR Kit was used to determine viral genome copy numbers. Briefly, 2 μL (100 ng) of viral RNAs were used as templates for the synthesis of cDNAs. PCR mixtures (20 μL total volume) contained 10 μL 2× One Step RT-PCR Buffer III, 0.4 μL Takara Ex Taq HS, 0.4 μL forward primer (10  μM), 0.4 μL reverse primer (10 μM), 0.8 μL 3D probe (10  μM), and RNase-free dH2O. Results were monitored using the QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) programmed for one cycle of 15 min at 42 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 10 s at 95 °C, 10 s at 55 °C, and 30 s at 72 °C. The cDNA sequences encoding FMDV, EV71, or EMCV-3D proteins were cloned into PCMV14 vectors. Standard curves showing a linear relationship between 3D gene copies and CT values were established to quantify FMDV, EV71, or EMCV genome copies. The amplification primers and TaqMan probes used are listed in Supplementary Data 11.

Statistics and reproducibilityFor comparison of two groups, the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for statistical analysis, and the F test was performed to confirm that the two populations had the same variance. The Shapiro–Wilk normality test was performed to confirm the normal distribution of all datasets. For comparison of multiple groups, two-way ANOVA analysis followed by Dunnett’s test was performed. All data were representative of at least two independent experiments with similar results.
