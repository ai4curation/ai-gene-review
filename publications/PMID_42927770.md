# Structural basis of DnaB ring opening by the ancestral helicase loader DciA.

Structural basis of DnaB ring opening by the ancestral helicase loader DciA.

Araújo-Bazán L(1), Arias-Palomo E(1).

Author information:
(1)Centro de Investigaciones Biológicas Margarita Salas, CSIC, 28040 Madrid,

Ring-shaped helicases must be opened and loaded onto DNA to initiate genome
replication. In bacteria, most species lack canonical AAA+ helicase loaders and
instead rely on DciA, an evolutionarily conserved, ATP-independent factor whose
mechanism has remained unclear. Here we define how DciA remodels the bacterial
replicative helicase DnaB. A 3.5 Å cryo-EM structure reveals a DnaB6·DciA3
complex in which DciA binds the periphery of the helicase and stabilizes a
right-handed open-helical conformation with a single cracked interface. Three
DciA molecules dock onto alternate linker-helix/docking-helix modules, while one
N-terminal domain (NTD) engages the cracked interface, occupying the pocket
vacated by the displaced linker helix and acting as a wedge to promote ring
opening. Functional analyses reveal how DciA couples ATPase suppression with
efficient helicase loading through coordinated actions of its NTDs and
C-terminal domains. Together, these findings define a minimal, ATP-independent
mechanism for helicase loading and show how an ancestral loader remodels DnaB to
overcome a fundamental topological barrier.

Introduction

Faithful genome duplication relies on the ordered assembly of replication factors that unwind DNA and generate bidirectional replication forks [1–4]. A central step in this process is the loading of the ring-shaped replicative helicase at the chromosome origin of replication (oriC) [5–8]. In bacteria, this step requires recruitment of the DnaB helicase to the melted origin and its stabilization in a configuration poised for fork formation.

Bacterial DnaB is a hexameric helicase organized as a trimer of dimers, with the N-terminal domains (NTDs) forming three dimeric caps above the C-terminal RecA ATPase motor (Fig. 1A and B) [9–15]. The two tiers are connected by an extended linker helix (LH) that inserts into a docking/determinant helix (DH) of the adjacent subunit, forming the LH–DH module that coordinates movements within the hexamer. These interfaces support conformational transitions of the helicase, including the “dilated” and “constricted” NTD arrangements observed in the free hexamer and in loader-bound complexes (Fig. 1B) [15–19].

In some classic model systems, helicase loading is assisted by dedicated AAA+ proteins such as DnaC or DnaI, which form defined oligomeric complexes and engage the C-terminal tier of DnaB to regulate its conformational state during initiation [16, 17, 20–26]. In Escherichia coli, DnaC promotes a right-handed open-helical arrangement of DnaB and contacts the LH–DH interface through a short N-terminal helix [17, 27].

Most bacteria, however, lack DnaC- or DnaI-type AAA+ loaders. Comparative genomic analyses have shown that these canonical loaders are restricted to a limited number of bacterial phyla, whereas the vast majority of bacteria rely on DciA (Dna[CI] Antecedent), an essential replication factor conserved across bacteria, including numerous human pathogens (Supplementary Fig. S1) [28, 29]. Genetic and biochemical studies indicate that DciA interacts with DnaB and promotes helicase deposition at replication origins [30, 31]. Members of the DciA family share a conserved KH-like core domain, while their N-terminal and C-terminal extensions vary substantially among lineages [29, 32]. In several well-studied organisms, including V. cholerae, DciA contains a C-terminal lasso that binds the LH–DH region of DnaB and contributes to helicase recruitment (Fig. 1A) [33]. How this ancestral factor remodels the DnaB hexamer and whether its distinct domains contribute differentially to efficient helicase deposition remain unknown.

Here we address these questions using the V. cholerae DnaB–DciA system. Negative-stain electron microscopy shows that DciA shifts the helicase toward the constricted N-terminal arrangement associated with loading. A 3.5 Å cryo-EM reconstruction reveals an open DnaB6·DciA3 complex in which the C-terminal lassos dock onto alternate LH–DH modules and a single NTD inserts into the displaced LH pocket. Functional assays reveal distinct contributions of the two domains to ATPase suppression, helicase activation, and loading. Together, these results define the architecture and mechanism by which an ancestral, ATP-independent loader remodels DnaB to promote ring opening.

Model building and refinementThe final 3.5 Å resolution globally sharpened map obtained after RELION post-processing was submitted to ModelAngelo [41] to generate an initial model of the complex. The previously determined crystal structure of DnaB (PDB 8A3V), split into individual domains, was then rigid-body fitted onto the ModelAngelo output to build a complete hexameric DnaB model. An AlphaFold3 prediction of DciA was initially rigid-body docked into the focused classification map, which provided improved definition of the KH-containing N-terminal region of DciA monomer 1. The resulting model was subsequently refined against the globally sharpened consensus reconstruction together with the remainder of the complex. For DciA monomers 2 and 3, only the CTDs could be reliably modelled due to limited local resolution of the KH-containing N-terminal region. The complete complex was refined through iterative rounds of real-space refinement in COOT [42] and PHENIX-1.21 [43] against the globally sharpened experimental map, using Ramachandran, rotamer, geometry and secondary-structure restraints. Model quality was assessed using MolProbity (PHENIX), the PDB validation server (OneDep: https://validate-rcsb-1.wwpdb.org) and additional validation tools in COOT. All structural figures were generated using ChimeraX 1.10 [44].

The previously determined crystal structure of DnaB (PDB 8A3V), split into individual domains, was then rigid-body fitted onto the ModelAngelo output to build a complete hexameric DnaB model. An AlphaFold3 prediction of DciA was initially rigid-body docked into the focused classification map, which provided improved definition of the KH-containing N-terminal region of DciA monomer 1. The resulting model was subsequently refined against the globally sharpened consensus reconstruction together with the remainder of the complex. For DciA monomers 2 and 3, only the CTDs could be reliably modelled due to limited local resolution of the KH-containing N-terminal region. The complete complex was refined through iterative rounds of real-space refinement in COOT [42] and PHENIX-1.21 [43] against the globally sharpened experimental map, using Ramachandran, rotamer, geometry and secondary-structure restraints. Model quality was assessed using MolProbity (PHENIX), the PDB validation server (OneDep: https://validate-rcsb-1.wwpdb.org) and additional validation tools in COOT. All structural figures were generated using ChimeraX 1.10 [44].

Our findings differ from previous observations in which the isolated NTD did not exhibit detectable loading activity on a bubble DNA substrate [45]. This apparent difference may reflect the distinct DNA substrates and mechanistic steps probed by the two assays, suggesting that the contribution of the individual DciA domains may vary depending on the DNA substrate and the stage of the loading reaction being examined. These observations indicate that the CTD does not actively promote ring opening but instead enhances the efficiency of helicase loading, although the underlying mechanism remains unknown. Its contribution may reflect increased affinity for DnaB, stabilization of the loader-bound complex, or other aspects of the loading reaction. This division of labor is reminiscent of that of DnaC, whose AAA+ domain stabilizes the helicase-loader complex while its N-terminal arm pries it open [16].

Although unrelated in sequence and structure, DnaC, λP and DciA exploit the same mechanical gateway of DnaB. Each targets the LH–DH interface, inserting a short helical element that separates adjacent subunits. Thus, despite their distinct evolutionary origins, the DciA N-terminal wedge occupies the same topological pocket engaged by the N-terminal α-helix of DnaC and the λP loader (Fig. 5B) [17, 18]. The conservation of the opening interface, crack geometry, and nucleotide state across distinct loader systems indicates that ring opening is not imposed by the loader, but instead reflects an inherent regulatory property of the DnaB helicase itself. The shared target also explains why these loaders are mutually exclusive [28], and underscores that DnaB itself dictates the permissible path for ring opening. Our results suggest that loaders do not forcibly distort the helicase but rather exploit pre-existing breathing motions to capture a transiently displaced LH and stabilize the open state. Consistent with this view, in the E. coli DnaB•DnaC structures the LH–DH–loader module adopts an essentially identical geometry in both the open-helical (OH) and closed-helical (CH) conformations, except for the cracked subunit (Supplementary Fig. S9) [17]. A similar invariance is observed in the V. cholerae system, where the LH–DH–loader arrangement is preserved between the open-helical complex described here and the previously reported closed-planar DnaB•DciA crystal structure [33]. This observation indicates that ring opening does not rely on cumulative deformation of the module itself, but rather on the loader acting as a wedge at the crack site to capture and stabilize a transiently open configuration. This “breathing-capture” mechanism contrasts with the previously proposed “distortion-accumulation” model [27], though both may cooperate: local strain may initiate separation, while the loader traps a naturally sampled open configuration. In this sense, DciA acts as a passive mechanical ratchet that may convert spontaneous helicase fluctuations into a long-lived, loading-competent state.

In evolutionary terms, the DnaB•DciA structure likely represents the ancestral bacterial solution to helicase loading. Comparative genomics indicates that DciA is ubiquitous and predates the acquisition of AAA+ loaders [28]. The minimal DciA architecture appears to contain the essential elements required for helicase regulation, with accessory regions tuning the efficiency and specificity of loading. Although the N-terminal and C-terminal extensions vary considerably across the four DciA subfamilies [29], representative homologs from each group retain the short N-terminal segment immediately preceding the KH domain, corresponding to the wedge identified in the V. cholerae structure (Supplementary Fig. S10), suggesting that this structural feature is broadly conserved across the DciA family. Our structure provides a framework for understanding the role of this region in DciA-mediated helicase loading, while the variable terminal extensions may provide additional docking or regulatory functions in specific lineages. This interpretation is supported by mutational and deletion analyses of the N-terminal region, which demonstrate that disruption of this domain abolishes DciA activity [31, 47]. The subsequent appearance of AAA+ loaders such as DnaC and DnaI can thus be viewed as elaborations that added regulatory control to a pre-existing mechanical core. DciA provides a window into this early stage of evolution, highlighting that ATP-independent factors can provide an endogenous solution for helicase loading in bacteria.

Overall, our findings show that DciA binds and remodels DnaB through a bridging-and-wedging mechanism that combines recognition, inhibition, and activation within a single small polypeptide. This ancestral mechanism not only explains how bacterial replication initiation occurs in the absence of AAA+ loaders but also reveals the shared structural logic underlying ring-opening reactions across evolution. Future work should clarify how DciA-mediated DNA binding contributes to helicase loading and release, and how its recruitment at the origin coordinates with other replication factors to establish bidirectional replication forks.
