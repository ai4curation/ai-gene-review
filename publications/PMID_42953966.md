Bacterial non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) is a key DNA double-strand break
(DSB) repair pathway that relies on the Ku-LigD complex to alleviate genomic
instability. The Mycobacterium tuberculosis Ku C-terminus has been highlighted
for its role in LigD recruitment to DSBs and stimulation of LigD ligase
activity. However, it remains unclear how the Ku C-terminus interacts with and
potentially influences other LigD activities. Here, we combine NMR spectroscopy,
structural modelling, and mutational analysis to define the interaction
interface between the Ku C-terminus and LigD. We identify critical residues in
Ku (E246, V248, S258, K260, and N266) and the LigD polymerase (D162, V194, R198)
and ligase (D522, K579, L580) domains that mediate this interaction. Functional
assays reveal that Ku stimulates LigD ligase activity through contacts with both
polymerase and ligase domains, while Ku attenuates template-dependent polymerase
activity, contrasting previous studies in Pseudomonas aeruginosa homologs.
Disrupting the Ku-LigD interface, either through Ku or LigD mutations, abolishes
ligase stimulation and restores polymerase activity, highlighting a dual
regulatory mechanism. Our data supports a model where Ku's C-terminal region
forms a bipartite interface with LigD to balance repair activity. These findings
provide mechanistic insight into the Ku-LigD repair mechanism and uncovers
species-specific differences in bacterial NHEJ.

Introduction

Bacterial genomes are constantly exposed to environmental pressures that cause DNA damage, including double-strand breaks (DSBs)—the most lethal form of DNA damage [1, 2]. Bacteria can use non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) repair to mitigate this damage [1, 2], through Ku, a DNA end-binding protein, and LigD, an ATP-dependent ligase with additional enzymatic functions [3]. Ku binds and stabilizes DNA ends and recruits its functional partner, LigD, to carry out the repair [1, 4].

Bacterial Ku contains an N-terminal core domain, homologous to the eukaryotic Ku70/Ku80 core domain, which binds DNA through its central pore, and a unique C-terminal region absent in eukaryotic counterparts (Fig. 1A) [3, 5]. This C-terminal region can be subdivided into a minimal conserved segment directly following the core, and a highly variable extended region that differs in length and amino acid composition across bacterial species [4, 6, 7]. Despite this variability, the Ku C-terminal region plays essential roles in DNA end recognition, bridging, translocation, and serves as the primary binding site for LigD [4, 6, 7].

Comparative domain architectures of bacterial Ku and LigD proteins. (A) Ku domain arrangement from model bacterial species M. tuberculosis (UniProt KB: P9WKD9). (B) LigD protein domain alignment from model bacterial species M. tuberculosis (UniProt KB: P9WNV2), P. aeruginosa (UniProt KB: Q9I1 × 7), B. subtilis (UniProt KB: O34398), and M. smegmatis (UniProt KB: A0R3R7). LigD domain arrangement varies between bacterial species.

LigD is unique among ligases as it is a multifunctional enzyme comprised of three domains: an ATP-dependent ligase domain, a polymerase domain, and a 3′-phosphoesterase domain [4, 8]. The ligase domain seals nicks in the phosphodiester backbone, while the phosphoesterase domain processes atypical 3′-phosphate DNA ends into 3′-hydroxyl groups for ligation and can also resect 3′ overhangs at DSBs [9–11]. The polymerase domain is capable of carrying out both template-dependent and template-independent DNA synthesis. Notably, the LigD polymerase domain preferentially incorporates rNTPs rather than dNTPs, while the ligase domain efficiently seals breaks with a single rNTP at the break site [12, 13].

The enzymatic domains of LigD vary in their composition and arrangement across bacterial species (Fig. 1B). For example, Bacillus subtilis LigD lacks the phosphoesterase domain, acting as a bifunctional enzyme [4, 7], while Pseudomonas aeruginosa LigD’s domain arrangement is shifted relative to Mycobacterium tuberculosis LigD. Such structural differences likely influence how Ku and LigD interact and how Ku affects LigD activity across species. Notably, the Ku C-terminal region has been identified as the binding hub for the LigD polymerase domain in both M. tuberculosis and B. subtilis, emphasizing the polymerase domain’s central role in forming the NHEJ repair complex [6, 7].

Previous studies have shown that Ku stimulates LigD’s polymerase activity in P. aeruginosa [12] and enhances ligase activity in M. tuberculosis and B. subtilis [6, 7, 14, 15]. It is understood that LigD is recruited to DNA DSBs through interactions with Ku, forming a multiprotein repair complex that coordinates DNA end protection, alignment, processing, and ligation. Therefore, the enzymatic activity of LigD is likely regulated within the structural and functional context of the Ku–LigD–DNA complex, rather than an isolated enzyme. It remains unclear, though, how Ku influences LigD’s polymerase activity in M. tuberculosis, and which specific interactions between the Ku C-terminal region and the polymerase domain of LigD are required to form and regulate the Ku–LigD repair complex. Understanding this regulatory interface is key to deciphering species-specific mechanisms of bacterial NHEJ and the balance between repair fidelity and efficiency.

Here, we take a quantitative approach to investigate how disruption of the M. tuberculosis Ku–LigD interaction affects LigD function. We find that the Ku C-terminal region forms multiple contacts with the polymerase domain of LigD, which are critical for Ku-stimulated ligase activity. Notably, we show that Ku attenuates LigD template-dependent polymerase activity, differing from the activity observed with homologs from P. aeruginosa, and that disrupting these Ku C-terminal interactions restores polymerase function—suggesting that Ku acts as a negative regulator of LigD’s template-dependent polymerase activity. Furthermore, we identify residues in both the polymerase (POL) and ligase (LIG) domains of LigD that interact with the Ku C-terminal region and contribute to the dual regulation of LigD activity. We propose that Ku enhances ligase activity when the Ku C-terminus binds to the polymerase domain. This interaction appears to also repress template-dependent polymerase activity, suggesting Ku regulates LigD ligation and polymerase activities, to coordinate DNA repair.

DNA polymerase assayDNA polymerase assays were performed for the Ku mutant proteins and the LigD mutant proteins using an assay adapted from Zhu et al. (2010) [12]. Twenty-microliter reaction mixtures containing polymerase buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, and 5 mM DTT), 25 µM of each dNTP (dATP, dTTP, dCTP, and dGTP), and 40 nM of a fluorescein-labelled 18-bp DNA substrate with an 18-nucleotide (nt) overhang were incubated with 0.5 µM LigD in either the presence or absence of 1 µM Ku at 37°C for 5 min. Samples containing Ku were allowed to pre-incubate with the reaction buffer and DNA for 10 min prior to adding LigD to allow Ku to interact with DNA. Reactions were quenched at 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, and 5 min, with 20 μl of polymerase quench buffer (98% formamide, 10 mM EDTA pH 8.0), and denatured at 95°C for 10 min prior to resolving on a 20% denaturing PAGE gel. Electrophoresis was performed in 1× Tris-Borate-EDTA buffer at 200 V for 2 h to resolve the samples. Gels were imaged using an Amersham Typhoon (GE Healthcare) and analysed using ImageJ. Experiments were performed in n = 3 technical replicates, and rates of polymerization were calculated using simple linear regression using Prism v.10.4.1 (GraphPad). P-values were calculated by a two-tailed Welch’s t-test in Prism v.10.4.1 (GraphPad). Polymerase activity was quantified by densitometric analysis of denaturing PAGE gels using ImageJ. For each time point, the fluorescence intensity of the DNA substrate band and the total fluorescence intensity of all extended DNA products were measured. Background fluorescence was measured from an equivalent blank region of the gel and subtracted from each measurement. The fraction of DNA converted to extended product at each time point was calculated relative to the total DNA substrate input by the total DNA signal at the initial time point (t = 0), which represents the DNA substrate alone. This fraction was then converted to the concentration of extended DNA product by multiplying the total DNA substrate concentration in the reaction (input DNA = 10 nM). The concentration of polymerized DNA product was plotted as a function of time (min), and the initial rate of polymerization was determined from the slope of the linear regression fit. Because quantification was based on conversion of substrate to product rather than absolute florescence intensity of individual product bands, differences in product length were not directly accounted for, and instead are measured as a total fluorescent signal across all extended DNA products.

LigD ligase domain interactions contribute to the Ku–LigD binding interface

To biochemically characterize the predicted LigD ligase domain interface identified by the AlphaFold3 model, we introduced charge-reversal mutations at LigD residues D522, K579, and L580. These mutations were independently supported by Morati et al. [25], who qualitatively examined the effect of these mutations on Ku-stimulated ligation activity. Here, we wanted to investigate whether these residues contribute to the Ku–LigD ligase domain interaction interface and whether perturbing this interface affects LigD enzymatic activity in a similar manner that we used to assess the polymerase domain interface. The secondary structure of all LigD ligase domain mutants were confirmed to be comparable to wild-type LigD by CD spectroscopy prior to functional characterization (Supplementary Fig. S19).

To determine whether these effects were associated with changes in Ku–LigD binding, we measured the interaction between Ku and each LigD ligase domain mutant using MST. LigD D522R and K579E exhibited increased Ku-binding affinity (KD = 0.054 ± 0.005 µM), and LigD L580E had decreased binding affinity for Ku (KD = 1.27 ± 0.15 µM) compared to wild-type LigD (Fig. 9A, B; Supplementary Table S14 and Supplementary Fig. S13). These results illustrate that changes to the interaction affinity between the Ku C-terminus and the LigD ligase domain correspond with Ku’s ability to efficiently stimulate ligase activity.

DiscussionPrevious studies have established that Ku enhances the enzymatic activities of LigD, with M. tuberculosis Ku stimulating ligation and P. aeruginosa Ku stimulating both ligation and polymerization [6, 26]; however, the molecular details of this interface remain undefined. In this study, we applied a structure-informed approach to characterize the Ku–LigD interaction, providing mechanistic insights into Ku-mediated repair activities associated with NHEJ. We find that the M. tuberculosis Ku C-terminal region forms important contacts with both the polymerase and ligase domains of LigD, and that these interactions contribute to enhanced ligation activity and attenuated polymerase activity.Due to the absence of an experimental structure of the Ku–LigD–DNA complex, we used AlphaFold3 [27] modelling to predict the architecture of the M. tuberculosis Ku–LigD complex bound to DNA (Fig. 7). Consistent with the reported cryo-EM structure of Ku bound to DNA, the core domain of the Ku homodimer threads onto DNA, forming a tightly bound complex to DNA ends [28]. Notably, this AlphaFold3 model predicts that the C-terminus of one Ku monomer contacts the polymerase domain of LigD, while the second C-terminus engages the ligase domain, suggesting a bipartite interaction interface. This model aligns with our previous biochemical data showing that Ku interacts with both the LigD ligase and LigD polymerase domains with a similar affinity [6].In alignment with this structural model, our NMR and MST protein-binding studies (Fig. 2) identified key residues in the Ku C-terminus (E246, V248, S258, and K260) predicted to interact with the LigD polymerase domain. Ku N266 is also important for interacting with LigD, as Ku N266A did not interact with LigD (Fig. 2); however, Ku N266 does not appear to interact with LigD in the AlphaFold3 model (Fig. 7). AlphaFold models of the Ku homodimer alone (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/entry/AF-0000000065975835) show the C-termini are predicted to be flexible, given the low confidence prediction for these regions [24, 29–32]. Therefore, it is feasible that an altered conformation from the AlphaFold3 model may exist where Ku N266 engages with LigD. Functionally, we found these Ku C-terminal amino acids, along with previously characterized Ku S258 [6], are needed for high rates of ligation (Fig. 3), as mutations to these amino acids abolish or reduce Ku-stimulated ligation.Previously, we and others have suggested that interactions between Ku and the LigD polymerase domain were necessary to stimulate ligation activity, where a purified LigD ligase domain did not have enhanced ligase activity in the presence of Ku, indicating that the polymerase domain was needed. However, we also observed a Ku–LigD ligase domain interaction of similar affinity to the Ku–LigD polymerase domain interaction [4, 6, 13, 14, 26, 33]. Here, we demonstrate that interactions between Ku and the LigD ligase domain are also necessary for Ku-stimulated ligation (Fig. 9). In particular, LigD D522R and L580E mutations altered Ku binding and reduced Ku-stimulated ligation, suggesting that while the polymerase domain may serve as one Ku docking interface, specific residues within the ligase domain also contribute to stabilizing the Ku–LigD complex and enabling Ku-stimulated ligation.While prior studies in P. aeruginosa have shown that Ku enhances LigD template-dependent polymerase activity [12], our findings revealed that this mechanism is not conserved in M. tuberculosis. Instead, we observed that the addition of Ku led to a modest, but consistent attenuation of LigD template-dependent polymerase activity, which appears to be mediated by specific Ku C-terminus-LigD contacts, as disrupting residues E246 and S258 restored polymerase activities comparable to LigD alone (Figs 4, 5, and 7). Interestingly, Ku V248 did not influence polymerase activity, indicating that not all residues critical for the Ku–LigD interaction play a role in polymerase regulation. We hypothesize this attenuation may be advantageous during NHEJ by limiting potentially mutagenic DNA synthesis prior to ligation, while still allowing LigD to stabilize and process DNA ends. Reduced polymerase engagement may also represent a species-specific adaptation in M. tuberculosis to limit mutations introduced by the error-prone polymerase domain, a selective pressure that would be particularly relevant in slow-growing organisms, which may also explain the difference in composition of Ku C-terminal regions between M. tuberculosis and P. aeruginosa [34, 35]. Of the five residues identified here, only S258 is strongly conserved, while V248 and K260 (I248 and R260 in P. aeruginosa) conserve similar side-chain properties [6, 7]. Unpublished data from our research group also shows that the interaction affinity between P. aeruginosa Ku and LigD is weaker than that of the M. tuberculosis homologs, which may be attributable to these differences in the Ku C-terminus. Collectively, these findings suggest a species-specific regulatory divergence in bacterial NHEJ.Unexpectedly, Ku K260A led to a significant decrease in polymerase activity, similar to the Kucore protein (Figs 5 and 7), contradicting our hypothesis that a loss of interaction between Ku and LigD would restore polymerase activity. Prior work has shown that the Ku C-terminal tail is needed to translocate along DNA [6, 14, 26], and studies from our laboratory using atomic force microscopy show Kucore trapped at DNA ends, similarly limiting Ku-stimulated UvrD1 helicase activity (Warner et al., manuscript in revision). Previous studies have established that Ku enhances the enzymatic activities of LigD, with M. tuberculosis Ku stimulating ligation and P. aeruginosa Ku stimulating both ligation and polymerization [6, 26]; however, the molecular details of this interface remain undefined. In this study, we applied a structure-informed approach to characterize the Ku–LigD interaction, providing mechanistic insights into Ku-mediated repair activities associated with NHEJ. We find that the M. tuberculosis Ku C-terminal region forms important contacts with both the polymerase and ligase domains of LigD, and that these interactions contribute to enhanced ligation activity and attenuated polymerase activity.

Due to the absence of an experimental structure of the Ku–LigD–DNA complex, we used AlphaFold3 [27] modelling to predict the architecture of the M. tuberculosis Ku–LigD complex bound to DNA (Fig. 7). Consistent with the reported cryo-EM structure of Ku bound to DNA, the core domain of the Ku homodimer threads onto DNA, forming a tightly bound complex to DNA ends [28]. Notably, this AlphaFold3 model predicts that the C-terminus of one Ku monomer contacts the polymerase domain of LigD, while the second C-terminus engages the ligase domain, suggesting a bipartite interaction interface. This model aligns with our previous biochemical data showing that Ku interacts with both the LigD ligase and LigD polymerase domains with a similar affinity [6].

In alignment with this structural model, our NMR and MST protein-binding studies (Fig. 2) identified key residues in the Ku C-terminus (E246, V248, S258, and K260) predicted to interact with the LigD polymerase domain. Ku N266 is also important for interacting with LigD, as Ku N266A did not interact with LigD (Fig. 2); however, Ku N266 does not appear to interact with LigD in the AlphaFold3 model (Fig. 7). AlphaFold models of the Ku homodimer alone (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/entry/AF-0000000065975835) show the C-termini are predicted to be flexible, given the low confidence prediction for these regions [24, 29–32]. Therefore, it is feasible that an altered conformation from the AlphaFold3 model may exist where Ku N266 engages with LigD. Functionally, we found these Ku C-terminal amino acids, along with previously characterized Ku S258 [6], are needed for high rates of ligation (Fig. 3), as mutations to these amino acids abolish or reduce Ku-stimulated ligation.

Previously, we and others have suggested that interactions between Ku and the LigD polymerase domain were necessary to stimulate ligation activity, where a purified LigD ligase domain did not have enhanced ligase activity in the presence of Ku, indicating that the polymerase domain was needed. However, we also observed a Ku–LigD ligase domain interaction of similar affinity to the Ku–LigD polymerase domain interaction [4, 6, 13, 14, 26, 33]. Here, we demonstrate that interactions between Ku and the LigD ligase domain are also necessary for Ku-stimulated ligation (Fig. 9). In particular, LigD D522R and L580E mutations altered Ku binding and reduced Ku-stimulated ligation, suggesting that while the polymerase domain may serve as one Ku docking interface, specific residues within the ligase domain also contribute to stabilizing the Ku–LigD complex and enabling Ku-stimulated ligation.

While prior studies in P. aeruginosa have shown that Ku enhances LigD template-dependent polymerase activity [12], our findings revealed that this mechanism is not conserved in M. tuberculosis. Instead, we observed that the addition of Ku led to a modest, but consistent attenuation of LigD template-dependent polymerase activity, which appears to be mediated by specific Ku C-terminus-LigD contacts, as disrupting residues E246 and S258 restored polymerase activities comparable to LigD alone (Figs 4, 5, and 7). Interestingly, Ku V248 did not influence polymerase activity, indicating that not all residues critical for the Ku–LigD interaction play a role in polymerase regulation. We hypothesize this attenuation may be advantageous during NHEJ by limiting potentially mutagenic DNA synthesis prior to ligation, while still allowing LigD to stabilize and process DNA ends. Reduced polymerase engagement may also represent a species-specific adaptation in M. tuberculosis to limit mutations introduced by the error-prone polymerase domain, a selective pressure that would be particularly relevant in slow-growing organisms, which may also explain the difference in composition of Ku C-terminal regions between M. tuberculosis and P. aeruginosa [34, 35]. Of the five residues identified here, only S258 is strongly conserved, while V248 and K260 (I248 and R260 in P. aeruginosa) conserve similar side-chain properties [6, 7]. Unpublished data from our research group also shows that the interaction affinity between P. aeruginosa Ku and LigD is weaker than that of the M. tuberculosis homologs, which may be attributable to these differences in the Ku C-terminus. Collectively, these findings suggest a species-specific regulatory divergence in bacterial NHEJ.

Unexpectedly, Ku K260A led to a significant decrease in polymerase activity, similar to the Kucore protein (Figs 5 and 7), contradicting our hypothesis that a loss of interaction between Ku and LigD would restore polymerase activity. Prior work has shown that the Ku C-terminal tail is needed to translocate along DNA [6, 14, 26], and studies from our laboratory using atomic force microscopy show Kucore trapped at DNA ends, similarly limiting Ku-stimulated UvrD1 helicase activity (Warner et al., manuscript in revision). We propose that a comparable effect occurs with Ku K260A, where Ku K260 is necessary for efficient Ku translocation, and when mutated, Ku K260A has reduced translocation, hindering the LigD polymerase domain access to the DNA. Supporting this theory, Ku K260A shows an 8-fold increase in affinity for dsDNA relative to wild-type Ku (Fig. 6), similar to Kucore DNA-binding affinity and consistent with a defect in translocation.

In light of these findings, that Ku regulates LigD activity, a critical open question is how a bipartite Ku–LigD interaction would function within an NHEJ complex, where two broken DNA ends must be held in proximity for ligation. Recent cryo-EM studies provided the first structural views of mycobacterial Ku in higher-order DNA-bound assemblies and identified a Ku-mediated DNA synapsis mechanism unique to prokaryotes [28, 36]. This work revealed that Ku forms higher-order oligomeric super complexes on DNA, with multiple Ku dimers bridging two DNA molecules in a configuration consistent with synapsis of broken ends. These higher-order oligomeric super complexes are also seen in cryo-EM models of B. subtilis Ku [37], suggesting that this DNA synapsis mechanism may be a conserved feature of prokaryotic NHEJ, distinct from the eukaryotic system where synapsis is achieved through a more elaborate multi-subunit apparatus. Importantly, the Ku C-terminal tails were not part of the cryo-EM structures, consistent with the known flexibility of this region, and supports the idea that the Ku C-terminus remains available to engage LigD.

With this synaptic assembly, comprised at a minimum of two Ku dimers and two DNA ends, it remains unclear whether a single LigD molecule or two LigD molecules are recruited for repair. The bipartite interaction model emerging from our data, where the two C-terminal tails of a Ku homodimer independently engage the polymerase and ligase domains of LigD raises the possibility that a single LigD can be positioned at the DNA double-strand break by one Ku dimer. Alternatively, the symmetric arrangement of two Ku dimers in the synaptic complex could accommodate two LigD molecules, one per DNA strand break end. The flexibility of the Ku C-terminal tails could help orient LigD to carry out the necessary DNA end-processing reactions (addition or removal of nucleotides) prior to ligation. This idea is supported by structural and biochemical models of classical NHEJ that suggest a synaptic complex may accommodate two XRCC4–Ligase IV complexes, each with a ligase positioned to act on one DNA end [38–40]. Distinguishing between these models is an important goal for future structural work on the Ku–LigD–DNA complex.

In summary, our findings define a dual regulatory role for the Ku C-terminal region in modulating LigD enzymatic activity during NHEJ (Fig. 12). We propose that in bacterial NHEJ, Ku dimers recognize and form a synaptic complex at the DNA DSB. The Ku C-terminus, including K260, then facilitates translocation along DNA to make room for LigD at the break site. The synaptic complex of Ku may recruit one or more LigD proteins. Depending on the damage at the break site, Ku may attenuate LigD polymerase activity through interactions with the ligase domain and stimulate ligation through bipartite contacts with both the polymerase and ligase domains, likely modulating the conformation of the protein complex on DNA. Whether this happens with one or more LigD proteins first modifying the DNA ends prior to ligation remains to be determined. This dual regulatory function, stimulating ligation while suppressing potentially mutagenic polymerization, contrasts with the purely stimulatory activity described in P. aeruginosa highlighting a mechanistic divergence in bacterial NHEJ regulation. Together, these insights enhance our understanding of the molecular basis of Ku–LigD cooperation in NHEJ, illustrating how species-specific adaptations fine-tune DNA repair mechanisms.

Loss of Ku-stimulated ligation through mutations shown here to the Ku C-terminus, or the LigD ligase or polymerase domains, should increase M. tuberculosis sensitivity to ionizing radiation for cells in late stationary phase grown in minimal media, similar to observations for NHEJ-deficient M. smegmatis [41]. The impaired Ku–LigD interaction would also be expected to increase polymerase error rates, due to loss of Ku-attenuated polymerization, similar to the repair of chromosomal DSB breaks in M. smegmatis, when HR is repressed, resulting in mutations at the break site [41].

The Ku–LigD interface also has direct translational relevance for phage therapeutics. Recent work has demonstrated that mycobacterial NHEJ is critical for antiphage defence, with NHEJ-deficient strains showing increased susceptibility to phage infection, as evidenced by elevated plaque counts and reduced bacterial survival [42]. Since phage-induced DSBs must be repaired through NHEJ to restore host viability, disrupting the Ku C-terminus–LigD interaction offers a rational strategy to suppress this repair capacity and potentiate phage-based therapeutics, a particularly promising strategy given the rise of multidrug-resistant M. tuberculosis.
