Abstract

Leucocyte adhesion receptor integrin CD11aCD18 and the transmembrane receptor-like protein tyrosine phosphatase (RPTP) CD45 mediate immune synapse formation and signalling during antigen presentation. Previous cocapping studies on human naïve T cells demonstrate an interaction between CD11aCD18 and CD45. CD45 cross-linking also has an effect on the ligand-binding activity of CD11aCD18. However, the mode of interaction between CD11aCD18 and CD45 remains unclear. Herein, yeast two-hybrid analysis identified a partial CD45 cytoplasmic tail interacting with that of CD11a. The CD45 cytoplasmic tail comprises a membrane proximal (Mp) region, protein tyrosine phosphatase domain 1 (D1), spacer, D2, and carboxyl terminus. CD45 Mp-D1 was found to be the main interacting region for the CD11a cytoplasmic tail. In contrast, the full-length CD45 cytoplasmic tail interacted weakly with that of CD11a. It has been reported that CD45 Mp-D1 but not the full-length cytoplasmic tail forms a homodimer whose enzymatic activity is inhibited. Our in vitro binding and enzymatic assays showed that the homodimeric CD45 cytoplasmic tail interacts with that of CD11a. The biological function of CD45 dimerization and its association with CD11a remains to be investigated.

Keywords: adhesion molecules, protein kinases/phosphatases, cell surface molecules

Introduction

Integrins comprise a family of type I transmembrane heterodimeric (αβ) adhesion molecules required for a wide repertoire of biological processes. 1 , 2 The leucocyte-restricted CD11CD18 integrins are composed of four members namely CD11aCD18 (leucocyte function-associated antigen-1, αLβ2), CD11bCD18 (Mac-1, CR3, αMβ2), CD11cCD18 (p150,95, CR4, αXβ2), and CD11dCD18 (αDβ2).

Increasing evidence indicate the α cytoplasmic tail determines integrin functional specialization. Integrin CD49bCD29 (very late antigen-2 (VLA-2), α2β1) showed differential cell migration on collagen and laminin when the CD49b cytoplasmic tail was replaced by that from integrin CD49d or CD49e. 3 Integrin CD49dCD29 (VLA-4, α4β1) exhibited differential adhesion properties to ligand vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 under shear flow analyses when CD49d cytoplasmic tail was exchanged with that from integrin CD49b or CD49e. 4 Of note, the CD11 cytoplasmic tails also conferred CD11aCD18 and CD11bCD18 different activation kinetics upon chemokine stimulation suggesting CD11a and CD11b cytoplasmic tails interacting with different molecules. 5

We performed yeast two-hybrid screening with a human leucocyte library and integrin CD11a cytoplasmic tail as bait. We report association of integrin CD11a cytoplasmic tail with that of CD45 (leucocyte-common antigen), a prototypic type I transmembrane receptor-like protein tyrosine phosphatase (RPTP) with ubiquitous expression on all nucleated haematopoietic cells and is critical for T- and B-cell development in response to antigens. 6 Multiple CD45 isoforms (180 000–220 000 MW) exist as a result of cell-type specific alternative splicing of exons 4, 5, and 6, commonly referred to as A, B, and C. 7 Multiple O-linked glycosylation sites are encoded in these exons, hence the isoforms differ substantially not only in dimension (ectodomain: ∼28–51 nm) and tertiary structure but also in negative charges, which are implicated in its homodimerization. 8

To date, attempts to identify CD45 counter-receptors have been largely unsuccessful. However, a list of membrane-bound molecules CD2, CD4, CD26, CD90, T-cell receptor (TCR), interferon receptor α, and lymphocyte phosphatase-associated phosphoprotein are found to associate non-covalently with CD45. 9 CD45 and CD11aCD18 are reported to colocalize in the supramolecular activation cluster (SMAC) of the immune synapse during antigen presentation 10 and cross-talk between these molecules demonstrated. 11 – 15 Co-capping analyses in phytohaemagglutinin preactivated human T lymphocytes revealed interaction between CD45RA and CD11aCD18. 16 However, the mode of interaction between CD45 and CD11aCD18 remains unclear. Here, we extend our two hybrid analyses to investigate the specificity and sites of interaction between CD45 and CD11aCD18 cytoplasmic tails.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

Human embryonic kidney (HEK) epithelial 293T cells and human T-lymphoblast cells MOLT-4 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA). Mammalian expression constructs pcDNA3 containing full-length integrin CD11a, CD11b, CD11c, and CD18 were described previously. 17 CD11a cytoplasmic tail cDNA was PCR-cloned from human leucocyte cDNA library (Clontech, BD Biosciences, CA). Mammalian expression vector pXJ40-HA (haemagglutinin) was reported previously. 18 , 19 The monoclonal antibodies (mAb) MHM24 (CD11a specific) 20 , 21 was kindly provided by Prof. A. J. McMichael (John Radcliffe Hospital, Oxford, UK). IB4 (CD11CD18 integrins heterodimer-specific mAb) 22 and MEM48 (CD18-specific, activating mAb) were described previously. 23 Anti-CD45 mAb clone 69 was from BD Biosciences. The following antibodies were purchased from various sources: rabbit anti-c-Myc antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO); rabbit anti-HA antibody (Delta Biolabs, Campbell, CA); horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit antibody (Amersham, Piscataway, NJ); fluoroscein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated sheep anti-mouse F(ab′) 2 secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich). Unless otherwise stated, all other reagents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

DNA constructions

The constructions of two-hybrid vector pGBKT7 CD11a (Lys 1088 –Asp 1145 ), pGEX-CD11a (Lys 1088 –Asp 1145 ) and variants, pGEX-CD11b (Lys 1114 –Gln 1137 ), pGEX-CD11c (Lys 1110 –Lys 1144 ), pGEX-CD11d (Lys 1108 –Ser 1145 ), pGEX-CD18 (Lys 702 –Ser 747 ), partial- and full-length HA-tagged CD45 cytoplasmic domain in mammalian expression vector pXJ40-HA followed standard recombinant DNA procedures. Amino acids are numbered according to full-length protein sequence excluding the signal peptide. 24 Site directed mutagenesis was performed using the Quikchange XL kit (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA) with appropriate complementary primers encoding the amino acid in concern. All constructs were verified via sequencing (Research Biolabs, Singapore).

Yeast two-hybrid screening

Yeast two-hybrid screening was performed according to manufacturer's instructions using the Matchmaker GAL4 two-hybrid system 3, yeast strain AH109, and human leucocyte AD/cDNA library (Clontech, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). Reagents and amino acids required for making standard dropout (SD) plates for prototrophy screening were obtained from USBIO (MA) and Sigma-Aldrich. The pGBKT7 CD11a plasmid, which has the CD11a cytoplasmic tail fused to the GAL4 DNA binding domain was transformed into AH109 via the lithium acetate method and selected for tryptophan prototrophy. Surviving transformants were screened for expression of GAL4 DNA BD-CD11a fusion by immunoblotting with rabbit anti-c-Myc antibody. The human leucocyte AD/cDNA library, having leucocyte cDNAs cloned into pACT2 plasmids, was transformed into AH109 transformant expressing high level GAL4 DNA BD-CD11a fusion protein, and selected for leucine and histidine prototrophy for 5–7 days at 30°. Transformation efficiency was 2·2 × 10 3 c.f.u./µg library DNA. Self-activation by pGBKT7 CD11a alone was tested negative. Colonies were further selected for adenine prototrophy and surviving TRP1 + , LEU2 + , HIS3 + , and ADE2 + colonies were subjected to X-α-Gal filter assay for detection of MEL1 activation. Total plasmids were extracted from these putative positive interaction yeast colonies and transformed into Escherichia coli MC106 competent cells by electroporation followed by isolation of the pACT2-cDNAs. To retest the positive interaction, individual clone of pACT2-cDNAs was cotransformed with pGBKT7 CD11a into AH109 and analysed aforementioned. Positive and negative controls were included. After confirmation of interaction, pACT2 × cDNAs were sequenced (Research Biolabs) and analysed by comparison to GenBank sequences.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK 293T cells were grown in media Dulbecco's modified Eagle's minimal essential medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) HI (heat inactivated)-fetal bovine serum (FBS, 100 IU/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin (JRH Biosciences Inc, Victoria, Australia) at 37°, 5% CO 2 . Plasmids (5 µg each) were transfected into 293T cells using the calcium phosphate method. 25 , 26 Transfected cells were detached with 0·5 m m ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for subsequent assays. MOLT-4 cells were grown in media RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% (v/v) HI-FBS, 100 IU/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin (JRH Biosciences) at 37°, 5% CO 2 .

Flow cytometric analysis

Flow cytometric analysis was performed as reported previously. 27 Briefly, cells were incubated with 20 µg/ml primary mAb in cell growth-media for 1 hr at 4°. Cells were washed once and incubated with 1 : 400 dilution of FITC-conjugated sheep anti-mouse F(ab′) 2 secondary antibody for 45 min at 4°. Stained cells were washed once in cell growth-media and fixed in 1% (v/v) formaldehyde in PBS. Cells were analysed on a FACSCalibur flow cytometer system (Becton Dickinson, San Jose, CA). Data were analysed using the CELLQuest software (Becton Dickinson).

Coimmunoprecipitation and detection

Coimmunoprecipitation was performed essentially as described with minor modifications. 27 Briefly, 293T cells cotransfected with different pXJHA-CD45 constructs together with pcDNA3-CD11a, pcDNA3-CD11b, or pcDNA3-CD11c with pcDNA3-CD18 were lysed in lysis buffer (100 m m NaCl, 0·5 m m MgCl 2 , 0·15 m m CaCl 2 , 1%(v/v) NP-40, 10 m m Tris-HCl, pH 8·0) containing appropriate protease inhibitors, and cell debris was pelleted at 10 000 g for 10 min at 4°. Lysate was precleared with an irrelevant mAb (5 µg, isotype matched to mAb IB4) and Protein-A/G Sepharose mixture (Sigma-Aldrich). Thereafter, lysate was immunoprecipitated with mAb IB4 (5 µg) and Protein-A/G Sepharose mixture. The immunoprecipitated complex was resolved on a 12% sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel under reducing-conditions followed by immunoblotting.

For coimmunoprecipitation using MOLT-4 cells, 1 × 10 7 cells was lysed in lysis buffer (150 m m NaCl, 1% (v/v) Triton-X-100, 20 m m Tris-HCl, pH 7·4) containing appropriate protease inhibitors and immunoprecipitation performed essentially as described above except the anti-CD45 mAb clone 69 and anti-CD18 mAb MEM48 were used. Immunoblotting to detect CD45 was performed with mAb clone 69. Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) stimulation of MOLT-4 was performed by growing cells in the presence of 100 ng/ml PMA for 24 hr.

Glutathione-S-transferase (GST) fusion protein expression

GST fusion constructs containing the integrin CD11a (and variants), CD11b, CD11c, CD11d or CD18 cytoplasmic tails were expressed in E. coli DH5α following induction with 1 m m isopropyl β- d -thiogalactopyranoside. Bacteria were lysed by sonication in PBS and protease inhibitors. Fusion proteins were purified from lysates by adsorption to glutathione-conjugated Sepharose and eluted with reduced gluthathione (Amersham). Protein concentration was determined by standard Bradford assay (BioRad Laboratories, Hercules, CA).

ELISA-based GST pull-down assay

GST-fusion protein (3 µg for each sample) in 50 m m bicarbonate buffer pH 9·0 (50 µl) was dispensed into each well of the Polysorb microtitre plate (Nunc, Roskilde, Denmark) and incubated overnight at 4°. Wells were washed twice in PBS and non-specific binding sites were blocked in 3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin for 1 hr at 37°. 293T cells expressing HA-tagged CD45 cytoplasmic fragments were lysed followed by anti-HA immunoblotting to ensure comparable level of protein expression. The lysates were incubated with the coated GST-fusion protein overnight at 4°. Solutions were decanted and wells were washed three times in PBS. Rabbit anti-HA antibody (0·25 µg) was added to each well and incubated for 2 hr at room temperature. Solutions were decanted and wells were washed three times in PBS. Alkaline phosphatase (AP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G secondary antibody (50 µl/well; 1 : 5000 dilution) and p -nitrophenylphosphate ( p -NPP) substrate (Sigma-Aldrich) were used for detection. Colorimetric measurement was performed at 405 nm using a Benchmark system plus microplate spectrophotometer (BioRad).

Fluorimetric measurement of cd45 activity

Activity of CD45 cytoplasmic fragments bound to GST-CD11a was measured directly in the microtitre plate. Measurement was performed in reaction buffer containing 30 µ m fluorescein diphosphate (FDP; Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR), 50 m m dithiothreitol, 0·1 m m CaCl 2 , 50 m m Tris-HCl, pH 7·0 for 90 min at 37°. 28 Fluorescence, which corresponds to CD45 activity, was measured using a FL600 fluorescent plate reader (Bio-Tek Instruments, Winooski, VT) with 485/530 nm filters. The procedure for protein coating and capture was described under enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Inhibition of CD45 activity was performed in the presence of 1 m m sodium vanadate (Na 3 VO 4 ; Sigma-Aldrich).

Results

Yeast-two hybrid screening of proteins interacting with the CD11a cytoplasmic domain

The cDNA encoding the CD11a cytoplasmic tail (Lys 1088 –Asp 1145 ) was subcloned into plasmid pGBKT7 generating the bait plasmid pGBKT7 CD11a ( Fig. 1a ). An approximately 32 000 MW protein band, which was consistent with the predicted MW of GAL4BD-Myc-CD11a fusion protein (referred as BD-CD11a henceforth), was detected in lysate of yeast AH109 transformed with pGBKT7 CD11a ( Fig. 1b ). Controls were untransformed AH109 and AH109 transformed with pGBKT7. AH109 transformant expressing high level of BD-CD11a was employed for yeast-two hybrid screening using a human leucocyte AD/library.

Figure 1.

A total of 1·4 × 10 6 TRP1 + , LEU2 + colonies were screened of which 305 colonies were also HIS3 + . From these, 20 colonies (αLC1–αLC20) were ADE2 + and tested positive for X-α-gal filter lift assay indicating MEL1 reporter gene activation ( Figs 1c, d ; data not shown). The AD/library plasmids were rescued from these colonies and the cDNA inserts subjected to sequence analyses. Clone CD11aC17 contained a cDNA insert of approximately 1·7 kbp with an open reading frame of 900 bp encoding a partial human leucocyte-common antigen (CD45) cytoplasmic domain. The cytoplasmic domain of CD45 is linearly organized into five distinct regions: membrane proximal (Mp), protein tyrosine phosphatase domain 1 (herein referred to as D1), spacer (Sp), D2, and carboxyl-terminus (Ct) ( Fig. 2a ). The cDNA sequence identified is identical to sequence spanning Mp and D1 (Ser 584 –Glu 874 ).

Figure 2.

CD45 interacts with integrin CD11a cytoplasmic tail in vitro

To verify the interaction between CD11a cytoplasmic tail and CD45 detected by two-hybrid analysis, we performed GST pull-down experiment using ELISA. Full-length CD11a cytoplasmic domain was cloned into pGEX-KG vector followed by expression and purification of GST-CD11a protein. cDNA encoding CD45 full-length cytoplasmic tail (FL) was amplified from the human leucocyte cDNA library by PCR and cloned into mammalian expression vector pXJ40-HA encoding a HA tag at the N-terminus. Four other HA-tagged CD45 constructs encompassing different regions of its cytoplasmic tail were also generated ( Fig. 2a ). These constructs were expressed in 293T cells with Mr corresponding to that predicted ( Fig. 2b ). Competition ELISA was performed with immobilized GST-CD11a incubated with either HA-Mp-D1 or HA-FL cell lysate in the presence of GST-CD11a or GST as the competitor ( Fig. 3a ). HA-Mp-D1 bound with high affinity to GST-CD11a as compared to HA-FL. Association to integrin CD11a cytoplasmic tail was specific because increasing amount of competing GST-CD11a and not GST effectively decreased the binding signal detected between GST-CD11a and HA-Mp-D1.

Figure 3.

We next determined whether the other CD45 constructs HA-Sp-D2-Ct, HA-Mp, and HA-D1 interact with CD11a cytoplasmic tail ( Fig. 3b ). HA-Mp-D1 showed high level of binding to GST-CD11a. HA-FL binding to GST-CD11a was low while other CD45 constructs showed minimal binding to GST-CD11a similar to that detected with GST.

Most functional studies on CD45 were performed in T and B lymphocytes. These cells express integrin CD11aCD18 but not other CD11CD18 integrins. However, leucocytes such as neutrophils and macrophages can endogenously express at the same time more than one member of the CD11CD18 integrins, for example CD11a, CD11b, and CD11c, albeit at different levels. 24 Furthermore, the CD11 cytoplasmic tail sequences vary after the membrane proximal sequence containing the GFFKR motif, which is highly conserved in all integrin α subunits ( Fig. 4a ). To this end, we tested the specificity of CD45 interaction towards these CD11 cytoplasmic tails by expressing GST fusion of these tails and performing ELISA with cell lysates aforementioned ( Fig. 4b ). CD18 cytoplasmic tail was also included to determine whether it could also interact with CD45 cytoplasmic domains. Significant interaction was detected between HA-Mp-D1 and GST-CD11a whilst minimal interaction was detected with other GST-fusion proteins. Binding of HA-FL to GST-CD11a was low but detectable as compared to other GST-fusion proteins. Taken together, we demonstrate that CD45 cytoplasmic tail interacts with CD11a and not CD11b, CD11c, CD11d or CD18 cytoplasmic tails.

Figure 4.

CD45 interacts with CD11aCD18 in mammalian cells

The interaction of CD45 Mp-D1 with integrin CD11a cytoplasmic tail was further characterized in mammalian cells. 293T cells were transfected with either CD45 HA-Mp-D1 or HA-FL together with CD11aCD18, CD11bCD18, or CD11cCD18 and coimmunoprecipitation analyses performed using mAb IB4, which recognizes all three integrin heterodimers but not monomeric CD11 or CD18 chain. We do not have access to full-length CD11d expression construct and relevant mAbs, hence it was not included in this study. Cell surface expression level of CD11aCD18, CD11bCD18, and CD11cCD18 were comparable as determined by immunofluorescence staining followed by flow cytometry ( Fig. 5a ). Expression levels of CD45 HA-Mp-D1 and HA-FL in the transfectants were similar as determined by immunoblotting ( Fig. 5b, c ). Therefore, any differences in association with the integrin heterodimers detected in subsequent assays could not be caused by differences in the level of protein expression. Significant amount of HA-Mp-D1 coprecipitated with CD11aCD18 but minimally with CD11bCD18 and CD11cCD18 ( Fig. 5b ). Similar result was observed for HA-FL ( Fig. 5c ) albeit at a low level consistent with the ELISA data ( Fig. 4b ).

Figure 5.

Interaction of CD45 with CD11aCD18 was determined using T cell-line MOLT-4 with and without PMA treatment followed by coimmunoprecipitation and immunoblotting for CD45 ( Fig. 5d ). CD45 protein bands could be detected in mAb clone 69 (CD45 specific) immunoprecipitate under both conditions. CD45 could also be detected in mAb MEM48 (CD18 specific) immunoprecipitate after PMA treatment of MOLT-4. mAb MEM48 was employed to prevent possible disruption of CD45 and CD11a interaction as a result of using a CD11a specific mAb; further, MOLT-4 only expresses CD11aCD18 and not other members of the CD11 integrins.

The CD11a cytoplasmic tail region E 1123 –D 1145 associates with CD45 Mp-D1

To define the region in integrin CD11a cytoplasmic tail required for the association with CD45 Mp-D1, GST-fusion constructs were generated with different deletions of the CD11a cytoplasmic tail ( Fig. 6a ). Interaction studies were performed using ELISA aforementioned. CD45 HA-Mp-D1 bound significantly to all GST-CD11a constructs but showed minimal binding to control GST and significant decrease in association with GST-CD11a KQ ( Fig. 6b ). The shortest GST-fusion construct is GST-CD11a ED. Because this region Glu 1123 –Asp 1145 is absent from CD11b, CD11c, and CD11d cytoplasmic tails ( Fig. 4a ), it may account for the specificity shown in CD11a and CD45 Mp-D1 association. A Cys 1129 residue is present in integrin CD11a cytoplasmic tail and there are six Cys residues (inclusive of the catalytic centre) within CD45 Mp-D1 (Lys 575 –Leu 897 ). In our ELISA procedures, cell lysates were incubated with GST-fusion proteins O/N, which could lead to oxidation of free cysteines. To rule out the possibility that the binding signal detected was caused by cystine formation between CD11a and CD45 Mp-D1, we generated GST-CD11a C1129A by site directed mutagenesis and tested its interaction with CD45 Mp-D1 ( Fig. 6c ). Binding was comparable to GST-CD11a, therefore eliminating this possibility.

Figure 6.

Catalytic activity of CD45 cytoplasmic fragments in association with CD11acd18

CD45 is pivotal in lymphocyte development and its regulation has drawn much attention. Receptor dimerization was shown to inhibit CD45 catalytic activity via an inhibitory wedge contributed by opposing units of CD45 cytoplasmic tails. 29 , 30 However, the regulation of CD45 activity by its associated molecules cannot be excluded. 9 To this end, we examined whether catalytic activity could be detected in CD45 HA-Mp-D1 and HA-FL associating with GST-CD11a. Although CD45 HA-Mp-D1 showed high binding affinity to GST-CD11a as shown previously, its catalytic activity was minimal. In contrast, the low level of HA-FL bound to GST-CD11a showed significant phosphatase activity that was effectively abrogated in the presence of vanadate (data not shown). The lack of activity in HA-Mp-D1 may be attributed to homodimer formation that was reported in expression studies of recombinant murine CD45 Mp-D1-Sp. 31 Therefore, two CD45 HA-Mp-D1 variants were generated. E624R in which the inhibitory wedge Glu 624 was substituted by Arg 30 and C828S having the D1 catalytic centre Cys 828 substituted by Ser. We rationalized that if homodimer CD45 HA-Mp-D1 is associated with integrin CD11a cytoplasmic tail, minimal phosphatase activity should be detected as a result of the inhibitory Glu 624 unless this is abolished by E624R substitution. In contrast, monomeric HA-Mp-D1 in association with CD11a should exhibit detectable catalytic activity. In both possibilities, C828S mutation should abrogate their activities.

First, we determined that these substitutions did not affect the expression and binding property of E624R and C828S. Both variants exhibited similar expression profiles and bound effectively to GST-CD11a comparable to that of wild-type HA-Mp-D1 ( Fig. 7a, b ). Next, we determined the phosphatase activity of these variants via FDP assay 28 in the presence or absence of 1 m m tyrosine phosphatase inhibitor sodium vanadate. 32 To ensure that any difference in catalytic activity detected was not due to differential binding to GST-CD11a, activity was presented as the ratio of fluorescence intensity obtained from FDP assay over OD405 value obtained from the GST-CD11a binding assay ( Fig. 7c ). Minimal phosphatase activity was detected in C828S, consistent with the abrogation of D1 catalytic centre. Activity of wild-type HA-Mp-D1 was low whilst variant E624R exhibited augmented activity as a result of inhibitory wedge disruption. HA-FL maintained high level of phosphatase activity. We reasoned that the catalytic activity in E624R was lower than HA-FL because of its requirement for Sp-D2-Ct as reported previously. 31 Collectively, these data suggest GST-CD11a can associate with CD45 HA-Mp-D1 homodimer but does not effect on its activity.

Discussion

PTPs and kinases are key molecules in cellular signal transduction. PTP can be broadly divided into two groups: cytosolic and receptor-like (RPTP). CD45 is a type I transmembrane RPTP ubiquitously expressed on all nucleated haematopoietic cells and exists in different isoforms caused by exon-splicing of its ectodomain. 9 Like most RPTPs, the enzymatic activity of CD45 resides in its cytoplasmic tail, which contains two in tandem PTP domains (D1 and D2). CD45 dephosphorylates a negative regulatory phosphotyrosine residue near the carboxy terminus of Src family tyrosine kinases p56 lck , p59 fyn , and p53/56 lyn , which are required for T- and B-cell development and proliferation. 9 CD45 is also a Jak phosphatase that negatively regulates interferon and cytokine receptor-mediated signalling. 33 In humans, deleterious CD45 mutations cause severe-combined immunodeficiency. 34 , 35

Many lines of evidence suggest cross-talk between CD45 and the cell adhesion molecule integrin CD11aCD18. Receptor cross-linking studies using anti-CD45 mAbs demonstrate a role of CD45 in regulating homotypic aggregation of lymphocytes via CD11aCD18. 11 – 14 CD45 inhibited calcium mobilization by CD11aCD18 in human natural killer cells 15 and is excluded from the core but clusters into a ring surrounding CD11aCD18 in SMAC. 10 CD45RA and CD11aCD18 are also shown to interact by cocapping studies in preactivated T lymphocytes. 16 Nonetheless, how interaction between CD45 and CD11aCD18 is mediated remains unclear.

We showed CD45 and CD11a cytoplasmic tails interaction. The interacting region involved Glu 1123 –Asp 1145 of CD11a cytoplasmic tail. This sequence is absent in CD11b and divergent from that of CD11c and CD11d, and may account for the specific association between CD45 and CD11a. We note that CD45 was shown to colocalize with integrin CD11bCD18 at adhesion sites of murine bone marrow-derived macrophages and CD45 is required for sustained CD11bCD18-mediated adhesion. 36 This does not conflict with our observations because it is possible that cross-talk between CD45 and CD11bCD18 involves other molecule(s). Indeed, it was shown in the same study that the Src kinases p53/56 lyn and p56/59 hck were involved although the actual mechanism remained to be elucidated. 36 In addition, distinct roles of CD45 on different haematopoietic cells have been reported. 37

CD45 cytoplasmic tail comprises several regions: Mp, D1, Sp, D2, and Ct ( Fig. 2a ). We showed that its Mp-D1 region is required for interaction with CD11a cytoplasmic tail. This is interesting because the Mp-D1 region is required for CD45 phosphatase activity. Further, homodimerization of Mp-D1 is important in the regulation of CD45 and other RPTP functions. 38 Structural data of murine RPTPα Mp-D1 homodimer revealed inhibition of catalytic site on one RPTPα D1 by an opposing unit via a helix-turn-helix Asp 228 wedge. 29 In concordance, dimerization inhibited activity of RPTP α and RPTP σ D1. 39 Chimeric EGFR-CD45 with point mutation that abolished the inhibitory wedge exhibited less inhibitory effect on calcium mobilization because of TCR-crosslinking as compared to wild-type. 30 Recombinant murine CD45 Mp-D1 was also shown to exist as a dimer with minimal catalytic activity. 31

CD45 and CD11aCD18 are important players in immune synapse formation and the activity of CD45 must be regulated. Hence, we asked whether association with CD11a can directly regulate CD45 activity by disrupting the inhibitory wedge in CD45 Mp-D1 dimer. Our mutational and enzymatic assays involving the inhibitory wedge (E624R) and the catalytic site (C828S) revealed that association of CD11a with CD45 Mp-D1 did not modulate its enzymatic activity. The exact function of such association remains to be determined.

It is intriguing as to why only CD45 MP-D1 and not its full-length cytoplasmic tail interacts with high affinity with CD11a. It is possible that other regions in CD45 cytoplasmic tail influence the interaction between CD45 MP-D1 and CD11a. Indeed, RPTP activity can be regulated by intramolecular interactions within its cytoplasmic tail. 31 , 40 Recent study screening for suramin analogues as potent inhibitor against PTPs in conjunction with mutagenesis analyses demonstrates the importance of D2 in the direct intramolecular regulation of D1 via conformational change. 41 This interaction may be modulated by p50 csk and casein kinase 2 phosphorylation. 42 , 43 Taken together, it is possible that in our study CD45 D2 masks the interaction site of CD11a on Mp-D1 in full-length CD45 cytoplasmic tail, thereby preventing their effective association. In intact cells, we found that CD45 could be coprecipitated with CD11aCD18 from MOLT-4 cells upon PMA treatment. PMA activates protein kinase C (PKC) 44 and CD45 phosphorylation by PKC regulates its phosphatase activity. 45 Hence, it is possible that in MOLT-4 cells, CD45 cytoplasmic tail undergoes conformational changes upon PMA treatment allowing its association with CD11a.

In conclusion, an equilibrium between monomeric and dimeric CD45 isoforms exists on the cell surface and the shift of this equilibrium influences the activity of CD45 on a cell. 8 , 9 Our study shows cytoplasmic interaction between CD45 and CD11aCD18. It would be of interest in future studies to define the functional implication of such interaction.
