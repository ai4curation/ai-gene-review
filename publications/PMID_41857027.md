Cyclin-dependent protein kinase 16 (CDK16) regulates both physiological and
pathological processes, including autophagy, spermatogenesis and cancer. Unlike
other CDKs, CDK16 is regulated by an unclear mechanism involving phosphorylated
cyclin Y (CCNY) in complex with 14-3-3 proteins rather than CCNY alone. The
present study aims at elucidating this mechanism by structurally characterizing
CDK16 in complex with CCNY and 14-3-3 using several biophysical techniques. As
shown by cryo-EM analysis and hydrogen/deuterium exchange coupled to mass
spectrometry, 14-3-3 binding modulates the conformation of a key moiety of the
CDK binding surface of CCNY, thereby enabling CDK16 activation. CDK16 interacts
with the cyclin box of CCNY, while 14-3-3 provides additional contacts,
including with the activation segment of CDK16. CDK16 activation also requires
interactions of CCNY with the N-terminal extension of CDK16. These findings not
only clarify the role of CCNY and 14-3-3 in CDK16 activation but also highlight
the potential of targeting CDK16 protein-protein interactions for cancer
therapy.

Introduction

Cyclin-dependent protein kinases (CDKs) play key roles in cell cycle regulation, transcription, mRNA processing, and DNA damage and repair, among other functions1,2. In humans, the CDK kinase family consists of 20 members3, but based on their functional specialization during evolution, CDKs can be divided into eight subfamilies4. The CDK5 subfamily includes two subgroups of atypical CDKs whose αC helix contains either the “PFTAIRE” (CDK14 and CDK15) or the “PCTAIRE” (CDK16-18) sequence.

Ubiquitously expressed, CDK16 regulates various physiological processes, including neurite outgrowth5, AMPK-dependent autophagy6 and the terminal steps of spermatogenesis7. In addition, CDK16 has been implicated in neuronal and brain disorders such as X-linked intellectual disability (XLID)8,9 and in cancer cell proliferation10–13 and resistance to anti-mitotic drugs14. However, the mechanism of regulation of this protein kinase is still poorly understood. Unlike other CDKs, CDK16 activation requires phosphorylated cyclin Y (CCNY) or YL1 (CCNYL1) in complex with scaffolding 14-3-3 proteins, rather than CCNY alone15,16. As with other CDKs, CDK16 requires a proline residue C-terminal to the phosphorylated S/T, and its preferred consensus motif is “S/T-P-K/R-ϕ-K/R/H”, where ϕ stands for a small aliphatic residue at the position +3 (e.g. Ala, Val, etc.) relative to the phosphorylated S/T17.

CCNY was originally described as a highly conserved gene associated with a broad range of developmental processes in Drosophila18 and with polarized trafficking of presynaptic components in Caenorhabditis elegans19. Cyclins stimulate the catalytic function of CDKs by interacting with their kinase domain. Cyclin binding induces rotation of the αC helix from the N-lobe of the kinase domain, partly rearranging the ATP-binding site1,20. This interaction improves access to the active center by changing the conformation of the activation segment. But in contrast to most cyclins, with two cyclin box domains (CBD), CCNY has a single CBD. Its CBD is located in the center of the molecule, bordered by flexible segments containing two highly conserved 14-3-3 binding motifs (S100 and S326) (Fig. 1a). The C-terminal motif S326 is phosphorylated by AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) in response to nutrient deprivation or other autophagy-promoting factors, which explains the key role of the active CDK16:CCNY:14-3-3 complex in AMPK-dependent autophagy6. This lack of a second CBD has raised speculation, with some researchers postulating that a bidentate interaction between the 14-3-3 protein dimer and CCNY may help to reorganize the structure of CCNY for binding to its cognate CDK partners6,7,21. However, the involvement of direct interactions between CDK16 and 14-3-3 in the ternary complex cannot be ruled out. And unlike other CDKs, the kinase domain of CDK16 cannot bind to CCNY without a 50-residue long N-terminal extension upstream of the kinase domain highly conserved among all PCTAIRE CDKs5. cAMP-dependent protein kinase (PKA) phosphorylation of two serine residues in this extension, S119 and S153, modulates CCNY binding, which may provide an additional regulatory mechanism of PCTAIRE CDKs5,7. Therefore, further structural data must be acquired to better understand CDK16 regulation and to develop new effective drug therapies targeting PCTAIRE kinases.

Considering the above, this study aims at structurally and functionally characterizing CDK16:CCNY:14-3-3η and CCNY:14-3-3η complexes. For this purpose, we apply several biophysical and biochemical methods, including cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), hydrogen/deuterium exchange coupled to mass spectrometry (HDX-MS), sedimentation velocity analytical ultracentrifugation (SV AUC), site-directed mutagenesis and kinase activity measurements. Our data reveal that 14-3-3η protein binding modulates the conformation of a key region of the CDK binding surface of phosphorylated CCNY, thereby enabling CDK16 activation. CDK16 interacts with the CBD of CCNY, while the C-terminal portion of 14-3-3η provides additional contacts, including with the activation segment of CDK16. CDK16 activation also requires interactions of the N-terminal part of CCNY with the kinase domain and the upstream N-terminal extension of CDK16. Overall, our findings not only clarify the role of CCNY and 14-3-3η in CDK16 activation but also provide key mechanistic insights into its regulation.

To prepare CCNY with quantitatively phosphorylated 14-3-3 binding motifs, we designed a CCNY mutant containing the Y98R, S99A and S324A mutations (hereafter referred to as CCNY). In the central channel of the 14-3-3η dimer, both phosphorylated 14-3-3 binding motifs of pCCNY dock to binding grooves of the 14-3-3η protomers (Fig. 2a). This channel also contains the entire C-terminal segment of pCCNY (residues 291-341), but not CBD (residues 168-260). While lying outside, CBD is kept separated from the C-terminus of one of the 14-3-3η protomers formed by helices H8 and H9 through a platform created by helices αN3 and αC1 of pCCNY (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 13a, b and 14). pCCNY interacts with the C-terminus of 14-3-3η through several polar and hydrophobic contacts, primarily involving C-terminal regions of pCCNY. The phosphate group and other contacts in the 14-3-3 binding groove for pS100 and pS326 motifs are similar to those of other 14-3-3 protein complexes (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 13c–f)16.

The pCCNY chain contains only one CBD consisting of four α-helices (α1-α4) (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15a). Three of these helices (α2-4) are oriented similarly to the CBD helices (α2, 3 and 5) of cyclins CCNA2 (PDB ID: 6GUF)27, CCNC (PDB ID: 5BNJ)28 and CCNT1 (PDB ID: 3BLH)29 (Supplementary Fig. 16). However, the region that forms helix α4 in CCNA2, CCNC and CCNT2 adopts a loop structure in the pCCNY CBD and the N-terminal helix α1 has a different orientation, with no major structural differences in pCCNY between binary and ternary complexes (Supplementary Fig. 15b, c); the only difference is the lack of N-terminal residues 38-76 in the pCCNY:14-3-3η complex, most likely due to the high flexibility of this segment in the absence of CDK16. Similarly, no major structural difference was observed in the 14-3-3η dimer between binary and ternary complexes (Supplementary Fig. 15d). Therefore, beyond stabilizing the N-terminal segment of pCCNY, binding to CDK16 does not induce a significant conformational change in the pCCNY:14-3-3η complex.

These results further corroborate the key role of the N-terminal extension in CDK16 binding to the pCCNY:14-3-3η complex.

The activation segment of CDK16 is ordered through interactions with pCCNY:14-3-3η

The PCTAIRE αC helix adopts an inward position, typical of the active conformation. In this conformation, the conserved residue E211 in the PCTAIRE sequence can form a salt bridge with the catalytic K194 in the β3 strand (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 17b). The activation segment starts with a conserved DFG motif (residues 304 − 306, shown in pink in Fig. 3b) that adopts an active “DFG-in” conformation, mirroring the structure of the CDK2:CCNA complex (PDB ID: 1FIN)30 (Supplementary Fig. 19a, b). As expected, ATP-γ-S binds to the active site of CDK16, as in other CDK:cyclin complexes31,32 with the γ-thiophosphate moiety of ATP-γ-S positioned close to the P-loop (GxGxxG motif located between β1 and β2 strands) and to the triad of catalytic residues K194, E211, and D304. Mutations K194R and D304A completely abrogated CDK16 activity (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 7), without significantly affecting the stability of the ternary complex (Supplementary Fig. 22).

The activation segment (residues 304-331) adopts an ordered conformation stabilized by interactions with the main chain of the αE-β7 segment of CDK16, by pCCNY helices α2, α3 and αC1 and by the H8-H9 loop of 14-3-3η (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Figs. 17d, 18, and 23). These interactions are disrupted when mutating CDK16 residues I313 and P314 located at the tip of the activation segment, significantly decreasing CDK16 activity (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 7). This decreased activity likely results from the conformational change of the activation segment as evidenced by SV-AUC measurements. These measurements revealed that the I313A mutation had only a weak effect on the stability of the ternary complex (Supplementary Fig. 22).

Full activation of many CDKs requires the phosphorylation of a conserved S/T residue within the activation segment (e.g., T160 in CDK2). This phosphorylation changes conformation of the activation segment through interactions with several positively charged residues in the helix αC, the N-terminus of the activation segment and the catalytic HRD motif3,31. The equivalent residue in CDK16 is S319, whose side chain is close to D229 in pCCNY (Fig. 3d). However, neither S319 phosphorylation nor any role of this residue in CDK16 activation has been reported so far. Moreover, the phosphorylation-mimicking mutation S319D significantly decreased CDK16 activity (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 7), albeit without affecting the stability of the ternary complex (Supplementary Fig. 22). In line with these results, our kinetic analysis showed that the apparent KM values of ATP were significantly lower in both I313A and S319D mutants than in wild type CDK16 (Supplementary Fig. 9g). Based on these results, the activation of CDK16 differs from that of classical CDKs for relying on binding to the pCCNY:14-3-3η complex.

Decreased kinase activity was also observed when mutating pCCNY residues W227 and Y271 in helices α3 and αC1 and 14-3-3η residue D209 in the N-terminus of helix H9, which interacts with S269 in pCCNY helix αC1 (Figs. 2b and 3d,e). Both pCCNY W227A + Y271A and 14-3-3η D209A mutations significantly reduced the stability of their ternary complexes, thereby explaining their effect on CDK16 activity (Supplementary Fig. 22). The same region also contains the hydrogen bond between CDK16 K281 near the HRD motif (residues 284–286) and 14-3-3η L211, the only polar contact between CDK16 and 14-3-3η (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 17d). Removing this contact by introducing the K281A mutation to CDK16 slightly reduced its activity (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 7). These results indicate that the interface between CDK16, pCCNY and 14-3-3η stabilizes the conformation of the activation segment of CDK16, thus promoting its activation.

The conformation adopted by active CDK16, including the catalytic residues, the DFG motif, the activation segment and the PCTAIRE helix αC, in our cryo-EM reconstruction (Supplementary Fig. 19c, d) differed from previously reported crystal structures of CDK16 kinase domain complexes with the inhibitors indirubin E804 and rebastinib (PDB ID: 3MTL and 5G6V)33. In the complex with rebastinib, a type II kinase inhibitor, the αC helix is tilted away from the active site, with the activation segment blocking the ATP binding site. In contrast, in the structure with indirubin E804, a type I kinase inhibitor, the αC helix is partly unfolded, with the partly disordered activation segment adopting a conformation similar to that of active CDK16. These conformational differences corroborate the previously suggested conformational plasticity of CDK1633, especially of its PCTAIRE helix αC region. CDK16 interacts primarily with the CBD of pCCNY, with pCCNY helices αN3 and αC1 and the C-terminal portion of 14-3-3η acting as a second CBD and providing additional stabilizing contacts, including with the activation segment of CDK16 (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 23). But CDK16 activation also requires interactions of the N-terminal segment of pCCNY with its N-terminal extension and N-lobe. This 55-residue-long N-terminal extension that precedes the kinase domain and promotes CCNY binding is a common feature of PCTAIRE CDKs7,37.

CCNY is the binding partner of not only PCTAIRE (CDK16-18) but also PFTAIRE (CDK14 and CDK15) CDKs, yet contains only one CBD38. Many other cyclins, such as CCNA1-2, CCNB1-2, CCND1-3 and CCNE1-2, boast two CBDs that interact with each other. The N-terminal domain is responsible for CDK binding and activation, while the C-terminal domain stabilizes the cyclin molecule38. Our study suggests that the interaction with 14-3-3η compensates for the lack of a C-terminal CBD in pCCNY and enables CDK16 binding by changing the conformation of a key region of the CDK16 binding surface of this domain, the α4-αC1 loop (Fig. 4a, b). In this interaction, the 14-3-3η dimer functions as a scaffold that modulates the pCCNY structure, as in the regulation of Raf kinases39,40, neutral trehalase Nth141, and serotonin N-acetyltransferase42. Structural modulation of CCNY by 14-3-3 may also regulate other CDKs, such as CDK1421, through a similar mechanism.

Our cryo-EM reconstruction of the CDK16:pCCNY:14-3-3η complex unveiled a unique structural feature, the N-terminal extension of CDK16, which interacts with the N-terminal segment of pCCNY (Fig. 3c). This extension is conserved amongst all three PCTAIRE CDKs as a key element of the binding interface between CDK16 and pCCNY. Case in point, its disruption or deletion restrains CDK16 activation (Fig. 3e)5,7. The reason why CDK16 requires this N-terminal extension for pCCNY:14-3-3η complex binding may be unclear, but a previous structural analysis of CDK16 kinase domain highlighted its high conformational flexibility33. As such, this larger binding interface may be specifically required to stabilize the kinase domain of CDK16 and to induce its active conformation.

Previous structural studies revealed that cyclins often play a key role in the ability of CDK complexes to recognize protein substrates and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors3. However, our structure does not suggest that pCCNY and/or 14-3-3η play a role in binding or recognizing substrates. For instance, the pCCNY region, which is formed by helices α1 and α3, significantly differs from the equivalent region in CCNA given the absence of a helical segment that corresponds to helix α4 in CCNA (Supplementary Fig. 16a, 16b). This hydrophobic region in the N-terminal CBD of CCNA mediates binding to the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p2743, and it may recruit both substrates and inhibitors to various CDK:cyclin complexes by increasing their local concentration44. Nevertheless, these interactions may affect the binding or recognition of large, multi-domain protein substrates. Thus, we cannot rule out the involvement of pCCNY and/or 14-3-3η in substrate binding.

Cryo-EM image processing and analysisAll images were processed in CryoSPARC 4.1.250. Once imported and gain-corrected, the movies were subjected to patch motion correction and CTF estimation. Micrographs with an estimated CTF resolution of >5.5 Å and a relative ice thickness >1.2 were discarded; then, the remaining micrographs were visually curated to exclude those with excessive aggregation, ice artifacts, or artifacts in power spectra. The initial particle set was picked from 500 randomly selected micrographs using a blob picker tool with a 70–200 Å particle size. After visual inspection, particles were extracted with a box size of 300 × 300 pixels, and after 2D classification, suitable classes were used to generate 2D templates. Particle picking was then repeated using a template-based picker. Particle picks were extracted with a box size of 300 × 300 pixels and subjected to 2D classification. Particles within suitable 2D classes were used for Ab Initio 3D reconstruction. Suitable classes were further refined using a combination of heterogeneous, homogeneous, and non-uniform refinements with one additional round of Ab-initio reconstruction and refinements for a separate class, as depicted in Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8. The resolution of final maps was determined by Fourier shell correlation (threshold 0.143). The final map of the CDK16:pCCNY:14-3-3η complex was sharpened using phenix. Autosharpen51, whereas the final map of the pCCNY:14-3-3η complex was sharpened in CryoSPARC 4.1.2 using a B-factor value of 156.2 Å2.
