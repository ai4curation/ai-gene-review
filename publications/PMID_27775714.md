Recognition of histone covalent modifications by 'reader' modules constitutes a
major mechanism for epigenetic regulation. A recent upsurge of newly discovered
histone lysine acylations, such as crotonylation (Kcr), butyrylation (Kbu), and
propionylation (Kpr), greatly expands the coding potential of histone lysine
modifications. Here we demonstrate that the histone acetylation-binding double
PHD finger (DPF) domains of human MOZ (also known as KAT6A) and DPF2 (also known
as BAF45d) accommodate a wide range of histone lysine acylations with the
strongest preference for Kcr. Crystal structures of the DPF domain of MOZ in
complex with H3K14cr, H3K14bu, and H3K14pr peptides reveal that these non-acetyl
acylations are anchored in a hydrophobic 'dead-end' pocket with selectivity for
crotonylation arising from intimate encapsulation and an amide-sensing hydrogen
bonding network. Immunofluorescence and chromatin immunoprecipitation
(ChIP)-quantitative PCR (qPCR) showed that MOZ and H3K14cr colocalize in a
DPF-dependent manner. Our studies call attention to a new regulatory mechanism
centered on histone crotonylation readout by DPF family members.

## Full Text

Covalent histone modifications carry critical epigenetic information for gene regulation and cell fate decisions1. These modifications are recognized by a plethora of histone-binding “reader” modules, such as bromodomain, chromodomain, and plant homeodomain finger (PHD), in a type- and site-specific manner, and transduced into functional outcomes2-4. Histone lysine acetylation (Kac) was first discovered in the early 1960s with a primary function associated with active transcription. The enzymatic generation and removal, selective readout and dynamic regulation of histone Kac are implicated in many cellular functions, and have been extensively studied in the past twenty years5. Thanks to an integrated and mass spectrometry-based proteomic approach, an array of non-acetyl histone lysine acylations, such as lysine propionylation (Kpr), butyrylation (Kbu) and crotonylation (Kcr), have been identified in cells6,7, posing a new challenge in the field to characterize the corresponding enzymatic machineries and downstream effectors.

The crotonyl group is a four-carbon, planar chemical moiety containing a C=C bond. Histone Kcr is evolutionarily conserved from yeast to human and twenty-eight Kcr sites have been identified in human histones8. Histone Kcr is particularly enriched at promoters and enhancers of actively transcribed genes. Histone Kcr but not Kac preferentially marks “escapee genes” during post-meiotic sex inactivation in mouse testis, suggesting a unique role for Kcr9. The level of histone Kcr is intimately correlated with the availability of cellular crotonyl-CoA, thus coupling cellular metabolism to epigenetic regulation10. Identification of effector proteins for Kcr is indispensable for elucidating how Kcr functions in active transcription.

Bromodomain, Double PHD finger (DPF) and YEATS are three major classes of acetyllysine reader modules11-14. Recently, we and other groups reported that the Kac-binding YEATS domains but not the bromodomains are preferential Kcr readers15-17. Notably, our cell-based functional studies on human AF9 YEATS show that histone Kcr potentiates expression of inflammatory genes in a YEATS-dependent manner13. The DPF domains of MOZ/MORF (a.k.a. KAT6A/KAT6B) and DPF1/2/3 (a.k.a. BAF45b/d/c) have been reported to recognize histone H3K14 acetylation (H3K14ac) and regulate gene transcription12,13,18-20. MOZ and MORF are two paralogous histone acetyltransferases implicated in leukemogenesis21-24, whereas DPF1/2/3 are non-catalytic subunits of the BAF ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes that carry diverse functions in development and cancer25.

Here we report that DPF domains of MOZ and DPF2 engage a wide range of acylations at H3K14 with the highest affinity for crotonylation. Crystal structures of MOZ DPF domain in complex with H3K14cr, H3K14bu, and H3K14pr peptides reveal that these histone acylations are trapped in a β2 hydrophobic pocket with the Kcr preference originating from the intimate encapsulation and crotonylamide-sensing hydrogen bonding. Immunofluorescence and ChIP-qPCR studies suggest colocalization of MOZ and H3K14cr in a manner dependent on DPF domain. Our work highlights a new regulatory mechanism of transcription control centered on readout of histone Kcr by DPF family members.

Structural analysis reveals three signatures in the recognition of H31–25K14cr peptide (Fig. 2a, b; Supplementary Fig. 1b). Firstly, H3 R2 and K4 are inserted into two acidic pockets respectively at the β1 surface of the second PHD finger (PHD2), and stabilized by electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions as described previously18,20. Secondly, H3K14cr is anchored into a hydrophobic pocket at the β2 surface of the first PHD finger (PHD1). Thirdly, H3 L20 and K23 within the H317–25 α-helix bestride the hydrophobic “I208–L213” saddle pair of MOZ PHD1 and contribute to MOZDPF-H3 interaction (Fig. 2c). The importance of the hydrophobic saddle pair is supported by ITC titrations using I208A and L213A MOZDPF mutants, which lose 3.8- and 6.5-fold binding affinity respectively in engaging H31–25K14cr (Supplementary Fig. 1c). In addition, using the dually crotonylated H31–25K14crK23cr peptide, we measured a binding KD of 3.76 μM (Supplementary Fig. 1c), which is about 1.6-fold tighter than the singly crotonylated H31–25K14cr peptide. Such an affinity enhancement is presumably due to increased hydrophobicity at H3K23 following crotonylation, underscoring the role of hydrophobic contacts around the saddle pair for H3 recognition.

The crotonylamide group of H3K14cr is planar due to π-electron conjugation. We observed a deep and snug insertion of the crotonylamide group into the reader pocket of PHD1 (Fig. 2d, e). This reader pocket is located next to the β2 strand and the second zinc finger motif of PHD1. It consists of residues N235–G237 of β2 strand, I228–C230 of β1 strand, and S210, F211, L242, W257, C259, I260, E261 around the zinc finger coordination sphere (Fig. 2f). Sequence and structural alignment reveal that a glycine residue (G237) within the β2 strand is critical for the β2 pocket formation owing to its side-chain free feature (Fig. 2g, h). In most classical histone H3K4-binding PHD fingers (such as BPTF), this glycine residue is substituted by a bulky residue such as tyrosine or phenylalanine, which fills up the β2 pocket and blocks Kcr insertion (Fig. 2h).

The four-carbon side chain of the crotonyl group is stably positioned at the bottom of the confined hydrophobic pocket (Fig. 2f). Interaction analysis reveals four pairs of hydrophobic contacts, four water-mediated hydrogen-bonding interactions involving side chains of S210, N235, and C259 as well as main chains of I260–E261 (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Collectively, our structure shows that MOZDPF PHD1 makes use of a hydrophobic β2-pocket for Kcr-specific readout through tight encapsulation, which is further strengthened by additionally organized hydrogen-bonding network.

DiscussionA variety of non-acetyl histone lysine acylations have been identified, yet their cellular function and their functional distinction from acetylation are still largely unknown. In the case of Kcr, p300/CBP are effective “writers” of H3K18cr10, and several Sirtuin family members (e.g. SIRT1–3) can serve as crotonylation “erasers”28,29. Evidence about Kcr-specific downstream readers that link histone Kcr to particular biological outcomes is still lacking. Recently, we and others reported that the YEATS family proteins are preferential “readers”15-17 of histone Kcr. With ITC assays, designer nucleosome pulldown, structural and colocalization studies, we demonstrate here that the DPF domains of MOZ and DPF2 are novel Kcr-favorable readers and integrate these two proteins into the “write/read/erase” regulatory loop centered on Kcr.Two induced α-helices (H34–11 and H317–25) contribute to the recognition of H3K14cr by MOZDPF. Helix H34–11 spans and engages the PHD1-PHD2 junction and may facilitate sampling of histone modification status between H3 K4 and T11 by MOZDPF20. Helix H317–25 forms hydrophobic contacts with a saddle pair next to the β2 pocket (Fig. 2a–c). To our knowledge, the induced formation of H317–25 helix has not been reported previously, highlighting a unique mode of recognition by MOZDPF. Additional crotonylation at H3K23 enhances the binding of the peptide by 1.6-fold, indicative of a minor avidity effect. In sum, the engagement of the entire H31–25 tail by MOZDPF highlights a feature of multivalent readout30, and holds intriguing potential for sophisticated epigenetic regulation by sensing versatile patterns or cross-talks of histone modifications on H3 tail.The hydrophobic β2 pocket within MOZDPF PHD1 is size-selective for Kcr (Fig. 2f). It is interesting to note that PHD fingers are able to make use of distinct surfaces for recognizing partners31. In the case of MOZDPF, two PHD fingers are highly integrated to form a compact folding unit, which utilizes the β1 surface of PHD2 for recognizing H3 R2 and K4, and the β2 pocket of PHD1 for readout of H3K14 acylation (Fig. 2a). Notably, a side-chain free glycine residue (G237 of MOZDPF) within the β2 strand is critical for the pocket generation among acyllysine-binding PHD fingers (Fig. 2g, h). In the classical H3K4-binding PHD fingers, such as those of BPTF, ING2, BHC80 and the second PHD fingers of MOZDPF and DPF2DPF, the glycine residue is substituted by a bulky residue (e.g. F or Y), which occupies the β2 pocket and blocks acyllysine insertion (Fig. 2h). By contrast, this side-chain free glycine residue is conserved among the PHD1 domains of human MOZ, MORF, DPF1, DPF2, DPF3, and PHF10 (Fig. 2g), suggesting their common acyllysine-reading activity.Crotonylation differs from other acylations in its rigidity and planar configuration. Compared to Kbu, the alkenyl side chain of Kcr lacks two hydrogens and is flat and slim in shape (Fig. 4a). We observed a snug fitting of Kcr into the hydrophobic β2 pocket and optimal shape-complementarity between them, which result in high-affinity binding. Strikingly, the β2 pocket is so size-selective that the bulkier Kbu cannot achieve such a high-affinity mode of encapsulation, but has to flip its butyramide group and adopt a different conformation instead to avoid steric clashes caused by the branched butyryl hydrogens (Fig. 4b). Such size-selectivity is reminiscent of the mono- and di-methylation state-specific readout by L3BMTL1, in which a narrow and deep aromatic pocket acts as a size-selective filter to discriminate against the bulkier and more branched trimethyllysine group2,32. In sum, the design of acylation-reader pocket can be so exquisite that a size difference as minor as a pair of hydrogens can be sensed for preferable readout of Kcr.In addition to β2 pocket encapsulation, a water-mediated hydrogen-bonding network around the acylamide group further contributes to the readout of H3 K14cr, K14bu, and K14pr by MOZDPF (Fig. 3c). Interestingly, such a hydrogen-bonding network is not observed for H3K14ac due to a deeper insertion of the acetylamide group into the β2 pocket (Fig. 3b). Notably, residues S210 and N235 of MOZDPF are located at the rim of the β2 pocket and help to stabilize the hydrogen-bonding network. S210 and N235 are two signature residues that distinguish MOZ/MORF from the DPF1/2/3 subfamilies (Supplementary Fig. 3), S210 and N235 in the former are substituted by D and R in the latter, respectively (Fig. 5a). DPF2DPF displays a higher H3K14cr-binding affinity than MOZDPF, and S210D/N235R double mutation in MOZDPF enhances binding of H3K14cr by threefold. Direct hydrogen bond forms between S210D and the NH of crotonylamide in the structure of H3K14cr in complex with MOZDPFDM, which likely accounts for the observed binding enhancement of MOZDPFDM over wild-type MOZDPF, and partly explains the high-affinity binding of H3K14cr by DPF2DPF. In sum, the molecular design of crotonylamide-sensing pocket is divergent between the two DPF domains in this report, and the in vivo functional significance of this divergence remains to be explored.Bromo, DPF and YEATS domains are three major classes of histone lysine acylation readers with different binding capacity and recognition mechanisms for various lysine acylations14,15. In general, the bromodomains adopt a “side-open” pocket for readout of acyllysines with a binding preference of Kac>Kpr>Kbu⋙Kcr (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Most bromodomains do not recognize Kcr due to steric hindrance and only a few members such as TAF1 and BRD9 bind Kcr with compromised affinity15,33. By contrast, the YEATS domains harbor an “end-open” aromatic sandwich pocket for readout of acyllysines with a binding preference of Kcr>Kpr~Kbu>Kac (Supplementary Fig. 5b)15,16. In the case of DPF domains in this report, a “dead end” hydrophobic pocket is utilized for Kcr-specific recognition (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Although the DPF domains here display similar Kcr preference as YEATS, the origin of selectivity for Kcr is different. Aromatic-π stacking between the crotonylamide plane and aromatic sandwiching residues account for Kcr-specificity in the case of YEATS domains15-17; whereas, intimate hydrophobic encapsulation and coordinated hydrogen bonding are exploited for the selective readout of Kcr by DPF domains. Given the prevalence of histone acylations, the evolution of different reader pockets for Kcr recognition suggests more readers or effector modules await discovery for the functional readout of, but not limited to, histone acylations.In summary, here we demonstrate that the DPF domains of MOZ and DPF2 are specific readers of H3K14cr. MOZ (KAT6A) is a transcriptional co-activator that acetylates histone H3 (K14, K9) and H4 (K5, K8, K12, K16) in vitro, and acts as a key epigenetic regulator of hematopoiesis23,34; while the ubiquitously expressed DPF2 (BAF45d) is required for the expression of genes associated with hematopoietic stem cells35,36. Given the important functions of MOZ and DPF2 in gene regulation, the observed 4- and 8-fold enhancement in their binding of H3K14cr over H3K14ac suggest regulatory mechanisms in which Kcr is a better potentiator for transcription control than Kac. MOZDPF and DPF2DPF are also well responsive to H3K14pr and H3K14bu, which likely play a role in gene regulation depending on the availability of the corresponding acyl-CoA. Often the level of each acyl-CoA is closely related to intracellular metabolic conditions10,15,26. Overall, the readout of histone Kcr by DPF domains calls attention to a new regulatory mechanism that links cellular metabolism to epigenetic regulations associated with chromatin modification and remodeling.

Recently, we and others reported that the YEATS family proteins are preferential “readers”15-17 of histone Kcr. With ITC assays, designer nucleosome pulldown, structural and colocalization studies, we demonstrate here that the DPF domains of MOZ and DPF2 are novel Kcr-favorable readers and integrate these two proteins into the “write/read/erase” regulatory loop centered on Kcr.

Two induced α-helices (H34–11 and H317–25) contribute to the recognition of H3K14cr by MOZDPF. Helix H34–11 spans and engages the PHD1-PHD2 junction and may facilitate sampling of histone modification status between H3 K4 and T11 by MOZDPF20. Helix H317–25 forms hydrophobic contacts with a saddle pair next to the β2 pocket (Fig. 2a–c). To our knowledge, the induced formation of H317–25 helix has not been reported previously, highlighting a unique mode of recognition by MOZDPF. Additional crotonylation at H3K23 enhances the binding of the peptide by 1.6-fold, indicative of a minor avidity effect. In sum, the engagement of the entire H31–25 tail by MOZDPF highlights a feature of multivalent readout30, and holds intriguing potential for sophisticated epigenetic regulation by sensing versatile patterns or cross-talks of histone modifications on H3 tail.

The hydrophobic β2 pocket within MOZDPF PHD1 is size-selective for Kcr (Fig. 2f). It is interesting to note that PHD fingers are able to make use of distinct surfaces for recognizing partners31. In the case of MOZDPF, two PHD fingers are highly integrated to form a compact folding unit, which utilizes the β1 surface of PHD2 for recognizing H3 R2 and K4, and the β2 pocket of PHD1 for readout of H3K14 acylation (Fig. 2a). Notably, a side-chain free glycine residue (G237 of MOZDPF) within the β2 strand is critical for the pocket generation among acyllysine-binding PHD fingers (Fig. 2g, h). In the classical H3K4-binding PHD fingers, such as those of BPTF, ING2, BHC80 and the second PHD fingers of MOZDPF and DPF2DPF, the glycine residue is substituted by a bulky residue (e.g. F or Y), which occupies the β2 pocket and blocks acyllysine insertion (Fig. 2h). By contrast, this side-chain free glycine residue is conserved among the PHD1 domains of human MOZ, MORF, DPF1, DPF2, DPF3, and PHF10 (Fig. 2g), suggesting their common acyllysine-reading activity.

Crotonylation differs from other acylations in its rigidity and planar configuration. Compared to Kbu, the alkenyl side chain of Kcr lacks two hydrogens and is flat and slim in shape (Fig. 4a). We observed a snug fitting of Kcr into the hydrophobic β2 pocket and optimal shape-complementarity between them, which result in high-affinity binding. Strikingly, the β2 pocket is so size-selective that the bulkier Kbu cannot achieve such a high-affinity mode of encapsulation, but has to flip its butyramide group and adopt a different conformation instead to avoid steric clashes caused by the branched butyryl hydrogens (Fig. 4b). Such size-selectivity is reminiscent of the mono- and di-methylation state-specific readout by L3BMTL1, in which a narrow and deep aromatic pocket acts as a size-selective filter to discriminate against the bulkier and more branched trimethyllysine group2,32. In sum, the design of acylation-reader pocket can be so exquisite that a size difference as minor as a pair of hydrogens can be sensed for preferable readout of Kcr.

In addition to β2 pocket encapsulation, a water-mediated hydrogen-bonding network around the acylamide group further contributes to the readout of H3 K14cr, K14bu, and K14pr by MOZDPF (Fig. 3c). Interestingly, such a hydrogen-bonding network is not observed for H3K14ac due to a deeper insertion of the acetylamide group into the β2 pocket (Fig. 3b). Notably, residues S210 and N235 of MOZDPF are located at the rim of the β2 pocket and help to stabilize the hydrogen-bonding network. S210 and N235 are two signature residues that distinguish MOZ/MORF from the DPF1/2/3 subfamilies (Supplementary Fig. 3), S210 and N235 in the former are substituted by D and R in the latter, respectively (Fig. 5a). DPF2DPF displays a higher H3K14cr-binding affinity than MOZDPF, and S210D/N235R double mutation in MOZDPF enhances binding of H3K14cr by threefold. Direct hydrogen bond forms between S210D and the NH of crotonylamide in the structure of H3K14cr in complex with MOZDPFDM, which likely accounts for the observed binding enhancement of MOZDPFDM over wild-type MOZDPF, and partly explains the high-affinity binding of H3K14cr by DPF2DPF. In sum, the molecular design of crotonylamide-sensing pocket is divergent between the two DPF domains in this report, and the in vivo functional significance of this divergence remains to be explored.

Bromo, DPF and YEATS domains are three major classes of histone lysine acylation readers with different binding capacity and recognition mechanisms for various lysine acylations14,15. In general, the bromodomains adopt a “side-open” pocket for readout of acyllysines with a binding preference of Kac>Kpr>Kbu⋙Kcr (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Most bromodomains do not recognize Kcr due to steric hindrance and only a few members such as TAF1 and BRD9 bind Kcr with compromised affinity15,33. By contrast, the YEATS domains harbor an “end-open” aromatic sandwich pocket for readout of acyllysines with a binding preference of Kcr>Kpr~Kbu>Kac (Supplementary Fig. 5b)15,16. In the case of DPF domains in this report, a “dead end” hydrophobic pocket is utilized for Kcr-specific recognition (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Although the DPF domains here display similar Kcr preference as YEATS, the origin of selectivity for Kcr is different. Aromatic-π stacking between the crotonylamide plane and aromatic sandwiching residues account for Kcr-specificity in the case of YEATS domains15-17; whereas, intimate hydrophobic encapsulation and coordinated hydrogen bonding are exploited for the selective readout of Kcr by DPF domains. Given the prevalence of histone acylations, the evolution of different reader pockets for Kcr recognition suggests more readers or effector modules await discovery for the functional readout of, but not limited to, histone acylations.

In summary, here we demonstrate that the DPF domains of MOZ and DPF2 are specific readers of H3K14cr. MOZ (KAT6A) is a transcriptional co-activator that acetylates histone H3 (K14, K9) and H4 (K5, K8, K12, K16) in vitro, and acts as a key epigenetic regulator of hematopoiesis23,34; while the ubiquitously expressed DPF2 (BAF45d) is required for the expression of genes associated with hematopoietic stem cells35,36. Given the important functions of MOZ and DPF2 in gene regulation, the observed 4- and 8-fold enhancement in their binding of H3K14cr over H3K14ac suggest regulatory mechanisms in which Kcr is a better potentiator for transcription control than Kac. MOZDPF and DPF2DPF are also well responsive to H3K14pr and H3K14bu, which likely play a role in gene regulation depending on the availability of the corresponding acyl-CoA. Often the level of each acyl-CoA is closely related to intracellular metabolic conditions10,15,26. Overall, the readout of histone Kcr by DPF domains calls attention to a new regulatory mechanism that links cellular metabolism to epigenetic regulations associated with chromatin modification and remodeling.

Sonicated chromatin was diluted in dilution buffer (16.7 mM Tris-HCl pH8.0, 1.1% Triton X-100, 1.2 mM EDTA, 167 mM NaCl, freshly added protease inhibitors), and was incubated with pre-treated beads and antibodies by rotation at 4°C overnight. Then beads were washed with washing buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 8.0, 1% NP-40, 0.7% DOC, 0.5 M LiCl, freshly added protease inhibitors) for 5 times followed by wash with TE buffer once. 100ul elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 1mM EDTA, 1% SDS) was added and beads were incubated in Thermo mixer at 65°C for 30 min with max speed. Supernatant was collected and treated with protease K at 55°C for 2 h, then purified with DNA purification kit (Tiangen, Cat. DP203-02). RNA was removed by treatment of RNase at 37°C for 1 hour.
