Despite the importance of exon junction complexes (EJCs) to co- and
post-transcriptional gene control, how and when the EJC core of EIF4A3, RBM8A,
and MAGOH, and the peripheral factors of the alternative RNPS1-EJC and
CASC3-EJC, assemble on spliceosomes remain unclear. Existing characterizations
that order EJC assembly on spliceosomes offer different conclusions. Using
immunoprecipitations (IPs) of EIF4A3, RNPS1, or CASC3 in the presence of RNase I
followed by liquid chromatography followed by tandem mass spectrometry
(LC-MS/MS) analyses of human HEK293T cells, we expand upon those splicing
factors that associate with EJC constituents. Referencing these data against
existing cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) spliceosome structures at
different stages of pre-messenger RNA (pre-mRNA) splicing, coupled with data
from a series of western blots of nuclear fractions before and after IP, we
report that EIF4A3, RBM8A, MAGOH, and RNPS1 associate with assembled but
inactive spliceosomes. This is followed by CASC3 joining to late activated
spliceosomes. Our work bridges long-standing gaps in understanding EJC assembly
on spliceosomes.

Introduction

Splicing i.e. the removal of introns from nuclear pre-messenger RNAs (pre-mRNAs) during mRNA biogenesis, involves carefully orchestrated interactions and structural rearrangements among spliceosome proteins and small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs) [1]. Most mammalian pre-mRNAs harbor multiple introns that must be removed to produce mRNAs that contain only exons and encode proteins with the correct sequence, subcellular location, and expression level. Intron removal is coupled to the deposition of an exon junction complex (EJC) at a position centered 27-nucleotides upstream of ∼80% of the resulting exon–exon junctions [2–4]. EJCs play crucial roles in both nuclear and cytoplasmic mRNA metabolism [5]. As examples, EJCs in the nucleus physically occlude m6A modifications [6] and promote mRNA export to the cytoplasm by recruiting ALYREF, a constituent of the TREX complex [7, 8]. EJCs in the cytoplasm augment mRNA translation by recruiting translation initiation factors, and they trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) should one reside sufficiently downstream of a termination codon [5, 9].

All EJCs are thought to contain EIF4A3, which directly binds the RNA at the EJC deposition site, and a heterodimer containing RBM8A (also called Y14) and MAGOH [10]. Many EJCs also contain UPF3B (also called UPF3X), and the alternative RNPS1- or CASC3-EJCs contain, respectively, RNPS1, Acinus 1 (ACIN1 or, in its place, Pinin), SAP18, and UPF2, or CASC3 (also called BTZ or MLN51) and the cell-signaling kinase AKT [11–15]. Insulin, which augments AKT signaling [16], increases the number of AKT-containing EJCs and NMD efficiency [11]. However, there is much about EJCs that remains unclear. Here, we sought to determine how and when EJC core constituents and the alternative EJC constituents RNPS1 and CASC3 assemble on spliceosomes, and if any steps of EJC formation on spliceosomes are regulated by insulin.

Using liquid chromatography followed by tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to identify proteins from HEK293T-cell lysates that co-immunoprecipitate with EIF4A3 in the presence of RNase I, we identified constituents shared by pre-B and B spliceosome complexes. We also identified the splicing factors CWC27 and CWC22, which are first detected in Bact-I [17]. Notably, the eight characterized Bact complexes describe multiple sub-stages having different structural conformations and splicing factor compositions [17–19]. Unexpectedly, we found that CWC27 and CWC22 form a complex with EIF4A3, even when pre-B complex formation, which precedes Bact-I formation, was inhibited using Pladienolide B (PB). Thus, we hypothesize that a second mechanism utilizes CWC27 and CWC22 as an “EIF4A3 recruitment complex”, consistent with previous reports that CWC27 and CWC22 are important for EIF4A3 recruitment to spliceosomes and deposition upstream of exon–exon junctions [20–22]. We find that not only EIF4A3 but also the entire EJC core join spliceosomes as early as the pre-B or B complex with the joining of RBM8A and MAGOH to EIF4A3. RNPS1 is also detected in pre-B and/or B complexes, while CASC3 is recruited to Bact complexes that lack detectable CWC27, i.e. likely after CWC27 departs the spliceosome. Together, our data show for the first time that EJC assembly in cells begins on spliceosomes prior to their activation and can proceed using multiple pathways.

ResultsPre-B- and B-complex splicing factors co-immunoprecipitate with EIF4A3Several models exist for the timing of EJC core assembly on spliceosomes during pre-mRNA splicing. In one model, based on data demonstrating that unspliced pre-mRNA co-immunoprecipitates with EIF4A3 in in vitro splicing reactions using HeLa-cell nuclear extracts, EIF4A3 was proposed to join the spliceosome in either the A or the B complex [29]. However, factors specific to either complex were not shown to associate with EIF4A3 [29]. In a different model, based on the findings that (i) EIF4A3 interacts directly, via CWC22, with a heterodimer of CWC27 and CWC22 in in vitro pulldowns of purified proteins and co-immunoprecipitates with FLAG-CWC27 in HeLa-cell lysates [21], and (ii) CWC27 is resolved in the cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the early Bact complex but not the mature Bact complex [18], EIF4A3 was proposed to join the Bact complex after CWC27 and CWC22 joining [21]. In yet another model, based on cryo-EM structures of spliceosomes demonstrating that EIF4A3 is not detected in any Bact complex but is detected in C and later complexes via a direct interaction with CWC22 and EFTUD2, EIF4A3 was proposed to join during the transition of the B* complex (for which there is no structure of the human B* spliceosome) to the C complex [17, 18, 30]. In any case, since in silico structural modeling indicates that RBM8A and CWC27 bind to EIF4A3 in a mutually exclusive manner [21], and yeast two-hybrid data indicate that RBM8A and MAGOH exist in cells as a heterodimer [10], RBM8A and MAGOH were predicted to join EIF4A3 on the C complex after CWC27 departs (Supplementary Fig. S1A) [17, 18, 21].We sought to supplement these data, initially by assaying for spliceosome constituents that co-immunoprecipitate with EIF4A3. We performed anti-EIF4A3 IPs in triplicate and, as a negative control, a rabbit (r)IgG IP, each in the presence of RNase I, using HEK293T-cell lysates. In these and subsequent IPs, treatment with RNase I enabled the analysis of individual EJCs (Supplementary Fig. S1B and C and Supplementary Data) and individual spliceosomes (Supplementary Fig. S1D and E and Supplementary Data). This is critical since many newly made transcripts contain more than two exons and, thus, are typically bound by multiple EJCs and/or spliceosomes at different stages of formation. The resulting HEK293T-cell lysates, before and after anti-EIF4A3 IPs, were then analyzed by LC-MS/MS (IP-MS; Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Data). As expected, anti-EIF4A3 IPs, relative to the rIgG IP, were enriched in the known EJC constituents RBM8A, MAGOH, UPF3B, RNPS1, ACIN1, PNN, SAP18, and CASC3 (Supplementary Fig. S1F). Several models exist for the timing of EJC core assembly on spliceosomes during pre-mRNA splicing. In one model, based on data demonstrating that unspliced pre-mRNA co-immunoprecipitates with EIF4A3 in in vitro splicing reactions using HeLa-cell nuclear extracts, EIF4A3 was proposed to join the spliceosome in either the A or the B complex [29]. However, factors specific to either complex were not shown to associate with EIF4A3 [29]. In a different model, based on the findings that (i) EIF4A3 interacts directly, via CWC22, with a heterodimer of CWC27 and CWC22 in in vitro pulldowns of purified proteins and co-immunoprecipitates with FLAG-CWC27 in HeLa-cell lysates [21], and (ii) CWC27 is resolved in the cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the early Bact complex but not the mature Bact complex [18], EIF4A3 was proposed to join the Bact complex after CWC27 and CWC22 joining [21]. In yet another model, based on cryo-EM structures of spliceosomes demonstrating that EIF4A3 is not detected in any Bact complex but is detected in C and later complexes via a direct interaction with CWC22 and EFTUD2, EIF4A3 was proposed to join during the transition of the B* complex (for which there is no structure of the human B* spliceosome) to the C complex [17, 18, 30]. In any case, since in silico structural modeling indicates that RBM8A and CWC27 bind to EIF4A3 in a mutually exclusive manner [21], and yeast two-hybrid data indicate that RBM8A and MAGOH exist in cells as a heterodimer [10], RBM8A and MAGOH were predicted to join EIF4A3 on the C complex after CWC27 departs (Supplementary Fig. S1A) [17, 18, 21].

We sought to supplement these data, initially by assaying for spliceosome constituents that co-immunoprecipitate with EIF4A3. We performed anti-EIF4A3 IPs in triplicate and, as a negative control, a rabbit (r)IgG IP, each in the presence of RNase I, using HEK293T-cell lysates. In these and subsequent IPs, treatment with RNase I enabled the analysis of individual EJCs (Supplementary Fig. S1B and C and Supplementary Data) and individual spliceosomes (Supplementary Fig. S1D and E and Supplementary Data). This is critical since many newly made transcripts contain more than two exons and, thus, are typically bound by multiple EJCs and/or spliceosomes at different stages of formation. The resulting HEK293T-cell lysates, before and after anti-EIF4A3 IPs, were then analyzed by LC-MS/MS (IP-MS; Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Data). The nuclear cap-binding protein heterodimer CBP80–CBP20 and the cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding protein PABPC1 were not detected in either IP (Supplementary Fig. S1F), consistent with efficient RNase I treatment.

Together, these data indicate that the earliest spliceosome constituents that are detectably associated with EIF4A3 are found in the pre-B and/or B complexes.

To increase the sensitivity of assaying spliceosome-associated EIF4A3, and to independently corroborate our IP-MS data, we repeated the anti-EIF4A3 and rIgG IPs in the presence of RNase I using nuclear fractions of HEK293T cells and assayed for spliceosome constituents using western blotting (Fig. 1B, upper).
