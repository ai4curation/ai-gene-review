The NACHT-, leucine-rich-repeat- (LRR), and pyrin domain-containing protein 3
(NLRP3) is emerging to be a critical intracellular inflammasome sensor of
membrane integrity and a highly important clinical target against chronic
inflammation. Here, we report that an endogenous, stimulus-responsive form of
full-length mouse NLRP3 is a 12- to 16-mer double-ring cage held together by
LRR-LRR interactions with the pyrin domains shielded within the assembly to
avoid premature activation. Surprisingly, this NLRP3 form is predominantly
membrane localized, which is consistent with previously noted localization of
NLRP3 at various membrane organelles. Structure-guided mutagenesis reveals that
trans-Golgi network dispersion into vesicles, an early event observed for many
NLRP3-activating stimuli, requires the double-ring cages of NLRP3.
Double-ring-defective NLRP3 mutants abolish inflammasome punctum formation,
caspase-1 processing, and cell death. Thus, our data uncover a physiological
NLRP3 oligomer on the membrane that is poised to sense diverse signals to induce
inflammasome activation.

INTRODUCTION

Inflammasomes are multi-protein complexes that activate inflammatory caspases in response to infection or “damaged-self” signals (Broz and Dixit, 2016; Collison, 2019; Hoffman et al., 2001a; Martinon et al., 2002). Activated caspase-1 in turn processes proinflammatory cytokines pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their mature forms and all inflammatory caspases can cleave GSDMD resulting in pore formation, cytokine release and pyroptotic cell death (Broz and Dixit, 2016). The NACHT, leucine-rich-repeat (LRR) and pyrin domain (PYD)-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) (Figure 1A) senses diverse stimuli, from bacterial toxins (e.g. nigericin), extracellular ATP, to particulate substances (e.g. uric acid crystals and amyloids) (Liu et al., 2020; Mariathasan et al., 2006; Martinon et al., 2006), although none binds to NLRP3 directly. Genetic mutations of NLRP3 cause serious auto-inflammatory disorders, including familial cold autoinflammatory syndrome and Muckle-Wells syndrome (Hoffman et al., 2001a; Hoffman et al., 2001b). Association of NLRP3 with inflammation-driven human diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases, neurological diseases and cancer, further underlies its importance as a clinical target (Collison, 2019; Hamarsheh and Zeiser, 2020; Swanson et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018).

NLRP3 belongs to the nucleotide-binding domain (NBD) and LRR containing protein (NLR) family. NBD is part of the NACHT domain that also contains helical domain 1 (HD1), winged helix domain (WHD) and helical domain 2 (HD2) (Wang et al., 2021) (Figure 1A). Based on the paradigm established from other NLR proteins such as NLRC4, the inactive state of an NLR is monomeric, and a disk-like oligomer is formed via the NACHT and LRR domains upon inflammasome stimulation (Wang et al., 2021). The disk structure then brings together the effector domains to form a template for engaging the apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC) adaptor, which in turn recruits and activates caspase-1. Previously, we used extensive protein engineering to stabilize a monomeric form of PYD-deleted NLRP3 (ΔPYD) (Sharif et al., 2019). This construct was then used to form a complex with NEK7, the centrosomal serine/threonine kinase that plays a scaffolding function in NLRP3 activation (He et al., 2016; Schmid-Burgk et al., 2016; Shi et al., 2016), to derive the molecular basis for the NLRP3–NEK7 interaction (Sharif et al., 2019). However, the physiological form of full-length NLRP3 in its native form remained unknown.

In this study, we found that endogenous full-length NLRP3 forms an inactive double ring cage that is primarily membrane localized, which is consistent with previously reported localization of NLRP3 on various membranous organelles – from endoplasmic reticulum (ER), to mitochondria, and to Golgi (Guo et al., 2018; Misawa et al., 2013; Subramanian et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2011). We showed that this cage structure is required to convert an NLRP3 activating stimulus to trans-Golgi network (TGN) dispersion, an established early event in NLRP3 activation (Chen and Chen, 2018; Daussy et al., 2021). Because dispersed TGN vesicles are further transported to the centrosome (also known as the microtubule-organizing center, or MTOC) to engage the centrosomal NEK7 and form the active NLRP3 inflammasome speck (Li et al., 2017; Magupalli et al., 2020), our studies uncovered the structural basis for this early step in the pathway that licenses NLRP3 activation.

RESULTSFull-Length NLRP3 Forms Large Oligomeric StructuresTo pursue structure determination of full-length NLRP3 in a physiological form, we stably reconstituted it with N-terminal FLAG and mScarlet tags into HEK293T cells which do not express inflammasome proteins and used the cytosolic extract for NLRP3 purification. Anti-FLAG affinity chromatography and FLAG-mScarlet tag removal followed by sucrose gradient revealed large NLRP3 species in heavy sucrose fractions (45–55%) (Figure 1B). Although a small amount of endogenous NEK7 co-eluted with NLRP3, it was mainly in the light fractions, not the heavy fractions (Figure 1B). Negative-staining electron microscopy (EM) of the heavy fractions revealed 20–25 nm-sized oligomers of NLRP3 (Figure 1C), which should be inactive as we did not stimulate the cells expressing them. Since NLRP3 can be stabilized by ADP and the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 (Sharif et al., 2019), and dATP was successfully implemented to stabilize the apoptosome (Zhou et al., 2015) and a plant resistosome (Wang et al., 2019), we also performed purifications in the presence of these additives, which resulted in similar NLRP3 particles (Figure 1C). In addition, the same complexes were observed in the presence of N- or C-terminal tags, indicating that the tags did not interfere with the oligomeric NLRP3 arrangement (Figure S1A). We collected cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) data on these samples. 2D classification of these datasets revealed that dATP gave most homogenous complexes with finer details, as compared to samples in ADP + MCC950 or with no nucleotide (Figure 1D); an ATPase activity assay showed that dATP, like ATP, was hydrolyzed by NLRP3 (Figure S1B).Cryo-EM Reveals Double Ring Cage Structures of Full-length NLRP3Cryo-EM structure determination by 3D reconstruction uncovered striking NLRP3 double ring cages (Figure 1E, F, S1C, D, S2, S3). The ADP and MCC950-containing sample gave rise to 6-, 7- and 8-fold complexes, with 12, 14 and 16 NLRP3 monomers, respectively (Figure 1E, S1C, S2). These three species are equally distributed indicating heterogeneity in the oligomerization. Despite at relatively low resolutions (5.8 Å, 7.7 Å and 9.5 Å for the 6-, 7- and 8-fold cages, respectively), each NLRP3 monomer with the NACHT and LRR density was clearly recognizable. The 6-fold cage appeared circular from the top view and was refined with the 6-fold symmetry; the 7- and 8-fold cages appeared somewhat elliptical in shape, which disallowed symmetry averaging and thus influenced the resolutions of the complexes (Figure 1E and S1C). The NLRP3 sample purified with dATP gave rise to the major class of a 6-fold double ring cage structure at 4.2 Å resolution which was used for model building (Figure 1F, S1D, S3). Local resolution estimation indicated lower resolutions at the NACHT domains, likely indicative of flexibility, as compared to the better resolved LRRs (Figure 1E, F, S1C, D). To our surprise, all NLRP3 maps showed presumed density of PYD - the only missing region - inside the NACHT-LRR cage (Figure 1E, F, S1C, D), though this domain of NLRP3 was least resolved (see below).NLRP3 Cage Formation Is Facilitated by LRR-LRR and PYD-PYD InteractionsThe final atomic model contains NACHT and LRR domains, and we did not model the PYD due to the absence of any specific features in its density. The NLRP3 cage is mainly formed by interactions between the curved LRRs in two interfaces, “face-to-face” and “back-to-back” (Figure 2A–C), with ~1,200 Å2 and ~500 Å2 buried surface areas, respectively, as determined by the PISA server (Krissinel and Henrick, 2007) (Figure S4A). Unlike active apoptosomes and NLRC4 oligomeric rings (Dorstyn et al., 2018; Tenthorey et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2015) in which the NACHT domains interact extensively, the NACHT domains in the NLRP3 cage are barely in contact. In addition, they assume an inactive conformation almost identical to those in the autoinhibited NLRC4 and the NLRP3–NEK7 complex structures (Hu et al., 2013; Sharif et al., 2019), with only a small, 5° rotation relative to the LRR domain (Figure S4B), which most probably emerges from the “pulling” on the NACHT domains by PYD-PYD interactions.The larger “face-to-face” interface in the NLRP3 cage is formed by charged and polar residues interacting in agreement with charge complementarity (Figure 2B, S4C, D). The smaller “back-to-back” interface is less charged, with involvement of many hydrophobic residues (Figure 2C, S4C, E). Mutational analysis revealed that both interfaces are crucial for the double ring cage formation. Three structure-guided mutants from the “face-to-face” interface (LRRm1: W830A_Y858C; LRRm2: N1008R_R1009E_E1010R_R1013E; and LRRm3: R771E_W773T_R776E) and two from the “back-to-back” interface (LRRm4: D809R_F810A_R813E; and LRRm5: H781E_Q782R_F785A) exhibited notable shifts in the sucrose gradient toward lighter fractions in comparison with WT NLRP3 and were impaired in NLRP3 double ring cage formation shown by negative-staining EM (Figure 2D and S5A, B). Only one mutant, the K970E_Q1001R_F1029A (LRRm6) from the “face-to-face” interface, showed a similar migration pattern as WT NLRP3 and retained the ability to form the double ring cage (Figure 2D and S5A, B). Surprisingly, deletion of PYD (ΔPYD) was also sufficient to disrupt the NLRP3 double ring structure (Figure 2D and S5A, B), indicating that PYDs interact and stabilize the complex despite exhibiting only weak density.The “face-to-face” interface in the double ring cage overlaps with the NEK7-binding surface and superposition of the NLRP3-NLRP3 complex with the NLRP3–NEK7 complex indicates a steric clash between NEK7 and an LRR (Figure 2E). We thus wondered if NEK7 competes with cage formation. To address this question, we incubated excess recombinant NEK7 with purified NLRP3 cage followed by sucrose gradient. This experiment showed that NEK7 addition resulted in a decrease of the peak for the NLRP3 cage and induced the appearances of smaller NLRP3 species (Figure 2F, S5C), which suggested that NEK7 competed and partly dissociated the double ring cage, as also supported by negative-staining EM (Figure 2G). However, NEK7 is a centrosomal kinase and resides predominately on the MTOC in interphase cells (Kim et al., 2007; Magupalli et al., 2020). Thus, NLRP3 may not engage NEK7 in resting cells; it colocalized with NEK7 once it was trafficked to the MTOC upon inflammasome stimulation (Li et al., 2017; Magupalli et al., 2020). The positive immunoprecipitation between NEK7 and NLRP3 in resting cells (He et al., 2016; Schmid-Burgk et al., 2016; Shi et al., 2016) was likely largely due to the cell lysis when proteins from different compartments encounter each other. We hypothesize that this spatial separation of NLRP3 and NEK7 in the resting state serves as one of the mechanisms to avoid premature activation of NLRP3. Of note, NEK7 interaction is not the only requirement for NLRP3 inflammasome formation; a conformational change to an active conformation in the NLRP3 NACHT domain also needs to occur at a certain step after stimulation (Sharif et al., 2019; Tapia-Abellán et al., 2021) to allow the assembly of an oligomeric, active NLRP3–NEK7 inflammasome at the MTOC.PYDs Are Shielded within Two NACHT-LRR Rings to Avoid Nucleating ASCAlthough the NACHT domain conformation of the NLRP3 monomer within the double ring cage suggests that the observed oligomeric complex is inactive (Figure S4B), the extra density (Figure S1C, D) and the role of the PYD in double ring cage formation (Figure 2D) imply a possibility that the cage contains oligomerized PYD and might be capable of initiating ASCPYD filament assembly. Therefore, we investigated whether NLRP3 cages could induce ASCPYD oligomerization using fluorescence quenching of Alexa488-labeled ASCPYD upon filament formation (Figure 3A, B, S5D–F). ASCPYD alone exhibited a low oligomerization rate in the absence of any nucleator. When mixed with NLRP6PYD-NACHT or NLRP3PYD-NACHT shown previously to nucleate its filament formation (Lu et al., 2014; Shen et al., 2019), ASCPYD exhibited increased initial rates of fluorescence quenching indicative of increased oligomerization in a manner dependent on NLRP6PYD-NACHT concentrations (375 nM - 1 μM) (Figure 3A, B) or NLRP3PYD-NACHT concentrations (600 nM - 3 μM) (Figure S5E, F). However, the same concentrations of the NLRP3 cages were unable to nucleate ASCPYD filament formation (Figure 3A, B and S5E), confirming that the double ring cage is inactive not only in its NACHT domain conformation, but also in its ability to nucleate ASC.We attempted to fit the presumed PYD density inside the double ring cavity with two short helical models of PYD filaments composed of 6, 7 or 8 subunits each in the 6-, 7- or 8-fold NLRP3 double ring cage (Figure 3C, D). There are two potential directions that the short helical segment could fit, with the C-terminal helices (H6) of the PYD oligomer pointing out or pointing in the cavity (Figure 3D). With the connectivity of the PYD to the NACHT domain, we chose to place the segments with H6 pointing out to connect more readily to the N-terminus of the NACHT domain. Interestingly, directionality of oligomerization has been observed in the death fold family including for PYD (David et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2014; Matyszewski et al., 2021), and the fitting we selected would place the elongating ends of the NLRP3 PYD segments (~80 × 80 × 5 nm) for interacting with ASC PYD inside the cavity, with the filaments straight along the symmetry axis or lying obliquely (Figure 3D). Given the narrow opening of the cage (~5.5 nm), it is tempting to hypothesize that the helical PYD segments are trapped inside, explaining why these cages do not nucleate ASCPYD oligomerization. Together, these observations imply that the cage arrangement may serve as a mechanism to shield PYDs within the NACHT-LRR double ring, thus combining oligomerization and inactivity.Overexpressed and Endogenous NLRP3 Are Mainly Membrane BoundWe noticed during the NLRP3 purification that most NLRP3 overexpressed in HEK293T cells was in the membrane pellet rather than the cytosolic extract (Figure S5G). Because NLRP3 has been shown to associate with various membranes (Chen and Chen, 2018; Guo et al., 2018; Misawa et al., 2013; Subramanian et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2011), we performed NLRP3 purification from the membrane pellet after solubilizing it with the n-Dodecyl β-D-maltoside detergent. Visualization of the purified protein using negative-staining EM revealed particles of similar size and shape as observed from the cytosolic extract (Figure 4A), suggesting that despite not being an integral membrane protein, NLRP3 is mainly membrane bound, perhaps as a peripheral membrane protein. Because the cytosolic purification gave cleaner particles, likely due to less membrane contamination, we did not further pursue structure determination of the NLRP3 particles from the membrane purification.We also examined the cytosolic versus membrane extracts of endogenous NLRP3 from LPS-treated immortalized bone marrow-derived macrophages (iBMDMs) and found a similar enrichment of NLRP3 in the membrane fraction in comparison with the cytosol fraction (Figure 4B). Furthermore, NLRP3 species from the membrane extract migrated mainly in the heavy sucrose fractions, though lighter NLRP3 species were also present (Figure 4C). Since we could not purify endogenous, tag-less NLRP3, we used native PAGE followed by Western blotting to estimate their oligomerization state. We observed that the NLRP3 membrane extract of LPS-treated iBMDMs, despite containing equal amount of NLRP3 with the cytosolic extract due to the loss of the protein during membrane solubilization, displayed a distinct band that ran at the similar position as purified NLRP3 oligomers (Figure 4D). By contrast, neither cytosolic nor membrane extract from NLRP3−/− iBMDMs showed signals on the same anti-NLRP3 Western blot. Thus, endogenous NLRP3 from iBMDMs is also mainly membrane bound, and the membrane-localized NLRP3 contains an NLRP3 oligomer similar in size to the purified NLRP3 cage from HEK293T cells.Interestingly, the cytosolic fraction of LPS-treated WT iBMDMs contained a number of smaller NLRP3 species (Figure 4D). Since native PAGE separates proteins based on both mass and charge, we performed Blue native PAGE analysis of this fraction, in which proteins are separated by mass (Figure S5H). Predominantly monomeric NLRP3 was observed, but dimeric NLPR3 was also present. Together, these data support that unlike other inflammasome sensors that are cytosolic and monomeric before activation, NLRP3 likely exists in an equilibrium among smaller species and the physiological cage structure on membranes that may be poised to sense NLRP3-activating signals.Membrane Association Promotes Double Ring Cage FormationTo investigate the membrane association of NLRP3, we used a lipid strip assay and found that the purified NLRP3 cage sample bound robustly to mono-, di- and tri-phosphorylated phosphatidylinositides (PtdIns) but not the unphosphorylated PtdIns, as well as phosphatidic acid (PA) (Figure 4E), similar to the previous data for short NLRP3 peptides (Chen and Chen, 2018). By contrast, NLRP3 with its PYD removed showed reduced lipid binding, likely due to the loss of avidity in the interaction (Figure 4E) since the construct was monomeric (Figure S5I). We further addressed whether membrane association of NLRP3 is crucial for the cage formation using mutagenesis. Because the conserved polybasic region at the PYD to NACHT linker has been shown previously to mediate TGN association (Figure 4F) (Chen and Chen, 2018), and is predicted to be exposed on the outside of the cage structure (Figure 3D), we first mutated the conserved polybasic residues to alanines in the monomeric, PYD-deleted NLRP3 (Figure 4F). In comparison with the WT monomeric NLRP3, the linker mutant displayed an even more reduced binding (Figure 4E), consistent with the role of these linker residues. We also introduced the same linker mutations to full-length NLRP3. Importantly, although the polybasic region is not at the double ring-forming interfaces, this linker mutant displayed markedly reduced formation of double ring cages, shown by sucrose gradient profiles (Figure 4G and S5J) and negative-staining EM (Figure 4H), indicating that membrane binding promotes NLRP3 cage formation.To address whether the loss of interaction with membranes was responsible for the impaired double ring cage formation in the full-length linker mutant, we targeted it to Golgi as a model membrane source by coupling it to a well described Golgi-binding pleckstrin homology domain of oxysterol-binding protein 1 (OSBP) (Balla and Várnai, 2009). Native PAGE analysis revealed that the OSBP-tag restored the linker mutant for double ring cage formation on the membrane, while the original linker mutant did not form any large complexes even on the membrane (Figure S5K). Further purification of the OSBP-tagged linker mutant (OSBP-linker mutant) from the membrane of reconstituted HEK293T cells revealed the similar double ring cages as for the membrane-purified WT protein under negative-staining EM (Figure 4I, compare to Figure 4A). We also generated an OSBP-tagged LRRm2 mutant and found that the mutant remained defective in cage formation (Figure 4I), indicating that the membrane localization per se is not sufficient and that the cage-forming interfaces are also required. We do not know whether a specific, or any membrane localization promotes cage formation. Nonetheless, these data suggest that membranes may serve as a scaffolding platform to promote NLRP3 cage formation. Of note, OSBP tagging did not interfere with function of the WT FLAG-mScarlet-NLRP3 (Figure S6A) and restored NLRP3 inflammasome activation of the linker mutant, but not the LRRm2 mutant (see below).NLRP3 Double Ring Cage Formation is Essential for TGN Dispersion and NLRP3 ActivationBecause our NLRP3 cage is an inactive NLRP3 form, we tested its role in TGN dispersion into small vesicles, an early event previously observed for diverse NLRP3 stimuli and viral infection (Chen and Chen, 2018; Daussy et al., 2021). In WT iBMDMs, but not NLRP3−/− iBMDMs, nigericin treatment induced the dispersion of intact TGN from its perinuclear cap-like morphology shown by TGN38 immunofluorescence (IF), and generated NLRP3 inflammasome puncta at the MTOC (Figure 5A, B, S6B–C). This effect was validated by another NLRP3 antibody together with an anti-58K Golgi antibody in WT and NLRP3−/− iBMDMs (Figure S6D–F), and channel controls (Figure S6G–I), although residual undispersed 58K Golgi staining remained upon nigericin stimulation due to its localization at both cis- and trans-Golgi. Stable expression of N-terminal mScarlet-tagged NLRP3 in NLRP3−/− iBMDMs rescued TGN dispersion upon nigericin treatment (Figure 5C). Importantly, treatment with the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 (Coll et al., 2019; Coll et al., 2015; Tapia-Abellán et al., 2019) prior to nigericin stimulation did not affect TGN dispersion while blocking NLRP3 activation (Figure 5D, S6J, K). These experiments confirmed that TGN dispersion is an upstream event in NLRP3 activation rather than a consequence of NLRP3 activation and pyroptosis. Moreover, the NLRP3 inflammasome puncta formed upon activation co-localized with the TGN38 marker (Figure 5A, C) – an effect also observed previously that is likely due to trafficking of TGN vesicles to the MTOC (Magupalli et al., 2020).To address the functional role of NLRP3 cage formation in NLRP3 activation, we reconstituted NLRP3−/− iBMDMs with WT FLAG-mScarlet-NLRP3 and 4 representative cage-disrupting mutants – 2 at the larger “face-to-face” interface (LRRm2 and LRRm3), 1 at the smaller “back-to-back” interface (LRRm5), and 1 with ΔPYD – and imaged the cells for NLRP3, ASC, 58K Golgi and Hoechst upon LPS priming or further nigericin stimulation (Figure 5E–I). Mouse Anti-58K Golgi was used instead of rabbit anti-TGN38 to allow simultaneous staining for ASC, which also utilized a rabbit antibody. In WT NLRP3-reconstituted NLRP3−/− iBMDMs, TGN dispersed upon nigericin treatment, and the NLRP3 and ASC inflammasome specks also contained 58K Golgi (Figure 5E), further supporting the trafficking of TGN vesicles. In NLRP3−/− iBMDMs reconstituted with the 4 cage-defective mutants, impaired TGN dispersion and defective inflammasome punctum formation were observed upon nigericin treatment (Figure 5F–I). Of note, such impairment in TGN dispersion emerged solely from disruption of NLRP3 cage and did not correlate with lower expression levels of NLRP3 (Figure S6L). Indeed, even lower expressing NLRP3 with intact cage formation (e.g. WT NLRP3 or OSBP-linker mutant) was capable to induce TGN dispersion, while higher expressing cage-deficient mutants were defective in TGN dispersion.We used OSBP-linked NLRP3 linker mutant and LRRm2 mutant to further dissect the role of the NLRP3 cage in NLRP3 activation. While the linker mutant, which was defective in cage formation (Figure 4H), also reduced TGN dispersion and inflammasome punctum formation (Figure 5J), the OSBP-linker mutant, which was able to form the cage structure (Figure 4I), mediated TGN dispersion and inflammasome punctum formation upon nigericin stimulation (Figure 5K). By contrast, OSBP did not rescue cage formation of the LRRm2 mutant (Figure 4I) and did not induce TGN dispersion and inflammasome punctum formation upon nigericin stimulation (Figure S6M). Thus, the double ring cage structure is required for nigericin-induced TGN dispersion and NLRP3 activation.Quantified percentages of cells with TGN dispersion upon nigericin stimulation from the microscopy images exceeded 90% for WT iBMDMs, NLRP3−/− iBMDMs reconstituted with WT NLRP3, and NLRP3−/− iBMDMs reconstituted with OSBP-linker mutant (Figure 6A). Importantly, pre-treatment of WT iBMDMs with MCC950 prior to NLRP3 activation did not reduce TGN dispersion (Figure 6A). 38% of the linker mutant-reconstituted iBMDMs exhibited TGN dispersion, and iBMDMs reconstituted with the remaining mutants showed even less TGN dispersion upon nigericin stimulation (≤ 18%) (Figure 6A). Further assessment of inflammasome activation from caspase-1 processing by Western blotting (Figure S7A) and cell death by release of the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) (Figure 6B) was consistent with the degrees of TGN dispersion. Importantly, at least LRRm2 and LRRm5 retained similar NEK7 interaction as the WT (Figure S7B), further supporting that their defective functions are due to impaired ability to disperse TGN. Thus, TGN dispersion and inflammasome activation upon nigericin treatment is dependent on cage-forming NLRP3.

NLRP3 Cage Formation Is Facilitated by LRR-LRR and PYD-PYD InteractionsThe final atomic model contains NACHT and LRR domains, and we did not model the PYD due to the absence of any specific features in its density. The NLRP3 cage is mainly formed by interactions between the curved LRRs in two interfaces, “face-to-face” and “back-to-back” (Figure 2A–C), with ~1,200 Å2 and ~500 Å2 buried surface areas, respectively, as determined by the PISA server (Krissinel and Henrick, 2007) (Figure S4A). Unlike active apoptosomes and NLRC4 oligomeric rings (Dorstyn et al., 2018; Tenthorey et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2015) in which the NACHT domains interact extensively, the NACHT domains in the NLRP3 cage are barely in contact. In addition, they assume an inactive conformation almost identical to those in the autoinhibited NLRC4 and the NLRP3–NEK7 complex structures (Hu et al., 2013; Sharif et al., 2019), with only a small, 5° rotation relative to the LRR domain (Figure S4B), which most probably emerges from the “pulling” on the NACHT domains by PYD-PYD interactions.The larger “face-to-face” interface in the NLRP3 cage is formed by charged and polar residues interacting in agreement with charge complementarity (Figure 2B, S4C, D). The smaller “back-to-back” interface is less charged, with involvement of many hydrophobic residues (Figure 2C, S4C, E). Mutational analysis revealed that both interfaces are crucial for the double ring cage formation. Three structure-guided mutants from the “face-to-face” interface (LRRm1: W830A_Y858C; LRRm2: N1008R_R1009E_E1010R_R1013E; and LRRm3: R771E_W773T_R776E) and two from the “back-to-back” interface (LRRm4: D809R_F810A_R813E; and LRRm5: H781E_Q782R_F785A) exhibited notable shifts in the sucrose gradient toward lighter fractions in comparison with WT NLRP3 and were impaired in NLRP3 double ring cage formation shown by negative-staining EM (Figure 2D and S5A, B). Only one mutant, the K970E_Q1001R_F1029A (LRRm6) from the “face-to-face” interface, showed a similar migration pattern as WT NLRP3 and retained the ability to form the double ring cage (Figure 2D and S5A, B). Surprisingly, deletion of PYD (ΔPYD) was also sufficient to disrupt the NLRP3 double ring structure (Figure 2D and S5A, B), indicating that PYDs interact and stabilize the complex despite exhibiting only weak density.The “face-to-face” interface in the double ring cage overlaps with the NEK7-binding surface and superposition of the NLRP3-NLRP3 complex with the NLRP3–NEK7 complex indicates a steric clash between NEK7 and an LRR (Figure 2E). We thus wondered if NEK7 competes with cage formation. To address this question, we incubated excess recombinant NEK7 with purified NLRP3 cage followed by sucrose gradient. This experiment showed that NEK7 addition resulted in a decrease of the peak for the NLRP3 cage and induced the appearances of smaller NLRP3 species (Figure 2F, S5C), which suggested that NEK7 competed and partly dissociated the double ring cage, as also supported by negative-staining EM (Figure 2G). However, NEK7 is a centrosomal kinase and resides predominately on the MTOC in interphase cells (Kim et al., 2007; Magupalli et al., 2020). Thus, NLRP3 may not engage NEK7 in resting cells; it colocalized with NEK7 once it was trafficked to the MTOC upon inflammasome stimulation (Li et al., 2017; Magupalli et al., 2020). The positive immunoprecipitation between NEK7 and NLRP3 in resting cells (He et al., 2016; Schmid-Burgk et al., 2016; Shi et al., 2016) was likely largely due to the cell lysis when proteins from different compartments encounter each other. We hypothesize that this spatial separation of NLRP3 and NEK7 in the resting state serves as one of the mechanisms to avoid premature activation of NLRP3. Of note, NEK7 interaction is not the only requirement for NLRP3 inflammasome formation; a conformational change to an active conformation in the NLRP3 NACHT domain also needs to occur at a certain step after stimulation (Sharif et al., 2019; Tapia-Abellán et al., 2021) to allow the assembly of an oligomeric, active NLRP3–NEK7 inflammasome at the MTOC.

Therefore, we investigated whether NLRP3 cages could induce ASCPYD oligomerization using fluorescence quenching of Alexa488-labeled ASCPYD upon filament formation (Figure 3A, B, S5D–F). ASCPYD alone exhibited a low oligomerization rate in the absence of any nucleator. When mixed with NLRP6PYD-NACHT or NLRP3PYD-NACHT shown previously to nucleate its filament formation (Lu et al., 2014; Shen et al., 2019), ASCPYD exhibited increased initial rates of fluorescence quenching indicative of increased oligomerization in a manner dependent on NLRP6PYD-NACHT concentrations (375 nM - 1 μM) (Figure 3A, B) or NLRP3PYD-NACHT concentrations (600 nM - 3 μM) (Figure S5E, F). There are two potential directions that the short helical segment could fit, with the C-terminal helices (H6) of the PYD oligomer pointing out or pointing in the cavity (Figure 3D). With the connectivity of the PYD to the NACHT domain, we chose to place the segments with H6 pointing out to connect more readily to the N-terminus of the NACHT domain. Interestingly, directionality of oligomerization has been observed in the death fold family including for PYD (David et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2014; Matyszewski et al., 2021), and the fitting we selected would place the elongating ends of the NLRP3 PYD segments (~80 × 80 × 5 nm) for interacting with ASC PYD inside the cavity, with the filaments straight along the symmetry axis or lying obliquely (Figure 3D). Given the narrow opening of the cage (~5.5 nm), it is tempting to hypothesize that the helical PYD segments are trapped inside, explaining why these cages do not nucleate ASCPYD oligomerization. Together, these observations imply that the cage arrangement may serve as a mechanism to shield PYDs within the NACHT-LRR double ring, thus combining oligomerization and inactivity.

DISCUSSIONIn summary, our discovery of a double ring cage structure of full-length NLRP3 sheds light on how the NLRP3 activation pathway may be organized. First, at its constitutive state without stimulation, NLRP3 is already mainly localized on membranes, and the membrane association acts as a scaffolding platform to promote the assembly of an inactive cage structure that is likely in equilibrium with inactive smaller species. This observation is consistent with a resting-state oligomer implicated previously by bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) (Compan et al., 2012). Importantly, the observed double ring cage shields the signal transducing PYDs within the assembly and thus prevents premature ASC recruitment. This strategy of using a “cage” to restrict protein activity is a recurring theme in biology, e.g., in the DegP and the proteasome system in which the proteolytic activity is caged inside the oligomers (Groll and Clausen, 2003; Jiang et al., 2008). We do not know exactly which membrane these NLRP3 cages reside; however TGN membrane localization of NLRP3 is visible before nigericin stimulation (Figure 5), and additional NLRP3 may be recruited to the TGN upon nigericin treatment as suggested previously (Chen and Chen, 2018). Because almost all NLRP3 activating stimuli converge to K+ efflux (Muñoz-Planillo et al., 2013; Yaron et al., 2015), it is reasonable that a drop in intracellular K+ concentration could enhance charged interactions and TGN recruitment of NLRP3.Second, all cage disrupting mutants of NLRP3 were defective in mediating TGN dispersion to stop the pathway at this early event common to many NLRP3 stimulations (Chen and Chen, 2018). Though the exact mechanism of the NLRP3 cage-mediated TGN dispersion remains to be discovered, both inactive and the presumed active NACHT conformations are compatible with the double ring structure (Figure 7), and NLRP3 does not require a conformational change in its NACHT domain to support TGN dispersion. Indeed, MCC950, which was shown to block inflammasome activation by keeping NLRP3 in a “closed” inactive conformation (Coll et al., 2019; Coll et al., 2015; Sharif et al., 2019; Tapia-Abellán et al., 2019), did not interfere with nigericin-induced TGN dispersion. On the other hand, gain-of-function NLRP3 disease mutants such as R258W (human R260W) which are supposed to be in an active conformation require NEK7 to signal (He et al., 2016) and induced TGN dispersion when overexpressed in HEK293T cells without stimulation (Tapia-Abellán et al., 2019). During the preparation of this manuscript, a complementary preprint on human NLRP3 decamer cage in complex with MCC950 was released (Hochheiser et al., 2021). Together, these findings suggest that the inactive conformation of NLRP3 within the cage is compatible with its hypothesized role in TGN dispersion.Third, we hypothesize that NLRP3 cage serves as a sensor coupling NLRP3-activating stimuli with NLRP3 transport on TGN vesicles to its activation center at the MTOC (Li et al., 2017; Magupalli et al., 2020). Without TGN dispersion, there would have been no NLRP3-containing vesicles to transport. Thus, by controlling TGN dispersion, the NLRP3 cage licenses NLRP3 transport and subsequent activation. If NLRP3 gets transported to MTOC in the form of the double ring cage, it is tempting to propose that once reaching the MTOC, the resident protein NEK7 might break the NLRP3 cage on TGN vesicles to enable further conformational rearrangement. However, we do not know when and where the NACHT domain changes to its active state that is likely needed for oligomerization of the NLRP3–NEK7 complex (Sharif et al., 2019; Tapia-Abellán et al., 2021).Because several phosphorylation sites have been reported for NLRP3 (Spalinger et al., 2016; Stutz et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2017) we also mapped these residues onto the NLRP3 model (Figure S7C, D). Residues S3 in the PYD and Y858 in the LRR were shown to inhibit NLRP3 activation when phosphorylated (Spalinger et al., 2016; Stutz et al., 2017). S3 situates at a PYD-PYD interface in an NLRP3 filament model derived from the ASCPYD filament structure (Lu et al., 2014) and its phosphorylation may inhibit cage formation due to loss of the PYD-PYD interaction, as well as ASC recruitment (Figure S7C). Similarly, Y858 participates in the LRR-LRR interaction and its phosphorylation may disrupt the NLRP3 cage, TGN dispersion and NLRP3 activation (Figure S7D). Residue S803 at the LRR was shown to be phosphorylated during priming but dephosphorylated during NLRP3 activation to enable NEK7 binding (Niu et al., 2021). S803 is located close to positively charged residues from the adjacent LRR (e.g. R1009), and the phosphorylation may stabilize NLRP3 cage to support TGN dispersion (Figure S7D). Consistent with the negative effects or the priming dependence of the phosphorylation events, mass spectrometry analysis found that our sample was not phosphorylated at Y858 or S803 (Figure S7E).Finally, some reports suggested that certain LRR-lacking NLRP3 variants can also form active inflammasomes (Hafner-Bratkovič et al., 2018; Rahman et al., 2020). Given that the NLRP3 cage is formed mainly by LRR-LRR interactions, these NLRP3 variants should not form cages, but may still reside on membranes due to their intact polybasic linker region. We hypothesize that they could form smaller active “speckles” on membranes upon stimulation, and that they bypass the transport on microtubules towards MTOC to engage NEK7, but rather rely on the relatively high concentration for their activation under overexpression conditions. Furthermore, NLRP3 in primed human monocytes with enhanced expression could be activated independently of NEK7 and without ASC speck formation at the MTOC (Gaidt et al., 2016; Schmacke et al., 2019), together suggesting an alternative NEK7-independent NLRP3 pathway. Of note, we expect that the stimuli driving conformational changes in the NACHT domain of NLRP3 may be the same for all pathways. METHOD DETAILS

Constructs and cloningFull-length, PYD-deficient (aa 126–1033, ΔPYD) and LRR-deficient (aa 1–665, NLRP3PYD-NACHT) mouse and human NLRP3 were cloned into pLenti CMVie-IRES-BlastR (Addgene plasmid #119863). The resulting constructs were further modified by addition of N-terminal FLAG-tag followed by fluorescent protein mScarlet and Tobacco Etch Virus (TEV) protease cleavage site upstream of the NLRP3 gene. An additional N-terminal FLAG and pleckstrin homology domain of oxysterol-binding protein 1 (OSBP) domain (aa 87–185) were added to selected constructs. Full-length mouse NLRP3 was also amplified with addition of N-terminal FLAG and C-terminal TEV sequences and cloned into an in-house modified pLV-eGFP vector, in which eGFP was replaced by mNeonGreen (mNG), for FLAG-NLRP3-TEV-mNG expression. Initial rounds of 2D classifications were performed in Relion3.1 (Scheres, 2012; Zivanov et al., 2018), after which the 2D classes were manually sorted into 6-fold, 7-fold and 8-fold species (190,566, 222,359 and 170,877 particles, respectively), imported into cryoSPARC (Punjani et al., 2017) and processed separately. For each species 3 initial models were generated with ab initio reconstruction. The best model was then used for 3D classification after a 35 Å low-pass-filtering in cryoSPARC (Punjani et al., 2017) using the heterogenous refinement mode for 3 or more outputs. The best 3D classes with the least particle distortion were merged and used for the non-uniform 3D refinement using symmetry D6 for a 6-fold double ring cage or C1 for 7- and 8-fold double ring cages, which resulted in final cryo-EM maps at 5.8 Å, 7.7 Å and 9.5 Å resolutions generated from 59,107, 174,967 and 108,875 particles, respectively. A dataset of NLRP3 purified in the presence of ADP and MCC950 yielded 1,376,027 particles picked by crYOLO (Wagner et al., 2019), which were extracted with 2x binning resulting in 1.65 Å pixel size. Initial rounds of 2D classifications were performed in Relion3.1 (Scheres, 2012; Zivanov et al., 2018), after which the 2D classes were manually sorted into 6-fold, 7-fold and 8-fold species (190,566, 222,359 and 170,877 particles, respectively), imported into cryoSPARC (Punjani et al., 2017) and processed separately. For each species 3 initial models were generated with ab initio reconstruction. The best model was then used for 3D classification after a 35 Å low-pass-filtering in cryoSPARC (Punjani et al., 2017) using the heterogenous refinement mode for 3 or more outputs. The best 3D classes with the least particle distortion were merged and used for the non-uniform 3D refinement using symmetry D6 for a 6-fold double ring cage or C1 for 7- and 8-fold double ring cages, which resulted in final cryo-EM maps at 5.8 Å, 7.7 Å and 9.5 Å resolutions generated from 59,107, 174,967 and 108,875 particles, respectively. The described cryo-EM workflow for this dataset is also presented in Figure S2 with gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) for each map. Local resolution estimation was calculated with Local Resolution Estimation module in cryoSPARC (Punjani et al., 2017). Post-processing of a 6-fold double ring cage map was performed with DeepEMhancer (Sanchez-Garcia et al., 2020).

A dataset of NLRP3 purified in the presence of dATP yielded 845,243 particles picked by crYOLO (Wagner et al., 2019), which were extracted with no binning resulting in 0.825 Å pixel size. Further processing was performed in Relion 3.1 (Scheres, 2012; Zivanov et al., 2018). The extracted particles were subjected to multiple 2D classifications until the classes appeared homogenous. Initial model was built with de novo reconstruction, low-pass-filtered to 40 Å and used for subsequent multiple rounds of 3D classification and refinement. The refinement of the best class containing 122,941 particles using D6 symmetry resulted in a 4.3 Å map, which could be further improved up to 4.2 Å by multiple rounds of per-particle CTF refinement followed by 3D refinement in D6. Bayesian polishing or beam tilt CTF refinement did not result in any further improvement (Zivanov et al., 2019, 2020). The final map was post-processed in Relion 3.1 (Scheres, 2012; Zivanov et al., 2018) for calculation of FSC curves and DeepEMhancer (Sanchez-Garcia et al., 2020) for model building and representation. The described cryo-EM workflow for this dataset is also presented in Figure S3. Local resolution estimation was calculated with Relion 3.1 (Scheres, 2012; Zivanov et al., 2018).

Model building and structure representationThe atomic model was built using cryo-EM map obtained from the NLRP3 complex purified in the presence of dATP (4.2 Å). 12 molecules of NLRP3NACHT-LRR without NEK7 from the structure of the NLRP3:NEK7 complex (Sharif et al., 2019) (PDB ID 6NPY) were first fit into the cryo-EM map using UCSF-Chimera (Pettersen et al., 2004) rigid body fit. From each chain NBD-HD1-WHD (aa 133–535) segments were extracted. Residues corresponding to the mouse NLRP3 sequence different from the human model were mutated in Coot (Emsley and Cowtan, 2004) and the rotamers were selected to position the side chains close to those from 6NPY NLRP3 model resulting in mouse NBD-HD1-WHD fragment (aa 131–533). LRR (aa 678–1033) and HD2 (aa 534–677) segments were extracted from the AlphaFold prediction of mouse NLRP3 structure (Jumper et al., 2021) and all domains were fitted separately into the cryo-EM map in UCSF-Chimera (Pettersen et al., 2004). Due to the modest resolution (4.2 Å) no further manual adjustments of the model were performed and the model was subjected to real-space refinement in Phenix (Adams et al., 2010; Klaholz, 2019) with the starting model as a reference. The final model represents a mouse NLRP3 dodecamer and includes residues Asp131-Trp1033 of each monomer. Interface analysis of the resulting model was performed with PISA (Krissinel and Henrick, 2007). Structure representations were generated using ChimeraX (Goddard et al., 2018) and Pymol (DeLano, 2002).
