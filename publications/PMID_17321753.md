Abstract

Synaptotagmin 1, encoded by the snt-1 gene in C. elegans , is a major synaptic vesicle protein containing two Ca 2+ -binding (C2) domains. Alternative splicing gives rise to two synaptotagmin 1 isoforms, designated SNT-1A and SNT-1B, which differ in amino acid sequence in the third, fourth, and fifth β-strands of the second C2 domain (C2B). We report here that expression of either SNT-1 isoform under control of a strong pan-neural promoter fully rescues the snt-1 null phenotype. Furthermore, C-terminal fusions of either isoform with GFP are trafficked properly to synapses, and are fully functional, unlike synaptotagmin 1::GFP fusions in mice. Analysis of isoform expression with genomic GFP reporter constructs revealed that the SNT-1A and -1B isoforms are differentially expressed and localized in the C. elegans nervous system. We also report molecular, behavioral, and immunocytochemical analyses of twenty snt-1 mutations. One of these mutations, md259 , specifically disrupts expression of the SNT-1A isoform and has defects in a subset of synaptotagmin 1-mediated behaviors. A second mutation, md220 , is an in-frame 9-bp deletion that removes a conserved tri-peptide sequence (VIL) in the second β-strand of the C2B domain and disrupts the proper intracellular trafficking of synaptotagmin. Site-directed mutagenesis of a functional SNT-1::GFP fusion protein was used to examine the potential role of the VIL sequence in synaptotagmin trafficking. Although our results suggest the VIL sequence is most likely not a specific targeting motif, the use of SNT-1::GFP fusions has great potential for investigating synaptotagmin trafficking and localization.

Introduction

Synaptotagmin 1 was first identified as an abundant integral membrane protein in synaptic vesicles ( Matthew et al., 1981 ). Later, additional related proteins were identified based on sequence similarity, and it became apparent that synaptotagmins comprise a large family of structurally similar molecules, with a single transmembrane domain near the N-terminus, and two domains with strong similarity to the Ca 2+ -binding C2 domains of Protein Kinase C ( Perin et al., 1990 ; Perin et al., 1991 ).

Synaptotagmin 1 plays an important role in synaptic vesicle fusion and endocytosis. In vitro biochemical studies have demonstrated that synaptotagmin 1 can form a complex with negatively charged phospholipids, and this complex binds Ca 2+ at physiological concentrations ( Brose et al., 1992 ; Davletov and Südhof, 1993 ; Chapman and Jahn, 1994 ; Fernandez et al., 2001 ). Therefore, it has been proposed that synaptotagmin 1 functions as a Ca 2+ sensor for the regulated exocytosis of neurotransmitters. Strong support for this conclusion has come from recent studies showing that changing the apparent Ca 2+ affinity of the synaptotagmin/phospholipid complex in vivo changes the Ca 2+ sensitivity of release ( Fernandez-Chacon et al., 2001 ; Pang et al., 2006 ). Current models suggest that synaptotagmin 1 regulates vesicle fusion by preventing constitutive fusion until Ca 2+ entry (see Südhof and Rizo, 1996 ).

Specific binding interactions have been reported between synaptotagmin 1 and a number of other presynaptic proteins, including syntaxin ( Kee and Scheller, 1996 ), neurexins ( Hata et al., 1993 ), and β-SNAP ( Schiavo et al., 1995 ). Some of these interactions appear to involve simultaneous multiple components, such as the 7S fusion complex, which includes synaptotagmin, synaptobrevin/VAMP, syntaxin, and SNAP-25 ( Söllner et al., 1993 ). These interactions suggest that synaptotagmin 1 may be involved in the formation and/or stabilization of exocytotic intermediates. Binding interactions have also been reported between synaptotagmin 1 and the endocytosis adaptor complex AP-2 ( Zhang et al., 1994 ), suggesting a role for synaptotagmin in endocytosis.

Studies of synaptotagmin 1 mutants, including gene knockouts, have been reported for Drosophila , C. elegans , and mice ( Nonet et al., 1993 ; Diantonio et al., 1993 ; Littleton et al., 1993 ; Diantonio and Schwarz, 1994 ; Geppert et al., 1994 ). A surprising result from these studies is that, although synaptic function is dramatically impaired in synaptotagmin-deficient animals, there is still some residual synaptic activity. Synaptotagmin 1-deficient ( snt-1 ) mutants of C. elegans exhibit behavioral defects, resistance to cholinesterase inhibitors, increased acetylcholine levels, decreased numbers of synaptic vesicles, and electrophysiological abnormalities ( Nonet et al., 1993 ; Nguyen et al., 1995 ; Jorgensen et al., 1995 ; Raizen et al., 1995 ; Miller et al., 1996 ). However, C. elegans snt-1 null animals are relatively active, and respond to touch, indicating that some regulated transmission remains.

Recent studies have shown that alternative splicing contributes to the structural and functional diversity of synaptotagmins. For example, alternative splicing of synaptotagmin 1 in Aplysia , mice, and humans specifies a change in the juxtamembrane region (reviewed in Craxton, 2004 ); this splicing appears to alter the release properties of synaptotagmin ( Nakhost et al., 2003 ). In addition, the C2B domain of synaptotagmin 1 undergoes alternative splicing in Caenorhabditis and Aplysia , and RNA editing in Anopheles and Drosophila ( Hoopengardner et al., 2003 ; Nakhost et al., 2004 ). In C. elegans and Aplysia , there are two alternative forms of the sixth exon, designated exons 6A and 6B. Crystallization studies of the C2 domains indicate that each consists of a β sandwich formed by two four-stranded β sheets (reviewed in Rizo and Südhof, 1998 ). Alternative splicing of exons 6A and 6B creates structural diversity within the third, fourth, and fifth β-strands of the C2B domain.

We report here that the two synaptotagmin 1 isoforms in C. elegans are differentially expressed and localized in the nervous system. SNT-1A is the predominant synaptic isoform; elimination of SNT-1A expression leads to severe growth and movement defects. In contrast, eliminating the minor SNT-1B isoform has no significant effects on behavior, although this isoform is sufficient in vivo to mediate at least one behavior, the expulsion step of the defecation motor program. Despite these differences, however, either isoform will fully rescue the snt-1 mutant phenotypes when expressed from a strong pan-neural promoter; therefore, both are capable of mediating excitation-secretion coupling in vivo . We also report our molecular, behavioral, and immunocytochemical analysis of a large set of snt-1 mutants. One of these mutations specifically eliminates expression of the SNT-1A isoform, while another disrupts intracellular trafficking of synaptotagmin.

Materials and Methods

Origin of mutants

All of the snt-1 mutants with md allele designations were isolated in our laboratory as spontaneous mutants resistant to the acetylcholinesterase inhibitor aldicarb ( Nonet et al., 1993 ; Nguyen et al., 1995 ; Miller et al., 1996 ), and were outcrossed at least 6 times. snt-1(ad596) (originally named ric-2 ) was isolated by Leon Avery ( Avery, 1993 ). snt-1(n2665) was isolated by Erik Jorgensen and H. R. Horvitz. The first report of n2665 indicated that it was more uncoordinated and grew more slowly that the putative null allele snt-1(md290) ( Jorgensen et al., 1995 ). However, we were able to separate n2665 from an unrelated slow-growth mutation present in the original strain; the outcrossed version of n2665 was used for the experiments described in the present study.

Growth and culture

C. elegans was grown on solid medium as described by Brenner (1974) , modified by the addition of streptomycin and mycostatin to reduce contamination, and the use of the streptomycin-resistant bacterial strain OP50/1 ( Johnson et al., 1988 ).

Sequence analysis

Sequencing primers were synthesized at the Molecular Biology Resource Facility at the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center, and most of the sequencing of mutants was performed by the DNA Sequencing Center at Oklahoma State University. The wild-type SNT-1A cDNA sequence was previously reported ( Nonet et al., 1993 ). The genomic sequence of the snt-1 region was determined for both strands by “primer walking”, using plasmid subclones from cosmid F58D4 as templates. This region was subsequently analyzed by the C. elegans Genome Sequencing Consortium (cosmid F31E8; Genbank Accession number U55856 ), with identical results.

Mutations were analyzed by amplification of specific 1-2 kb snt-1 genomic regions from individual mutant animals ( Barstead and Waterston, 1991 ; Williams et al., 1992 ). Many of the mutants had altered fragment lengths and/or missing primer-binding sites, which enabled us to determine the approximate nature of the rearrangements. The precise deletion endpoints or insertion sites were then determined by sequencing purified PCR products. The remaining mutations were analyzed by sequencing of PCR-amplified genomic DNA.

Behavioral tests

All behavioral measurements were performed at ∼22°C on young adult hermaphrodites. The procedures for observing and quantifying pharyngeal pumping, swimming, and expulsion failure have been described ( Miller et al., 1996 ). For expulsion measurements, 10 young adults of each strain were observed for 10-12 consecutive cycles, and the success rate was determined as the percent of defecation cycles that culminated in an enteric muscle contraction (EMC). Statistical significance tests used the Mann-Whitney U test.

Immunofluorescence Staining of Nematodes

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) to UNC-17/VAChT and polyclonal antibodies (pAbs) to synaptotagmin 1 have been described previously ( Lickteig et al., 2001 ). Nematodes were stained using a freeze-fracture procedure adapted from Duerr et al. (1999) . For anti-synaptotagmin staining, fixation consisted of 2% formaldehyde (electron microscopy grade from Ted Pella, Inc., Redding, CA) in 0.1 M phosphate for 4 hours. For double staining with anti-synaptotagmin and anti-UNC-17 antibodies, fixation consisted of 1% formaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate for 30 minutes.

Microscopy and Imaging

Epifluorescent images ( Figure 5 ) were collected on a Zeiss Axioplan photomicroscope using KODAK T-MAX 400 film, digitized, and cropped and annotated using Adobe Photoshop CS2. Confocal images (Figures 3 and 6 ) were collected on a Leica TCS NT confocal microscope. Images were collected with 40x or 100x magnification objectives, at 512×512 or 1024×1024 pixels, with 0.5 micron Z-steps. Images were cropped to size, assembled, and annotated using Adobe Photoshop CS2. All images within a given experiment were collected in the same session using identical settings and were processed identically for publication.

Primary cell culture

Embryonic cells were isolated and cultured in a procedure modified from Christensen et al. (2002) . Briefly, worms were grown in 2xYT liquid media with E. coli (OP50/1 strain) and harvested by centrifugation. Eggs were released by treatment with a bleach/ NaOH solution, treated with chitinase, and passed through a 5-μm filter in egg buffer. Dissociated cells were plated onto peanut lectin-coated coverslips at a density of ∼1×10 6 cells/ml, and allowed to differentiate and grow at 20°C in L-15-10 media for 7-10 days.

Generation and analysis of snt-1 cDNAs

A primer homologous to sequence 387 bp downstream of the termination codon (∼60 bp upstream of the putative snt-1 polyadenylation signal) was used in reverse transcription reactions to generate snt-1 cDNA from total C. elegans RNA. To generate full-length cDNAs, the single-stranded cDNA was amplified by PCR using a primer homologous to the SL1 sequence in combination with a primer 149-bp downstream of the termination codon. The resulting PCR products were subcloned into pBS (SK+) and then into a C. elegans expression vector ( Fire et al., 1990 ) containing the snb-1 (synaptobrevin/VAMP) promoter ( Nonet et al., 1998 ). To assess relative abundance of the SNT-1A and -1B transcripts, partial cDNAs were generated from total C. elegans RNA using primers in exons 5 and 8. PCR products were subcloned into the pCRII-TOPO vector and clones were analyzed by PCR using exon 6A- and 6B-specific primers in combination with a primer in exon 8.

Generation of mutant snt-1 cDNAs

To construct the SNT-1(ΔVIL) cDNA, a PCR fragment spanning the mutation was amplified from snt-1(md220) genomic DNA, and cloned into a synaptotagmin expression vector. Other mutations were introduced into the wild-type snt-1 cDNA using Stratagene's QuikChange protocol. Expression vectors containing the mutant cDNAs were used either directly for microinjection, or as templates to generate fusion products with the green fluorescent protein (GFP or variants).

Transgenic Methods

Transgenic nematodes were obtained by microinjection of DNA, essentially as described by Mello et al. (1991) . Transformation markers included the pBX plasmid (Heinke and Ralf Schnabel, Max-Plank-Institute fur Biochemie), which rescues the temperature sensitive lethality of pha-1(e2123) mutants. We constructed appropriate recipient strains containing the pha-1(e2123) mutation for transformation. In some experiments, GFP or GFP variant (CFP/YFP) fusions generated by PCR were used as transformation markers. Extrachromosomal arrays were integrated by gamma irradiation ( Schade et al., 2005 ).

Results

Sequence and exon structure of the snt-1 gene

The genomic structure of the C. elegans synaptotagmin 1 ( snt-1 ) gene is shown in Figure 1 . There are nine exons, including two alternate forms of exon 6. The alternative sixth exons were first identified in C. elegans ( Craxton, 2001 ), but were subsequently noted in Aplysia as well ( Nakhost et al., 2004 ). Exon 6A is 134 bp and encodes 45 residues, while exon 6B is 140 bp and encodes 47 residues. We obtained full-length cDNAs representing each of the alternate isoforms; these differ only in the identity of exon 6 ( Figure 1 ). We analyzed sixty cDNA clones and found fifty-seven clones corresponding to SNT-1A, one clone corresponding to SNT-1B, and two clones corresponding to a third gene product, containing both exon 6B and 6A. This third gene product, if expressed, would encode a truncated protein missing most of the C2B domain; we suspect that this transcript results from aberrant splicing. In contrast to previously published results ( Craxton, 2001 ), our data indicate that the SNT-1B-encoding transcript is far less abundant than SNT-1A transcripts.

Figure 1.

Pan-neural expression of either synaptotagmin 1 isoform rescues the snt-1 null phenotype

We used three quantitative assays of behavioral function to assess the extent of phenotypic snt-1 rescue. These assays measure repetitive neuromuscular function associated with different neural circuitry, muscle types, and frequencies. One assay measures the rate of pumping of the pharynx, a well-characterized stereotypic motor program ( Avery and Horvitz, 1989 ), normally occurring approximately 240 times per minute in wild-type adults ( Horvitz et al., 1982 ). A second assay measures the rate of swimming in liquid, which consists of approximately 150 body bends per minute for wild-type adults ( Miller et al., 1996 ). The third assay measures the frequency with which the defecation motor program, once initiated, goes to completion ( Thomas, 1990 ). This well-characterized program is normally initiated approximately every 45 seconds in adults that are feeding, and terminates in an enteric muscle contraction (EMC) which is mediated by GABA ( McIntire et al., 1993 ). The data from this assay are presented as the success rate of the enteric muscle contraction.

We engineered constructs in which the full-length SNT-1A and SNT-1B cDNAs were placed under control of the pan-neural snb-1 (synaptobrevin/VAMP) promoter ( Nonet et al., 1998 ), and fused to the coding region of GFP ( Horton et al., 1989 ; Hobert, 2002 ). We found that both constructs fully rescued snt-1 mutant phenotypes; rescued animals grew faster and were larger than their non-transgenic siblings, and the rates of swimming and expulsion success ( Figure 2 ) were not significantly different from those of wild-type animals. Rescue was strictly associated with the presence of the transgene, and there were no significant differences between the isoforms in ability to rescue the mutant phenotypes. Fluorescence was strongly associated with synaptic regions, and was similar to that observed with anti-SNT-1 antibody staining ( Nonet et al., 1993 ), although more fluorescence was observed in the neuronal cell bodies, perhaps because of high expression of the transgene. Clearly, when expressed under the control of a strong pan-neural promoter, both SNT-1 isoforms are capable of providing synaptotagmin 1 function. The ability of C. elegans synaptotagmin 1 to rescue the mutant phenotype when expressed as a C-terminal fusion with GFP contrasts with previously reported results on mouse synaptotagmin 1 ( Han et al., 2005 ).

Figure 2.

SNT-1 isoforms are differentially expressed in the C. elegans nervous system

We used a novel reporter-based system to examine the differential expression of the SNT-1 isoforms in vivo . Preliminary experiments indicated that a 5.7-kb genomic PCR product, beginning 542-bp upstream of the start codon, and ending just upstream of the poly-adenylation signal, is sufficient to rescue snt-1 mutant phenotypes. A fusion of this genomic product with GFP also rescued snt-1 mutant phenotypes ( Figure 2 ). To examine the alternative expression patterns of the SNT-1 isoforms, we engineered two mutant genomic reporters, one with a nonsense mutation in exon 6A, designated SNT-1B-only, and the other with a nonsense mutation in exon 6B, designated SNT-1A-only. These clones should contain all necessary sequences for appropriate splicing of the snt-1 pre-mRNA, but express only a single functional isoform (either SNT-1A or SNT-1B). These mutant constructs were injected into snt-1(md290) mutants as PCR-based CFP or YFP fusions.

We found that the SNT-1A-only construct fully rescued swimming ( Figure 2 ) and all other observed snt-1 mutant phenotypes. In contrast, the SNT-1B-only construct rescued the snt-1 defecation defect, but did not significantly rescue the growth and movement phenotypes ( Figure 2 ). The phenotype of SNT-1B-only transgenic animals was somewhat dependent on the dosage of the transgene; at high dosages, neither movement nor defecation behaviors were rescued (data not shown). Since expression of either isoform under control of a strong pan-neural promoter provides full rescue of snt-1 mutant phenotypes, these observations suggest that SNT-1 isoforms are differentially expressed in vivo .

To characterize these differences in expression, we integrated SNT-1A-only::CFP and SNT-1B-only::YFP transgenes separately, and constructed a strain containing both integrated transgenes. Imaging this strain revealed significant differences in SNT-1 isoform expression and localization. The SNT-1A isoform is abundantly expressed in most neurons ( Figure 3 ), and the distribution of this isoform is synaptic, resembling that of the endogenous SNT-1 ( Nonet et al., 1993 ). In contrast, the SNT-1B isoform is expressed at lower levels and in fewer neurons than SNT-1A ( Figure 3 ). Many neurons express both SNT-1A and -1B, although the relative amount of each isoform varies greatly between individual cells, and some neurons appear to express only the SNT-1A isoform. We also observed strong exclusive expression of the SNT-1B isoform in a single ventral cell located just anterior to the terminal bulb of the pharynx; we believe this is the excretory duct cell ( Figure 3 ). In addition, SNT-1B is strongly expressed in a cluster of tail neurons, including DVB; DVB is a GABAergic neuron required for the expulsion step of the defecation cycle ( McIntire et al., 1993 ).

Sequence analysis of snt-1 mutant alleles

We identified the sequence alterations for twenty snt-1 mutations. A cursory description of eight of these alleles ( md125 , md172 , md178 , md184 , md290 , md1167 , ad596 , and n2665 ) was reported previously ( Nonet et al., 1993 ). Sequence analysis of the mutant alleles ( Table 1 ) indicates that most are associated with DNA rearrangements. The md290 mutation is associated with a 3.3-kb deletion that removes ∼85% of the coding sequence for synaptotagmin, leaving only part of the N-terminal intravesicular region ( Figure 1 ). Therefore, we consider it to be a “molecular null” allele, and suggest that it should be considered the canonical snt-1 allele. As we previously reported, snt-1(md290) homozygotes are viable and fertile ( Nonet et al., 1993 ).

Six mutants are associated with small-scale alterations - insertions or deletions of 1 to 4 bp. Four alleles are associated with Tc1 transposon insertions, and one allele is associated with a tandem duplication. Although none of the alleles in the present study is a simple missense mutation, four of the mutations are predicted to preserve the reading frame of the protein. The md212 allele has a 66-bp deletion leading to the loss of 22 amino acids in the C2A domain; md220 has a 9-bp deletion leading to the loss of 3 amino acids in the C2B domain; md325 changes a G to CACT, converting a methionine to an isoleucine-threonine dipeptide in the C2B domain; and md178 is a 1-bp deletion of the T in the TAA termination codon, resulting in a predicted extension of the protein by 64 residues.

Behavioral analysis reveals residual function associated with some alleles

We measured pharyngeal pumping rates, swimming rates, and completion of the defecation motor program in the different snt-1 mutants ( Figure 4 ). Measurements for most of the mutants were similar to those obtained with the snt-1 null allele md290 ; these presumably also represent null alleles. However, four or five of the alleles led to performance that was markedly better than that of the null mutant in at least one behavior, and four other alleles displayed marginal improvement.

Figure 4.

Most snt-1 alleles lead to greatly reduced protein levels

We used an anti-synaptotagmin 1 polyclonal antibody ( Lickteig et al., 2001 ) to assess the degree of residual synaptotagmin expression in the snt-1 mutants. Most of the snt-1 mutants had little or no detectable synaptotagmin immunoreactivity ( Table 1 ), while retaining normal levels of UNC-17 immunostaining. Several of the mutants ( md125 , md184 , md249 , and md290 ) are predicted to lack anti-synaptotagmin immunoreactivity due to deletion of coding sequence or a frameshift near the N-terminus of the protein. However, as most of the alleles retain some or all of the snt-1 coding sequence, we conclude that either the mRNA or the truncated protein derived from these mutants is degraded.

The md259 mutation specifically disrupts expression of the SNT-1A isoform

The md259 mutation is associated with a 2-bp deletion in exon 6A ( Figure 1 ); this mutation should specifically disrupt expression of the SNT-1A isoform. Behavioral analysis of snt-1(md259) mutant animals indicates that although movement is severely impaired, the expulsion step of the defecation motor program is wild-type ( Figure 4 ). This result indicates that the snt-1(md259) mutation is not null, and the endogenous SNT-1B isoform is sufficient to mediate at least one behavior - the expulsion step of the defecation motor program. We note that the phenotype of the SNT-1B-only transgenic animals described above mimics that of the endogenous snt-1(md259) mutation. We stained snt-1(md259) mutant animals with an anti-SNT-1 antibody and did not observe detectable staining (data not shown). We conclude that endogenous expression levels of SNT-1B is too low to detect with our antibody; this observation is consistent with the apparent scarcity of SNT-1B cDNAs, and suggests that the SNT-1A isoform accounts for most of the synaptotagmin 1 function in C. elegans .

The md220 mutation alters the localization of synaptotagmin

The md220 mutation is an in-frame 9-bp deletion in exon 5 that eliminates the tri-peptide VIL from the second β-strand of the C2B domain ( Figure 1 ). md220 mutants express essentially wild-type levels of SNT-1 as evidenced by antibody staining ( Table 1 ), although the protein is entirely retained within the cell bodies instead of being trafficked to synapses ( Figure 5 ). Within the cell bodies, the immunostaining is non-uniform, suggesting that the mutant protein is associated with some intracellular compartment. As expected from the altered distribution of the mutant synaptotagmin, snt-1(md220) mutants are behavioral nulls ( Figure 4 ).

The localization of the vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT/UNC-17) appeared normal in md220 mutants ( Figure 5 ), as did the localization of other known synaptic and synaptic vesicle proteins, including choline acetyltransferase, the vesicular monoamine transporter, and UNC-13 (data not shown). Thus, the mislocalization of synaptotagmin is not due to a general mislocalization of synaptic vesicles or other synaptic components, but rather to a failure of the md220 mutant synaptotagmin to be properly trafficked.

Analysis of SNT-1 trafficking using a functional SNT-1::GFP fusion protein

As described above, a SNT-1A::GFP fusion expressed under control of the snb-1 promoter rescues the snt-1 mutant phenotypes. To analyze the effect of the md220 mutation on synaptotagmin trafficking, we introduced the corresponding deletion (ΔVIL) into the Psnb-1 ::SNT-1A expression construct, and then fused it to GFP. We found that the GFP fusion product did not rescue snt-1 mutant phenotypes (Figures 6 and 7 ). Furthermore, as determined by GFP fluorescence in both living animals and primary cell culture ( Figure 6 ), the mutant synaptotagmin is completely restricted to neuronal cell bodies, accurately reflecting the localization of endogenous SNT-1 in md220 mutants. Similar results were observed with SNT-1B(ΔVIL) (data not shown), demonstrating that the ΔVIL mutation disrupts trafficking of both SNT-1 isoforms.

Figure 7.

The SNT-1 trafficking defects observed with deletion of the VIL tri-peptide could result either from elimination of a specific trafficking motif, or from disruption of protein folding, leading to retention by monitors of protein folding such as chaperonins. Therefore, we made a series of mutations to distinguish between these two possibilities. When we replaced the VIL tri-peptide with three alanine residues (VIL > AAA), we found that constructs carrying this substitution also failed to rescue the snt-1 mutant phenotypes, and the GFP-tagged protein was restricted to cell bodies (Figures 6 and 7 ). Replacing residues directly adjacent to VIL with AAA (LTVVIL > AAAVIL) also failed to rescue and did not restore proper protein localization ( Figure 7 ). The effect on protein trafficking appeared to be unique to the C2B domain, as an AAA substitution for TVI in the analogous position of the C2A domain permitted partial rescue of the snt-1 phenotypes, and proper synaptic localization ( Figure 7 ). However, replacing the VILE motif from the C2B domain with TVIQ from C2A fully rescued snt-1 mutant phenotypes and restored proper SNT-1 localization ( Figure 7 ). This result indicates that the VIL sequence is not strictly necessary for proper intracellular trafficking of synaptotagmin, and it implies that the effects on trafficking are most likely the result of improper protein folding.

Discussion

Differential expression and function of synaptotagmin 1 isoforms in C. elegans

Alternative splicing of synaptotagmin 1 pre-mRNA gives rise to two distinct protein isoforms in C. elegans , as well as some other invertebrates ( Craxton, 2001 ; Nakhost et al., 2004 ). Two alternative exons, designated 6A and 6B, encode the third, fourth, and fifth β-strands in the C2B domain, and alternative splicing of these exons creates structural diversity within this domain. Overall, these exons are 54% identical in amino acid sequence.

We find that the two synaptotagmin 1 isoforms are differentially expressed in the C. elegans nervous system; SNT-1A is the more abundant isoform and is expressed throughout the nervous system, while SNT-1B is expressed in fewer cells and is generally present at much lower levels. Nakhost et al. (2004) also noted that the Aplysia form corresponding to SNT-1B is the less abundant isoform, comprising approximately 25% of the total amount of synaptotagmin 1.

Both of the C. elegans synaptotagmin 1 isoforms are capable of functioning in the release of neurotransmitters. We found that expression of either isoform under control of a strong pan-neural promoter fully rescued snt-1 mutant phenotypes. However, loss of the endogenous SNT-1A isoform results in severe defects in synaptic function, whereas loss of endogenous SNT-1B expression has no obvious effects on synaptic function in the presence of high levels (over-expression) of SNT-1A. These phenotypic differences appear to reflect the relative abundance and distribution of the synaptotagmin isoforms.

The role of the minor SNT-1B isoform is unclear; while this isoform is sufficient to mediate certain behaviors, such as enteric muscle contraction, it appears not to be necessary. The SNT-1B isoform appears to be the only isoform expressed in the excretory duct cell. This cell is part of the excretory system, which functions as a kidney, excreting fluid to maintain osmotic balance, and to remove metabolites ( Nelson and Riddle, 1984 ; Buechner et al., 1999 ). Nelson and Riddle (1984) found that when the excretory duct cell was ablated, the animals filled with fluid within 24 hours and died within a few days, producing very few progeny. Since elimination of SNT-1B expression does not phenocopy excretory duct cell ablation, we conclude that SNT-1B is not essential for the function of this cell. We cannot exclude the possibility that there are more subtle effects on excretory duct cell function, such as sensitivity to osmotic shock or defects in metabolite excretion. Likewise, our behavioral analyses do not exclude the possibility that SNT-1B modulates a particular set of behaviors not tested in our assays, or has more subtle effects that are not detectable in our behavioral paradigms.

The biological function of this alternative splicing is currently under investigation. As noted earlier, the C2B domain participates in a wide variety of protein-lipid and protein-protein interactions. Specific binding interactions have been reported between synaptotagmin 1 and anionic phospholipids, SNARES, and molecules involved in endocytosis, such as the clathrin adapter protein complex (reviewed in Tucker and Chapman, 2002 ). In addition, numerous biochemical and genetic studies suggest that the C2B domain participates in Ca 2+ -dependent oligomerization ( Osborne et al., 1999 ; Littleton et al., 2001 ). Nakhost et al. (2004) found in Aplysia that the t-SNARE SNAP-25 binds synaptotagmin 1B with a higher affinity than synaptotagmin 1A. These results suggest that this alternative splicing alters the biochemical properties of the C2B domain, potentially modulating interactions with other synaptic proteins.

Molecular, behavioral and immunocytochemical analysis of snt-1 mutants

The md290 mutation is a large deletion that removes most of the snt-1 coding sequence and therefore is a molecular null allele. This is in agreement with our phenotypic analysis: no other snt-1 allele confers a stronger phenotype. The viability of snt-1 null mutants argues that synaptotagmin 1, although important for proper control of regulated secretion, is not essential for synaptic transmission ( Nonet et al., 1993 ). This is in contrast to C. elegans genes known to be essential for synaptic transmission, such as snb-1 , unc-64 , and unc-104 - not only are some alleles of these genes lethal, but the viable alleles are quite variable and form a graded series of phenotypes ( Hall and Hedgecock, 1991 ; Nonet et al., 1998 ; Saifee et al., 1998 ).

All of the snt-1 alleles we examined are associated with insertions or deletions. We believe this reflects the method of mutant isolation; all of the md alleles were isolated as spontaneous mutations in mutator strains. Since most of the snt-1 mutations in our collection are associated with DNA rearrangements, it is not surprising that many of these mutations lead to a null phenotype. However, four (or perhaps five) of the snt-1 mutants must retain some gene function because their performance in at least one of the behavioral assays was markedly better than that of the null mutants. Two of these alleles, md1062 and md1220 , are associated with Tc1 transposon insertions; the mild phenotypes of these alleles probably reflect removal of the transposon from the mRNA through a splicing mechanism ( Rushforth and Anderson, 1996 ). We note that the behavioral assays appear to provide a more stringent assessment of residual gene function than immunostaining; several of the mutants with no detectable immunostaining have measurably non-null behavior.

The role of the C2B domain in synaptotagmin 1 trafficking

The md220 mutation is an in-frame deletion that removes a conserved VIL tripeptide from the C2B domain; loss of this conserved motif does not significantly affect expression levels, but the mutant synaptotagmin is not incorporated into synaptic vesicles. We have not determined the precise accumulation site of the altered synaptotagmin, but it appears to be a membrane compartment, probably the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). This conclusion is based on the presence of a normal transmembrane domain in the md220 mutant synaptotagmin, as well as the appearance of the synaptotagmin immunoreactivity.

Based on these observations, we generated several additional mutations in a rescuing SNT-1A::GFP construct. Deleting the VIL tri-peptide, or replacing it with three alanines, phenocopied the effect of the md220 mutation on synaptotagmin trafficking. A mutation in the analogous region of the C2A domain did not disrupt protein trafficking, suggesting that this region of C2B domain may either be specifically important for trafficking, or particularly susceptible to structural perturbation. However, replacing the VILE motif from the C2B domain with TVIQ from C2A restored proper SNT-1 localization and function, indicating that the VIL sequence is not strictly necessary for proper intracellular trafficking of synaptotagmin. Overall, the most likely explanation for our results is that alterations in the second β-strand are incompatible with proper protein folding, and lead to retention of the mutant synaptotagmin in the ER by chaperonins and other general regulators of protein trafficking.

However, it is also possible that the VIL sequence in the C2B domain is part of a specific protein-sorting signal. This sequence is absolutely conserved across animal phyla for synaptotagmin 1. The comparable part of the C2A domain is not nearly as well conserved, suggesting that this trafficking function is specific to the C2B domain. The C2B domain of synaptotagmin has been implicated in a number of specific protein-protein interactions (reviewed by Tucker and Chapman, 2002 ). It is possible that mutating the VIL motif specifically, or perhaps the second β-strand generally, disrupts interactions with other binding partners, and these interactions regulate the trafficking of synaptotagmin.

Our analysis of synaptotagmin trafficking was facilitated by the construction of functional synaptotagmin 1 fusions with GFP. In contrast to previously reported results on mouse synaptotagmin 1 ( Han et al., 2005 ), we found that C. elegans synaptotagmin 1 is fully functional when expressed as a C-terminal fusion with GFP. The ability of SNT-1::GFP fusions to rescue the mutant phenotype was not dependent on the presence of a “linker” between synaptotagmin and GFP; we found that fusions with no linker or a thirty amino acid linker gave comparable results in rescue experiments (our unpublished results). We note that a C-terminal fusion of Drosophila synaptotagmin 1 to a seventeen amino acid sequence, which includes a tetracysteine motif for in vivo labeling with biarsenical dyes, is also fully functional ( Marek and Davis, 2002 ). Han et al. (2005) suggest a model of synaptotagmin function in which “synaptotagmin 1 has to fit into a constrained space in association with SNARE complexes on docked and primed vesicles”. These authors hypothesize that the bulky GFP tag makes it impossible for synaptotagmin 1 to fit into this space, preventing synaptotagmin from functioning properly. Given that a C-terminal GFP tag does not block synaptotagmin function in C. elegans , and assuming that vesicle fusion in C. elegans is mechanistically and structurally similar to that in mice, we favor the alternate idea suggested by Han et al. (2005) that the GFP tag blocks an uncharacterized binding reaction specific to mouse vesicle fusion.
