In the hippocampus, signaling through G protein-coupled receptors is modulated
by Regulators of G protein signaling (Rgs) proteins, which act to stimulate the
rate of GTP hydrolysis, and consequently, G protein inactivation. The R7-Rgs
subfamily selectively deactivates the G(i/o)-class of Gα subunits that mediate
the action of several GPCRs. Here, we used co-immunoprecipitation,
electrophysiology and immunoelectron microscopy techniques to investigate the
formation of macromolecular complexes and spatial relationship of Rgs7/Gβ5
complexes and its prototypical signaling partners, the GABAB receptor and Girk
channel. Co-expression of recombinant GABAB receptors and Girk channels in
combination with co-immunoprecipitation experiments established that the
Rgs7/Gβ5 forms complexes with GABAB receptors or Girk channels. Using
electrophysiological experiments, we found that GABAB -Girk current deactivation
kinetics was markedly faster in cells coexpressing Rgs7/Gβ5. At the electron
microscopic level, immunolabeling for Rgs7 and Gβ5 proteins was found primarily
in the dendritic layers of the hippocampus and showed similar distribution
patterns. Immunoreactivity was mostly localized along the extrasynaptic plasma
membrane of dendritic shafts and spines of pyramidal cells and, to a lesser
extent, to that of presynaptic terminals. Quantitative analysis of immunogold
particles for Rgs7 and Gβ5 revealed an enrichment of the two proteins around
excitatory synapses on dendritic spines, virtually identical to that of Girk2
and GABAB1 . These data support the existence of macromolecular complexes
composed of GABAB receptor-G protein-Rgs7-Girk channels in which Rgs7 and Gβ5
proteins may preferentialy modulate GABAB receptor signaling through the
deactivation of Girk channels on dendritic spines. In contrast, Rgs7 and Girk2
were associated but mainly segregated from GABAB1 in dendritic shafts, where
Rgs7/Gβ5 signaling complexes might modulate Girk-dependent signaling via a
different metabotropic receptor(s).

Introduction

G protein-coupled signalling is a major cellular mechanism for controlling excitability in central neurons. Signalling is initiated upon binding of ligand to a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) that catalyzes GDP/GTP exchange on the heterotrimeric G protein, leading to dissociation of Gα-GTP and Gβγ subunits and their subsequent modulation of enzymes or ion channels (Smrcka, 2008). An integral part of G protein-coupled signalling pathway are the regulator of G-protein signalling (Rgs) proteins, which accelerate rates of G-protein deactivation by acting as GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs) for G protein α-subunits, resulting in faster response termination of GPCR signal transmission (Ross and Wilkie, 2000; Hollinger and Hepler, 2002; Anderson et al., 2009).

Rgs proteins represent a diverse family of over 30 members that are classified into six subfamilies (Zheng et al. 1999; Anderson et al., 2009). The R7-Rgs subfamily consists of four highly-homologous proteins (Rgs6, 7, 9 and 11) which are predominantly expressed in the nervous system (Gold et al., 1997), where they play a key role in synaptic transmission, light perception, neuronal development, and sensitivity to addictive drugs by regulating several GPCR pathways (Anderson et al., 2009; Slepak, 2009). Therefore, alteration of the expression or function of these proteins is expected to have a significant effect on neuronal function.

The R7-Rgs subfamily is unique in its ability to heterodimerize with the type 5 G protein beta (Gβ5) subunit (Cabrera et al. 1998; Makino et al. 1999). This association is essential for the folding, stability and expression of R7-Rgs proteins (He et al., 2000; Witherow et al., 2000) and accordingly, ablation of the Gβ5 gene in mice results in functional elimination of the entire R7 family (Chen et al., 2003). R7-Rgs/Gβ5 complexes selectively deactivate the Gi/o-class of Gα subunits that mediate the diverse actions of several GPCRs (Posner et al., 1999; Rose et al., 2000; Hooks et al., 2003), including GABAB receptors.

One of the best characterised effectors modulated by GABAB receptors is the G protein-gated inwardly rectifying K+ (Girk or KIR3) channel (North, 1989). Neurons in the hippocampus express high levels of Girk channels (Karschin et al., 1996), GABAB receptors (Kaupmann et al., 1998; Kulik et al., 2003), Rgs7 (Gold et al., 1997) and Gβ5 proteins (Zhang et al., 2000). Recently, we demonstrated that GABAB-Girk signalling in hippocampal pyramidal neurons is negatively-modulated by Rgs/Gβ5 (Xie et al., 2010). It is not clear, however, whether Girk channels and cognate GABAB receptors function within macromolecular assemblies that include regulatory Rgs proteins in vivo. Here, we probed for the possible functional macromolecular complexes between Rgs7, Gβ5 and GABAB receptors or Girk channels using co-immunoprecipitation and electrophysiology. In addition, we studied the subcellular localization of Rgs7, Gβ5, GABAB1 and Girk2 and determined their compartment-dependent localization in pyramidal cells by using quantitative immunohistochemical techniques. Our findings indicate that the four proteins are similarly distributed in dendritic spines adjacent to excitatory synapses, while there is a segregation of Rgs7/Gβ5-Girk channels and GABAB receptors in dendritic shafts.

ElectrophysiologyCoverslips containing transfected HEK293 cells were transferred to a chamber containing a low-K+ bath solution (in mM): 145 NaCl, 4 KCl, 1.8 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 5.5 D-glucose, 5 HEPES/NaOH (pH 7.4). Transfected cells were visualized using an Olympus IX-70 microscope with epifluorescence detection. Whole-cell currents were measured with hardware (Axopatch-200B amplifier, Digidata 1320) and software (pCLAMP v. 9.0) from Molecular Devices, Inc. (Sunnyvale, CA). Borosilicate patch pipettes (3–5 MΩ) were filled with (in mM): 130 KCl, 10 NaCl, 1 EGTA/KOH (pH 7.2), 0.5 MgCl2, 10 HEPES/KOH (pH 7.2), 2 Na2ATP, 5 phosphocreatine, 0.3 GTP. Basal and baclofen-induced whole-cell currents were measured at a holding potential of −70 mV, at room temperature, in a high-K+ bath solution (in mM): 120 NaCl, 25 KCl, 1.8 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 5.5 D-glucose, 5 HEPES/NaOH (pH 7.4). The high-K+ bath solution (+/− 100 μM baclofen) was applied directly to the cell with an SF-77B rapid perfusion system (Warner Instruments, Inc.; Hamden, CT), which permits complete solution exchange in <20 ms. Baclofen (R-(+)-b-(aminomethyl)-4-chlorobenzenepropanoic acid hydrochloride) was purchased from Sigma (St. Louis, MO). Baclofen was dissolved as a high concentration (100 mM) stock solution and divided into single-use aliquots stored at −20°C.Currents were low-pass filtered at 1 kHz, sampled at 2 kHz, and stored on computer hard disk for subsequent analysis. Basal currents were defined as the component of the holding current measured in high-K+ bath solution that was blocked by 0.3 mM Ba2+. Peak and steady-state baclofen-induced current densities were measured for each experiment (Posokhova et al., 2010). Only those experiments in which the access resistances were stable and low (<15 MΩ) were included in the final analysis. Baclofen-induced current activation rates were extracted from a single exponential fit (standard Boltzmann equation) of the current trace corresponding to the onset of drug effect and the peak evoked current, while deactivation rates were extracted from an exponential fit of the trace corresponding to the return of current to baseline following removal of drug (Posokhova et al., 2010).
