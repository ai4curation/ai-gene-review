The Sex-linked orange mutation in domestic cats causes variegated patches of
reddish/yellow hair and is a defining signature of random X inactivation in
female tortoiseshell and calico cats. Unlike the situation for most coat color
genes, there is no apparent homolog for Sex-linked orange in other mammals. We
show that Sex-linked orange is caused by a 5-kb deletion that leads to ectopic
and melanocyte-specific expression of the Rho GTPase Activating Protein 36
(Arhgap36) gene. Single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) studies from fetal cat
skin reveal that red/yellow hair color is caused by reduced expression of
melanogenic genes that are normally activated by the melanocortin 1 receptor
(Mc1r)-cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)-protein kinase A (PKA) pathway, but
Mc1r and its ability to stimulate cAMP accumulation is intact. Instead, we show
that expression of Arhgap36 in melanocytes leads to reduced levels of the PKA
catalytic subunit (PKAC); thus, Sex-linked orange is genetically and
biochemically downstream of Mc1r. Our findings resolve a longstanding
comparative genetic puzzle, provide in vivo evidence for the ability of Arhgap36
to inhibit PKA, and reveal a molecular explanation for a charismatic color
pattern with a rich genetic history.

Introduction

Patchwork coloration in calico and tortoiseshell cats has played a central role in the conceptualization and exemplification of X-chromosome-inactivation1. In eutherian females, random inactivation of an X chromosome in embryogenesis is stably and clonally maintained2, producing a mosaic in which patches of cells express genes from only one of the two X chromosomes. Calico and tortoiseshell female cats are heterozygous for the Sex-linked orange (O) mutation—orange patches represent clones in which the ancestral (o, nonorange) allele has been inactivated, while brown or black patches represent clones in which the derived (O, orange) allele has been inactivated (Figure 1A). These developmental genetic events contributed to Mary Lyon’s original theory for X-inactivation in 19611 but the underlying molecular cause has remained obscure.

The pigmentary phenotype of Sex-linked orange resembles that caused by loss-of-function for Melanocortin 1 receptor (Mc1r)3, an autosomal gene that encodes a seven transmembrane receptor expressed in melanocytes4,5 whose natural ligand is an antagonist, Agouti signaling protein (Asip)6,7. Mc1r is a Gs-coupled receptor with constitutive activity; thus, when Mc1r is expressed in melanocytes, cyclic Adenosine Monophosphate (cAMP) levels and Protein Kinase A (PKA) activity increase, while cAMP levels and PKA activity are decreased in the presence of Asip8. Sex-linked orange and Mc1r mutant cats9,10 resemble each other due to a high content of red/yellow pheomelanin and a low content of black/brown eumelanin; however Sex-linked orange is X-linked while Mc1r is autosomal. The switch between pheomelanin and eumelanin is regulated by a set of cAMP-responsive melanogenic genes including Dopachrome tautomerase (Dct), Tyrosinase related protein 1 (Tyrp1), and Premelanosome protein (Pmel)11.

Asip and Mc1r are evolutionarily conserved pigment type-switching components with highly restricted functions and, consequently, are common targets for mutations that cause red/yellow color8,12. In contrast, sex-linked yellow or orange coat color is only observed in two species, the domestic cat13,14 and the Syrian hamster15. Surprisingly, pedigree-based mapping studies identify distinct genetic intervals in each species, indicating different genes are involved16,17. The absence of similar sex-linked traits in other species raises intriguing questions about genetic mechanism and the underlying biology.

Sex-linked orange was one of several Mendelian coat color variants segregating in a multigenerational, domestic cat pedigree established at Nestle-Purina18. Schmidt-Küntzel and her colleagues mapped the locus to a 9.7 Mb genetic interval on the X chromosome17. In what follows we use a combination of genetic, transcriptomic, histologic, and functional assays to characterize the genetic basis and biological mechanism for orange coat color in the domestic cat. Our work confirms the essential role of PKA in pigment type-switching, demonstrates the role of a recently characterized PKA inhibitor protein, and provides a likely explanation for the absence of calico dogs, mice, and other mammals.

In scRNA-seq libraries prepared separately from Arhgap36del/Y and Arhgap36+/Y littermates, UMAP projections of co-clustered cells reveal significant overlap between fibroblast and keratinocyte populations, whereas melanoblast populations display distinct topologies, likely reflecting substantial differences in gene expression between Arhgap36del/Y and Arhgap36+/Y melanoblasts (Figure 2D). As described below, hundreds of genes are differentially expressed between Arhgap36del/Y and Arhgap36+/Y melanoblast populations, including Arhgap36 itself (expressed at high levels in Arhgap36del/Y cells, middle panel, Figure 2D) and targets of Mc1r signaling such as Dct (lower panel, Figure 2D). Notably, Arhgap36 expression was detectable only in melanoblasts and only from the Arhgap36del/Y sample.

A total of 457 genes show differential expression between Arhgap36del/Y and Arhgap36+/Y melanoblasts (adjusted P-value < 0.05, Wilcoxon rank sum test, and > 2-fold expression difference, Figures 2D, S3A), nearly an order of magnitude more than comparisons of equivalently sized keratinocyte populations. To refine and validate the set of differentially expressed genes between Arhgap36del and Arhgap36+ cells, we examined a melanoblast-enriched scRNA-seq library from an Arhgap36+/del female, in which X chromosome inactivation causes clonally inherited mosaicism in an otherwise isogenic background. Clustering revealed two large (and two smaller) melanoblast populations defined by 473 differentially expressed genes (adjusted P-value < 0.05, Wilcoxon rank sum test, and > 2-fold expression change, Figures 2D, S3B). Because Arhgap36 is expressed predominantly in one of the two populations (P-value =1.93×10−142, Figure 2D), we infer that the clustering is driven by whether the Arhgap36+ or Arhgap36del bearing X chromosome was inactivated. Considering the intersection between the two experiments—comparison of male littermates, and comparison of melanoblast populations from a mosaic female—283 genes are differentially expressed, with 166 and 117 genes showing higher and lower expression, respectively, in Arhgap36del melanoblasts (Figure S3C, Table S3).

Two distinct biologic signatures are apparent from our scRNA-seq results. First, Arghap36del melanoblasts exhibit a significantly reduced expression of two pigment cell-specific genes, Tyrp1, and Dct, required for eumelanin (but not pheomelanin) synthesis (Figure S4A). These observations, together with the reduced eumelanogenic gene expression observed in postnatal, hair follicle melanocytes (Fig. 2), provide a straightforward explanation for the similar red/yellow coat color phenotypes of orange cats and those of loss-of-function Mc1r mutants in cats9,10 and other mammals12. Second, Arhgap36del melanoblasts exhibit a significant elevation of Mc1r expression, a significant reduction in expression of the Protein kinase inhibitor A beta gene (Pkib), and altered expression of eight genes involved in regulating intracellular cAMP levels (Figure S4B, Tables S3 and S4). Decreased expression of eumelanogenic genes that are normally activated by cAMP together with altered expression of genes that regulate PKAC activity suggests that the fundamental defect in Arhgapdel cells affects cAMP-PKA signal transduction.

In the littermate and the calico datasets described above (Figure 2D), Arhgap36 expression was both melanoblast- and Arhgap36del-specific. We also generated a scRNA-seq library from whole skin of an Arhgap36del/Y embryo at mid-gestation. We identified multiple different cell types besides melanoblasts including myoblasts, vascular and lymphatic epithelium, neural crest cells, dendritic cells, and dermal fibroblasts, but melanoblasts were the only cell type in which Arhgap36 expression was detected (Figure S5). These results together with the absence of any transcript counts from Arhgap36+ melanoblasts shows that ectopic expression of Arhgap36 in pigment cells (but not other, closely related neural crest-derived cell types) is a molecular signature for the Sex-linked orange mutation.

The genetics of pigment type-switching has provided fundamental insight into many different cell types, tissues, and disease states, largely because key components of the melanocortin system—inverse agonists, accessory proteins, and Gs-coupled receptors that act to increase intracellular cAMP—are conserved in both structure and function across mammalian evolution8,33. The history of Sex-linked orange has piqued the curiosity of scientists as well as cat lovers. In early population genetic surveys of pan-global feral cat populations, orange, calico, and/or tortoiseshell cats were found to be present across multiple continents and, by contrast to several other coat color variants, exhibited little clinal variation in allele frequency34–36. Historical evidence for dispersal of Sex-linked Orange includes depictions of calico cats in Renaissance art, such as Alesandro Vitali’s “Annunciation”, painted in 1604 (https://gndm.it/), and in a twelfth century Chinese painting by Li Tang (https://theme.npm.edu.tw/opendata). Taken together with the absence of sex-linked orange coat color in modern wildcats and our observation that all orange cats in our dataset share a common haplotype, these observations point to a single origin more than 900 years ago, prior to breed development but after the beginning of cat domestication (about nine thousand years ago37,38).

Our focus on the Arhgap36 5 kb deletion as the cause of Sex-linked Orange stems from both genetic and genomic logic—recombination mapping that delineates a 1.28 Mb interval and association studies of all variants in the region, respectively. Similar association data was reported recently by Toh et al.39. Although a 1.28 Mb haplotype is unexpectedly large for an allele that is hundreds of years old and distributed across multiple continents and breeds, the haplotype lies within a 7.9 Mb interval (chrX:104,234,252–112,110,384)) that represents one of three recombination deserts on the cat X chromosome40.

Arhgap36 is not normally expressed by pigment cells, but melanoblasts that carry the 5 kb deletion on the active X chromosome express high levels of Arhgap36. The deletion includes evolutionarily conserved regulatory elements shown to bind several transcriptional activators, including JUNB/Fos/Mef2/NPAS4 in developing cortical neurons, and Tbx2, Gata2/3, and EP300 in neuroblastoma cells lines22. However, our gene expression results from a broad series of non-pigmentary cells and tissues suggest these regulatory elements are not required for normal Arhgap36 expression. It is possible that regulatory elements within the deletion can function as repressors of Arhgap36 expression specifically in melanocytes, but a more likely explanation is that the deletion generates ectopic enhancer function by shuffling binding motifs in a genomic interval primed for enhancer activity. If so, ectopic expression of Arhgap36 in orange melanocytes would depend on precise juxtaposition of nearby regulatory motifs. Irrespective of the specific molecular mechanism, ectopic but melanocyte specific expression in Sex-linked orange is an unusual type of mutation that is unlikely to recur, which helps to explain the absence of homologous alleles in dogs, laboratory mice, and humans.

Arhgap36 was named for its sequence similarity to a family of rho GTPase activating proteins but is missing a critical arginine finger motif41 and lacks detectable GAP activity42. Instead, Arhgap36 was identified in a genome-wide screen as an activator of the hedgehog signaling pathway21, a role that was later shown to be mediated by inhibition of PKA signaling, through direct interaction with and subsequent lysosomal degradation of PKAC25. Arhgap36 knockout mice are viable and fertile but exhibit abnormalities in spinal cord patterning that is thought to involve disruption of hedgehog signaling26.

Our functional and expression studies point to a fundamental defect in Arhgapdel pigment cells that affects hundreds of genes downstream of cAMP and PKA signaling (Figures S3, S4). As predicted by the coat color phenotype, key melanogenic genes required to produce eumelanin instead of pheomelanin are reduced (Dct, Pmel, Tyrp1). In addition, many genes involved in cAMP metabolism exhibit altered expression: a PKA inhibitor (Pkib) and 5 phosphodiesterase genes (Pde3a, Pde3b, Pde4b, Pde4d, and Pde10a) are decreased, while 2 phosphodiesterase genes (Pde1c, Pde11a), Mc1r, and a guanylate cyclase gene (Gucy1a2) are increased. Pkib and Pde4 proteins are well-established targets for transcriptional feedback inhibition of cAMP and PKA43; our results suggest that Pde3 proteins and Mc1r may behave in the same way. Guanylate cyclase activity can cross-activate PKA43; thus, increased Gucy1a2 expression is also likely explained by a cAMP-PKA feedback inhibition circuit. A rationale for increased expression of Pde1c and Pde11a is less obvious; however, our observation that MNT1 cells transfected with Arhgap36 exhibit a potentiated cAMP response to MC1R activation points to a net effect in which cAMP levels are increased, but ineffective in the face of PKAC degradation.

In humans, increased somatic expression of ARHGAP36 has been implicated in medulloblastoma, neuroblastoma, and several endocrine cancers21,25,44,45. There is one reported case of a germline ARHGAP36 gain-of-function mutation in which a chromosomal rearrangement leads to enhancer hijacking and widespread expression of ARHGAP3646. The affected individual died at 8 years of age due to severe heterotopic ossification that was thought to be caused by ARHGAP36-induced activation of bone formation in joints and muscle. In orange cats, however, the only documented phenotype besides their coat color is lentigo simplex47, a benign hypermelanosis and hyperplasia of epidermal melanocytes evident in glabrous regions, especially the lips, nose, eyelids, and gingiva. The lentigines resemble those typical of Carney Complex syndrome in humans, which is caused by dominant inactivating mutations in genes encoding PKAR subunits48–50.

Random X-inactivation in female embryos heterozygous for the Arhgap36 deletion underlies both the tortoiseshell and calico color patterns (Figure 1A), in which patches of red/yellow or black/brown hair represent clonal expansion of a developing melanocyte in which either the normal or deletion-bearing X chromosome has been inactivated. The difference between tortoiseshell and calico cats is the presence of an additional white spotting mutation in calico that negatively affects the ability of developing melanocytes to survive as they migrate away from the neural crest, allowing melanocyte clones that do survive to expand in a larger body region. Indeed, that characteristic—larger patch size of a mosaic coat color mutation in the presence of white spotting—is a developmental genetic signature of a melanocyte-autonomous process51. This line of reasoning is consistent with our observation that Arhgap36 ectopic expression occurs only in pigment cells. It also helps to explain why Arhgap36del is epistatic to cat mutations in Asip17 and Mc1r9, which are responsible for black and amber coat color, respectively, since disruption of PKA signaling by Arhgap36 in orange cats is biochemically downstream of Asip and Mc1r.

Color patches in tortoiseshell or calico cats have distinct developmental, molecular, and phylogenetic characteristics from tabby markings, which refer to color patterns consisting of dark stripes, spots, and swirls in the domestic cat. The distribution of tortoiseshell color patches is stochastic and involves only pigment cells or their precursors. By contrast, spacing between tabby markings is regular and periodic, due to a molecular pre-pattern established in embryonic mesenchymal and epithelial cells18 that is later implemented by paracrine release of Endothelin 3 (Edn3) from dermal papilla cells38. Tortoiseshell/calico patterns are found only in domestic cats while tabby markings arise from an evolutionarily conserved process11,52 and are a defining aspect of morphological diversity in felid species.

The interaction of tabby and tortoiseshell/calico patterns53 exemplifies the distinct developmental origin of each pattern type since tabby markings manifest differently in orange and nonorange backgrounds. In a non-orange cat (or a non-orange patch in a tortoiseshell or calico cat), tabby markings are dark and eumelanic, but in an orange cat (or an orange patch in a tortoiseshell or calico cat), tabby markings are a dark orange. This indicates that Edn3 signaling, through its Gq-coupled receptor and Protein Kinase C (PKC)54–57, increases melanogenic enzyme activity independently of PKA58, whereas its effect on pigment-type switching is PKA dependent59. In an orange cat, the presence of tabby markings despite the absence of pigment-type switching implies that increased melanogenesis suffices to implement tabby patterns when PKA signaling is impaired (Figure S7).

Molecular characterization of variant alleles facilitates broader surveys of both modern and ancient cats, with the potential to resolve incongruities and extend observations of earlier population genetic studies. In this respect, Sex-linked orange has distinct advantages over other mutations. X chromosome haplotypes can be confidently determined from short read sequencing in males. In addition, low recombination rates near Sex-linked orange17,40, which complicated genetic mapping, are a potential advantage for population genetic and ancient DNA studies. The genomic interval harboring Sex-linked orange should approximate non-recombining parts of the genome, such as the Y chromosome and the mitochondrial genome, in which mutations occurring in the absence of meiotic recombination are widely used to trace ancestry and migration patterns60,61.

Forward genetic studies of coat color in the laboratory mouse have significantly contributed to a detailed molecular understanding of pigment cell biology, and more generally, the pathways underlying cell migration, stem cell biology, organelle biogenesis/trafficking, and cell signaling.62 Similar forward genetic approaches in non-traditional genetic models, enabled by expanding genomic capacity, extend the opportunity for biologic insight.

Dorsal skin was excised with micro scissors and treated overnight in Dispase (Stem Cell Technologies). Epidermal sheets, comprised of keratinocytes and migrating melanoblasts, were isolated by gentle scraping with a No.15-blade scalpel and dissociated with trypsin. Coverslips were transferred to an imaging chamber with Ringer’s solution. Sequential fluorescence images were acquired with MetaMorph software on an inverted microscope every 10 s using CFP and FRET filter cubes: λex = 430 nm and CFP and YFP emissions were detected simultaneously using 470±20 nm and 530±25 nm band-pass filters.
