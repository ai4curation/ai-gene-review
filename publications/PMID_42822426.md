### Summary

Mitochondria arose from the endosymbiosis of a bacterium with an archaea-related host cell about 2 billion
years ago. To understand their origins and evolution, we compared experimentally defined mitoproteomes
from the MitoCarta Tree of Life project. Across eight organisms, we identified 8,619 distinct mitochondrial
proteins within 3,199 families, of which 43% lack Pfam domains. We report 33 protein families conserved
in eukaryotic pathogens yet absent in humans, representing promising candidate targets for protozoan infectious diseases. Leveraging our experimentally defined mitoproteomes, we retrained a classifier based on a
protein language model to predict mitoproteomes of ∼200 eukaryotes. From this expanded set, we detail
the evolutionary trajectories of mitochondria, ranging from clade-specific gene family expansions to extreme
mitoproteome reductions seemingly en route to complete organelle loss. Finally, we reconstruct the last eukaryotic common ancestor (LECA) mitoproteome, revealing that LECA possessed a complex mitochondrion
capable of both aerobic and anaerobic metabolism.

INTRODUCTION
Mitochondria evolved from the endosymbiosis of a bacterium
with an archaea-related host cell about 2 billion years ago.1–7
The mitochondrial endosymbiosis was a singular event in the
process of eukaryogenesis that gave rise to the complex eukaryotic cell and culminated in the last eukaryotic common ancestor
(LECA), a species that lived about 1.5–2.0 billion years ago.5–7
The LECA possessed mitochondria as well as other signature
features found in present-day eukaryotic cells,8 including a nucleus, a complex cytoskeleton, and an endomembrane system.
Most extant mitochondria retain a genome (mtDNA) that is a
vestige of their origin from the bacterial endosymbiont.9,10
Over the course of eukaryotic evolution, mtDNA has undergone
massive reduction as genes were relocated to the nuclear
genome or lost altogether. The most gene-rich mtDNA genomes
discovered to date contain up to 66 protein-coding genes,11–13 a
relic compared with extant bacterial genomes that typically
encode thousands of proteins. Reduction of organelle genomes
is observed in other endosymbiont-derived organelles, such as
the chloroplast in plants and the chromatophore in Paulinella,

which were acquired about 1.0–1.5 billion years ago14 and 100
million years ago,15 respectively.
As the mtDNA was reduced, the nuclear genome’s contribution to the mitochondrial proteome expanded independently in
different lineages. Nuclear genes encoding mitochondria-localized proteins are transcribed in the nucleus and translated in
the cytosol, and then their protein products are imported into
the organelle. About 99% of the mitoproteome is nuclear-encoded in the thoroughly characterized mitoproteomes of human
(MitoCarta16) and Saccharomyces yeast.17 While all eight
mtDNA-encoded proteins in yeast are conserved in humans,
only about two-thirds of the mitoproteome is conserved between
humans and yeast,16,17 indicating that the mitoproteome—especially the nuclear-encoded component—has been remodeled
independently in the animal and fungal lineages since divergence from their common ancestor. A complete understanding
of the diversification of mitoproteomes across the tree of life requires detailed characterization of the major fraction of mitochondrial proteins that are nuclear-encoded, which have historically been difficult to identify using computational predictions
alone.16,18,19

Beyond the well-studied mitochondria of humans and yeast,
mitochondria in other eukaryotes have an even broader range
of morphology and function.4 Some mitochondria have lost components of the electron transport chain, and others possess the
capability to perform anaerobic metabolism. Some single-celled
eukaryotes have completely lost the electron transport chain and
mtDNA. One free-living species, Monocercomonoides exilis, appears to have lost its mitochondrial organelle entirely.20 The diversity of mitochondria across species suggests a large variation
in the size and composition of their mitoproteomes.
Some of the most divergent mitochondria are seen in pathogenic protozoan eukaryotes, which cause major human diseases, including malaria and giardiasis. Mitochondria are an established target for antiparasitic agents, such as atovaquone
(a drug for Plasmodium21 and Babesia22 that targets mtDNA-encoded cytochrome b23). As resistance emerges to these established therapies targeting mtDNA and its products, there is a
pressing need for the identification of other mitochondrial targets
that are essential and specific for pathogens.
To characterize the variation in mitoproteomes across eukaryotes and discover new therapeutic targets, we selected a set of
six eukaryotic organisms for mitochondrial proteomics as part of
the MitoCarta Tree of Life (MitoTOL) project. Together with the
pre-existing high-quality human24 and yeast17 mitochondrial inventories, these organisms span major clades across the eukaryotic tree of life and include five pathogenic protozoa that
are collectively responsible for an estimated 1 billion infections
per year. Here, we report a comparative analysis and evolutionary reconstruction of this expanded repertoire of high-quality
mitoproteomes in the context of the broader tree of life.
RESULTS
High-quality experimentally defined mitochondrial
proteomes across diverse eukaryotes
We experimentally characterized the mitochondrial proteomes
of six eukaryotes (five protozoan pathogens and one plant) as
part of the MitoTOL project.25–29 Two other species (humans
and Saccharomyces yeast) had high-quality mitochondrial protein inventories already available17,24 and were included in
downstream analyses. The eight core species span major clades
across the eukaryotic tree of life (Figure 1A).
Collectively, the eight inventories contain 8,619 mitochondrial
proteins (Figure 1B). The size of the mitoproteome varied over
28-fold from 59 proteins in Giardia to 1,666 proteins in Trypanosoma, while the size of the total proteome in each species varied
about 7-fold (Figure 1B). The fraction of mitochondrial proteins
with a Pfam domain was >86% in well-studied model organisms
(humans, yeast, and Arabidopsis), while other organisms had
fewer Pfam annotations suggestive of novel unstudied biology.
To study the mitoproteomes in the broader evolutionary
context of the tree of life,4 we developed a methodology to identify homologs across large evolutionary distances, which is a
longstanding challenge in homology inference.30,31 Our method
builds on the state-of-the-art OrthoFinder32 by incorporating
sequence profile and structural homology to identify homologs
in distantly related species across the tree of life (Figure S1;
see STAR Methods, michaelzhuchen GitHub). The new method

improved performance on the Quest for Orthologs (QfO) benchmarks33,34 (Figure S2), the manually curated Orthobench benchmark30,35 (Figure S3), and five conserved complexes/pathways
for which cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures and/or
prior biochemical studies were available to support orthology assignments (Figure S4). The new method also recovered more
remote homologs than the state-of-the-art deep learning-based
homology methods Foldseek36 and PLMSearch/PLMAlign37
(Figure S4).
We applied the new method to a protein sequence dataset
consisting of the whole proteomes of 673 eukaryotes (8 core
species and 665 other species) and representative sequences
of 34 prokaryotic outgroup clades, encompassing 29 bacterial
kingdoms and 5 archaeal superfamilies (Table S1). The new
method clustered the ∼16.7 million proteins in the dataset into
1.5 million orthogroups (OGs), an operational definition of a protein family comprising the set of proteins that descend from a
single ancestral gene in the last common ancestor of the species
with a homolog (Figure 1C; data available in the Zenodo repository). Of the 1.5 million OGs, there were 447,417 OGs that had a
homolog in at least one of 203 representative eukaryotes,
selected based on genome annotation quality and balanced
taxonomic representation across major eukaryotic clades
(Figure 1C; Table S1). The 8,619 experimentally defined mitochondrial proteins were contained in 3,199 distinct OGs, hereafter referred to as mitochondrial OGs. Of these 3,199 mitochondrial OGs, 68 (2%) contained an mtDNA-encoded protein
(Figure 1C).
Comparative analysis of experimentally defined
mitoproteomes
Using protein OGs to capture homologous relationships, we
identified a spectrum of conservation across mitochondrial
OGs that ranged from universally conserved to clade-specific
(Figure 2A). The 13 universally conserved protein orthogroups
contained the human members of the iron-sulfur cluster biogenesis (ISC) pathway and protein import machinery (Figure 2A).
Excluding the highly reduced Giardia, there were 132 OGs
shared across all mitochondria, which contained human members involved in solute transport, translation (e.g., the mitochondrial ribosome), and core metabolic pathways, such as the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and respiratory complexes II, III, IV, and
V (Figure 2A). Each mitoproteome shared about 25%–75% of its
proteins with other mitoproteomes (Figure 2B). Most mitochondrial OGs (71%, 2,278) were only found in single clades; however, a majority of these (56%, 1,265) were not powered to detect
homologs in outgroup clades based on abSENSE-HMM analysis
(Figures 2C and S5), suggesting that these may not be truly
clade-specific but rather highly diverged homologs in the twilight
or midnight zones of sequence similarity.38,39
Across all 3,199 mitochondrial OGs, 1,381 (43%) had no
known Pfam domain in any of their protein members
(Figure 2D). These OGs with no Pfam domains may reflect novel
uncharacterized mitochondrial protein families, or they may be
highly divergent homologs of known proteins that elude detection. Using abSENSE-HMM, we estimate that 384 (28%) of these
Pfam-lacking mitochondrial OGs are sufficiently powered to
detect homologs in outgroups, suggesting these unknowns are

To identify candidate targets conserved in pathogens but absent in humans while maximizing the specificity of the identified
homologs, we split each orthogroup into one or more phylogenetically resolved orthogroups at the Eukaryota domain level
(Eukaryota PhROGs) by the independent acquisitions by eukaryotes on reconciled protein phylogenies (Figure S1B). This procedure split the 3,199 mitochondrial OGs into 3,369 mitochondrial Eukaryota PhROGs (Figure 3A) and separated proteins
with ancient homology that are members of distinct complexes,
such as the mitochondrial and cytosolic ribosomes (to which
specific inhibitors have been developed). Filtering the mitochondrial PhROGs by the presence of any pathogen mitochondrial
protein resulted in 2,068 pathogen mitochondrial PhROGs, of
which 335 lacked a human ortholog and were powered to detect
a human ortholog (if one existed) by abSENSE-HMM (Figure 3B).
Further filtering by shared membership in at least two pathogen
clades resulted in a set of 33 candidate mitochondrial targets
(Figure 3B; Table S3). Of this set, 17 candidate targets had a homolog in Plasmodium knowlesi; these P. knowlesi proteins
scored as more essential than other proteins in a recent
genome-wide transposon knockout screen41 (Figure 3C).
Among the 17 candidate targets with a homolog in P. knowlesi,
13 were essential and 4 were non-essential.
The 33 candidate targets were manually partitioned into five
classes: metabolic enzymes, caseinolytic protease (Clp)
(comprised of the ClpY chaperone and the ClpQ proteolytic
core), mitochondrial ribosome, DNA/RNA-related, and other
(Figure 3D). Reassuringly, we recovered the type-II NADH dehydrogenase (NDH2) and class I fumarate hydratase (class I FH) as
candidates, which have been investigated as drug targets.42–48
We identified three pathogen targets (ClpQ, class I FH, and
phospholipase D [PLD]-type cardiolipin synthase [CLS]) that
perform functions similar to three human mitochondrial proteins
(ClpP, class II FH, and cytidine-diphosphate [CDP]-type CLS),
despite having no sequence or structural similarity. Notably,
the pathogen and human versions are found in largely distinct organisms (Figure 3E), reinforcing that humans lack the pathogen
versions. In principle, this separation supports the specificity of
targeting these pathogen proteins without disrupting their functional counterparts in humans.
Computational prediction of mitoproteomes across
hundreds of sequenced eukaryotes
To characterize mitoproteomes of diverse eukaryotes beyond
humans, yeast, and the six MitoTOL species, we adapted

and retrained the DeepLoc2.0 model49 using our experimentally
defined mitochondrial protein inventories to improve mitochondrial localization prediction from protein sequence (Figure 4A).
The new model (DeepLoc2.0-mito) was trained on a dataset
that integrated the original Swiss-Prot training dataset and
localization data from the MitoTOL project that was based
solely on experimental proteomics (to avoid circularity), which
increased the taxonomic diversity (Figure S6A) and the number
of mitochondrial labels (Figure S6B) in the training dataset
(Table S4). DeepLoc2.0-mito performed better than the original
model on a leave-one-species-out cross-validation task, especially for proteins lacking a canonical mitochondrial targeting
sequence (MTS) as predicted by TargetP2.0 (Figure S6C).
When predicting mitochondrial proteins from the experimental
inventories, DeepLoc2.0-mito attended to canonical N-terminal
MTSs as well as other non-canonical sorting signals, particularly for proteins in the intermembrane space and mitochondrial
outer membrane, which often lack an MTS (Figure S7).
Together, these results indicate that mitochondrial proteins
contain non-MTS targeting information at the primary sequence
level that is recovered by protein language-based models such
as DeepLoc2.0-mito.
Using DeepLoc2.0-mito, we predicted the mitoproteomes for
203 fully sequenced representative eukaryotes (Figure 4A;
Table S5). Using experimentally defined localizations across
the eight core species, we observed an enrichment of mitochondrial predictions that were likely false positives (having experimentally defined homologs that were all non-mitochondrial) in
OGs that had a mitochondrial prediction in fewer than 5 out of
203 species (Figure S6D). These results motivated the use of
an OG-based minimum species threshold to reduce the false
positive rate when running large-scale predictions on millions
of proteins from the whole proteomes of hundreds of species.
Predicted mitochondrial proteins in OGs that had a mitochondrial prediction in at least 5 species or an experimentally defined
mitochondrial homolog were marked as high-confidence predictions and were used for downstream analyses. Using this
threshold, the predicted high-confidence mitoproteomes exhibited substantial variation in the number of mitochondrial proteins and the mitochondrial fraction of the whole proteome
across eukaryotic clades (Figure 4B). In species that retain
mtDNA, the size of the mitoproteome was not tightly correlated
with the number of mtDNA-encoded proteins (Figure S8), exemplified by the relatively small predicted mitoproteome of Mantamonas sphyraenae (353 proteins) despite having one of the most
gene-rich mtDNAs (encoding 62 proteins12). Across 203 eukaryotes, DeepLoc2.0-mito predicted 1,420 additional high-confidence mitochondrial OGs that were not found in any of the
experimentally defined mitochondrial inventories, of which 943
(65%) were powered to detect homologs in outgroups by
abSENSE-HMM (Figure 4C). These additional predicted

Predicted mitoproteomes in species lacking mtDNA
reveal trajectories of organelle reduction or loss
Mitochondrial organelles have independently lost their mtDNA in
certain lineages with anaerobic or parasitic lifestyles, giving rise
to reduced organelles such as hydrogenosomes and mitochondrion-related organelles (MROs).4,50 In line with this idea, we
observed depletions in the predicted mitoproteomes of the five
clades that independently lost mtDNA (Figure 4B). Across these
five clades, we found varying degrees of conservation of mitochondrial OGs (Figure 4D), implying distinct trajectories of reductive organelle evolution. Despite these differences between
clades, there was a highly conserved subset of mitochondrial
OGs related to protein import, biogenesis of cofactors (including
iron-sulfur clusters), and solute transport (Figure 4E), which may
represent the most basal functions of the mitochondrial
organelle.
Across the 203 eukaryotes, the species with the fewest highconfidence mitochondrial predictions were in Metamonada, a
clade whose known species have lost mtDNA (including Giardia).
Reassuringly, the fewest number of predicted mitochondrial proteins was in the metamonad Monocercomonoides exilis, which is
reported to have lost its mitochondrial organelle entirely.20 The
23 predicted mitochondrial proteins in M. exilis comprised just
∼0.1% of its proteome, and none of the predicted mitochondrial
proteins had an MTS predicted by TargetP2.0. Another metamonad, Skoliomonas litria, is also proposed to have entirely lost its
mitochondrial organelle51 and had 44 predicted mitochondrial
proteins (∼0.4% of its proteome), two of which had an MTS predicted by TargetP2.0. To investigate these claims, we searched
for homologs of the predicted mitochondrial proteins in M. exilis
and S. litria across the entire collection of experimentally defined
and computationally predicted mitoproteomes. Unlike M. exilis
(Figure S9A), S. litria had homologs to classic mitochondrial
membrane protein families conserved across multiple clades
that lost mtDNA (Figure 4E), including the SLC25A mitochondrial
solute carrier family, TOM40, and SAM50 (Figure S9B). The presence and predicted mitochondrial localization of these signature
proteins raises the hypothesis that S. litria may still possess a
highly reduced MRO.
Identification of conserved mitochondrial targets for
plant pathogens
We used the computationally predicted high-confidence mitoproteomes across eukaryotes to identify candidate mitochondrial targets for phytopathogens that infect land plants, which
are of global importance for ecosystems and food security. Analogous to the identification of human pathogen targets, we
sought to identify a set of OGs that were conserved across at
least two of the predicted mitoproteomes of the diverse phytopathogens Phytophthora infestans (a stramenopile), Plasmodio
phora brassicae (a rhizarian), and the union of four phytopathogenic fungi (including Ustilago maydis and three additionally
predicted mitoproteomes for Puccinia graminis, Fusarium oxysporum, and Colletotrichum orchidophilum) but were confidently
absent from the proteome of Arabidopsis (as a representative of
land plants). We report 31 candidate protein targets fulfilling
these criteria (Figure S10; Table S6), including the sideroflexins,
COQ7, and cytochrome c biogenesis system-III (HCCS), which
are all known to be absent in land plants.52–54 To avoid non-specific toxicities toward humans and other animals, future followup studies may prioritize the subset of targets that are also absent in humans.
Reconstruction of ancestral mitochondria and the gains
and losses on the path to extant organelles
Leveraging the full collection of experimentally defined and
computationally predicted mitoproteomes, we reconstructed
the mitoproteomes of eukaryotic ancestors and the gains and
losses that occurred along the path from these ancestors to
extant mitochondria. Deep-time ancestral reconstructions are
challenging due to various factors, including duplication and horizontal gene transfer (HGT), which bias estimates of ancestral
gene content if not explicitly accounted for.8,55,56 We used a
hybrid approach (see STAR Methods) combining deterministic
and probabilistic species-tree-aware reconciliation to explicitly
account for duplication and HGT (Figure S1B). We inferred
ancestral localizations by propagating extant protein localizations to ancestral nodes of the reconciled protein phylogenies
(Figure S1C). For OGs that were of particular interest for ancestral reconstruction (anaerobic enzymes in the next section), we
manually reviewed the phylogenies to screen for artifacts and resampled two OGs (pyruvate:ferredoxin oxidoreductase [PFOR]
and hydrogenase maturation factors HydE/F) that had complex
multidomain rearrangements or fusions (see STAR Methods).

Pathways and functions of LECA mitochondria
From a human-centric perspective, 790 of 1,135 (∼70%) human
mitochondrial proteins were derived from ancestral proteins in
the LECA mitochondria. The human MitoPathways24 with the
highest fraction of proteins derived from LECA mitochondria
included translation factors, the ISC pathway, the TCA cycle, solute transport, protein import and sorting, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), and the mitochondrial ribosome (Figure 5E). Human MitoPathways with a small fraction of proteins derived from
LECA mitochondria included apoptosis, fission, the immune
response, and organelle contact sites, which is consistent with
the later expansion of these pathways in animal mitochondria
(Figure 5E). Notably, 240 (∼90%) of the 266 protein-coding
genes linked to human mitochondrial diseases57 were derived
from LECA, and 205 (∼77%) were specifically derived from
LECA mitochondria, implying an association between disease/

functional importance and evolutionary ancestry. For instance,
one of these human disease genes, ATAD3A, is recovered in
LECA mitochondria and is involved in mitochondrial protein quality control as well as intramitochondrial and mitochondrial-ER
membrane contacts.64 Disruptions in the ATAD3 family have
been linked to multiple human mitochondrial diseases64–67 as
well as impaired growth and altered mitochondrial morphology
in plants,68,69 implying a conserved function in mitochondria
potentially dating back to LECA.
The MCU and calcium-binding EF-hand proteins were recovered in the reconstructed LECA mitoproteome. Proteins with calcium-binding EF-hand domains are conserved in the mitoproteomes of diverse extant eukaryotic lineages (Figure S11). In
species that independently lost the calcium uniporter (Saccharomyces yeast, Babesia, and the metamonad Anaeramoeba),
these homologs are sometimes present in mitochondria but
usually lack the mitochondrial EF-hand domain, reflecting
coevolution of calcium signaling at the protein domain level
(Figure S11). The mitochondrial Rho GTPase (MIRO) family of
cytosol-facing mitochondrial outer membrane proteins
(MIRO1/2 in humans) is highly conserved and has EF-hand domains even in organisms lacking the calcium uniporter
(Figure S11). The widespread presence of the calcium uniporter
and matrix or IMS-localized EF-hand proteins across eukaryotes
suggests that calcium handling and signaling are ancestral mitochondrial functions. Moreover, the high conservation of EFhand-containing MIRO homologs across eukaryotes suggests
that an ancestral MIRO protein may have facilitated calciumdependent interactions between mitochondria and other cellular
components in early eukaryotic cells.
LECA mitochondria also contained multiple pairs of non-homologous enzymes with similar activities, such as class I and
class II FHs, CDP-type and PLD-type CLSs, and the ClpXP
and ClpYQ proteases, some of which are candidate pathogen
targets. The presence of these pairs of alternative systems in
LECA is supported by a broad distribution across eukaryotic
clades and the presence of both pairs in certain species
(Figure 3E). Taken together, these observations suggest that
both pairs of systems were likely present in LECA and then differentially lost in a complementary fashion.

aerobic and anaerobic components (Figure 6C). While the presence of both O2-dependent and O2-labile systems in the same
organelle may seem incompatible, some extant organisms
have mitochondria with both capacities, each of which can be
induced in the appropriate environment. For example, the Acanthamoeba mitoproteome contains a highly branched aerobic
electron transport chain as well as many of the anaerobic enzymes present in LECA, including the HydA that was previously
described,80,86 whose complete maturation machinery and
enzymatic activity have now been definitively localized to mitochondria.26 In normoxic conditions, the organism’s aerobic enzymes are active while the anaerobic enzymes are degraded or
lowly expressed. In anoxic conditions, purified mitochondria
from the organism produce H2(g) that is characteristic of hydrogenase activity in the reductive direction 2H+ + 2e– ➔ H2(g). The
phylogeny of the HydA orthogroup indicates an ancient acquisition of a prokaryotic HydA gene by a stem eukaryote; this gene
then duplicated to give rise to multiple paralogs dating back to
LECA: a mitochondrial HydA, a cytosolic HydA, and a cytosolic
Nar1/Narf/Ciao3 protein that may have functioned in the cytosolic iron-sulfur cluster assembly (CIA) pathway found in most
eukaryotes (Figure S18). Taken together, these results suggest
that LECA used H+ as an electron acceptor in anaerobic conditions to maintain redox balance, lending support to the idea
that the early eukaryote was a facultative anaerobe that could
tolerate a range of environmental O2 tensions.
Late acquisition of mitochondria
The emergence of the first eukaryotic cells from a prokaryotic
world is one of the greatest mysteries in evolutionary biology.
The relative order in which core eukaryotic cellular features—
including the nucleus, microtubule-based cytoskeleton, and
other organelles—emerged has been widely debated.58,61,87,88
It remains unclear whether the mitochondrial endosymbiosis
occurred relatively early (‘‘mito-early’’) or late (‘‘mito-late’’)
compared with the emergence of other eukaryotic cellular components (Figure 7A). Prior studies have used normalized phylogenetic branch lengths to estimate the origination time of
LECA proteins that were acquired from prokaryotic donors.58,87
We extended this approach to our mitoproteome and phylogenomic datasets, estimating the relative acquisition time of all prokaryote-derived LECA proteins under the primary species tree
topology (Figure 7B; Table S9) as well as the five alternative topologies, which yielded consistent results (data available in
Zenodo).

acquisition postdates LECA.14,83
Focusing on eukaryogenesis, we found that LECA proteins of
Asgard archaeal (host) origin were acquired early, while proteins
of alphaproteobacterial (endosymbiont) origin were acquired late
(Figure 7C), implying a late acquisition of mitochondria consistent
with previous findings.58,87 In principle, this observation could be
an artifact of different evolutionary rates in the endosymbiont/
mtDNA genome vs. the host genome. However, regardless of
their origin from the endosymbiont or other sources, mitochondrial proteins consistently have shorter stem lengths and later inferred origins than host or non-mitochondrial LECA proteins
(Figure S21), supporting a late acquisition of mitochondria.
To assess the timing of mitochondrial acquisition in relation to
other core eukaryotic features, we assigned Gene Ontology (GO)
cellular components to LECA proteins based on the GO annotations of their extant descendants. We found that mitochondrial
proteins were on average acquired later than most other cellular
components in LECA (such as the nucleus, proteasome, cytoskeleton, Golgi apparatus, and ER) but on a similar time frame
as peroxisomal proteins (Figure 7D). These findings are consistent with the notion that the mitochondrion was a relatively late
addition to the emerging eukaryotic cell, lending support to
earlier studies58,84,87 with the benefit of improved phylogenomic
reconstructions leveraging mitoproteomes across the tree of life.
To explore the emergence of mitochondrial pathways in LECA,
we assigned pathway annotations to LECA mitochondrial proteins based on the MitoPathway24 memberships of their descendants or based on significant enrichment in anaerobic prokaryotes (Z score > 1, including HydA, HydE, HydF, hybrid cluster
protein [HCP], and class I FH). We found that the inferred acquisition times for core mitochondrial pathways and anaerobe-enriched mitochondrial proteins were similar (Figure 7E). These results support a parsimonious scenario in which these pathways
emerged concurrently with the mitochondrial endosymbiosis,

implying that the nascent organelle was already capable of
both aerobic and anaerobic energy metabolism.
DISCUSSION
Extant mitochondria have been shaped by a mix of reductive and
expansive evolution—whereby the original endosymbiont’s
genome (i.e., mtDNA) has dramatically shrunk or been lost altogether—while in parallel the nuclear genome’s contribution has
grown in different lineages to encode the vast majority of the organelle’s proteome. Here we jointly analyzed eight experimentally defined mitoproteomes in a broader evolutionary context
to characterize mitochondrial pathways and functions, identify
candidate pathogen targets, predict the mitoproteomes of hundreds of fully sequenced eukaryotes, and trace the evolutionary
trajectory of extant mitochondria from LECA. This work, together
with the companion studies from the MitoTOL project, lays a
foundation for future mechanistic investigations of organelle
biology across the tree of life.
Although the conventional, textbook view of mitochondria
tends to be human-centric, our comparative analysis indicates
that in some cases human mitochondria may be the exception.
Across eight experimentally defined mitochondrial proteomes,
we identified dozens of mitochondrial proteins (e.g., NDH2,
AOX, gamma carbonic anhydrase subunit of complex-I, and
CCA-adding enzyme) that are broadly distributed and reconstructed in LECA mitochondria but that are absent in humans.
We leveraged such variation to nominate a set of 33 candidate
targets that tend to be conserved in protist pathogen mitochondria but are completely absent in humans. The set of candidates includes NDH2, which has been studied as a potential
drug target.90–92 Among the candidates are three enzymes
(ClpYQ protease, class I FH, and PLD-type CLS) that are
conserved in pathogen mitochondria, absent in humans, and
essential in Plasmodium knowlesi.41 We similarly identified a
set of mitochondrial proteins common to multiple land plant
pathogens but not found in Arabidopsis. The candidate targets
reported here are ripe opportunities for follow-up investigation
and drug discovery.
An emerging theme from this work is the presence of mechanistically distinct enzymes that perform seemingly redundant
functions within mitochondria. This is exemplified by the class I
and class II FHs, which both catalyze the interconversion of
fumarate and malate. The class II FH (found in humans) is iron-independent, while the class I enzyme (not found in humans) contains an oxygen-sensitive [4Fe–4S] cluster and generally has a
faster catalytic rate.93,94 A small set of extant eukaryotes (spanning the eukaryotic tree) harbor both versions of the enzyme,
while most other eukaryotes have only one version, suggesting
the ancestral presence of both versions followed by differential
losses of one version or the other. Retaining both enzyme systems may have allowed certain ancestral or extant organisms
to balance tradeoffs in biochemical properties, thereby conferring metabolic flexibility and robustness.
Prior attempts to reconstruct ancestral mitochondrial proteomes61–63 relied heavily on conservation in Alphaproteobacteria, which limited the reconstructions to a small fraction
(10%–20%) of proteins with traceable alphaproteobacterial

ancestry.11,61,89 These studies inferred that the ‘‘protomitochondrion’’ had between 396 and 1,026 protein families61–63
and traced only 14%–16% of human mitochondrial proteins to
the ancestral mitochondrion, implying that 84%–86% of human
mitochondrial proteins originated later in eukaryotic evolution.
By contrast, this study benefited from a collection of experimentally defined and computationally predicted mitochondrial
proteomes and a greater availability of whole genome sequences, which supported comprehensive reconstruction of
the LECA mitoproteome. By estimating ancestral localization
based on extant mitoproteomes (rather than alphaproteobacterial ancestry), our reconstruction recovers a greater set of
mitochondrial proteins that are associated with a variety of prokaryotic lineages (in addition to, but not limited to, Alphaproteobacteria) or that are eukaryotic innovations. Our reconstruction
of the LECA mitoproteome contains 1,438 proteins in 1,056 families and traces 70% of human mitochondrial proteins back to the
LECA mitochondrion (in contrast to 14%–16% in the earlier
reconstructions).
The reconstructed LECA mitoproteome contains proteins for
anaerobic metabolism as well as aerobic metabolism, suggesting that LECA may have been a facultative anaerobe that was
well-adapted to fluctuating environmental O2 in the wake of the
Great Oxidation Event.6,70,71 Prior studies have noted that eukaryotic homologs of anaerobic proteins tend to cluster in protein phylogenies,74,75 but it has remained unclear whether these
proteins are ancestral in eukaryotes or are products of rampant
HGT between eukaryotes. Our reconstruction method addresses the difficult task of identifying HGT by screening putative
transfers for large phylogenetic incongruence, low likelihood of
vertical inheritance from a common ancestor, and predicted
HGT by a reconciliation model. Using these heuristics to account
for HGT, we find consistent support for the presence of anaerobic enzymes in LECA across six possible species tree topologies. We also report the presence of [FeFe]-hydrogenase
(HydA) and its three maturases (HydE, HydF, and HydG) in the
mitoproteomes of eight extant eukaryotes across major eukaryotic supergroups, supportive of their LECA mitochondrial
ancestry. Future studies on anaerobic mitochondrial metabolism
could focus on the precise role and activity of anaerobic proteins
found in reconstructed LECA mitochondria, potentially using
extant mitochondria lacking such proteins as model systems.
For example, prior studies functionally reconstituted the MCU
machinery (MCU and EMRE) in Saccharomyces yeast,95 an organism that lost this pathway during evolution.
Our phylogenetic analysis supports a mito-late scenario of eukaryogenesis,58,84,87 invoking a late mitochondrial acquisition
that postdates the emergence of the nucleus, endomembrane
system (including ER and Golgi), and cytoskeleton. We also
observe that peroxisomes were acquired relatively late, on a
timescale comparable to mitochondrial acquisition. This inference is consistent with experimental evidence that in human
cells, peroxisomes form from both ER-derived and mitochondria-derived precursors,96 implying tight evolutionary and functional coupling between these organelles.97–100 Although we
infer that LECA mitochondria had anaerobic capacities
(including [FeFe]-hydrogenase) that on the surface is reminiscent
of the hydrogen hypothesis,71 the hydrogen hypothesis specif

ically postulates a mito-early scenario. Our findings support
the relatively late acquisition of a mitochondrion that was
capable of anaerobic metabolism and sulfur oxidation (sulfide:quinone oxidoreductase [SQOR] and sulfite oxidase [SUOX]),
reminiscent of a revised syntrophy hypothesis.101
Our work provides insights into events following endosymbiosis in the transition to LECA, which was marked by acquisitions
from prokaryotes and eukaryotic innovations. Consistent with
current understanding,10,102 we predict that LECA mitochondria
had an aerobic ETC similar to those seen in extant Alphaproteobacteria and aerobic mitochondria, consisting of C-I (protonpumping type-I), C-II, C-III (bc1-type), cytochrome c, C-IV (aa3-type), and C-V (F-type). This core aerobic metabolism was
probably acquired from the Alphaproteobacteria-related endosymbiont. We find that eukaryotic innovations prior to LECA
expanded the endosymbiont-derived aerobic ETC (e.g., supernumerary subunits and ATP5IF1), elaborated pathways for protein import (e.g., TIM9/10/17/22/23, MIA40, and TOM40), and
created new systems for solute transport (e.g., SLC25A carrier
family, sideroflexins, mitochondrial pyruvate carrier, and calcium
uniporter). The acquisition and expansion of mitochondrial pathways likely supplanted many pre-existing host systems, resulting in their eventual loss (anaerobic pathways such as WoodLjungdahl103,104 and possibly an aerobic ETC105) or repurposing
(A/V-type ATPases for vacuolar acidification106). For the anaerobic proteins reconstructed in LECA mitochondria, we predict
that these proteins were acquired from prokaryotes in a similar
time frame as other mitochondrial pathways, but it remains unclear whether these proteins were derived from a mitochondrial
endosymbiont that was facultatively anaerobic71,107,108 or from
other prokaryotic sources.74,75 Although most extant Alphaproteobacteria are aerobic, we observe a faint presence of anaerobic proteins in extant Alphaproteobacteria, consistent with
prior studies.107,109,110 While on the surface this appears incongruent with an endosymbiotic origin for these anaerobic proteins, the sparse distribution could have arisen by reshuffling of
prokaryotic genomes by HGT, loss, and extinction over ∼2 billion
years, or due to sequencing bias favoring aerobic lineages.
Therefore, we do not exclude the parsimonious scenario of an
endosymbiotic origin for these anaerobic proteins.
The series of events that gave rise to eukaryotic life from prokaryotic precursors remains an enduring mystery despite an
exponential growth in genome data. As global sequencing efforts
continue to increase the availability of genome sequences for new
and understudied lineages, integrative approaches that combine
experimental biology and computational modeling are needed to
translate genomic information into mechanistic insights. It is estimated that only a small fraction (∼0.03%) of the roughly 8.7 million
eukaryotes on planet Earth are suitable for laboratory cell culture.111,112 The approach presented here—leveraging systematic
experimental data from diverse species to improve computational
predictions across the tree of life—holds promise for the study of
diverse eukaryotes that are experimentally intractable. As additional species are sequenced and characterized, we anticipate
that this approach can be further extended to reveal insights
into the many modern and ancient functions of mitochondria,
which may unearth the selective advantage that favored the singular mitochondrial endosymbiosis.

Limitations of the study
Our analysis of mitochondrial proteomes across the tree of life
provides new insights into the evolutionary history, function,
and global health relevance of the organelle, but these findings
should be considered in the context of several limitations. First,
although our dataset spans a diverse array of eukaryotes and
prokaryotes, the incomplete sequencing of extant organisms
and bias toward well-studied clades and model organisms
may conceal the full evolutionary history of mitochondria. Second, we have highlighted mitochondrial proteins common to
pathogens yet absent in their hosts as potential drug targets.
We acknowledge that additional factors, including protein structure and druggability, must be taken into consideration. Third,
detecting remote homologs continues to be challenging. We
have used current state-of-the-art methods that combine protein
sequence, sequence profiles, and structural information to
improve homology detection and adapted an existing method
to estimate detection power for missing homologs. With
continued advances in prediction and alignment of protein structures, we anticipate that some protein families that appear cladespecific may in fact have remote homologs, suggesting shared
function and ancestry. Fourth, phylogenetic inference at deep
timescales is complicated by diminishing phylogenetic signal
and phylogenetic artifacts (such as long branch attraction or
site saturation), which may obscure the true evolutionary relationships between proteins. While we attempt to account for
sources of phylogenetic error and discordance in our analyses,
we recognize that the limitations of current phylogenetic
methods may affect the reconstruction accuracy of deep timepoints such as LECA.

METHOD DETAILS
Retrieval of protein sequence data
Species proteomes were downloaded from UniProt, NCBI GenBank/RefSeq, VeuPathDB, and other sources from the literature. The
complete set of data sources and accessions is available in (Table S1). Species proteome FASTA files with a single best isoform per
gene were downloaded from UniProt and VeuPathDB. For species proteome FASTA files downloaded from GenBank/RefSeq, CDHIT (v4.8.1) was run with a 90% identity threshold and 80% alignment coverage (of the longer sequence) threshold to remove redundant isoforms using the command ‘cd-hit -i [inputfile] -o [outputfile] -M 2000 -T 1 -d 0 -c 0.9 -n 5 -aL 0.8’.
Prokaryotic downsampling
We downsampled prokaryotic proteins to improve the taxonomic balance of the dataset (counteracting sequencing bias towards
well-studied lineages) and to ensure that downstream analyses were computationally tractable. We grouped prokaryotic species
into 34 groups based on the NCBI taxonomy, consisting of 29 bacterial kingdoms (that each had ≥10 species) and 5 archaeal superfamilies. For the six most-sequenced bacterial kingdoms (Actinomycetota, Alphaproteobacteria, Bacillota, Bacteroidota/
Chlorobiota group, Betaproteobacteria, and Gammaproteobacteria) that collectively comprised more than 8,000 species, we
selected a single representative species for each class (in NCBI taxonomy) that had the highest genome annotation quality based
on BUSCO157 (highest % complete, breaking ties by lowest % fragmented followed by highest % single-copy). We clustered all
the sequences within each prokaryotic group (with >50% minimum sequence identity and >80% alignment coverage) using
MMseqs2149 (v15-0b27c9d) with the command ‘mmseqs easy-cluster [prokaryotic_group_FASTA_file] [output_file_prefix] [temporary_directory] --threads 1 --split-memory-limit 2G --min-seq-id 0.5 --cov-mode 2 -c 0.8 -v 1’. The longest sequence in each cluster
was selected as the representative protein from the prokaryotic group. In downstream analyses, each prokaryotic group was treated
as a single taxon that contained its downsampled representative proteins, unless otherwise noted.
Pfam domain assignment
Pfam domains were assigned to each protein by hmmsearch of Pfam-A (v35) against the proteome of each species using HMMER
(v3.3.2) using default settings. Each protein was annotated with the Pfam domain(s) that had full sequence E-value < 0.001. If multiple
domains overlapped by more than 50 amino acids, only the top scoring domain among the overlapping set was retained.
Orthogroup (OG) inference
Raw orthogroups. Orthology inference was performed using a custom pipeline. An initial set of raw OGs were inferred for a set of 656
eukaryotes and 34 prokaryotic groups using OrthoFinder v2.5.5. To accelerate all-vs-all alignments by DIAMOND, we precomputed
DIAMOND search databases and generated the set of all-vs-all DIAMOND commands using the command ‘orthofinder -f [FASTA_
directory] -op’ and then executed the DIAMOND commands in parallel. Raw OGs were inferred from the set of all-vs-all DIAMOND

alignments using the command ‘orthofinder -b [diamond_results_directory] -t 256 -a 32 -og’. Four eukaryotes (three plants: Eucalyptus grandis, Hordeum vulgare, Paspalum vaginatum; and the amoebozoan Entamoeba nuttalli) were excluded from downstream
analysis since they had low quality genome annotations, defined by identification of ≤ 8 of 20 universal ribosomal subunits by
HMMER (v3.3.2) hmmsearch using the Pfam domain(s) associated with each subunit (full sequence E-value < 0.001). The resulting
raw orthogroups spanned 652 eukaryotes and 34 prokaryotic groups.
Raw non-singleton OGs (>1 protein member) were refined by two merge steps that incorporated homology (based on sequence
profile and structure) and species overlap. Merging OGs based on homology alone would tend to over-merge OGs, potentially merging ancient homologs/paralogs (such as the cytosolic and mitochondrial ribosome subunits) that should ideally be in separate OGs.
To differentiate pairs of OGs that incorrectly splintered putative orthologs (splintered OGs) from pairs of OGs that correctly separated
ancient homologs/paralogs (paralogous OGs), we reasoned that pairs of splintered OGs would tend to have low overlap among their
member species, while pairs of paralogous OGs would tend to have high overlap among their member species. The species overlap
coefficient quantifies the degree of species overlap between a query OG and a target OG, accounting for the median copy number in

the target OG: species overlap coefficient(x; y) = min(|x|;|y|)

target OG, and y′ is the set of species in the target OG that have a copy number ≥ median(y). Since the merge procedure considered
each OG pair in order of alignment strength, the species overlap coefficient was recomputed on the fly using a matrix of copy counts
(OGs x species) that was updated to reflect already merged OG pairs.
First merge. For the first merge step, raw OGs were merged based on sequence profile similarity (HMM-HMM alignment by HHsuite v3.3.0). To reduce computational burden, downsampled multiple sequence alignments were computed for each raw OG. The
protein sequences in each orthogroup were downsampled by clustering similar sequences (with >50% minimum sequence identity
and >80% alignment coverage) using MMseqs2149 (v15-0b27c9d) with the command ‘mmseqs easy-cluster [OG_FASTA_file] [output_file_prefix] [temporary_directory] --threads 1 --split-memory-limit 2G --min-seq-id 0.5 --cov-mode 2 -c 0.8 -v 1’. Then, for each
raw OG, the set of downsampled sequences (the longest representative sequence per cluster) was aligned by MAFFT (v7.520) with
the command ‘mafft --auto --quiet --thread 1 --anysymbol [FASTA_file] > [MSA_file]’, trimmed by ClipKIT (v2.3.0) using the command
‘clipkit -q -m gappy [MSA_file] -o [trimmed_MSA_file]’, and any sequences containing only gaps were removed from the alignment
using a custom python script (michaelzhuchen GitHub). An HH-suite database was built from the set of trimmed alignments using a
custom script implementing HH-suite (v3.3.0). Using this database, each OG was exhaustively aligned against all other OGs in a
‘global’ mode using ‘hhsearch -i [query_OG_MSA_file] -d [database_name] -M 50 -maxres 65535 -E 1 -o [output_file] -v 0 -glob’.
For each query OG, the top 50 hits were filtered by E-value < 0.001 to obtain a candidate set of OG pairs for merging. Candidate
OG pairs were sorted by alignment strength (by lowest to highest E-value, breaking ties by highest to lowest bitscore). The set of
sorted candidate OG pairs were merged by a two-phase procedure. First, OG pairs that were bidirectional hits with E-value < 0.001
and had species overlap coefficient < 0.3 were merged in order of alignment strength. Second, OG pairs that were non-bidirectional
hits and passed a stricter filter (E-value < 1e-10 and species overlap coefficient < 0.05) were merged in order of alignment strength.
This merge step yielded the set of initially merged OGs.
Second merge. For the second merge step, the initially merged OGs were further merged using a similar procedure that incorporated structural homology to increase the sensitivity for remote homology detection. All protein sequences in each initially merged OG
were aligned by MAFFT (v7.520) with the command ‘mafft --auto --quiet --thread 1 --anysymbol [FASTA_file] > [MSA_file]’, trimmed
by ClipKIT (v2.3.0) using the command ‘clipkit -q -m gappy [MSA_file] -o [trimmed_MSA_file]’, and any sequences containing
only gaps were removed from the alignment using a custom python script (michaelzhuchen GitHub). An HH-suite database was built
from the set of trimmed alignments using a custom script implementing HH-suite (v3.3.0). Using this database, each OG was exhaustively aligned against all other OGs in a ‘global’ mode using ‘hhsearch -i [query_OG_MSA_file] -d [database_name] -M 50 -maxres
65535 -E 1 -o [output_file] -v 0 -glob’. For each query OG, the top 50 hits were filtered by probability > 30 and species overlap coefficient < 0.3 to obtain a candidate set of OG pairs for merging. The subset of OG pairs that were bidirectional hits by sequence
profile alignment were selected for structural alignment, guaranteeing a minimum level of sequence homology to prevent merging
based solely on (possibly convergent) structural homology. For these OGs, Alphafold2 predicted structures were downloaded
from AFDB or inferred by ColabFold (for some newly annotated Acanthamoeba proteins113). Foldseek (v9-427df8a) was used to
perform an exhaustive all-vs-all alignment between the structures in each pair of OGs using the command ‘foldseek easy-search
[first_OG_structures_directory] [second_OG_structures_directory] [output_file] [temporary_directory] --format-output "query,target,evalue,bits,lddt,prob,qcov,tcov,alntmscore" --exhaustive-search -e 10 --threads 1 -v 0’. For each pair of OGs, we calculated the
mean bitscore across all alignments and calculated the corresponding probability based on the fitted score distributions of
true positives and false positives on SCOPe40 by Foldseek. We computed the combined probability of homology by either sequence
profile or structure using the following equation, assuming independence: P(sequence profile homology or structure homology) =
1 − (1 − P(HMM homology)) ∗ (1 − P(Foldseek homology)). OG pairs that were bidirectional hits with a combined probability > 0.9 and with species overlap coefficient < 0.3 were merged in order of alignment strength (by highest to lowest probability,
breaking ties by highest to lowest bitscore). This merge step yielded the set of fully merged OGs.
Fully merged orthogroups. The fully merged OGs were used as the basis for the final phase in orthogroup refinement: the identification of fusion proteins, the addition of new proteins to the dataset, and the merging of singleton proteins. All protein sequences in
each fully merged OG were aligned by MAFFT (v7.520) with the command ‘mafft --auto --quiet --thread 1 --anysymbol

[FASTA_file] > [MSA_file]’, trimmed by ClipKIT (v2.3.0) using the command ‘clipkit -q -m gappy [MSA_file] -o [trimmed_MSA_file]’,
and any sequences containing only gaps were removed from the alignment using a custom python script (michaelzhuchen
GitHub). For each fully merged OG, HMMs were built using the HMMER (v3.3.2) hmmbuild command with default settings. These
fully merged OG HMMs were used for the following three steps, which were executed in parallel to eliminate dependencies: the identification of fusion proteins, the addition of new proteins to the dataset, and the merging of singleton proteins.
Identification of fusion proteins. Some ‘‘fusion’’ proteins had multiple non-overlapping domains or sequence segments that had
homology to multiple OGs but were initially assigned to a single primary OG. To add fusion proteins to each of their homologous
OGs, we first identified a candidate set of fusion proteins that were at least 50 amino acids longer than the median length of the proteins in their OG. We aligned each fully merged OG HMM against the set of candidate fusion proteins using HMMER (v3.3.2) with the
command ‘hmmsearch -o /dev/null --noali -Z 379668 -E 1e-10 --domE 1e-10 --tformat fasta --cpu 1 --domtblout [output_file]
[OG_hmm_file] [fusion_proteins_FASTA_file]’, using the -Z option to specify the size of the query set (379,668 non-singleton fully
merged OGs). The alignments were processed based on the per-domain independent E-values, filtering for E-value ≤ 1e-10 and
sorted by alignment strength (from lowest to highest E-value). For each candidate fusion protein, we identified the segment(s) of
the protein that aligned to its originally-assigned primary OG. Then, we identified the next strongest alignment that did not overlap
with any of the previously aligned segments of the protein, and this segment was added to the set of aligned segments for the protein.
This process was repeated until there were no more alignments for the protein. If there were any disjoint segments of the protein that
aligned to non-primary OGs, then the protein was added as a member of these non-primary OGs (in addition to continued membership in its originally-assigned primary OG).
Addition of species and mtDNA-encoded proteins. A small number of species proteomes were added or updated after the fully
merged OGs were generated (Table S1). The 21 added proteomes boosted the species representation from lineages of interest
(including parasitic plants and CRuMs), and some of the existing proteomes were updated to include mtDNA-encoded proteins
or to use up-to-date annotations. To integrate these additional species into the dataset and provide a means for future updates
as new sequencing/annotation data are generated, we used an HMM-based procedure to assign the added proteins to their homologous OGs. We aligned each fully merged OG HMM against the set of added proteins using HMMER (v3.3.2) with the command
‘hmmsearch -o /dev/null --noali -Z 379668 -E 1e-3 --domE 1e-3 --tformat fasta --cpu 1 --domtblout [output_file] [OG_hmm_file] [added_proteins_FASTA_file]’, using the -Z option to specify the size of the query set (379,668 non-singleton fully merged OGs). Each
added protein was merged into the OG that had the strongest alignment (lowest full-sequence E-value and highest bitscore) with full
sequence E-value < 1e-3; otherwise it was made a singleton. From the set of added proteins that were merged, fusion proteins were
identified and added to their non-primary OGs using the procedure described above. Obsolete protein sequences (that were supplanted by newer annotations) were removed from the dataset.
Merging singleton proteins. Some ‘‘singleton’’ proteins had homology to other proteins, despite being assigned to singleton OGs
with no other proteins. To merge singleton proteins with their homologous OGs, we aligned each fully merged OG HMM against the
set of singleton proteins using HMMER (v3.3.2) with the command ‘hmmsearch -o /dev/null --noali -Z 379668 -E 1e-3 --domE 1e-3
--tformat fasta --cpu 1 --domtblout [output_file] [OG_hmm_file] [singleton_proteins_FASTA_file]’, using the -Z option to specify the
size of the query set (379,668 non-singleton fully merged OGs). Each singleton protein was merged into the OG that had the strongest
alignment (lowest full-sequence E-value and highest bitscore) with full sequence E-value < 1e-3; otherwise it remained as an unmerged singleton. From the set of singleton proteins that were merged, fusion proteins were identified and added to their non-primary
OGs using the procedure described above.
Refined orthogroups. The refined OGs were obtained from the fully merged OGs after the fusion proteins, added proteins, and
singleton proteins were processed, spanning 673 eukaryotes. The refined OGs for a subset of 203 eukaryotes were used for downstream analysis by selecting species that had high genome annotation quality (based on BUSCO157) and balanced taxonomic representation across the major eukaryotic clades.
Species phylogeny inference
The species tree was inferred from a concatenated alignment of 20 universal ribosomal proteins across eukaryotes, bacteria, and
archaea, as done in a previous study.176 In each species, the top-scoring homolog for each ribosomal subunit was identified by
HMMER (v3.3.2) hmmsearch using the Pfam domain(s) associated with each subunit (full sequence E-value < 0.001). Each subunit
was aligned by MUSCLE (v5.1, PPP mode using ‘-align’), trimmed by ClipKIT (v2.3.0) using the command ‘clipkit -q -m gappy
[MSA_file] -o [trimmed_MSA_file]’, and any sequences containing only gaps were removed from the alignment using a custom
python script (michaelzhuchen GitHub). The resulting 20 trimmed alignments were concatenated into a supermatrix using a
custom script (michaelzhuchen GitHub). IQ-TREE (v2.3.6) was run to first infer the optimal per-gene partition model by
ModelFinder using the command ‘iqtree2 -s [MSA_file] -m MF+MERGE -cmin 4 -cmax 12 -p [partition_file] -safe -seed 42 -nt
[number_of_threads] -rcluster 10’. Then, the best-fit model was used to perform tree search and nearest neighbor interchange
(NNI)-optimized ultrafast bootstrap153 constrained by a guide tree topology using the command ‘iqtree2 -s [MSA_file] -p [partition_file] -g [guide_tree_file] -bb 1000 -alrt 1000 -safe -seed 42 -nt [number_of_threads] -bnni’. To minimize long-branch attraction
artifacts, the tree search was constrained by a guide tree based on the NCBI taxonomy, which was manually-refined to reflect

current understanding of species relationships. Each prokaryotic group was collapsed to a single leaf by averaging the branch
lengths of the species within the group. The tree was manually rooted at the midpoint of the branch connecting Bacteria and
archaea.
Protein phylogeny inference
Alignment and initial phylogenetic inference. To reduce the large computational burden of phylogenetic inference, we identified the subset of refined OGs that contained an experimentally defined mitochondrial protein or that were candidates for splitting homologs/paralogs/xenologs based on either 1) presence of prokaryotic homologs (indicating possible multiple independent acquisitions by eukaryotes) or 2) presence of multiple copies in any member species and dating to the level of eukaryotic kingdoms or earlier (indicating
possible ancestral duplication). Trimmed multiple sequence alignments were generated by aligning the protein sequences from
each orthogroup using MUSCLE (v5.1, PPP algorithm if ≤500 sequences using ‘-align’ option, Super5 algorithm if >500 sequences using ‘-super5’ option), trimmed by ClipKIT (v2.3.0) using the command ‘clipkit -q -m gappy [MSA_file] -o [trimmed_MSA_file]’, and any
sequences containing only gaps were removed from the alignment using a custom python script (michaelzhuchen GitHub). In rare
cases, extremely large alignments could not be aligned by MUSCLE; instead these were aligned by FAMSA with the command
‘famsa -t 2 [FASTA_file] [MSA_file]’. Maximum-likelihood phylogenies were inferred from the trimmed multiple sequence alignments using IQ-TREE (v2.3.6) with the best-fit substitution model selected by ModelFinder and with 1,000 NNI-optimized ultrafast bootstraps153
by the command ‘iqtree2 -s [MSA_file] -m MFP -cmin 4 -cmax 12 -bb 1000 -alrt 1000 -seed 42 -nt [number_of_threads] -wbtl -bnni’.
Origin inference, pruning, and rooting. A custom script was used to process the maximum-likelihood tree and set of 1000 ultrafast
bootstrap trees. The putative origin of each orthogroup was assigned based on the topology of the maximum-likelihood tree. The
clades in the maximum-likelihood tree were exhaustively screened to identify clades that had strong representation of prokaryotic
groups, which was assessed by the depth and breadth of prokaryotic group species within each clade, accounting for the downsampled prokaryotic groups. A prokaryotic group had strong representation in a clade if at least 50% of its subgroups (one taxonomic
level lower in the prokaryotic group) were well-represented, defined as having at least 25% of the subgroup species present in the
clade. An orthogroup was assigned ‘‘Prokaryotic origin’’ if there was any clade that had at least 90% prokaryotic species, strong representation of at least one prokaryotic group, and at least 10 prokaryotic species. Otherwise, an orthogroup was assigned ‘‘Eukaryotic origin’’ if >90% of all species in the tree were eukaryotic. Any remaining orthogroups were assigned ‘‘Indeterminate origin’’.
We pruned prokaryotic leaves that were possible prokaryotic contaminants or recent HGTs from eukaryotes to prokaryotes. For
orthogroups with ‘‘Eukaryotic origin’’, all prokaryotic proteins were pruned. For orthogroups with ‘‘Prokaryotic origin’’ or ‘‘Indeterminate origin’’, prokaryotic proteins were pruned unless they were found in a majority prokaryotic clade (at least 90% prokaryotic species) and had at least 10 prokaryotic species or a prokaryotic group with strong representation.
For each orthogroup, the maximum-likelihood tree and ultrafast bootstrap trees were rooted based on the putative origin of the
orthogroup and MAD (v2.2). For orthogroups with ‘‘Eukaryotic origin’’ or ‘‘Indeterminate origin’’, trees were MAD rooted. For orthogroups with ‘‘Prokaryotic origin’’, the largest prokaryotic clade (that had the most prokaryotic groups with strong representation
and the most prokaryotic species) was designated as the outgroup and trees were MAD rooted within this clade.
Reconciliation of protein phylogenies with the species phylogeny. To account for the species tree and confidence in the inferred
protein tree for each orthogroup, the rooted ultrafast bootstrap protein trees were reconciled with the species tree using
AleRax155,177 (version 1.2). AleRax takes as input a posterior distribution of protein trees and estimates the most likely protein tree
topology conditioning on a probabilistic model of duplication, transfer, and loss on the species tree. AleRax was run using the command ‘alerax -f [families_file] -s [species_tree] -p [output_directory] --rec-opt GRADIENT --gene-tree-samples 100 --model-parametrization [parametrization_file] --fraction-missing-file [fraction_missing_file] --seed 42 --gene-tree-rooting ROOTED’. The parameterization file specified two rate regimes for duplications, losses, and transfers, with one rate for prokaryotes and one rate for
eukaryotes (since prokaryotes and eukaryotes are known to have distinct rates of duplication and HGT). For each orthogroup,
AleRax inferred a reconciled majority rule consensus topology that lacked meaningful branch lengths, and a sample of 100 reconciled
protein trees that were annotated with inferred duplications, losses, and transfers.
To infer meaningful branch lengths for the reconciled majority rule consensus tree, IQ-TREE was used to perform tree search constrained by the consensus tree topology and using the original substitution model and pruned multiple sequence alignment (after
removing prokaryotic contaminants) with the command ‘iqtree2 -s [pruned_MSA_file] -g [consensus_tree_file] --prefix [output_prefix]
-m [original_substitution_model] -nt [number_of_threads] -seed 42 -quiet’.
Phylogenetically-resolved orthogroup (PhROG) inference
A phylogenetically-resolved orthogroup (PhROG) is defined as the set of proteins that descend from a single ancestral gene in last
common ancestor of the species with a homolog that are found within a focal taxonomic level of the species tree. We inferred
PhROGs for eukaryotic ancestral taxonomic levels by a two-step procedure that first identified the complete set of well-supported
clades from reconciled protein phylogenies and then extracted the subset of clades corresponding to a single ancestral protein at
each taxonomic level, accounting for duplications and horizontal gene transfers. PhROGs were created for the subset of refined
OGs for which a protein phylogeny had been inferred (above).
For each refined OG, a clade frequency table was constructed from the sample of 100 reconciled protein trees that tabulated the
frequency of each clade (containing the same protein members) across the sampled trees. Each clade was assigned a taxonomic

label based on the last common ancestor (LCA) of their member species on the species tree. Species that acquired the protein by
recent horizontal gene transfer (HGT) within the clade were excluded from LCA-based taxonomic label assignment. Clades that were
originally labeled as deep prokaryote–eukaryote ancestors but that had strong eukaryotic representation (> 90% eukaryotic species,
accounting for prokaryotic downsampling) were assigned a taxonomic label based on the LCA of their eukaryotic members on the
species tree.
To identify HGT and distinguish it from other sources of phylogenetic incongruence, we identified putative HGT based on three
main criteria: large phylogenetic incongruence, low prior likelihood of vertical inheritance from a common ancestor, and predicted
HGT by the AleRax reconciliation algorithm. To evaluate putative HGTs, a preorder traversal of each tree was performed and nodes
that were predicted to be HGT by the AleRax reconciliation algorithm were considered as candidates for HGT. HGT candidates were
filtered for large phylogenetic incongruence, defined as a candidate HGT node with two daughter clades that had an LCA at the level
of eukaryotic superfamilies or older. For each HGT candidate node, the direction of HGT was determined by the last common
ancestor (LCA) of the outgroup clade and the two daughter clades: the sister clade with an LCA that was closest to the LCA of
the outgroup clade (based on the number of branches separating the LCAs on the species tree) was assigned as the donor,
and the other sister clade was assigned as the recipient. If the two sister clades had LCAs that were equally distant from the LCA
of the outgroup clade, the direction of transfer could not be determined and the candidate was discarded. The candidate HGTs
were further filtered for a low prior likelihood of vertical inheritance from a common ancestor, operationally defined as a recipient
clade with ≤ 10 species retained in the clade (accounting for prokaryotic downsampling). This threshold was selected by benchmarking on conserved subunits of mitochondrial and chloroplast complexes, which are known to have initially entered eukaryotes
in LECA and Archaeplastida, respectively. Proteins were marked as having a ’’non-vertical’’ inheritance pattern if they descended
from a putative within-clade HGT that passed these filters in at least 10% of sampled trees containing the clade. Non-vertical proteins
were excluded from LCA-based assignments of taxonomic labels to each clade.
We identified putative duplications based on the species overlap coverage, similar to previous studies.32,58 For each clade, we
determined the species overlap coverage between the daughter clades, defined as the number of unique eukaryotic species present
in both daughter clades divided by the number of unique eukaryotic species in either clade. The average species overlap coverage for
each clade was computed by taking the average of the species overlap coverage across the sampled trees containing the clade. The
species overlap coverage support was defined as the fraction of sampled trees containing the clade that had a species overlap
coverage exceeding a threshold (default: > 0.1). This threshold was determined by benchmarking on OXPHOS/mitoribosome proteins (that are typically single-copy) and the pre-LECA duplications described in a recent study.84
PhROGs at a focal ancestral taxonomic level were inferred by applying a series of filters to identify clades that descended from a
single gene in the focal ancestor. We filtered for well-supported clades that were found in at least 50% of sampled trees. For each
clade, we computed the fraction of species that were eukaryotic, accounting for prokaryotic downsampling. We then restricted subsequent analyses to clades that were strongly enriched in eukaryotes (at least two-fold enriched compared to the background abundance of eukaryotes) in order to focus on gains into eukaryotes and their subsequent history. Duplications that occurred at or above
the focal taxonomic level were identified using species overlap coverage and support metrics (default: species overlap coverage > 0.1
and species overlap coverage support ≥ 0.5), and any clades that pre-dated these duplications were removed to split ancestral paralogs (that diverged at or above the focal taxonomic level) into separate clades. Unlike PhROGs at the LECA level (which were processed normally), PhROGs at the Eukaryota level did not consider duplications, since these PhROGs span the entire eukaryotic
domain and represent initial acquisitions into eukaryotes. We ordered the remaining clades by size and taxonomic depth and assigned each protein in the focal taxonomic level to its largest clade. The protein members of each remaining clade were assigned
a unique identifier to define them as members of a PhROG at the focal taxonomic level.
For orthogroups containing anaerobic proteins of interest for ancestral reconstruction, trees were manually reviewed to screen for
possible artifacts. NCBI taxonomy labels and Pfam domains were superimposed on the leaves of the tree to aid visual inspection. In
two orthogroups (PFOR; HydE/F), the trees grouped eukaryotes into several major clades, but there were multiple prokaryotic outgroups that had non/partially-overlapping Pfam domains, suggestive of complex multidomain rearrangements or fusions that may
compromise the quality of phylogenetic tree inference. To ensure high-quality phylogenetic inferences for these two orthogroups,
for each orthogroup we manually identified one or more conserved Pfam domain architectures shared by a large fraction of member
proteins (>30%) and retrieved the subset of proteins containing these conserved domains. Across the two orthogroups, we identified
three such subsets, which we called ‘‘resampled orthogroups’’. The resampled orthogroups were subjected to the full protein phylogeny and PhROG inference pipeline and were used for downstream analyses in place of the two original orthogroups.
Orthology benchmarking
A set of three independent benchmarks were used to assess the quality of OG and PhROG inference.
First, the new method was assessed using the 2020 benchmark from the Quest for Orthologs (QfO) consortium.33,34 The 2020
benchmark was selected to maximize the number of other methods available for comparison, as recommended by QfO. The species
proteome FASTA files for the 2020 benchmark were downloaded from the QfO website and refined OGs were inferred from these
FASTA files using the new orthogroup inference pipeline. Due to the heavy burden of pan-proteome phylogenetic inference,
PhROGs could not be systematically inferred for the QfO dataset. Instead, the complementary method in OrthoFinder (v2.5.5)
was used to efficiently infer phylogenetic hierarchical orthologous groups (HOGs) starting from the refined OGs, using the ‘‘MSA’’

mode with the command ‘orthofinder -fg [working_directory] -M msa -t 64 -a 4’. A custom script was used to convert the refined OGs
into a data format that was compatible with OrthoFinder (michaelzhuchen GitHub). Pairwise orthologs were extracted from the HOGs
and used as the input to the QfO benchmarking python script, which computed the benchmarks for the new method and generated
the associated plots.
Second, the new method was assessed by the manually-curated reference OGs (RefOGs) from Orthobench from 202030 (a revision
of the original release in 201135). The species FASTA files were downloaded from the Open Orthobench repository. Refined OGs and
PhROGs (at the focal taxonomic level N3) were inferred using the supplied species tree and three outgroup species, using the
methods described above. Performance metrics were computed by the benchmark python script from Open Orthobench.
Third, we assessed the new method using a curated set of experimentally defined orthologs that are conserved across diverse
eukaryotes. The reference orthologs were obtained from cytosolic ribosome cryo-EM structures (PDB 4UG0, 4V88, 4V8M, 8B2L,
7PWO), iron-sulfur cluster (ISC) pathway members from experimental evidence in literature, mitochondrial and chloroplast ribosome
cryo-EM structures (PDB 6HIV, 5MRC, 6GAW, 6ERI; EMDB EMD-4408), Complex-I cryo-EM structures (PDB 7TGH, 7ARB, 9GRX,
7WG5), and mitochondrial and chloroplast ATP synthase cryo-EM structures (PDB 8H9S, 6CP6, 8AP6, 6TML, 6FKF) and complexome data for Arabidopsis. Heatmaps were generated to compare the accuracy of the identified homologs to the experimentally
defined ground truth across a variety of methods, including best bidirectional hit (BBH) from the human protein against the target
species proteomes using BLASTp (E-value < 1), Foldseek (E-value < 1), or PLMSearch and PLMAlign (prefiltered hits with
PLMSearch score > 0.3 were reranked by PLMAlign using ProtT5 embeddings). Hierarchical orthogroups at the Eukaryota level
were retrieved from the OrthoDB (v12.1) and EggNOG (v6) databases.
Comparative analysis of experimentally defined mitochondrial proteomes
For each core species, the set of OGs that contained an experimentally defined mitochondrial protein in that species was identified.
These mitochondrial OGs were compared by visualizing the overlapping/distinct subsets using the ComplexUpset (v1.3.6) R package. OGs that contained a human MitoPathway member were assigned a human MitoPathway, and the human MitoPathways that
were contained in each overlapping set (at least 50% of pathway members present) were labeled on the UpSet plot.
Estimation of homology detection power
The abSENSE method predicts the power to detect a homolog of a focal query species protein in a specified target species, based on
an exponential model of protein sequence similarity as a function of evolutionary distance. The protein sequence similarity is estimated by the BLAST bitscore, while the evolutionary distance is estimated from the sum of species tree branch lengths between
the query and target species. The method has proven effective in distinguishing between the true absence of a homolog versus
the apparent absence of a homolog, which could simply be due to failure to detect a fast-evolving homolog across a large evolutionary distance. The power to detect homologs is important for orthogroups, which often are lineage-specific. The original abSENSE
method is unable to handle multiple query proteins, since the two inputs to the model (BLAST bitscore and tree distance) are only
defined over pairs of proteins or species, respectively. To estimate the detection power of multiple homologs in another species,
we developed an extension to the abSENSE approach that leverages profile HMMs, which we call abSENSE-HMM. Briefly, for
each protein in the orthogroup, the similarity score is estimated by leaving the query protein out of the MSA, then using HMMER
(v3.3.2) hmmbuild to build a profile HMM from this (n-1) MSA, and performing hmmsearch of this profile HMM against the left-out
query protein (with database size fixed to the total number of non-singleton orthogroups using ‘-Z’ option). In this manner, an
hmmsearch bitscore is computed for each protein in the orthogroup and the top hit homolog in each species is retained as the putative ortholog. For each species in the orthogroup, we estimate the evolutionary distance by computing the mean species tree distance between this species and all other species in the orthogroup. The exponential model is fit using the bitscore and mean evolutionary distance to estimate the power to detect homologs in outgroups. The model assumes vertical inheritance of proteins following
the species tree topology, which is violated by horizontal gene transfer. Therefore, phylogenetically-resolved orthogroups at Eukaryota are used to ensure that each analyzed family originated from a single independent gain into eukaryotes. If phylogeneticallyresolved orthogroups are not available, the method can also be run directly on orthogroups, assuming vertical inheritance along
the species phylogeny.
Identification and characterization of candidate targets
Candidate targets were identified based on shared presence in at least two pathogen kingdoms, as well as the absence of a human
ortholog and sufficient power to detect a human homolog if one existed (to reject possible false negatives). PhROGs at the Eukaryota
level were used for this analysis to split independent acquisitions from prokaryotes into distinct groups (e.g. mitochondrial ribosome
subunits versus chloroplast ribosome subunits). To incorporate experimental essentiality data, the Plasmodium knowlesi essentialome was obtained from a prior study.41 The 5,339 P. knowlesi proteins mapped to 4,158 PhROGs at the Eukaryota level, of which
4,055 (97.5%) had a hybrid model essentiality score from the prior study. In the case of multiple P. knowlesi paralogs in the same
PhROG, the mean hybrid model essentiality score across the paralogs was used. Each PhROG was assigned a label based on its
homologs in the eight core species: PhROGs were assigned a ‘‘mito’’ label if they contained a mito-localized homolog in a core species and were furthermore designated as a ‘‘Candidate mito target’’ if they contained a mito-localized candidate homolog in a core
species. PhROGs lacking a core species homolog were excluded. All other PhROGs were labeled as ‘‘Non-Mito’’. Based on the

thresholds in the original study,41 essentiality scores < 0.26 were deemed ‘‘essential’’, scores > 0.26 were deemed ‘‘non-essential’’,
and the subset of scores > 0.88 were deemed ‘‘dispensable’’.
Prediction of mitochondrial targeting sequences
Mitochondrial targeting sequences were predicted for all proteins in the orthogroups dataset by TargetP (v2.0 Linux x86_64). For
species with a primary plastid, the program was run in ‘‘plant’’ mode using the command ‘targetp -fasta [FASTA_file] -org pl -format
short -plot none -prefix [output_prefix]’. For all other species, the program was run in default mode using the command ‘targetp -fasta
[FASTA_file] -org non-pl -format short -plot none -prefix [output_prefix]’. Proteins that have Prediction = ‘‘mTP’’ were labeled as having an MTS, and other proteins that have an mTP score > 0.2 were labeled as possibly having an MTS.
Prediction of transmembrane domains
Transmembrane domains were predicted for all proteins in the orthogroups dataset by TMHMM (v2.0c) using the command ‘tmhmm
[FASTA_file] > [output_file]’.
Prediction of protein subcellular localizations
The DeepLoc2.0 model49 is a state-of-the-art model for predicting protein subcellular localization from sequence. To improve the
performance of mitochondrial localization prediction, we retrained the model on an augmented protein localization dataset
combining the original Swiss-Prot training dataset (Swiss-Prot dataset), the previously reported experimentally defined mitoproteomes of human24 and yeast,17 and the experimentally defined mitochondrial proteomes from the MitoTOL project (MitoViaPCP
dataset). For the MitoTOL core species, the experimentally defined MitoViaPCP datasets were used instead of the MitoCarta inventories (which integrate experimental and literature evidence) to avoid circularity with literature annotations and to leverage the PCP
scores for training label assignments.
To prepare the new training dataset, we used a custom script to assign a training label and a binary vector of subcellular localizations (with a value for each subcellular compartment predicted by the model) to each protein (michaelzhuchen GitHub). To assign
training labels to the MitoViaPCP dataset, the Pearson correlation of each expressed protein to the average expression of three
conserved mitochondrial marker proteins (Hsp60, Tom40, and IscU) was calculated following the procedure used for each species25–29. Labels were assigned to each protein based on the correlation and immunofluorescence microscopy evidence (when available): ‘‘non-mitochondrial’’ (r < 0), ‘‘mitochondrial’’ (r > 0.6 or dual-localized to mitochondria and other compartments by microscopy),
and ‘‘only-mitochondrial’’ (r > 0.95 or localized only to mitochondria by microscopy). For Giardia and Babesia, the ‘‘mitochondrial’’
label was omitted to reduce false positives, and the threshold for the ‘‘only-mitochondrial’’ label followed the strict thresholds
(r > 0.95 for Giardia, r > 0.96 for Babesia) that were used to define the respective MitoCartas27,28. For Arabidopsis, 44 ‘‘MOM_signature’’ proteins were labeled as ‘‘only-mitochondrial’’ based on strong correlation when excluding proteinase K treated samples25. Any
‘‘non-mitochondrial’’ protein found in a MitoCarta inventory was excluded from the training dataset since this indicated a conflict between the proteomics data and literature evidence. For the well-studied human and yeast proteomes, proteins were assigned as
‘‘mitochondrial’’ if they were found in the human24 or yeast17 mitochondrial proteomes, and ‘‘non-mitochondrial’’ otherwise. The binary vector of subcellular localizations for each protein was defined based on its label. For ‘‘only-mitochondrial’’ proteins, the Mitochondrion value was marked as 1 and all other localizations were marked as 0. For ‘‘mitochondrial’’ proteins, the Mitochondrion value
was marked as 1 and all other localizations were marked as missing (since they could be dual-localized). For ‘‘non-mitochondrial’’
proteins, the Mitochondrion value was marked as 0 and all other localizations were marked as missing, since their non-mitochondrial
localizations were unknown. Organelle-encoded proteins and proteins that were less than 40 amino acids long were excluded from
training, as done in the original DeepLoc2.0 publication.49 Protein sequences were trimmed to a maximum of 4000 amino acids, the
maximum input size permissible by the DeepLoc2.0 model in ‘‘Accurate’’ mode.
To partition the combined Swiss-Prot and MitoViaPCP training dataset into homology-aware partitions for training and cross-validation, we used ggsearch (v36.3.8i) to compute global sequence alignments between all proteins in the training dataset with
E-value < 1 using the command ‘ggsearch36 -E 1 -m 8 -T 2 [FASTA_file] [FASTA_file] > [ggsearch_results_file]’. For identical/
near-identical proteins that were found in the original Swiss-Prot training dataset and in the new proteomics dataset (defined as a
top hit with ≥ 90% sequence identity and E-value < 0.001 by ggsearch global alignment), the proteins were collapsed into a single
entry: the pre-existing localization vector from Swiss-Prot was used and its Mitochondrion value was updated based on the label in
the mitochondrial proteomics data. GraphPart (v1.0.2) was used to partition the combined Swiss-Prot dataset and MitoViaPCP dataset into five homology-aware partitions (<30% sequence identity between any two proteins in different partitions) using the command
‘graphpart precomputed --fasta-file [FASTA_file] --threshold 0.3 --out-file [output_file] --labels-name label --partitions 5 --edge-file
[ggsearch_results_file] --metric-column 2’.
To train the model on the combined dataset, we adapted and modified the python scripts from the original DeepLoc2.0 publication49 (michaelzhuchen GitHub). We trained DeepLoc2.0-mito in the ‘‘Accurate’’ mode, which uses embeddings from the 3 billion
parameter ProtT5-XL-UniRef50 protein language model. We modified the loss function to ignore any missing localization values in
the new training dataset and updated the localization weights based on the frequency of each localization in the new training dataset.
Five-fold cross-validation across the five partitions was used to assess model performance and determine the optimal threshold for
each subcellular localization by maximizing the Matthews Correlation Coefficient on the training data, as done in the original

DeepLoc2.0 publication.49 The model performance was further evaluated by leave-one-species-out cross-validation: for each
MitoTOL species, the model was trained on a training dataset that excluded the MitoViaPCP data from that species, and then the
model was used to predict the mitochondrial/non-mitochondrial localizations of the entire proteome of that species, excluding proteins with >30% sequence identity to any protein in the cross-validation training set to prevent leakage. For cross-validation on the
closely related Trypanosoma and Leishmania, we excluded both inventories from training.
To predict the entire mitochondrial proteome from the whole proteome of 206 fully sequenced eukaryotes (203 representative eukaryotes and 3 additional fungal phytopathogens), we used the fully retrained DeepLoc2.0-mito model in ‘‘Accurate’’ mode using the
command ‘deeploc2 -f [FASTA_file] -o [output_directory] -m Accurate’. Since the model is designed to predict localizations for proteins encoded by the nuclear genome, proteins that were mtDNA-encoded were manually labeled as mitochondrial, and proteins that
were encoded by plastid organelle genomes were manually labeled as non-mitochondrial. For species known to possess mtDNA but
with no annotated mtDNA available, there is ambiguity about whether a given protein is encoded by the nuclear genome or the
mtDNA. For these species, we omitted predictions for their plausibly mtDNA-encoded proteins, defined as having shared orthogroup
membership with an mtDNA-encoded protein from another species. Similarly, for plastid-containing organisms that had no annotated plastid genome available, we omitted predictions for their plausibly plastid genome-encoded proteins, defined as having
shared orthogroup membership with a plastid genome-encoded protein from another species.
Predicted mitochondrial proteins were identified by a predicted ‘‘Mitochondrion’’ score greater than or equal to the optimal
threshold identified during training (∼0.5904). Predicted mitochondrial proteins were then marked as high confidence or low confidence based on their primary OG memberships. A predicted mitochondrial protein was marked as high confidence if its primary OG
had at least 5 distinct species with a mitochondrial prediction or if it shared a primary OG with a protein that had experimental support
for mitochondrial localization (found in an experimentally defined mitoproteome or encoded by an mtDNA). Predicted mitochondrial
proteins that did not meet this criterion were marked as low confidence and excluded from the predicted mitoproteome in downstream analysis, since these proteins were enriched in false positives.
To generate attention outputs, we ran the DeepLoc2.0-mito model in ‘‘Accurate’’ mode on the eight core species using the command ‘deeploc2 -f [FASTA_file] -o [output_directory] -m Accurate -p’.
Prediction of ancestral mitochondrial protein localization
Ancestral mitochondrial localization states were inferred by propagating the experimental and predicted protein localizations from
the leaves to the ancestral nodes of the reconciled majority rule consensus trees (with inferred branch lengths) using a custom script
(michaelzhuchen GitHub). For each orthogroup, eukaryotic clades on the tree (each corresponding to an independent acquisition by
eukaryotes) were identified from PhROGs at the Eukaryota level. Each eukaryotic clade was extracted from the tree, and the mitochondrial localizations at ancestral nodes within the clade were inferred by two independent methods: a probabilistic Markov model
and a parsimony model.
First, a continuous-time Markov model (Mk model) with two states (mitochondrial/non-mitochondrial) and equal transition rates
was used to estimate ancestral mitochondrial localizations from the mitochondrial localization probabilities at the leaves. For proteins
in core species, binary localization probabilities were assigned based on presence/absence in the experimental mitochondrial inventories; for proteins in other species, scalar localization probabilities were assigned based on the predicted mitochondrial localization
probabilities from the DeepLoc2.0-mito model. The Mk model was implemented using the castor package (v1.7.11) in R with
the command ‘hsp_mk_model(tree=[eukaryotic_clade], tip_states=NULL, tip_priors=[mitochondrial_localization_probabilities],
Nstates=2, rate_model = "ER", Ntrials=5)’. If there was only one protein with a mitochondrial localization probability, the ancestral
mitochondrial localization probabilities were imputed from the mitochondrial localization probability of that protein; if there were
no proteins with a mitochondrial localization probability, the ancestral mitochondrial localization probabilities were imputed as 0.
Second, a parsimony model with two states (mitochondrial/non-mitochondrial) and symmetric transition costs (1 for state transition, 0 for no state transition) was used to estimate ancestral mitochondrial localizations using mitochondrial localization states that
were solely experimentally defined. For proteins in core species, binary localization states were assigned based on presence/
absence in the experimental mitochondrial inventories; proteins in other species were assigned ‘NA’ states to indicate that experimentally defined localizations were unavailable. The parsimony model was implemented using the castor package (v1.7.11) in R with
the command ‘hsp_max_parsimony(tree=[eukaryotic_clade], [mitochondrial_localization_states]+1, Nstates=2, transition_costs =
matrix(data=c(0,1,1,0), nrow=2, ncol=2), edge_exponent=0)’. If there was only one experimentally defined protein, the ancestral
mitochondrial localization states were imputed from the mitochondrial/non-mitochondrial localization of that protein; if there were
no experimentally defined proteins, the ancestral mitochondrial localization states were imputed as 0.
To attach these results to the PhROG dataset, the inferred mitochondrial localization probability at each internal node was assigned to the corresponding PhROG defined at the node.
The Arabidopsis proteome contained a small set of 54 proteins (0.2% of the proteome) that were unedited mtDNA-encoded proteins or putative nuclear mitochondrial DNA (NUMT) segments (identified as nuclear-encoded Arabidopsis sequences in the same
orthogroup as an Arabidopsis mtDNA-encoded protein). These proteins were not detected in proteomics experiments and are
not found in the Arabidopsis MitoCarta25; therefore, these proteins were assigned ‘NA’ states to indicate unknown localizations
for ancestral localization inferences by both methods above.

Reconstruction of gains, losses, and ancestral mitochondrial proteomes
We reconstructed mitochondrial proteome gains, losses, and ancestral copy numbers on the eukaryotic tree of life leveraging the full
collection of 203 mitoproteomes. To streamline this analysis and simplify visualization, we focused on reconstructing a set of last
common ancestral nodes (e.g., LECA, Opimoda, Amorphea, Opisthokonta, Diphoda, Diaphorectickes, Trypanosomatidae) that connected the eight core species in the study.
For each ancestral node, we retrieved the PhROGs at that taxonomic level and their predicted ancestral mitochondrial localization
probabilities (by the Mk model and parsimony model). In rare cases where mitochondrial probabilities at a node were missing, they
were imputed from the preceding PhROG at the most recent ancestral node, with missing LECA mitochondrial probabilities imputed
from PhROGs at the Eukaryota level. We identified a set of mitochondrial PhROGs by requiring that either the Mk model mitochondrial
probability ≥ 0.5 or the parsimony model mitochondrial probability ≥ 0.5, and that the underlying OG pass the criteria for high confidence mitochondrial predictions.
To assemble a table of reconstructed mitochondrial homologs along the tree, we iterated over nodes from root to leaves. At each
internal node we retained only proteins assigned to their primary OGs and required that a PhROG be either present at that node or
inherited vertically from the immediate ancestor. At the LECA node we additionally required each PhROG to be represented in at least
one major eukaryotic clade and in ≥4 species. At the leaves, we appended the experimentally defined mitochondrial proteome for
each core species.
The mtDNA-encoded proteins of three mtDNA-rich eukaryotes (Reclinomonas americana, Andalucia godoyi, and Mantamonas
sphyraenae) are believed to be ancestral to all eukaryotes,11–13 so their mtDNA-encoded proteins were added to the table if they
were absent from the dataset or not automatically inferred to be ancestral in LECA. We mapped their mtDNA-encoded proteins
to OGs using HMMER (v3.3.2) with the command ‘hmmsearch -o /dev/null --noali -Z 379668 -E 1e-3 --domE 1e-3 --tformat fasta
--cpu 1 --domtblout [output_file] [OG_hmm_file] [mtDNA_proteins_FASTA_file]’, using the -Z option to specify the size of the query
set (379,668 non-singleton fully merged OGs) and filtering for full sequence E-value < 0.001. We assigned each mtDNA-encoded protein to its top hit OG and added them to the table at the LECA node and at other ancestral nodes (e.g., Diphoda, Opimoda) where
applicable based on their distribution across the eukaryotic tree.
Using this mitochondrial reconstruction table, we mapped the evolutionary history of mitochondrial proteins along each branch of
the tree. For each parent–child branch, we collected all mitochondrial PhROGs present at the parent and child nodes, and matched
them by OG and protein ID to identify vertically inherited mitochondrial homologs. Remaining child PhROGs were designated as
gains on that branch; remaining parent PhROGs whose proteins were not recovered in the child were designated as losses. We
also tracked rare cases where a single child PhROG mapped to multiple parent PhROGs and used a post-processing step to merge
these split parental clades upstream so that counts remained consistent.
In parallel to the mitochondrial reconstruction table, we built a second reconstruction table containing all homologs (mitochondrial
and non-mitochondrial, including non-primary OGs) using an analogous vertical-propagation scheme, and appended complete proteomes of the core species at the leaves. This all-homolog reconstruction table was used to distinguish total loss of a gene family from
loss of mitochondrial localization only.
To distinguish true absence from homology detection failure, we incorporated estimates of homology detection power from abSENSE-HMM. For each PhROG, we used its estimated detectability in any outgroup lineage to classify it as ‘‘detectable’’ or ‘‘undetectable’’, and used per-species detection probabilities to determine where a homolog should be detectable.
Loss events on each branch were then categorized into biologically interpretable classes. For each node, we compared mitochondrial PhROGs present in the ancestor vs the descendant, and further asked whether the corresponding OGs and proteins were present anywhere in the descendant’s whole-proteome (all-homolog) reconstruction table. Losses were partitioned as: (i) loss of all
copies (OG absent in the descendant), (ii) loss of all mitochondrial copies (OG present in the descendant but only via non-mitochondrial homologs and with no direct descendant mitochondrial proteins), (iii) loss of some mitochondrial copies (some mitochondrial
homologs retained in the descendant clade), and (iv) relocation to non-mitochondrial compartments (direct descendant homologs
present but losing mitochondrial localization). Each of these classes was further split into ‘‘detectable’’ vs ‘‘possible loss (homology-limited)’’ using the homology power estimates, depending on whether a homolog would be expected to be detectable with
high probability in any descendant species. These categories (loss of all copies, loss of all mitochondrial copies, loss of some mitochondrial copies, relocation to non-mitochondrial compartments, and possible loss (homology-limited)) were summarized and
plotted as stacked bar charts across the branches of the tree.
Gain events were similarly decomposed. For each node, we began from the set of mitochondrial PhROG gains on the incoming
branch after excluding cases attributable to simple within-lineage duplication (detected when multiple PhROGs from the same
OG shared proteins with a single upstream PhROG). Among the remaining gains, we first identified ‘‘retargeted’’ gains: mitochondrial
PhROGs whose proteins matched pre-existing homologs in the ancestor’s full proteome, indicating recruitment of a non-mitochondrial protein into the mitochondrion. We next identified prokaryote-derived horizontal gene transfer (HGT) events as gains whose OGs
had a prokaryotic origin and where PhROGs at the Eukaryota level indicated these gains were initial acquisitions into eukaryotes. The
remaining gains were interpreted as gene births; these were split into ‘‘gene birth’’ vs ‘‘possible gain (homology-limited)’’ depending
on whether any associated proteins belonged to PhROGs classified as detectable in outgroup lineages. Finally, we recorded the

number of within-lineage duplications of mitochondrial homologs per node. These categories (HGT, retargeting, gene birth, duplication of mitochondrial homologs, and possible gain (homology-limited)) were summarized and plotted as stacked bar charts across
the branches of the tree.
We used the reconstruction to calculate point estimates of the number of mitochondrial proteins at each ancestral node (the total
number of mitochondrial protein copies represented at that node). To place these estimates in a conservative interval, we combined
two additional analyses. First, we treated all ‘‘possible’’ gains and losses (those limited by homology detection) as uncertain and, for
each node, estimated an upper bound on the number of missing mitochondrial proteins by combining the maximum number of uncertain gains that could have feasibly been present at the node (assuming 1:1 pairing of uncertain gains in descendants with uncertain
gains in outgroups) with the uncertain losses at the node itself. Adding this bound to the observed mitochondrial count yielded an
upper bound for the ancestral mitochondrial proteome size. Second, we obtained a lower bound by reconstructing mitochondrial
copy number per OG under Dollo-like parsimony using only experimentally defined mitochondrial proteomes from core species.
For each mitochondrial OG we counted mitochondrial copies per species, located the MRCA of all present species, and performed
maximum-parsimony reconstruction on the induced subtree using a proportional cost matrix in which gains from zero copies were
forbidden. The resulting expected copy numbers at internal nodes were summed across OGs to give a parsimony-based lower
bound for each node. Combining the observed counts, parsimony lower bounds, and detectability-based upper bounds, we reported
for each ancestral node an estimated number of mitochondrial proteins together with a conservative interval reflecting both phylogenetic and homology-detection uncertainty.

Horizontal gene transfer inference
Prokaryote-derived HGT was inferred by phylogenetic analysis. For OGs with inferred ‘‘Prokaryotic origin’’, the reconciled protein
phylogenies were screened for candidate prokaryote-derived HGT, defined as a well-supported clade (found in at least 50% of
sampled trees) that was strongly enriched in eukaryotes (at least two-fold enriched compared to the background abundance of eukaryotes) but had purely prokaryotic sister and cousin clades. The timing of each transfer was assigned based on the eukaryotic species in the clade, accounting for within-eukaryote HGT. To identify the putative prokaryotic donor group for each transfer, we calculated an enrichment score, defined as the fraction of species from each prokaryotic group that were found in the sister clade divided
by the background abundance of each prokaryotic group (accounting for prokaryotic downsampling). The prokaryotic group in the
sister clade that had the highest enrichment score ≥ 1 was assigned as the putative prokaryotic donor group.
Branch length timing analysis
Extraction of corrected stem lengths from reconciled phylogenies. Branch length timing analysis was performed using previously
described methods,58,87 which estimated the relative acquisition time of an ancestral protein by dividing the acquisition-to-ancestor
time (estimated from branch lengths connecting the eukaryotic acquisition node to the ancestral node) by the median ancestor-toextant-eukaryote time (estimated from branch lengths connecting the ancestral node to extant eukaryotic leaves). For each OG, the
branch-length-optimized majority rule consensus protein phylogeny was annotated based on PhROGs. For each PhROG at the level
of a focal ancestral species (LECA or Archaeplastida), the last common ancestor node of all the proteins in the PhROG was designated as the corresponding ancestral node on the phylogeny. Then, for each PhROG at the Eukaryota-level, the acquisition node was
defined as the node joining the eukaryotic clade with its prokaryotic sister if the OG had prokaryotic origin. Orthogroups for which
these nodes could not be identified were omitted. Branch lengths were extracted for each ancestor node. The stem length was
the distance from the acquisition node to the origination node. The ancestor-to-extant-eukaryote branch lengths were obtained
from the sum of branch lengths from the ancestral node to its extant eukaryotic descendants at the leaves. The corrected stem length
for each ancestral node was the stem length divided by the median of the ancestor-to-extant-eukaryote lengths, which was negative
log-transformed for visualization.
Assignment of GO cellular components to ancestral PhROGs. We annotated each PhROG in the focal ancestor with Gene Ontology
(GO) cellular component terms based on the literature/curation-based GO annotations of its extant descendants. First, we retrieved
the GO term(s) associated with each protein in an ancestral PhROG, if any. We then collapsed GO cellular component terms to a

curated set of higher-level ‘‘parent’’ terms based on the GO hierarchy. For each PhROG, we then quantified the support for each
parent GO term while down-weighting redundant proteins from the same species to account for annotation bias towards well-studied
species. Specifically, in each PhROG, we assigned each protein a weight of 1 divided by the number of unique proteins with a GO
annotation from that species. For each parent GO term within a PhROG, we summed these weights and normalized by the number of
species represented in the PhROG, yielding a species-weighted fraction of proteins annotated with that GO term. Parent GO terms
with normalized weight ≥ 0.5 (i.e. supported by the equivalent of at least half of the species in the PhROG) were assigned to that
PhROG. Following this procedure, each PhROG in the focal ancestor was assigned to zero, one, or more GO terms. These assignments were used to assess the relative origination time of GO cellular components.
Assignment of mitochondrial pathways to ancestral PhROGs. We annotated each PhROG in the focal ancestor with mitochondrial
pathways by leveraging human MitoPathway annotations. Each ancestral PhROG was linked to the set of MitoPathways that were
associated with its human members. Because some PhROGs lacked a direct pathway annotation—for example, when a human homolog was absent or lost in evolution—we implemented an imputation step based on pathway consistency among descendant nodes. For each ‘‘parent’’ PhROG at the Eukaryota level, we calculated the fraction of its ‘‘child’’ PhROGs at the LECA level that were
annotated with each MitoPathway. Parent PhROGs were assigned a MitoPathway if its annotated children all belonged to the same
pathway. Child PhROGs that lacked a direct pathway annotation inherited the imputed pathway(s) of their parent. Following this procedure, each PhROG in the focal ancestor was assigned to zero, one, or more mitochondrial pathways. These assignments were
used to assess the relative origination time of mitochondrial pathways.
Phylogenetic analysis under alternative species tree topologies
To account for uncertainty in the topology and root of the eukaryotic tree, we repeated the phylogenetic analysis under five alternative
species tree topologies that span a set of alternative roots and arrangements of the eukaryotic supergroups (Opimoda, Diaphoretickes, Discoba, and Metamonada). Using the procedures described above, we inferred a species phylogeny for each alternative
topology (enforced by a guide tree), then used this inferred species phylogeny to infer phylogenetically-resolved orthogroups,
perform ancestral reconstruction, and infer acquisition timings and HGT. The default topology used for the main analyses is referred
to as the primary species tree topology (number 1), and the five alternative topologies are numbered 2–6.
Computational environment
Analyses were performed across three computational platforms: 1) a Univa Grid Engine (UGER)-managed HPC cluster at the Broad
Institute for parallelizable workflows, 2) Google Cloud Compute Engine for computationally-intensive phylogenetic inference and machine learning, and 3) local workstations for data preprocessing and visualization. Analysis scripts are provided in the GitHub code
repository.
QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.3.1). For branch length timing analysis, all-vs-all pairwise Wilcoxon rank sum tests with
continuity correction and Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing were used to assess significance between groups.
