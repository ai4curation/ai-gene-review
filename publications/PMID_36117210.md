NMDA receptors have essential roles in the physiology of central excitatory
synapses and their dysfunction causes severe neuropsychiatric symptoms.
Recently, a series of genetic variants have been identified in patients,
however, functional information about these variants is sparse and their role in
pathogenesis insufficiently known. Here we investigate the mechanism by which
two GluN2A variants may be pathogenic. We use molecular dynamics simulation and
single-molecule electrophysiology to examine the contribution of GluN2A
subunit-residues, P552 and F652, and their pathogenic substitutions, P552R and
F652V, affect receptor functions. We found that P552 and F652 interact during
the receptors' normal activity cycle; the interaction stabilizes receptors in
open conformations and is required for a normal electrical response. Engineering
shorter side-chains at these positions (P552A and/or F652V) caused a loss of
interaction energy and produced receptors with severe gating, conductance, and
permeability deficits. In contrast, the P552R side chain resulted in stronger
interaction and produced a distinct, yet still drastically abnormal electrical
response. These results identify the dynamic contact between P552 and F652 as a
critical step in the NMDA receptor activation, and show that both increased and
reduced communication through this interaction cause dysfunction. Results show
that subtle differences in NMDA receptor primary structure can generate complex
phenotypic alterations whose binary classification is too simplistic to serve as
a therapeutic guide.

Introduction

N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptors are glutamate-gated excitatory channels critical for the normal development and function of the nervous system. They are principal mediators of synaptic formation, maturation, and plasticity throughout the lifespan. Both their insufficient and excessive activation are known to cause severe neuropathology. Recently, gene sequencing in patients with neuropsychiatric disorders identified numerous variants in GRIN genes encoding NMDA receptor subunits1–7. To help patient stratification and therapy development, several publicly-available databases centralize information on the increasing number of reported variants8 making apparent several challenges that obscure understanding disease mechanism.

First, rather than clustering to specific genetic/structural regions, variants span the entirety of all seven NMDA receptor subunits. Second, correlating receptor primary structure with function remains elusive. Lastly, how NMDA receptor responses affect physiology and which biophysical properties are important at a particular time and place is only superficially understood. With >4,000 human GRIN variants identified, half have no reported clinical phenotype and are classified as “benign.” The remainder display “mild” to “severe” phenotypes including developmental delay, epilepsy, schizophrenia, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorders, attention‐deficit hyperactivity disorder, visual impairment, hypotonia, speech disorders, movement disorders, and microcephaly9–11. This pleiotropy partly reflects the receptor’s diverse contributions to synaptic function and complex relationship between primary structure and healthy operation.

NMDA receptors are obligate heterotetramers assembling from two obligatory GluN1 subunits encoded by GRIN1, and a collection of two GluN2 or GluN3 subunits encoded by GRIN2A-D and GRIN3A-B, respectively. GluN1 subunits express ubiquitously across brain regions and development. Animals lacking GluN1 perish at birth from respiratory failure12, 13. GluN2 and GluN3 subunit expression is developmentally controlled. Animals lacking these subunits have severe, non-lethal phenotypes14. Tetrameric NMDA receptors are large (~4,500 residues) transmembrane proteins. About two-thirds is extracellular and organized into two layers each consisting of four globular domains. The membrane-distal N-terminal layer forms modulator-binding sites but is dispensable to agonist-dependent activation15, 16. The membrane-proximal ligand-binding layer consists of four globular domains containing binding sites for the physiologic co-agonists, glutamate and glycine. Agonists stabilize more compact domain conformations and increase mechanical tension of three short linkers connecting each ligand-binding domain to one of three transmembrane helices (M1, M3, and M4). Together, with a short M2 P-loop helix, these transmembrane helices surround the cation-permeable pore with residues on the M3 helix forming the gate. The ligand-binding (LBD) and transmembrane domains (TMD) together with the linkers connecting them form the core NMDA receptor channel necessary and sufficient for function. The cytoplasmic domain is large representing one-third of receptor mass. While dispensable for activation17, animals lacking this domain are non-viable18.

Fewer than half of pathogenic variants have been functionally examined, and even fewer have a proposed mechanism1, 5, 6, 19–29. This is partly because the activation mechanism is insufficiently understood30, 31 making correlations between in vitro and in vivo behavior difficult32, 33. To begin to explain how mutations alter receptor function and guide pharmacological restoration, it is necessary to outline how individual residues contribute to the receptor’s normal operation. We used molecular dynamics simulations to identify residue pairs whose interaction strength changes with activation. Among these, several corresponded to sites of pathogenic variation34. Specifically, GluN2A-F652 contacted, in a state-dependent manner, GluN2A-P552 (both sites of known disease variants) and GluN1-R801. We hypothesized that mutations at GluN2A-F652 or GluN2A-P552 work through the same mechanism. Here, we report direct coupling between F652 and P552 during receptor opening and show that mutations at these sites exhibit substantially different phenotypes despite operating through a conserved structural mechanism not predicted by functional characterization.

ResultsIntrasubunit coupling between GluN2A D1-M1 linker and M3 helixWe previously used targeted-MD simulations of the core GluN1/GluN2A construct (N1ΔNΔC/N2AΔNΔC) to identify side-chain pairs predicted to undergo state-specific interactions during activation34 (Figure 1a).
