# STING mediates lysosomal quality control and recovery through its proton channel function and TFEB activation in lysosomal storage disorders.

Lysosomes are essential organelles for cellular homeostasis. Defective lysosomes
are associated with diseases like lysosomal storage disorders (LSDs). How
lysosomal defects are detected and lysosomal function restored remain
incompletely understood. Here, we show that STING mediates a neuroinflammatory
gene signature in three distinct LSD mouse models, Galctwi/twi, Ppt1-/-, and
Cln7-/-. Transcriptomic analysis of Galctwi/twi mouse brain tissue revealed that
STING also mediates the expression of lysosomal genes that are regulated by
transcriptional factor EB (TFEB). Immunohistochemical and single-nucleus
RNA-sequencing (snRNA-seq) analysis show that STING regulates lysosomal gene
expression in microglia. Mechanistically, we show that STING activation leads to
TFEB dephosphorylation, nuclear translocation, and expression of lysosomal
genes. This process requires STING's proton channel function, the
V-ATPase-ATG5-ATG8 cascade, and is independent of immune signaling. Furthermore,
we show that the STING-TFEB axis facilitates lysosomal repair. Together, our
data identify STING-TFEB as a lysosomal quality control mechanism that responds
to lysosomal dysfunction.

INTRODUCTION

Lysosomes are cellular hubs for substrate and organelle turnover. Lysosomal dysfunction is associated with over 70 monogenic diseases, collectively referred to as lysosomal storage disorders (LSDs) 1. LSD can be caused by genetic mutations in lysosomal hydrolases, ion channels, membrane proteins, and other lysosomal proteins, often leading to the accumulation of undegraded substrates that obstruct lysosomal function. The clinical manifestation of LSD is heterogenous but often involves early-onset neurodegeneration and varying degrees of inflammation 2. Lysosomal dysregulation is also more broadly associated with other neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, familial amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD) 3.

We recently showed that STING, an adaptor protein in the DNA sensing pathway of the innate immune system, is continuously transported to the lysosome for degradation at homeostasis and after ligand activation 4,5. Disruptions of endolysosomal vesicular trafficking or lysosomal dysfunction activate tonic STING signaling. For instance, in Niemann-Pick disease type C1 (NPC1), loss of NPC1 and the resulting lysosomal defects impair STING degradation, leading to STING signaling activation that drives neuroinflammation and neuropathology 6. Similarly, STING degradation by the lysosome is impaired in cells lacking VPS13C (Vacuolar Protein Sorting 13 Homolog C), which is associated with familial early-onset Parkinson’s disease 7,8. C9ORF72, a commonly mutated gene associated with ALS, is essential for the autophagylysosome pathway at multiple levels 9–13; C9orf72-deficiency impairs lysosomal degradation of STING, leading to STING-mediated inflammation in mice 14. In addition, lysosomal defects can also lead to accumulation of damaged mitochondria which release mitochondrial DNA that activates cGAS-STING signaling 15,16.

By and large, STING-mediated inflammation due to lysosomal dysfunction is pathogenic. Innate immune signaling is often a “double-edged sword” that protects the host from infection but can also cause tissue damage. However, it is still puzzling why the cell connects lysosomal dysregulation to inflammation if the only outcome was immunopathology and demise to the organism. We wondered if there is any benefit for the cell to active STING when lysosomes become dysfunctional? Two recent studies showed that STING signaling induces lysosomal biogenesis 17,18. Here, we show that STING drives the expression of lysosomal genes through its proton channel function and the transcription factor EB (TFEB) in microglia of mouse brain with lysosomal storage disease. We further show that STING-mediated TFEB activation is a branch of STING effector function independent of its immune signaling and occurs in monogenic lysosomal storage disorders and facilitates lysosomal repair after acute damage. Our data identify STING-TFEB as a previously unappreciated lysosomal quality control and recovery mechanism that potentially benefit the cell.

RESULTSSTING drives a transcriptional program of lysosomal biogenesis in Krabbe diseaseThe Galctwi/twi mouse is a model of Krabbe disease, a lysosomal storage disorder, caused by Galactosylceramidase (GalC) loss-of-function. Galctwi/twi mice develop severe motor disease very early in life (28 days) and have an average life span of around 42 days 19,20. To determine the role of STING in Krabbe disease, we crossed Sting−/− with Galctwi/twi mice and generated cohorts of wild-type, Galctwi/twi, and Galctwi/twiSting−/− mice.At 32 days of age, Galctwi/twi mice exhibited a significant increase in the expression of type I interferon (IFN-I) and IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) measured by RT-qPCR in both the cerebrum and the cerebellum (e.g., Ifnb, Cxcl10, Isg15) which was substantially reduced in Galctwi/twiSting−/− mice (Figure S1A–D). Expression of inflammatory genes (e.g., Tnf, Il1b, Il6) and chemokine genes (e.g., Ccl2, Ccl3, Ccl4) were also drastically increased in GalcTwi/Twi mice compared to wild-type and heterozygous littermate controls; These genes were partially rescued in Galctwi/twiSting−/− mice (Figure S1A–D). Increased expression of glial genes (e.g., Gfap, Trem2, Clec7a) in Galctwi/twi mouse brain were also reduced in Galctwi/twiSting−/− mice (Figure S1A–D). We also analyzed spleens of these mice and did not observe any changes in ISG or inflammatory gene expression, suggesting a lack of systemic inflammation or STING activation in the periphery (Figure S1E).To further discover signaling pathways that are dependent on STING in the settings of lysosomal storage disorder, we performed bulk RNA-seq using the cerebrum of wild-type, Galctwi/twi, and Galctwi/twiSting−/− mice. Principle component analysis showed distinct clusters of samples from each genotype; Galctwi/twiSting−/− samples located between wild-type and Galctwi/twi samples, suggesting a partial rescue of the overall transcriptome landscape (Figure 1A). We first compared Galctwi/twi to wild-type to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with Krabbe disease. We observed 670 upregulated DEGs and 332 downregulated DEGs (Figure 1B). The majority of the upregulated DEGs are ISGs (1.8-248.8 fold increase, −log10(adjusted p-value)=1.2-176.8) (Figure 1B). The majority of the downregulated DEGs are less than 2-fold, which we did not analyze further. Ingenuity Pathway analysis (IPA) of DEGs enriched in Galctwi/twi compared to wild-type revealed IFN Signaling Pathways, Neuroinflammation Signaling Pathway and the CLEAR (Coordinated Lysosomal Expression and Regulation) Signaling Pathway amongst others (Figure 1C). The CLEAR pathway genes contain a palindromic 10-base site can be found in the promoters of lysosomal genes, which was induced 1.2-27.4 fold in Galctwi/twi compared to wild-type (Figure 1B, −log10(adjusted p-value)=1.43-141.4). TFEB is a key regulator of the CLEAR gene network 21.We next compared Galctwi/twiSting−/− to Galctwi/twi to identify STING-dependent genes. We observed 219 downregulated DEGs and 92 upregulated DEGs (Figure 1D). IPA analysis of these STING-dependent DEGs again identified IFN Signaling and Neuroinflammation Signaling pathways (Figure 1E–1G), which was consistent with RT-qPCR findings. Unexpectedly, lysosomal genes within the CLEAR gene network were also dependent on STING (Figure 1E). To further confirm this, we analyzed a broader panel of TFEB-target genes (based on literature 22,23) in the RNA-seq data and found that a significant number of TFEB-target genes were increased in Galctwi/twi in a STING-dependent manner (Figure 1H). We further validated expression of these lysosomal genes by RT-qPCR with additional mice (Figure S1F). Of note, we did not observe any change in the expression of Tfeb and Tfe3 mRNA across all three genotypes (Figure 1I). Together, our transcriptomic analysis suggest that STING mediates immune gene and lysosomal gene expression in the mouse model of Krabbe disease.STING plays a role in multiple lysosomal storage disordersWe next stained the whole brain of wild-type, Galctwi/twi, and Galctwi/twiSting−/− with IBA1 (microglia) and GFAP (astrocytes). Galctwi/twi mouse brain at 32 days old presented with severe microgliosis and astrogliosis in the brain, and we observed substantial improvement of both in Galctwi/twiSting−/− mice compared to Galctwi/twi mice (Figure S2A–D). However, the overall survival and body weight was not improved in Galctwi/twiSting−/− mice compared to Galctwi/twi mice (Figure S2E,2F). Interestingly, we also generated Galctwi/twiIrf3−/− mice that showed a small but significant improvement in survival compared to Galctwi/twi mice (from 44 days to 54 days, p<0.0001), although Galctwi/twiIrf3−/− did not rescue body weight. These data suggest that despite playing a role in the development of neuroinflammation, Sting−/− is not sufficient to rescue survival of Krabbe disease mouse.We also crossed Sting−/− mice with two other mouse models of lysosomal storage disorder. The Ppt1−/− mouse is a model of PPT1 (Palmitoyl-protein thioesterase 1)-deficiency caused by loss of function of a lysosomal thioesterase that removes palmitate from cysteine residue on proteins. Ppt1−/− mice develop mild motor disease around 4 months of age that progress to severe motor disease and paralysis around 7 months of age 24. We generated wild-type, Ppt1−/− and Ppt1−/−Sting−/− mice. At 2 months of age (before motor disease onset) we detected increased IFN/ISG signature in Ppt1−/− cerebrum compared to littermate wild-type mice (Figure S3A). At 6 months of age (after motor disease onset), Ppt1−/− mice showed a stronger increase in IFN/ISG, inflammatory and glial gene signatures in the cerebrum (Figure S3B, 3C). Importantly, Ppt1−/−Sting−/− mice showed decreased IFN/ISG, inflammatory and glial gene signatures compared to Ppt1−/− mice, suggesting a role for STING in mediating immune signaling in Ppt1−/− mice (Figure S3A–C).The Cln7−/− mouse is a model of CLN7-deficiency caused by loss of function of the lysosomal chloride channel. Cln7−/− mice develop motor disease around 6 months of age and mortality around 10 months of age 25,26. At 6 months of age (around motor disease onset), Cln7−/− mice exhibited modest but significant increase in immune gene signatures in the cerebrum, which were reduced in Cln7−/−Sting−/− mice (Figure S3D, 3E). Together, these data suggest that despite GALC, PPT1, and CLN7 having distinct functions in the lysosome, each gene knockout in mice resulted in a similar neuroinflammatory gene signature that is at least partially dependent on STING. We will focus on the Galctwi/twi mouse for further studies below because it develops the most severe disease of the three models with the strongest STING-dependent gene expression.STING drives lysosomal gene expression in microgliaSTING is highly expressed in microglia in the brain 27. To examine whether STING drives lysosomal gene expression in microglia, we first stained wild-type, Galctwi/twi, and Galctwi/twiSting−/− mouse brain tissue with antibodies against IBA1 and CTSD (a marker for lysosomes). We found that the CTSD protein level was significantly increased in Galctwi/twi microglia compared to wild type microglia, and this induction was significantly reduced in Galctwi/twiSting−/− microglia compared to Galctwi/twi microglia (Figure 2A,2B). Interestingly, in IBA1-negative cells which are mostly neurons, CTSD protein level was also significantly increased in the Galctwi/twi cerebral cortex, but this induction was not significantly reduced in the Galctwi/twiSting−/− cortex (Figure S4). This is consistent with low or no STING expression in most neurons. Together, these data suggest STING drives lysosomal gene expression in Galctwi/twi microglia.We next sought to corroborate this finding using another severe lysosomal storage disorder mouse model Npc1−/−. We have previously shown that STING plays a critical role in the development of neuroinflammation and neuropathology in Npc1−/− mice 6. We isolated and flash froze cerebellum from 8-weeks-old wild-type, Npc1−/−, and Npc1−/−Sting1−/− mice after transcardial perfusions with ice-cold PBS. We then prepared single nuclei (sn) RNA libraries using the 10x Genomics platform followed by NovaSeq sequencing. Subsequent data analysis and clustering revealed 17 known cell types based on published marker genes, including a distinct population of microglia marked by unique expression of Csf1r and Cx3cr1 (Figure 2C–E). In the microglia cluster, we found that many ISGs were induced in Npc1−/− microglia compared to wild-type microglia and these ISGs were decreased in Npc1−/−Sting−/− microglia compared Npc1−/− microglia, consistent the known function of STING in mediating IFN-I signaling (Figure 2F,2G). Remarkably, we also found that many lysosomal genes induced in Npc1−/− microglia were also decreased in Npc1−/−Sting−/− microglia (Figure 2H,2I). Together, these IHC and snRNA-seq data from two distinct mouse models suggest that STING drives lysosomal gene expression in microglia in lysosomal storage disorders.STING induces TFEB activation and lysosomal gene expressionWe next investigated the role of STING in TFEB-mediated lysosomal gene expression. We first stimulated wild-type and Sting−/− mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) with a mouse-specific STING agonist DMXAA. We also stimulated human foreskin fibroblasts BJ1 wild-type and STINGKO cells with another small molecule STING agonist diABZI. We performed bulk RNA-seq and selected 40 TFEB target genes, expressed in both MEFs and BJ1 cells, and are closely associated with lysosomal function. A majority of these TFEB target genes are induced by STING agonist treatment up to 2.1-fold in wild-type MEFs and up to 3.4-fold in wild-type BJ1 cells (Figure 3A–D). The extent of these fold changes was similar to published reports of TFEB-mediated lysosomal gene expression 28,29. We did not observe any induction of lysosomal genes in Sting−/− or STINGKO cells. Of note, Sting−/− MEFs showed reduced lysosomal gene expression at baseline compared to wild-type MEF; this is likely due to unknown differences in genetic background of mice where these MEFs were isolated from, because we did not observe such a difference in CRISPR-generated wild-type and STINGKO BJ1 cells derived from the same parental cell line (Figure 3C,3D). Lysosomal genes that were strongly induced by STING include: CTSS, NPC1, NPC2, GRN, GLA, GNS, GRS, GPNMB, HPSE, CLCN5, LAMP2, CD68, SCARB2, TMEM192.We next analyzed whether STING activation can lead to TFEB activation. TFEB activation requires dephosphorylation in the cytoplasm and translocation to the nucleus to promote the expression of lysosomal genes. STING agonist DMXAA treatment induced pSTING, pTBK1 and LC3 lipidation only in wild-type MEFs as expected (Figure 3E). We also observed a downward mobility shift of TFEB after DMXAA stimulation in wild-type but not Sting−/− MEFs (Figure 3E). This mobility shift of TFEB is commonly used as an indicator of TFEB dephosphorylation and activation 30–32. In BJ1 cells, STING activation led to a more drastic mobility shift of TFEB in wild-type cells but not in STINGKO cells (Figure 3F). This is consistent with stronger induction of lysosomal gene expression in BJ1 cells observed by RNA-seq compared to MEFs (Figure 3C,3D). Interestingly, a small molecule C53 that blocks human STING proton channel 33,34 also blocked diABZI-induced TFEB dephosphorylation as well as LC3 and GABARAP lipidation (both are ATG8 proteins, Figure 3F), suggesting a potential involvement of STING proton channel function in TFEB activation (more below). We also stimulated wild-type and STINGKO BJ1 cells with the natural STING ligand 2’3’-cGAMP, and we observed a similar mobility shift of TFEB only in wild-type cells (Figure 3G). Of note, acute STING activation by agonists led to reduced TFEB protein and mRNA levels; this was moderate in MEFs and more pronounced in BJ1 cells (Figure 3H).We next analyzed TFEB translocation to the nucleus. α-TFEB antibody staining of endogenous TFEB does not produce a strong enough signal for microscopy. Instead, we inserted a 6xMyc tag in-frame at the endogenous Tfeb gene locus to generate the Tfeb-6xMyc MEFs for microscopy with a α-Myc-tag antibody. TFEB is defused in the cytoplasm in wild-type Tfeb-6xMyc MEFs at steady state. After STING agonist diABZI stimulation, we observed rapid translocation of TFEB into the nucleus in as soon as 30 minutes (Figure 3I). This TFEB translocation was blocked by STING inhibitor H-151, but not by TBK1 inhibitor BX795 or NF-κB inhibitor BAY11-7082 (Figure 3I,3J). Together, these data suggest that endogenous STING activation leads TFEB dephosphorylation, nuclear translocation, and induction of lysosomal gene expression. Further, STING-mediated TFEB activation is likely independent of immune signaling but requires the proton channel function.STING-mediated TFEB activation is independent of immune signalingWe next examined the mechanism of STING-mediated TFEB activation. We first generated StingKO Tfeb-6xMyc MEFs by CRISPR (we call StingKO MEFs), and then reconstituted with wild-type human STING (StingKO/hSTING). This will allow us to eliminate the potential unknown background concern between wild-type and Sting−/− MEFs, and more importantly we will be able to perform both biochemical and microscopy analysis of TFEB activation in the same cell. The reason we use human STING for reconstitution is because C53 only inhibits the human STING proton channel but not mouse STING. We also reconstituted StingKO Tfeb-6xMyc MEFs with the hSTING-S366A mutant (StingKO/hSTING-S366A) that eliminates STING-mediated IFN-I signaling but retains all IFN-independent activities 35 as well as the hSTING-ΔCTT mutant (StingKO/hSTING-ΔCTT) that eliminates all STING-mediated immune signaling including IFN-I and NF-κB but retains non-canonical autophagy function and ER-Golgi-lysosome trafficking 36.We analyzed STING activation of immune signaling by Western blot and TFEB activation using both Western blot and microscopy. diABZI treatment of StingKO/hSTING MEFs led to robust LC3 lipidation as well as phosphorylation of STING, TBK1 and IRF3 (Figure 4A). diABZI treatment also induced substantial TFEB dephosphorylation and translocation into the nucleus, indicating TFEB activation (Figure 4A,4B). StingKO/hSTING-S366A cells showed no pSTING and pIRF3 and StingKO/hSTING-ΔCTT cells showed no pSTING, pTBK1 and pIRF3, confirming that these two STING mutants do not activate immune signaling (Figure 4A). Remarkably, both STING mutants still induced LC3 lipidation and TFEB dephosphorylation as well as TFEB nuclear translocation after diABZI treatment (Figure 4A,4B). In addition, RT-qPCR analysis showed that diABZI treatment induced expression of ISGs (e.g., Ifit3) in StingKO/hSTING cells and not in StingKO/hSTING-S366A cells; In contrast, diABZI treatment induced lysosomal genes (e.g., Gla, Gpnmb, Grn) in both cells (Figure S5A–E). These data collectively support that STING-mediated TFEB-activation is independent of immune signaling.Two recent studies showed that the transmembrane domain of STING acts as a proton channel, which can be inhibited by C53 33,34. STING activation and localization to the Golgi and post-Golgi vesicles including lysosomes increased the pH levels of these organelles leading to LC3 lipidation and non-canonical autophagy 33,34. To determine whether the proton channel function is required for STING-mediated TFEB activation, we stimulated StingKO/hSTING MEFs with either diABZI alone or in combination with C53. Remarkably, C53 blocked diABZI-induced LC3 lipidation as well as TFEB dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation (Figure 4C–E). These data suggest that STING-mediated TFEB activation is independent of IFN signaling and requires the proton channel function of STING.STING-mediated TFEB activation requires V-ATPase-ATG5-ATG8Previous studies showed that TFEB can be activated by the V-ATPase-ATG8 pathway; pH changes in the lysosome activates V-ATPase which then activates ATG8 lipidation, or the conjugation of ATG8 to single membranes (CASM) pathway, leading to TFEB activation 31,32,37,38. STING-mediated LC3 lipidation also requires V-ATPase 39. In our in vitro studies with MEFs and BJ1 cells, we consistently observed that STING agonist treatment induced both LC3 and GABARAP lipidation (both are ATG8 family proteins) as well as TFEB activation, and this is dependent on STING proton channel at least in BJ1 cells (Figure 3F). Then, we treated these cells with mock or DMXAA for 4 h. We found that both SopF and RavZ inhibited STING-mediated LC3 and GABARAP lipidation as well as TFEB dephosphorylation, whereas SopF-Y224D and RavZ-C258A had no effect (Figure 4F). The ATG16L1-ATG5-ATG12 complex is required for V-ATPase-mediated ATG8 lipidation 42. To probe the mechanism further, we next knocked down ATG5 with siRNA in wild-type BJ1 cells, then stimulated with diABZI. ATG5 knockdown also reduced STING-mediated LC3 lipidation and TFEB dephosphorylation (Figure 4G). Together, these data suggest that STING-mediated TFEB activation requires the V-ATPase-ATG5-ATG8 signaling cascade.STING facilitates lysosomal repair and recoveryOur data thus far showed that TFEB activation is a functional branch of STING that occurs at least in the settings of monogenic lysosomal storage disorders and endogenous STING activation by an agonist. We next explored whether STING also plays a role in lysosomal repair and recovery which would demonstrate a functional benefit to the cell. l-Leucyl-L-leucine methyl ester (LLOME) is a commonly used lysosomal damaging agent for studying lysosomal repair 43,44. We first compared StingWT and StingKO cells using a common protocol 45 that includes LLOME treatment for 2 hours followed by a washout and 2 hours recovery (Figure 5A). After 2 hours recovery, we stained cells for Galectin-3 (Gal3) puncta, a well-established marker of damaged lysosome, and analyzed by confocal microscopy. We analyzed two independent clones of StingKO cells and both showed significantly higher Gal3 staining compared to StingWT, indicating that the cell accumulates more damaged lysosomes when STING is lost (Figure 5B, 5C).We next compared StingKO to StingKO/hSTING cells. We included a dose curve of LLOME but maintained the same treatment regimen (LLOME treatment for 2 hours followed by a washout then image 2 hours later) (Figure 5D). We found that increasing concentrations of LLOME induced a dose-dependent increase in Gal3 staining in both StingKO and StingKO/hSTING cells; However, StingKO/hSTING cells exhibited reduced Gal3 staining at higher LLOME doses (Figure 5E, 5F). We also co-stained cells with Lamp1 to confirm that Gal3-positive puncta are indeed lysosomes (Figure 5E). These data suggest that STING facilitates lysosomal repair after acute damage.We next performed a time course study of lysosomal repair after LLOME treatment (Figure 5G). We treated cells with vehicle or LLOME for 2 hours, followed by a washout, and then imaged cells at 2, 4, 6, 8 hours later. Vehicle-treated StingKO cells did not show any Gal3 puncta at any of the time points (Figure 5H showing 2 h as an example). After LLOME treatment, Gal3 staining peaked at 2 h after washout and then the Gal3 signal gradually decreased reflecting the process of lysosomal repair, which was completed by 8 h in StingKO cells (Figure 5I,5J). We also stained cells for ALIX, which is an early responder of lysosomal repair 43. Alix signal was also the highest at 2 hours after washout which was more quickly resolved by 6 h compared to Gal3 (Figure 5C, E). In contrast, StingKO/hSTING cells showed faster lysosomal repair compared to StingKO cells as measured by significantly lower Gal3 staining at all time points and Alix staining at 2 h (Figure 5J,5K). We also tried to analyze the onset of lysosomal damage during the 2 h period of LLOME treatment before washout and did not observe any difference between StingKO cells and StingKO/hSTING cells (Figure S6A). Together, we conclude that STING facilitates lysosomal repair after acute chemical-induced damage.STING-mediated lysosomal repair requires the proton channel function and TFEBAs we and other have shown previously, either lysosomal dysfunction or blocking STING trafficking to the lysosome results in STING protein accumulation, oligomerization, and activation of downstream signaling 5,6. To examine whether STING is activated by lysosomal damage, we treated StingKO/hSTING MEFs with 2 mM LLOME for 2 h and then let them recover for 0-4 h. As a comparison, we treated separate StingKO/hSTING MEFs with a low dose range of STING agonist diABZI (2-50 nM) for 2 h to produce a gradient of STING dimer (inactive) to oligomer (active) transition (Figure S6B). SDS-PAGE analysis of denatured protein lysates showed that LLOME-treated cells showed higher levels of total STING protein compared to vehicle- or diABZI-treated cells (Figure S6B, bottom STING/Tubulin ratio). Native PAGE analysis of non-denatured protein lysate showed that LLOME-treatment led to detectable STING oligomerization as early as 2 h after treatment (0 h recovery), which continues to increase during the 4 h recovery phase. Of note, the banding pattern of STING oligomers resulting from LLOME treatment were somewhat different from those in diABZI treatment, indicating different sizes of STING oligomers. To further demonstrate that STING overexpression alone can activate downstream signaling, we expressed STING in HEK293T/IFN-Luc cells and observed modest but significant increase in luciferase activity (Figure S6C). These data suggest that LLOME-induced lysosomal damage results in STING protein accumulation and oligomerization.We next examined the mechanism of STING in lysosomal damage and repair. We treated StingKO and StingKO/hSTING cells with C53 concurrent with LLOME washout to block STING proton channel function during the subsequent repair and recovery. We analyzed STING activation of immune signaling by Western blot and TFEB activation using both Western blot and microscopy. diABZI treatment of StingKO/hSTING MEFs led to robust LC3 lipidation as well as phosphorylation of STING, TBK1 and IRF3 (Figure 4A). diABZI treatment also induced substantial TFEB dephosphorylation and translocation into the nucleus, indicating TFEB activation (Figure 4A,4B). StingKO/hSTING-S366A cells showed no pSTING and pIRF3 and StingKO/hSTING-ΔCTT cells showed no pSTING, pTBK1 and pIRF3, confirming that these two STING mutants do not activate immune signaling (Figure 4A). Remarkably, both STING mutants still induced LC3 lipidation and TFEB dephosphorylation as well as TFEB nuclear translocation after diABZI treatment (Figure 4A,4B). In addition, RT-qPCR analysis showed that diABZI treatment induced expression of ISGs (e.g., Ifit3) in StingKO/hSTING cells and not in StingKO/hSTING-S366A cells; In contrast, diABZI treatment induced lysosomal genes (e.g., Gla, Gpnmb, Grn) in both cells (Figure S5A–E). These data collectively support that STING-mediated TFEB-activation is independent of immune signaling.

Two recent studies showed that the transmembrane domain of STING acts as a proton channel, which can be inhibited by C53 33,34. STING activation and localization to the Golgi and post-Golgi vesicles including lysosomes increased the pH levels of these organelles leading to LC3 lipidation and non-canonical autophagy 33,34. To determine whether the proton channel function is required for STING-mediated TFEB activation, we stimulated StingKO/hSTING MEFs with either diABZI alone or in combination with C53. Remarkably, C53 blocked diABZI-induced LC3 lipidation as well as TFEB dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation (Figure 4C–E). These data suggest that STING-mediated TFEB activation is independent of IFN signaling and requires the proton channel function of STING.

STING-mediated LC3 lipidation also requires V-ATPase 39. In our in vitro studies with MEFs and BJ1 cells, we consistently observed that STING agonist treatment induced both LC3 and GABARAP lipidation (both are ATG8 family proteins) as well as TFEB activation, and this is dependent on STING proton channel at least in BJ1 cells (Figure 3F). Vehicle-treated StingKO cells did not show any Gal3 puncta at any of the time points (Figure 5H showing 2 h as an example). After LLOME treatment, Gal3 staining peaked at 2 h after washout and then the Gal3 signal gradually decreased reflecting the process of lysosomal repair, which was completed by 8 h in StingKO cells (Figure 5I,5J). DISCUSSIONLysosomes are essential cellular organelles for removal, recycling and reuse of cellular contents 46. Dysregulated lysosomes are associated with a wide range of human diseases 3. Given their importance, multiple quality control mechanisms exist for lysosomes, including autophagic turnover of lysosome (lysophage), lysosomal membrane repair by the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT), and the phosphoinositide-initiated membrane tethering and lipid transport (PITT) pathway 43,44,47,48. Here, we identify STING-mediated TFEB activation as a lysosomal quality control mechanism. This function of STING is detected in microglia of mice with monogenic lysosomal storage disorders, in fibroblasts treated with various STING agonists, and in cells recovering from chemical-induced lysosomal damage.We propose STING as an immunosensor of lysosomal activity, which is activated by lysosomal defects and then mediates lysosomal recovery or “bounce-back”. This resembles the “bounce-back” mechanism of the proteasome, which is another critical degradation machinery of the cell. In the case of proteasome, the transcription factor NRF1 is constantly degraded by the proteasome at the resting state 49. During proteotoxic stress, NRF1 accumulates in the cell and then drives the expression of proteasome subunit genes to produce more proteasomes. A similar “bounce-back” mechanism was described for the immunoproteasomes; Genetic defects of the immunoproteasome lead to accumulation of an intracellular cytokine IL-24, which then activates protein kinase R (PKR) 50. PKR induces NF-κB-mediated inflammation and inhibits protein translation through pEF4E, the latter potentially helping to reduce the protein load and to recover from the proteasomal defect. Therefore, continuous STING degradation by the lysosome at homeostasis potentially serves as a probe for lysosomal activity, which can detect lysosomal defects and then mediates inflammation as well as TFEB-mediated lysosomal gene expression to support lysosomal “bounce-back”. This mechanism is helpful when the cell experiences acute lysosomal stress or damage that is reversible, such as those modeled in the LLOME treatment and recovery studies. However, when the lysosomal defect is irreversible, such as those in the LSDs, STING signaling becomes chronic and induces neuroinflammation.We show that the dual activity of STING in response to lysosomal defects is mediated by different functional domains of the protein (see Graphical Abstract). IFN and inflammatory signaling is mediated by the C-terminal tail (CTT) 51 and we show here that TFEB activation is mediated by the N-terminal transmembrane (TM) domain acting as a proton channel. Mechanistically, we show that STING-mediated TFEB activation also requires the V-ATPase-ATG5-ATG8 cascade. A recently published study also found that STING-mediated TFEB activation is independent TBK1 and requires GABRAP lipidation 17. Each measurement is recorded on a scale of 0–3, with a combined total of 0–12. In brief, in the ledge test, mice were placed on the cage’s ledge. Walking along the ledge without losing balance will score 0; occasionally losing footing but otherwise coordinated will score 1; unable to use hind legs or landing on head rather than paws during descent will score 2; unable to move on the ledge will score 3. In the hindlimb clasping test, mice were lifted by the tail and observed for 10 s. Hindlimbs consistently extended will score 0; one hindlimb retracted for more than 50% of the time will score 1; both hindlimbs retracted more than 50% of the time will score 2; both hindlimbs entirely retracted and touching the abdomen for more than 50% of the time will score 3. In the gait test, mice were placed on a flat surface to observe walking from behind. Mouse body weight supported by all limbs will score 0; limp or tremor while walking will score 1; severe tremor or limp and duck feet will score 2; difficulty moving forward with lowered abdomen will score 3. In the kyphosis test, mice were placed on a flat surface to observe walking from the side. Straightened spine while walking will score 0; mildly bent spine but straightens while walking will score 1; mildly bent spine that does not straighten while walking will score 2; severely bent spine that does not straighten while walking will score 3.

In the kyphosis test, mice were placed on a flat surface to observe walking from the side. Straightened spine while walking will score 0; mildly bent spine but straightens while walking will score 1; mildly bent spine that does not straighten while walking will score 2; severely bent spine that does not straighten while walking will score 3.
