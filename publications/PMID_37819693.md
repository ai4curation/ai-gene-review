The chitin synthase Chs3 is a multipass membrane protein whose trafficking is
tightly controlled. Accordingly, its exit from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)
depends on several complementary mechanisms that ensure its correct folding.
Despite its potential failure on its exit, Chs3 is very stable in this
compartment, which suggests its poor recognition by ER quality control
mechanisms such as endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation (ERAD). Here we
show that proper N-glycosylation of its luminal domain is essential to prevent
the aggregation of the protein and its subsequent recognition by the
Hrd1-dependent ERAD-L machinery. In addition, the interaction of Chs3 with its
chaperone Chs7 seems to mask additional cytosolic degrons, thereby avoiding
their recognition by the ERAD-C pathway. On top of that, Chs3 molecules that are
not degraded by conventional ERAD can move along the ER membrane to reach the
inner nuclear membrane, where they are degraded by the inner nuclear
membrane-associated degradation (INMAD) system, which contributes to the
intracellular homeostasis of Chs3. These results indicate that Chs3 is an
excellent model to study quality control mechanisms in the cell and reinforce
its role as a paradigm in intracellular trafficking research.

INTRODUCTION

Correct protein folding is essential to maintain a functional proteome in the cell. To attain the appropriate conformation, proteins undergo various folding steps and, after this, they must reach the right cellular compartment to carry out their designated function. However, the folding process is inherently error prone and easily affected by genetic mutations or different environmental stresses (reviewed in Guerriero and Brodsky, 2012). Because misfolded species lead to loss-of-function or gain-of-function phenotypes linked to the appearance of a plethora of diseases (Hipp et al., 2014), all organisms have developed highly conserved quality control (QC) mechanisms to monitor protein folding and thus ensure protein homeostasis (Ellgaard et al., 1999). These QC mechanisms essentially retain, refold, or remove misfolded, unassembled, or improperly modified proteins and exist in every cellular compartment.

Given that the ER is the first destination of secretory and transmembrane (TM) proteins after their synthesis by ribosomes, and where protein folding starts, endoplasmic reticulum quality control (ERQC) is the first and most important barrier against misfolding. Within ERQC, the most relevant system to maintain proteostasis is the endoplasmic reticulum–associated degradation (ERAD) system, a branch of the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), which degrades not only misfolded or unassembled proteins, but also different folded substrates with a regulatory function (reviewed in Christianson and Carvalho, 2022). ERAD can be divided into four well-established stages: recognition, ubiquitination, retrotranslocation, and delivery of the substrate to the 26S proteasome for its degradation. Recently, researchers have described the inner nuclear membrane–associated degradation (INMAD) system that follows the same principles of ERAD, but in the inner nuclear membrane (INM), and therefore targets their substrates to the nuclear proteasome (reviewed in Smoyer and Jaspersen, 2019). The ERAD/INMAD system in yeasts is divided into multiple branches/pathways defined by the location of the misfolded signal in the protein (“degron”) that is recognized for degradation. These branches are: ERAD-L, if the degron is oriented to the ER lumen, ERAD-M, if it is in a TM region, or ERAD-C, if the misfolded region in the protein is exposed to the cytosol/nucleoplasm (reviewed in Mehrtash and Hochstrasser, 2019). All ERAD/INMAD branches converge on an ATPase complex (Cdc48 in yeasts and p97/VCP in mammals) that helps to pull ubiquitinated substrates from the ER/IN membranes, allowing their delivery to the proteasome for degradation (reviewed in Krshnan et al., 2022).

The ERAD/INMAD machinery is built around several E3 ubiquitin ligase complexes and, depending on the pathway, they use different additional factors involved in each step of the process. In the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Hrd1, Doa10, and Asi are the most important E3 ubiquitin ligase complexes involved in these processes. The Hrd1 complex, exclusively located in the ER membrane, mediates the ubiquitination of ERAD-M and ERAD-L substrates (Vashist and Ng, 2004; Carvalho et al., 2006). The Doa10 complex, located in both the ER and IN membranes, principally mediates the ubiquitination of ERAD-C substrates (Swanson et al., 2001; Carvalho et al., 2006), although it can also recognize some TM degrons (reviewed in Krshnan et al., 2022). The Asi complex resides in the IN membrane and ubiquitinates proteins with TM defects, which include ERAD-M substrates, mislocalized proteins, or orphan subunits of ER protein complexes that diffuse to the IN membrane, as well as integral membrane proteins with a regulatory function in the nucleus (Foresti et al., 2014; Khmelinskii et al., 2014; Natarajan et al., 2020). In mammals, the scenario is much more complex due to the existence of more than 25 different E3 ubiquitin ligases in the ER, so the different branches of ERAD are not clearly defined.

The best-characterized ERAD substrates are misfolded soluble ERAD-L glycoproteins. Most proteins in the secretory pathway are N-glycosylated in the ER, and this modification serves to monitor the folding status of the protein (reviewed in Xu and Ng, 2015). N-glycosylation occurs at the asparagine residues within the Asn-X-Ser/Thr sequon of a nascent polypeptide (reviewed in Helenius and Aebi, 2004), where the core glycan chain Glc3Man9GlcNAc2 is first added and later modified through a series of sequential reactions to generate Man9GlcNAc2 (Jakob et al., 1998; Hitt and Wolf, 2004). At this point, if the protein is terminally misfolded and a certain time in this state is exceeded, then a mannose trimming process initiated by Mns1 and followed by Htm1 mannosidases ends up yielding a glycan chain with an exposed α-1, 6-linked mannose (Clerc et al., 2009). This glycan is recognized by the lectin Yos9 that targets the substrate protein to the Hrd1 complex to complete its ubiquitination and degradation by the proteasome (Quan et al., 2008). Nonglycosylated proteins with luminal degrons are also targeted by the Hrd1 complex through the Hsp70 chaperone Kar2 assisted by its Hsp40 luminal partners Scj1 and Jem1 (reviewed in Ninagawa et al., 2021). ERAD-M and Asi substrates seem to be recognized directly via TM–TM interactions between the TM degron and a TM domain present in the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex without the necessity of soluble factors. ERAD-C substrates are recognized by Doa10 directly or through adaptor proteins, such as some cytosolic chaperones (reviewed in Mehrtash and Hochstrasser, 2019).

Polytopic proteins constitute an important class of macromolecules, representing between 20 and 30% of the proteome (Wallin and von Heijne, 1998), and perform some essential functions in cells. Due to their structural complexity, they are prone to misfolding and, in fact, the aggregation of many polytopic proteins is the cause of different diseases (reviewed in Aridor, 2007). However, only a handful of polytopic ERAD substrates have been described (reviewed in Krshnan et al., 2022), and identifying their degrons has not been an easy task due to their multiple domains and posttranslational modifications.

Chs3 is a plasma membrane (PM) polytopic protein involved in chitin synthesis in yeasts. Although its intracellular trafficking has been extensively studied–and it has proved to be an excellent model to study this cellular process (reviewed in Sanchez and Roncero, 2022)–how the folding of such a complex protein takes place to maintain Chs3 functionality along this trafficking process remains unknown. The exit of Chs3 from the ER depends on the action of the specific chaperone Chs7 (Trilla et al., 1999), which plays a similar role to Shr3, Gsf2, or Pho86 to prevent TM mismatching of their substrates, amino acid permeases (AAPs), Hxt1-2, and Pho84, respectively, as they are inserted into and folded at the ER membrane (Kota and Ljungdahl, 2005). However, it has recently been demonstrated that Chs7 interacts with Chs3 to form a complex that traffics to the PM (Dharwada et al., 2018), questioning the classic view of Chs7 as a chaperone. In addition, Chs3 is N-glycosylated (Cos et al., 1998; Zielinska et al., 2012) and palmitoylated (Lam et al., 2006) in the ER, where it also oligomerizes (Sacristan et al., 2013). Early work demonstrated that Chs3 had an extended half-life (Chuang and Schekman, 1996), which led to the general idea that Chs3 evades intracellular degradation. However, we have recently proved that vacuolar degradation contributes to the cellular homeostasis of Chs3 (Arcones et al., 2016), and additional evidence suggests that this protein is subject to Golgi quality control (GQC) when it is not properly oligomerized (Sacristan et al., 2013) or palmitoylated (Lam, 2009).

Unlike other polytopic proteins with trafficking routes that are similar to the AAPs, Chs3 presents a N-glycosylated luminal domain, a structural signature rather infrequent in yeasts but shared with ABC transporters. In mammals, most ABC transporters are N-glycosylated in their luminal domains, and this modification is important in their ERQC (Bungert et al., 2001; Gnann et al., 2004; Nakagawa et al., 2009). This phenomenon has not been demonstrated in yeasts even though some ABC transporters such as Pdr5, Snq2, and Adp1 are N-glycosylated. In this work, we address the potential ERQC mechanisms that monitor the folding of Chs3 by using different truncated versions as well as different mutants of the protein. Our results show how N-glycosylation at the luminal domain of Chs3 regulates the recognition of the protein by ERAD, confirming the importance of this posttranslational modification in the regulation of complex polytopic proteins in yeasts. In addition, our results demonstrate how ERAD as well as INMAD contribute to Chs3 cellular homeostasis.

Chs3 misfolding induces its proteasomal degradation

To confirm the microscopic evidence, we assessed the stability of several of these proteins by cycloheximide decay assays (Figure 2). In contrast to the nonglycosylated protein, the stability of wild-type Chs3 was increased in the chs7∆ mutant, probably because its retention in the ER prevents its normal trafficking to the vacuole (Arcones et al., 2016).

Taken together, these results indicate that glycosylation does not significantly affect the normal traffic or activity of Chs3, but it does prevent its recognition by ERAD in the case of its retention in the ER, acting as a sort of shield in order to warrant new chances for refolding.

Our analysis of the Chs3QQQ turnover in mutants of the canonical E3 ubiquitin ligases involved in ERAD, Hrd1, and Doa10, provided unexpected results compared with those of Chs31–490/QQQ (compare Figure 4D with Figure 5C). Individual deletions in of either of them in a chs7∆ background moderately increased the stability of the nonglycosylated Chs3QQQ protein, but only the elimination of both Hrd1 and Doa10 produced a considerable increase the protein stability (Figure 5C; Supplemental Table S1). Apparently, the absence of glycosylation in the wild-type protein results in its recognition by multiple ERAD pathways, some of which should recognize the C-terminal region of the protein, which is absent in Chs31–490.

To understand the mechanisms involved in the recognition of Chs3 by ERAD-L, we next analyzed the effect of mutating individually the different glycosylation sites on its turnover (Figure 5D; Supplemental Figure S5D). The elimination of N332 significantly reduced the stability of the protein, while individual elimination of N303 or N371 did not. However, the elimination of both N303 and N371 produced additive effects, reducing the stability of the protein compared with the control and individual mutants. Glycoproteins that are substrates of ERAD-L are normally recognized through the Yos9 lectin (Quan et al., 2008); therefore, we next compared the total amount of these individual mutated proteins in the yos9∆ mutant by western blot (Figure 5E). The absence of Yos9 significantly increased the abundance of the Chs3N303Q and Chs3N332Q mutant proteins, but it had only a moderate effect on the levels of the nonglycosylated protein Chs3QQQ (Figure 5E; see also Supplemental Figure S5E). In addition, the levels of the wild-type and Chs3N371Q proteins were only slightly increased in the yos9∆ mutant. Of note, deletion of Yos9 also increased the abundance of Chs31–490/N332Q but had no apparent effect on the levels of Chs31–490 or Chs31–490/QQQ (Figure 5E; see also Supplemental Figure S5E). Overall, our results suggest that Yos9 participates in the recognition of partially glycosylated versions of Chs3, indicating that glycosylation serves as signal for monitoring the correct folding of the protein in the ER, a phenomenon that has been described for many other proteins (reviewed in Xu and Ng, 2015). Interestingly, these results pointed to N371 glycosylation as the principal signal for ERAD recognition, which would be blocked if the other sites are efficiently glycosylated.

Although these results are consistent with the described role of glycosylation on the recognition of misfolded glycoproteins (Benitez et al., 2011; reviewed in Xu and Ng, 2015), it remains to be shown how glycosylation could prevent Chs3 recognition by ERAD. The most plausible effect would be that the lack of N-glycosylation led to conformational changes in the protein that could affect its recognition by ERAD. Based on blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BNPAGE), the nonglycosylated protein, Chs3QQQ, formed oligomeric protein complexes, similar to the fully glycosylated Chs3 (Figure 6A), a finding consistent with its functionality (Supplemental Figure S5). However, the nonglycosylated versions of the Chs31–490 and Chs3∆490–756 proteins produced high molecular weight (MW) bands in SDS–PAGE western blots, suggestive of potential aggregation of these proteins (Figure 6B, arrows). To evaluate this possibility, we performed a dithiobis-succinimidyl-propionate (DSP) crosslinker analysis on the Chs31-490 protein. DSP treatment did not significantly reduce the amount of monomeric Chs31–490 (Figure 6C, arrowheads), but it did significantly reduce the amount of monomeric nonglycosylated Chs31–490/QQQ, which was linked to a significant increase in the intensity of the aggregated band detected in the upper part of the gel (Figure 6C, arrow). This last result indicates that the nonglycosylated version is prone to aggregation, a fact that could favor ERAD recognition. Some nonglycosylated proteins can be directly recognized by Yos9 (Jaenicke et al., 2011), but most of them are directed to ERAD through the combined action of the Kar2 chaperone (a heat shock protein 70 [Hsp70] member) and its cofactors Jem1 and Scj1 (reviewed in Ninagawa et al., 2021), so we tested the effect of deleting them. The double deletion of SCJ1 and JEM1 did not affect Chs31–490 levels or aggregation, but significantly increased the aggregation of the nonglycosylated Chs31–490/QQQ (Figure 6D, see arrow), as well as its abundance based on the amount of the monomeric protein detected (arrowhead). These effects cannot be simply associated with the reduced activity of ERAD-L because the hrd1∆ mutant showed significantly lower aggregation and abundance of the protein than the scj1∆ jem1∆ double mutant. The aggregation of the nonglycosylated wild-type Chs3 protein was also detected in this double mutant (Figure 6D, arrow in the right panel).

DISCUSSIONChs3 is a complex protein with multiple TM domains, and its intracellular trafficking is strictly regulated. The different domains of the protein contribute to the regulation of this trafficking (reviewed in Sanchez and Roncero, 2022), although its structural complexity has made it difficult to investigate the specific functions of each domain.Our attempt to obtain truncated Chs3 proteins to study its trafficking resulted in a massive phenotype of ER retention that pointed to the ER exit as the critical point in the control of Chs3 trafficking. Moreover, in some cases, the ER retention was linked to lower stability of these proteins, which later proved to be associated with proteasomal degradation. The proteins generated provided us with the molecular tools to identify and characterize the potential ERAD degrons that contribute to the monitoring of Chs3 folding at the ER, participating also in the general homeostasis of the protein.N-glycosylation is the major signal for monitoring Chs3 foldingIt is generally expected that proteins will not be recognized by ERAD during their regular trafficking, but it has long been surprising that accumulation of Chs3 in the ER does not trigger its degradation (Trilla et al., 1999; Kota and Ljungdahl, 2005; Dharwada et al., 2018), in contrast to what has been described for many other proteins–for example, Gap1 (Kota et al., 2007). The results presented here indicate that the luminal domain of Chs3 makes the difference because early glycosylation of this domain contributes to protect Chs3 from ERAD recognition when retained in the ER, shielding potential degrons present in the protein.The characterization of Chs31–490 allowed us to identify an ERAD-L degron associated to the luminal domain of the protein. If not glycosylated, the Chs31–490 protein aggregates and is efficiently degraded by the ERAD-L pathway with the initial contribution of Scj1 and Jem1 chaperones, which help to maintain its solubility in the ER lumen, allowing its final targeting to the Hrd1 complex. The Scj1 and Jem1 chaperones would recognize this degron according to their described role on the recognition of misfolded glycosylated and nonglycosylated proteins for their degradation by ERAD-L (reviewed in Ninagawa et al., 2021). The nonglycosylated full-length Chs3QQQ protein, when retained in the ER, was also significantly stabilized in the hrd1∆ mutant, but to a greater extend in the hrd1∆ doa10∆ double mutant, a clear indication of the presence of additional degrons in the C-terminal part of the protein that might be recognized by additional ERAD pathways. Apparently, the correct insertion in the ER membrane of the two initial TM domains of the protein to form the luminal domain is effectively monitored through its glycosylation status, conferring a biological function to this posttranslational modification that otherwise does not seem to be required for either the trafficking or the enzymatic activity of Chs3 (Sacristan et al., 2013; Supplemental Figure S5). Interestingly, the ABC-type multidrug transporter Pdr5 also contains a luminal glycosylated domain. This protein is not an efficient ERAD substrate when retained in the ER (de Thozée et al., 2007), but changes on its glycosylation status increased significantly its sensitivity to ERAD (Plemper et al., 1998). Supposedly the presence of a prominent luminal glycosylated domain is a determining signature in the folding of these proteins. This probably explains the characteristic behavior of Chs3, which becomes prone to ERAD degradation in the absence of glycosylation, similarly to human proteins but in contrast to most yeast proteins that are normally more stable in absence of glycosylation (reviewed in Ninagawa et al., 2021).The role of N-glycosylation in Chs3 biology goes further as deduced from the different effects we observed through the individual elimination of its glycosylation sites. Chs3 contains three potential N-glycosylation sites, two of which, N303 and N332, have been demonstrated experimentally to be N-glycosylated (Zielinska et al., 2012). Our characterization of the individual mutants indicated that N371 could also be glycosylated (Sacristan et al., 2013; Supplemental Figure S5A). The elimination of the N371 site does not reduce protein stability (Figure 5D; Supplemental Figure S5D), a result that can be explained by two alternative possibilities: Either this glycosylation is irrelevant in the process of ERAD recognition, or it is the signal directly recognized by the soluble factors involved in ERAD-L. The results obtained with the yos9∆ mutant support the latter because all the mutated proteins maintaining the N371 glycosylation showed increased stability in this mutant. Apparently, the glycan chain built on N371 contains the α-1, 6 mannose that is normally recognized by Yos9 (Quan et al., 2008), and in its absence the protein is not recognized by ERAD. This hypothesis is compatible with previous evidence that point to the last glycosylation site of a protein as the critical position for ERAD recognition through Yos9 (Benitez et al., 2011). It should be noted that according to our results, Yos9 plays an additional role in the recognition of the totally glycosylated and nonglycosylated Chs3 proteins, a phenomenon probably associated with its more general role in the recognition and retention of proteins independently of their glycosylation status (Benitez et al., 2011; Jaenicke et al., 2011). Chs3 is a complex protein with multiple TM domains, and its intracellular trafficking is strictly regulated. The different domains of the protein contribute to the regulation of this trafficking (reviewed in Sanchez and Roncero, 2022), although its structural complexity has made it difficult to investigate the specific functions of each domain.

Our attempt to obtain truncated Chs3 proteins to study its trafficking resulted in a massive phenotype of ER retention that pointed to the ER exit as the critical point in the control of Chs3 trafficking. Chs3 folding is monitored at different levels

How then is the folding of Chs3 monitored? Chs3 contains a luminal domain, which is characteristic of all family II chitin synthases but is not present in other chitin synthases (Figure 9A; Supplemental Figure S8). The trafficking of Chs3 (Figure 9B) differs significantly from that of other chitin synthases (reviewed in Sanchez and Roncero, 2022), and in this case the luminal domain is strictly required for the correct folding of the protein and its exit from the ER. To date we cannot be sure about the biological significance of this domain in the folding of the protein into an active enzyme, but the three-dimensional structural predictions suggested that the TM domains of Chs3 flanking the luminal domain would complement the other two TM domains nearby of the conserved catalytic site of the protein in order to form the membrane channel that allows chitin extrusion (Supplemental Figure S8) as described for other chitin synthases (Ren et al., 2022).

Protein extracts and immunoblotting

A protocol based on the use of trichloroacetic acid (TCA; Wright et al., 1989) was carried out to process the samples for Western blot analysis. Protein was extracted using an equal number of cells from logarithmic cultures. Cells were centrifuged, washed in 20% TCA, and then disrupted in 1.5-ml tubes with 50 μl of 20% TCA and glass beads (0.45 mm, Sigma), during three pulses of 30 s each with an intensity of 5.5 in a Fast Prep shaker (FP120 Bio101, Savant). Lysates were diluted with 400 μl of 5% TCA and after vortexing, the beads were removed by centrifugation. The supernatants were centrifuged for 10 min at 800 g, and the pellets were resuspended in a 100 μl mix (containing 20 μl of 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate [SDS], 20 μl of water, and 60 μl of 1 M Tris base) and incubated at 95°C for 5 min. Samples were centrifuged for 2 min at 15,700 g, and the supernatants were collected. The protein concentration was measured using the Bradford method (Bradford, 1976). High-throughput analysis using a tFT approach and the SGA methodology

This analysis is based on the concept of tFTs, previously developed as tools to measure protein turnover (Khmelinskii et al., 2012; Khmelinskii et al., 2014). Proteins of interest were tagged chromosomally at their C-terminus with the mCherry-sfGFP tFT tag in the query strain used for the screening (strain Y8205). The integrative cassette was amplified from pFA6a-mCherry-sfGFP::hphNT1 (Supplemental Table S3). This tagging was direct in the case of the Chs3 and Chs31–490 proteins, but for the Chs3∆490–756 and ∆490Chs3 proteins, the tagging was first performed in the corresponding plasmids. These were later linearized using single restriction sites in the promoter and terminator regions of Chs3, and the resulting fragments were transformed into the Y8205 strain to incorporate each tagged truncation into its genome after homologous recombination. Correct integrations were first checked microscopically and further confirmed by direct sequencing.

Once the different query proteins tagged with the mCherry-sfGFP tFT were constructed, they were crossed to an array of UPS mutants using the SGA methodology (Tong et al., 2001; Baryshnikova et al., 2010). Crosses were performed in a 1536-colony format. Mating, selection of diploids, sporulation, and finally selection of the haploids carrying both a tFT-tagged allele and a mutant allele were made by sequential pinning of yeast colonies on appropriate selective media using a pinning robot (RoToR, Singer instruments). The fluorescence intensities of the final colonies were measured in medium containing adenine after 24 h of growth at 30°C for the first batch and after 24 and 48 h at 23°C for the second batch to evaluate the differences between both temperatures. The fluorescence measurements were performed using a Spark 20M plate reader with a monochromator (Tecan), equipped with stackers for automated plated loading, for mCherry (586/10 nm excitation, 612/10 nm emission, optimal detector gain) and sfGFP (488/10 nm excitation, 510/10 nm emission, optimal detector gain). The resulting measurements were first corrected for spatial effects using local regression, and background was subtracted using values from nonfluorescent wild-type strains located on each plate. For each query, z-scores for changes in protein abundance (sfGFP intensity) and stability (the mCherry/sfGFP ratio) were calculated by subtracting the median per query per plate from the corrected and adjusted values and dividing it by the median absolute deviation (MAD) per query per plate (Dederer et al., 2019). Next, p values were computed using a one-sample t test for each query; they were then adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. For plotting, the median z-scores for each growth condition are shown. For a more detailed description of the analysis, we suggest consulting a previous publication by the Khmelinskii group (Kong et al., 2021).
