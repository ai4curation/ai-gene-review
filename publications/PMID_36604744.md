Many human diseases are associated with the misfolding of amyloidogenic
proteins. Understanding the mechanisms cells employ to ensure the integrity of
the proteome is therefore a crucial step in the development of potential
therapeutic interventions. Yeast cells possess numerous prion-forming proteins
capable of adopting amyloid conformations, possibly as an epigenetic mechanism
to cope with changing environmental conditions. The ribosome-associated complex
(RAC), which docks near the ribosomal polypeptide exit tunnel and recruits the
Hsp70 Ssb to chaperone nascent chains, can moderate the acquisition of these
amyloid conformations in yeast. Here we examine the ability of the human RAC
chaperone proteins Mpp11 and Hsp70L1 to function in place of their yeast RAC
orthologues Zuo1 and Ssz1 in yeast lacking endogenous RAC and investigate the
extent to which the human orthologues can perform RAC chaperone activities in
yeast. We found that the Mpp11/Hsp70L1 complex can partially correct the growth
defect seen in RAC-deficient yeast cells, although yeast/human hetero species
complexes were variable in this ability. The proportion of cells in which the
Sup35 protein undergoes spontaneous conversion to a [PSI+ ] prion conformation,
which is increased in the absence of RAC, was reduced by the presence of the
human RAC complex. However, the toxicity in yeast from expression of a
pathogenically expanded polyQ protein was unable to be countered by the human
RAC chaperones. This yeast system can serve as a facile model for studying the
extent to which the human RAC chaperones contribute to combating cotranslational
misfolding of other mammalian disease-associated proteins.

INTRODUCTION:

The biological activity of a protein is dependent on its tertiary structure. Many proteins that contain glutamine and asparagine (Q/N)-rich domains can misfold to form stable β-sheet-rich amyloid fiber aggregates within the cell.1–5 Prions are a special class of infectious protein aggregates that can template and facilitate the acquisition of the amyloid conformation by the correctly folded functional version of the protein. Mammalian prions have been identified as the causative agents of transmission responsible for the family of Spongiform Encephalopathies, a group of fatal neurodegenerative diseases caused by the prion PrPSc, the misfolded aggregation-prone conformation of the cellular protein PrPC.6 First discovered as the infectious agent responsible for scrapie in sheep,7 misfolded PrPSc is also responsible for bovine spongiform encephalopathy as well as chronic wasting disease found in the cervids, deer and elk. In humans, prion diseases include Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, Kuru, and fatal familial insomnia.8 Additionally, an ever growing number of human disease processes are thought to include the misfolding and aggregation of amyloidogenic cellular proteins as a step in their progression, including Aβ in Alzheimer’s disease, α-synuclein in Parkinson’s disease, and pathogenically expanded polyglutamine (polyQ) in Huntington’s disease.5,9 As there is a paucity of treatment options for these serious diseases, understanding the basic biological mechanisms contributing to their pathology, including the possible role of amyloid biology, is of critical importance.

Baker’s yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, is an important model system for studying prions and the biological mechanisms related to their formation and prevention. To date more than ten yeast prions have been described.10,11 Additionally, bioinformatic studies have identified many more yeast proteins with amyloid forming regions theoretically capable of prion like conformational switching.12 By far the most extensively characterized yeast prion is [PSI+], the amyloid form of the translation termination factor Sup35. The [PSI+] prion was first identified in yeast as a dominant phenotype inherited in a non-Mendelian fashion.13 When in its prion form, Sup35 in the cell becomes sequestered in amyloid aggregates, resulting in the characteristic [PSI+] phenotype, which is typified by increased readthrough of stop codons during mRNA translation. Prions are common in wild yeast populations14 and exposure to environmental stresses increases the rate of prionogenesis in yeast.15 Observations that prions can confer an adaptive advantage in stressful environments has led to the suggestion that yeast prions and other amyloid containing proteins may act as an epigenetic mechanism to help ensure cellular survival when encountering sub-optimal environmental conditions.14–16 A better understanding of the mechanisms that yeast use to manage the acquisition of prion conformations within their proteome and understanding the similarities that may exist between these yeast systems and their higher eukaryotic counterparts may help to shed light on the ways that these factors can aid in combating mammalian disease processes.

Prions are characterized by their self-propagating ability to serve as templates to drive the adoption of an amyloid conformation in the correctly folded cellular proteins they encounter. However, the acquisition of an amyloid conformation can happen either in reaction to an encounter with a previously misfolded amyloid protein or spontaneously, in the absence of a templating agent, possibly as a response to elements in the cellular environment. Proteins are at their most vulnerable to misfolding events as they are being synthesized and before they can attain the tertiary structure formed by the complete amino acid chain. The emergence of susceptible domains from the ribosomal exit tunnel, prior to the more C-terminal portions of the polypeptide with which they may interact to form the final functional three-dimensional structure of the mature protein, may contribute to the adoption of a misfolded and aggregation-prone conformation. The crowed cellular setting, including the close association of nascent polypeptides found in polysomes, could also contribute to the adoption and propagative templating of the misfolded protein form during translation. This co-translational misfolding on the ribosome is the earliest time at which the cellular protein quality control mechanisms can act. Therefore, the ribosome-associated chaperones are at the front lines of the cellular defense mounted against disruption in proteome homeostasis.

The Ribosome-Associated Complex (RAC) consists of the Hsp40 family member Zuo1 paired with the Hsp70 family member Ssz1.17 Both of these RAC subunits are in physical contact with the newly synthesized polypeptide as it emerges from the ribosomal exit tunnel.18 Together these two components recruit the chaperone Ssb to the nascent polypeptide and facilitate its ATPase-dependent chaperone activity.19 In addition to general effects on fitness, such as lowered growth rate and cold sensitivity,17 loss of the RAC (and thus failure to recruit Ssb to ribosomes) results in an increase in the rate of the acquisition of the [PSI+] prion by [psi−] cells.20–22 Although mammals lack an orthologue of Ssb, mammalian orthologues of the yeast RAC components have been identified. The human proteins Mpp11 and Hsp70L1, shown to interact to form a stable complex in the cytosol, are also associated with ribosomes and are thus the best candidates for the functional equivalent of the yeast RAC chaperones Zuo1 and Ssz1 respectively.23 Similar to the role observed for the RAC in yeast, there is evidence for the involvement of the human RAC components in modulating the effect of stress on the cellular proteome. A loss of the Mpp11/Hsp70L1 RAC in mammals has revealed an essential function in the unfolded protein response as well as in the ER stress response.24 Further characterization of these cotranslational quality-control factors is essential to understanding their protective role in the prevention of amyloid-mediated human disease processes.

In this work we have examined the extent to which the facile yeast system can be used further elucidate the basic regulatory roles these chaperones play in amyloid biology. We show that the human RAC orthologues Mpp11 and Hsp70L1 demonstrate partial functional chaperone ability when replacing the yeast RAC components. We confirm previous reports23,25 that the human orthologues can functionally replace their yeast counterparts to partially rescue growth, and demonstrate for the first time that the human chaperones, like their yeast counterparts, can suppress formation of the [PSI+] prion. Despite their prion-suppressing capabilities, the human RAC orthologues were unable to suppress toxicity of pathogenically expanded polyQ.

The media and incubation conditions, as well as the distance from the camera and magnification for the photography, were kept constant for all strains compared. DISCUSSION:Many human diseases involve the misfolding and aggregation of amyloidogenic proteins. Currently there is a need to further our understanding of the protective mechanisms employed by cells against these misfolding events. Ribosome-associated processes that ensure quality control of nascent polypeptides are collectively emerging as a major mechanism by which proteostasis is maintained. Yeast has historically been a powerful tool in the search for understanding the basic workings of cellular processes and has greatly contributed to our understanding of how these systems work in higher eukaryotic cells. We therefore sought to develop a simple yeast model system to study the ability of the mammalian RAC to antagonize prion formation and ameliorate toxic protein misfolding. We found that mammalian RAC significantly suppresses prion formation but does not rescue toxicity due to pathogenically expanded polyQ in our system.Ribosome associated chaperones represent the earliest opportunity for the cell’s quality control systems to recognize and correct the acquisition of amyloid conformations. In this work we have set out to examine the similarities in function between a set of yeast ribosome associated chaperones and their human orthologues. In yeast, the RAC is a heterodimeric ribosome-anchored complex composed of a Hsp40(Zuo1)/Hsp70(Ssz1) pair. Previous studies on the function of a human orthologue of the Hsp40 RAC component, Mpp11, has shown that it is able to rescue the growth defect phenotype seen in yeast cells lacking Zuo1.23,25 We also see this rescue effect from supplying Mpp11 alone to cells engineered to lack both RAC components. However, there is very little if any effect on the growth when supplying either Szz1 or Hsp70L1 alone. The Hsp40 component of RAC anchors the complex to the ribosome, which explains why there is little or no rescue seen with the Hsp70 RAC component Ssz1/Hsp70L1 alone, since it would not be recruited to ribosomes. In addition to possible ribosome-associated activity, this rescue from the Hsp40 RAC components Zuo1 or Mpp11 by themselves without an Hsp70 component could be due to a cytosolic interaction of Zuo1/Mpp11 with a cytosolic chaperone, such as Ssa1, whose activation by Zuo1/Mpp11 may not require the Hsp70 RAC component.In addition to the rescue of the growth defect in cells lacking RAC, we examined whether there are other more specific physiological consequences, related to protein folding, of expressing the human RAC orthologues in yeast. Our previous work has shown that the presence of RAC on ribosomes in yeast decreases the acquisition of the prion [PSI+], the amyloid conformation of the translation termination factor Sup35.20 More recent work by Son and Wickner has demonstrated that most [PSI+] variants (amyloid polymorphisms) that arise in the absence of RAC can be cured by normal levels of Zuo1 and Ssz1.22 Indeed, the de novo formation of prions depends on the balance between amyloid seed nucleation and clearance, and chaperones such as the disaggregase Hsp104 and the Hsp70/40 pair Ssa1 and Sis1 play critical roles in yeast prion propagation.33,34 Amyloid disaggregation by Hsp104 can lead to either fragmentation (which enables propagation) or dissolution, and the presence of the prion form of Rnq1 (termed [PIN+] or [RNQ+]) was shown to promote [PSI+] formation by titrating Hsp104 activity on Sup35 amyloid.33 Given the well documented requirement for [RNQ+] in facilitating [PSI+] appearance, our experiments were all carried out in [RNQ+] strains. Here we demonstrate that the human RAC complex composed of Mpp11 and Hsp70L1 is also capable of significantly reducing the spontaneous folding of Sup35 into prion conformations. Indeed, although human RAC does not fully compensate for yeast RAC in suppressing prion formation, the extent to which it does so is somewhat surprising given that it only partially rescues the growth defect of cells lacking endogenous RAC. This may indicate that the human RAC pair can effectively perform some, but not all, of the yeast RAC functions in the context of the yeast cellular environment. DISCUSSION:

Many human diseases involve the misfolding and aggregation of amyloidogenic proteins. Currently there is a need to further our understanding of the protective mechanisms employed by cells against these misfolding events. Ribosome-associated processes that ensure quality control of nascent polypeptides are collectively emerging as a major mechanism by which proteostasis is maintained. Yeast has historically been a powerful tool in the search for understanding the basic workings of cellular processes and has greatly contributed to our understanding of how these systems work in higher eukaryotic cells. We therefore sought to develop a simple yeast model system to study the ability of the mammalian RAC to antagonize prion formation and ameliorate toxic protein misfolding. We found that mammalian RAC significantly suppresses prion formation but does not rescue toxicity due to pathogenically expanded polyQ in our system.

Ribosome associated chaperones represent the earliest opportunity for the cell’s quality control systems to recognize and correct the acquisition of amyloid conformations. In this work we have set out to examine the similarities in function between a set of yeast ribosome associated chaperones and their human orthologues. In yeast, the RAC is a heterodimeric ribosome-anchored complex composed of a Hsp40(Zuo1)/Hsp70(Ssz1) pair. Previous studies on the function of a human orthologue of the Hsp40 RAC component, Mpp11, has shown that it is able to rescue the growth defect phenotype seen in yeast cells lacking Zuo1.23,25 We also see this rescue effect from supplying Mpp11 alone to cells engineered to lack both RAC components. However, there is very little if any effect on the growth when supplying either Szz1 or Hsp70L1 alone. The Hsp40 component of RAC anchors the complex to the ribosome, which explains why there is little or no rescue seen with the Hsp70 RAC component Ssz1/Hsp70L1 alone, since it would not be recruited to ribosomes. In addition to possible ribosome-associated activity, this rescue from the Hsp40 RAC components Zuo1 or Mpp11 by themselves without an Hsp70 component could be due to a cytosolic interaction of Zuo1/Mpp11 with a cytosolic chaperone, such as Ssa1, whose activation by Zuo1/Mpp11 may not require the Hsp70 RAC component.

In addition to the rescue of the growth defect in cells lacking RAC, we examined whether there are other more specific physiological consequences, related to protein folding, of expressing the human RAC orthologues in yeast. Our previous work has shown that the presence of RAC on ribosomes in yeast decreases the acquisition of the prion [PSI+], the amyloid conformation of the translation termination factor Sup35.20 More recent work by Son and Wickner has demonstrated that most [PSI+] variants (amyloid polymorphisms) that arise in the absence of RAC can be cured by normal levels of Zuo1 and Ssz1.22 Indeed, the de novo formation of prions depends on the balance between amyloid seed nucleation and clearance, and chaperones such as the disaggregase Hsp104 and the Hsp70/40 pair Ssa1 and Sis1 play critical roles in yeast prion propagation.33,34 Amyloid disaggregation by Hsp104 can lead to either fragmentation (which enables propagation) or dissolution, and the presence of the prion form of Rnq1 (termed [PIN+] or [RNQ+]) was shown to promote [PSI+] formation by titrating Hsp104 activity on Sup35 amyloid.33 Given the well documented requirement for [RNQ+] in facilitating [PSI+] appearance, our experiments were all carried out in [RNQ+] strains. Here we demonstrate that the human RAC complex composed of Mpp11 and Hsp70L1 is also capable of significantly reducing the spontaneous folding of Sup35 into prion conformations. Indeed, although human RAC does not fully compensate for yeast RAC in suppressing prion formation, the extent to which it does so is somewhat surprising given that it only partially rescues the growth defect of cells lacking endogenous RAC. This may indicate that the human RAC pair can effectively perform some, but not all, of the yeast RAC functions in the context of the yeast cellular environment. Inadequate coordination of Mpp11 and/or Hsp70L1 with the yeast orthologues of RAC interacting partners, such as Ssb and potentially the nascent polypeptide-associated complex (NAC), may explain the incomplete growth rescue we observe. Future studies aimed at introducing human orthologues of Ssa and NAC into our yeast model may help to address this issue.
