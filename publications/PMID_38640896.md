We recently reported the distribution of N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) in HeLa mRNA
at base resolution through chemical reduction and the induction of C:T
mismatches in sequencing (RedaC:T-seq). Our results contradicted an earlier
report from Schwartz and colleagues utilizing a similar method termed ac4C-seq.
Here, we revisit both datasets and reaffirm our findings. Through RedaC:T-seq
reanalysis, we establish a low basal error rate at unmodified nucleotides that
is not skewed to any specific mismatch type and a prominent increase in C:T
substitutions as the dominant mismatch type in both treated wild-type
replicates, with a high degree of reproducibility across replicates. In
contrast, through ac4C-seq reanalysis, we uncover significant data quality
issues including insufficient depth, with one wild-type replicate yielding 2.7
million reads, inconsistencies in reduction efficiencies between replicates, and
an overall increase in mismatches involving thymine that could obscure ac4C
detection. These analyses bolster the detection of ac4C in HeLa mRNA through
RedaC:T-seq.

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, there has been a substantial effort to develop novel methods to characterize the breadth and function of mRNA modifications such as N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C). However, biological, experimental, and computational differences have led to inconsistent and controversial results regarding the prevalence and stoichiometry of mRNA modifications1. We previously identified thousands of acetylated regions in HeLa mRNA using acetylated RNA immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (acRIP-seq)2. These zones were generally enriched in coding sequences (CDS) and 5’untranslated regions (5’UTRs), though exact acetylated cytidines were not resolved. As precise positioning of modified bases is essential to study their function, we developed RedaC:T-seq, which involves NaBH4 reduction of ac4C to tetrahydroacetylcytidine (tetrahydro-ac4C)3. Tetrahydro-ac4C preferentially base pairs with adenine in cDNA synthesis, and ac4C sites can thus be determined through C:T mismatches during deep sequencing3. Through this approach, we uncovered location-specific impact for ac4C in the regulation of mRNA translation: while CDS acetylation promoted translation elongation, 5’UTR acetylation inhibited translation initiation and resulted in aberrant upstream initiation3.

Our findings provided mechanistic insights that align with studies from numerous groups that have detected ac4C in mRNA through several orthogonal approaches, cell types and eukaryotic model systems2–10. However, in their Matters Arising, Georgeson and Schwartz raise concerns about our RedaC:T-seq results, which directly contradict a previous report from their group utilizing a similar method for ac4C mapping that they term ac4C-seq11. In response, we present a reanalysis of the RedaC:T-seq and ac4C-seq results through the lens of the highlighted criticisms. Our comprehensive reevaluation substantiates the identification of cytidine acetylation within HeLa mRNA through RedaC:T-seq, providing a base-resolved understanding of its distribution and impact of ac4C in mRNA translation.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONC:T mismatches represent the dominant mismatch type in wildtype HeLa RedaC:T-seqThe argument presented by Georgeson and Schwartz hinges on the uneven occurrence of C:T mismatches between wildtype HeLa replicates. The authors note that in comparing C:T mismatches in our RedaC:T-seq results, reduced wildtype replicate 2 (WT Rep2) globally achieved a higher mismatch rate than reduced wildtype replicate 1 (WT Rep1). We do not dispute this fact and a minor skew is also observed at known ac4C sites in 18S rRNA, suggestive of batch-to-batch variability in NaBH4-mediated reduction to tetrahydro-ac4C (Georgeson and Schwartz, Fig. 1). Unintended deacetylation in the basic reaction conditions represents one factor that limits ac4C reduction through NaBH4 and offers a potential explanation for the capture of only ~25% of ac4C through C:T mismatches at fully acetylated 18S rRNA sites (Georgeson and Schwartz, Fig. 1)12,13. Notably, the representation provided by Georgeson and Schwartz does not take depth into consideration and the high stoichiometry sites shown in WT Rep2 are associated with low sequencing coverage where percent mismatch rates can become artificially inflated (Fig. S1A). We revisit the important issues of sequencing depth and batch effects in our reanalysis of the ac4C-seq results from Schwartz and colleagues below.Georgeson and Schwartz suggest that detection of ac4C in HeLa RedaC:T-seq represents an artifact stemming from a single low-complexity replicate, and that the significance of these mismatches are exaggerated in the pooled analysis due to PCR duplication. While pooling replicates is a standard practice to enhance statistical robustness in computational analyses, we acknowledge that this approach masks variations between individual replicates14. We thus reevaluated our RedaC:T-seq data with an emphasis on replicate concordance. First, we examined for absolute mismatch abundance by type in unprocessed alignments per replicate, and confirmed that C:T mismatches represent the dominant mismatch type in both wildtype RedaC:T-seq replicates, contrasting with untreated inputs, where A:G mismatches predominate (Fig. 1A). We further note that though WT Rep2 shows the strongest skewing to C:T mismatches, the absolute abundance of C:T mismatches is higher in WT Rep1 (Fig. 1A).Preferential occurrence of C:T mismatches in reduced wildtype HeLa RNA in the absence of computational filtering supports the conclusion that both replicates significantly contribute to detection of ac4C in our pooled data analysis. We next explicitly test this premise through identifying originating source for reads with C:T mismatches in wildtype and NAT10−/− HeLa RedaC:T-seq. Consistent with the results presented in Fig. 1A, the absolute number of unique reads demonstrating C:T mismatches was substantially elevated in both wildtype replicates compared to both NAT10−/− RedaC:T-seq samples (Fig. 1B). This relationship is preserved when considering the total number of reads supporting C:T mismatch detection at RedaC:T-seq defined ac4C sites, and the majority of detected ac4C sites are characterized by multiple unique reads originating from both wildtype replicates (Fig. 1C). An upset plot depicting cross-sample C:T mismatch detection at statistically enriched sites emphasizes this point through demonstrating that both wildtype replicates substantially contribute to ac4C detection with robust cross-replicate agreement: ~2000 cytidines showing evidence of C:T mismatches in both wildtype results and a complete absence in NAT10−/− RedaC:T-seq (Fig. 1D).Notably, the ~2000 sites with C:T mismatches in both wildtype replicates achieved a higher sequencing depth as compared to the ~3500 locations with mismatches in WT Rep1 or WT Rep2 alone (Fig. S1B). This distinction is relevant considering the extensive supplemental browser shots presented by Georgeson and Schwartz in Data S1. Summarily, their presented examples correspond to mRNAs with the lowest sequencing coverage, reaching ~30 or fewer reads in the pooled results (Figs. 1E, S1C). Given our approximate 25% conversion rate at uniformly acetylated sites in 18S rRNA—and expecting even lower rates in mRNA—it is reasonable that mismatches would not appear in both replicates within this low-depth segment. In response, we showcase higher-depth instances where mismatches are consistently observed across replicates (Fig. S1D). Overlapping acRIP-seq signal in wildtype but not NAT10−/− HeLa results provides additional orthogonal evidence for ac4C mapping accuracy and achieves the burden-of-proof to assert that HeLa mRNA is acetylated in a NAT10-dependent manner (Fig. 1F).The prevalence of unique reads supporting the detection of ac4C in both wildtype HeLa RedaC:T-seq replicates dispels the notion that ac4C detection is an artifact of a single sample. However, Georgeson and Schwartz raised additional concerns regarding the presence of reads with identical 5’ and 3’ termini in our dataset, which they interpreted as PCR duplicates. Though this conclusion is not evidence backed, the authors offer their occurrence as rationale to down-sample our dataset through their removal and random simulations. Through these measures, they effectively eliminate the skew towards increased C:T mismatches in wildtype relative to NAT10−/− results that was present in the complete dataset. Notably, the rigor of the presented simulations was not validated against established benchmarks.Though we are unaware of any computational pipeline that endorses arbitrary exclusion of sequencing reads, the critique by Georgeson and Schwartz centers on the elimination of identical reads, necessitating a discussion on their role in high-throughput sequencing and ac4C detection. Fundamentally, the assertion that identical reads result exclusively from PCR duplication dismisses a breadth of evidence to the contrary. Rather, natural duplication is an inevitable consequence of high-throughput sequencing that is influenced by the range of RNA expression and inherent biological features that lead to biased over-representation of certain sequences. The extent to which duplicate reads can be attributed to natural origins was elegantly documented in a comprehensive RNA-seq analysis, which determined that 70–95% of duplicate reads are not a consequence of PCR amplification15. The ac4C-seq study by Schwartz and colleagues illustrates this point, wherein approximately 1–2.3% of reads persisted as natural duplicates after excluding true PCR duplicates using unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), despite achieving low-depth and high complexity (Table S1). Given the high sequencing yield in our RedaC:T-seq, it is reasonable to expect an excess of natural duplications (Table S1)16. It is further unclear whether borohydride reduction introduces cleavage biases that may exacerbate the prevalence of reads with identical 5’ and 3’ termini in sequencing results. Consequently, indiscriminate removal of duplicated reads, particularly when PCR sequence identifiers are absent, is overly restrictive and lacks computational validity.As our study did not utilize UMIs, precluding us from distinguishing between PCR-induced and naturally occurring identical reads, we turned to inter-sample comparisons to demonstrate natural duplication. This approach revealed a significant presence of identical reads across replicates and between wildtype and NAT10−/− results (Fig. 2A, select examples). These findings solidify the presence of natural duplicates in our data and highlight their removal as unjustified. The potential presence of PCR duplicates notwithstanding, as our pipeline relies on filtering relative to input and NAT10−/− results, it is difficult to envision how such duplication would specifically lead of overrepresentation of reads with C:T mismatches in wildtype samples. Indeed, in examining the distribution of duplicates per individual RedaC:T-seq samples, we find a comparable prevalence in all wildtype and NAT10−/− results with a somewhat reduced range for WT Rep2 (Fig. 2B, Table S1). This tendency is preserved in reads that directly overlap RedaC:T determined ac4C sites, though slightly elevated in magnitude (Fig. 2B). Considering that enrichment of C:T mismatches in wildtype relative to NAT10−/− results is a key feature of our computational pipeline, it is unlikely that elevated duplication artifactually compromised our analysis. In contrast, in removing identical reads from their reanalysis of our results, the elevated level observed surrounding ac4C sites would certainly bias against the identification of ac4C.The collective findings discussed in this section robustly affirm that the identification of ac4C in wildtype HeLa mRNA is corroborated across both wildtype RedaC:T-seq replicates, reinforcing the conclusion that its presence is a genuine biological signal rather than a technical artifact.Sources of inconsistencies in ac4C detection through reduction and sequencingMultiple factors, both biological and technical, could contribute to discrepancies in mismatch penetrance at ac4C sites. Biologically, mRNA acetylation may exhibit stochastic fluctuation, affecting the levels at specific substrates within cellular pools. Technically, variations in reduction efficiency, RNA fragmentation, and cDNA synthesis could all contribute to the differential occurrence of C:T mismatches at ac4C sites. In addition, general sequencing error can influence detection of ac4C in downstream data processing. A basic understanding of how these determinants are influenced by protocol specific parameters, including the choice of reducing agent, may aid the proper interpretation of divergent findings across studies. To begin to resolve inconsistencies between the RedaC:T-seq and ac4C-seq studies, we highlight a few key points here for consideration.Reduction efficiency.A primary limitation in detecting ac4C through existing chemical methods is associated with the efficiency of its reduction to tetrahydro-ac4C. As highlighted above, for protocols involving NaBH4, this capacity is restricted by off-target deacetylation in the basic reaction conditions12. In contrast, NaCNBH3 reduction occurs under acidic conditions, protecting ac4C from this off-target effect in ac4C-seq workflows11. However, there could be other factors that hinder conversion to tetrahydro-ac4C when using NaCNBH3. For instance, we were unable to achieve efficient NaCNBH3-mediated reduction of acetylated spike-ins under conditions that effectively reduced 18S rRNA (Fig. 3A). Given that ac4C facilitates the formation of structures and NaCNBH3 reduction is carried out at 20°C, inefficient denaturation of structures and consequent occlusion of ac4C represents a possible source of this discrepancy. Notably, a recent study pointed to abundance as an additional factor that could mitigate the utility of NaCNBH3 for ac4C mapping in cellular RNA: while abundant C:T mismatches were observed in a spike-in that was included at a 1:1 ratio, no mismatches were observed in the same sequence spiked-in at a 1:5000 ratio17. The authors thus concluded that ac4C-seq was insufficient for mapping ac4C in mRNA.Crucially, when analyzing C:T mismatch frequency in acetylated spike-ins used in the ac4C-seq study by Schwartz and colleagues, we noted significant variability in efficiency across replicates. The authors reported employing four synthetic RNA spike-ins, each containing a single ac4C within distinct sequence contexts (ACA, GCA, UCA, and ACU), and devoid of other cytidines11. Notably, among the four sequences, only reads associated with the ‘ACA’ context were recovered. Whether the inability to detect the remaining spike-ins relates to their selective loss during processing, or insufficient sequencing depth (expanded upon below) is unclear. However, based solely on the ‘ACA’ spike-in, we uncovered a stark variability in C:T mismatch detection, ranging from 0.69% to 32.45% across three wildtype replicates, and 1.83% to 36.25% across two NAT10−/− replicates (Figs. 3B, 3C). As these spike-ins were added to isolated RNA to control for reduction efficiency and sequencing quality, the expectation is that mismatch rates should be comparable between conditions.Together, these findings suggest that under conditions where borohydride efficiently reduces ac4C in abundant 18S rRNA, it may fail to reduce ac4C within less abundant mRNAs, highlighting the importance of exercising caution when drawing conclusions from negative results. Furthermore, considering the variability observed amongst the ac4C-seq study spike-ins, pooling reads, as in our RedaC:T-seq analysis, emerges as a justified approach to buffer against intra-sample variability in ac4C reduction efficiency.Fragmentation.An additional distinction between NaBH4 and NaCNBH3 reduction of RNA relates to their impacts on RNA integrity. Alkaline hydrolysis is a natural byproduct of the reaction conditions employed in NaBH4 reduction18, resulting in RNA that is fragmented to a size that is directly used for sequencing library construction with no perceptible loss in overall RNA levels (Fig. 3D). In contrast, NaCNBH3 reduction results in a general loss of RNA levels that is not detected in HCl treated controls. Degradation is time dependent, culminating in a substantial loss in RNA integrity at the 20-minute time point used in the ac4C-seq study11 (Fig. 3C). Considering that NaCNBH3 treated RNA is subjected to additional fragmentation during library construction, compounded losses may have contributed to the low ac4C-seq yield, as expanded upon below.Reverse transcription.In both the RedaC:T-seq and ac4C-seq datasets, sequencing read yields were lower for wildtype than for NAT10−/− HeLa cells, despite identical and parallel processing methods being employed (Table S1). This discrepancy hints at two possible issues: RNA from wildtype HeLa cells may be more prone to fragmentation due to borohydride treatment or it could be adversely affected during reverse transcription. The latter is supported by evidence showing that tetrahydro-ac4C can hinder certain reverse transcriptases (RTases), leading to truncated sequences, or RT-stops13. These RT-stops can decrease sequencing read yield and may also generate cDNAs with matching 3’ ends, which could be misinterpreted as duplicate reads.General sequencing integrity.Though C:T transitions represent the dominant mismatch type in our RedaC:T-seq results by a substantial margin, other mismatch types showed a statistical enrichment in wildtype as compared to NAT10−/− results (Fig. 1A)3. Georgeson and Schwartz interpret this statistical skew as reflective of general sequencing error. However, the authors did not analyze the rates and distributions of other mismatch types, leaving their impact unclear. Based solely on absolute detection, other mismatch types do not approach the relative magnitude of C:T mismatches, particularly when considering their distribution in untreated input (Fig. 1A). Georgeson and Schwartz acknowledge this fact and stipulate that elevated C:T mismatch detection in our NaBH4-treated results is a byproduct of cytidine deamination. However, they do not substantiate this claim with evidence or provide insight into why such deamination would disproportionately affect wildtype over NAT10−/− RNA.To distinguish the impact of general sequencing errors from specific effects in HeLa RedaC:T-seq and ac4C-seq analyses, we assessed per nucleotide error rates in sequences aligned to residual 18S and 28S rRNA. The advantage of this approach is two-fold: 1) sequencing coverage at rRNA is extensive, resulting in >100,000 reads per molecule, per condition and 2) locations and levels of modified RNA nucleotides are well-defined for both molecules (Tables S2 and S3). Given that various RNA modifications are sensitive to borohydride reduction, knowledge of their locations is particularly useful for discriminating general sequencing error from base-specific reduction19–22.Focusing first on our RedaC:T-seq results and canonical nucleotides, mismatch levels were overall low and generally fell below the 1.25% cut-off threshold applied in our analytical pipeline (Figs. 4A, 4B). Rare exceptions are illustrated as outliers in the 18S rRNA results and are not restricted to either the wildtype or NAT10−/− condition (Fig. 4A). Direct comparison of aggregate data from the 18S and 28S rRNA results reinforces this observation: median mismatch rates were consistently below 0.2%, C:T mismatches included, and showed no particular increase in wildtype compared to NAT10−/− results (Fig. S2A). Thus, examination of mismatch rates at unmodified cytidines dispels the notion that the specific increase in C:T mismatch detection in wildtype HeLa RedaC:T-seq is a consequence of cytidine deamination. Considering the relative inefficiency of NaBH4 reduction and expectation that mRNAs will achieve only partial acetylation, these low basal mismatch levels further bolster the appropriateness of applying a minimum threshold of 1.25% as a starting point in our computational pipeline comparing wildtype RedaC:T-seq to NAT10−/− and untreated controls.As compared to the low basal error detected in rRNA RedaC:T-seq, an unexpectedly high incidence of mismatches involving thymine (A:T, G:T and C:T) was observed in the ac4C-seq results. C:T mismatches were particularly elevated, with outliers approaching ~40% mismatch rates (Figs. 4A, 4B). To avoid artifacts associated with elevated error rates at the ends of sequenced molecules, we excluded mismatches at the 5’ and 3’ ends of the rRNAs from this analysis. Instead, the extreme C:T mismatch outliers are derived from three unmodified cytidines in the loop region of the 3’ minor domain (Fig. 4C). Mismatches were not observed in the RedaC:T-seq data at these locations, suggesting that something related to the chemistry of NaCNBH3 reduction rendered the relatively exposed nucleotides susceptible to alteration (Fig. 4D). However, it is unclear why overall mismatch rates at these nucleotides was elevated in wildtype ac4C-seq as compared to NAT10−/− and mock-treated controls (Fig. 4D). It is also unclear why there was a specific inclination towards thymine across all canonical bases (Figs. 4A, 4B). Considering the particularly high rate of C:T mismatches at unmodified cytidines in the ac4C-seq study, detection of ac4C sites through computational filtering would have been exceedingly difficult, particularly given low depth achieved.Analyzing mismatch rates at nucleotides with known modification statuses within 18S and 28S sequencing data provided further insights. For example, m1A and m1acp3Ψ exhibited high mismatch rates that were unrelated to treatment (Fig. 4E). In contrast, m3U and m7G displayed sensitivity to reduction that differed between RedaC:T-seq and ac4C-seq. Specifically, m3U produced prevalent mismatches in ac4C-seq across all samples, including untreated controls, whereas mismatch rates displayed sensitivity to reduction in RedaC:T-seq and were notably higher in NaBH4-treated samples than in controls (Fig. 4E). The case of m7G was even more pronounced: mismatches were specifically observed in NaBH4-treated RedaC:T-seq, but not in untreated inputs nor in NaCNBH3-reduced ac4C-seq results (Fig. 4E). Elevated mismatches at m7G sites after NaBH4 reduction was previously reported and attributed to the production of abasic sites20. However, the resistance associated with NaCNBH3 reduction is unexpected and may reflect a distinction in the reaction chemistries. Together, these findings suggest that increased G:T mismatch detection in HeLa RedaC:T-seq could be partially attributed to m7G modifications in mRNA, which were not captured in ac4C-seq analyses.Beyond m7G, dihydrouridine (DU) and 3-methylcytosine (m3C) are other mRNA modifications known to react to NaBH4 reduction19–22. However, since neither DU nor m3C are found in rRNA, they cannot be directly assessed through our computational approach. As m3C could present a challenge for mapping cytidine modifications, we additionally examined the impact of NaBH4 and NaCNBH3 on free m3C in mass spectrometry analysis and found that NaCNBH3 results in a complete loss of m3C detection (Fig. S2B). Though the consequences of m3C loss in the ac4C-seq study is unclear, these findings highlight the extreme caution that must be exercised in interpreting sequencing data derived from chemically treated RNA. Overall, in examining for mismatch rates in rRNA sequencing results, we find no evidence for pervasive cytidine deamination in our RedaC:T-seq results and further uncover significant issues with the ac4C-seq analysis that would have compromised detection of ac4C.Detection of ac4C in HeLa mRNA is preserved in stringent reanalysis incorporating cell and mismatch type error correctionGeorgeson and Schwartz also raised several concerns related to our computational pipeline. In brief, the authors expressed concern about whether intrinsic differences between wildtype and NAT10−/− HeLa and between specific mismatch types were adequately considered during basal error rate correction. Accordingly, we reevaluated our RedaC:T-seq results through a rigorous approach that calculates and corrects for intrinsic mismatch-specific error based on input RNA-sequencing results from wildtype and NAT10−/− HeLa. As the NAT10−/− cells were derived from our wildtype HeLa, we previously relied on wildtype input for SNP correction and established thresholds based on an average detected error rate of 0.8%3. However, inputs from both cell types were sequenced in parallel, allowing us to retroactively error correct in a cell-specific manner. In brief, trimmed reads from untreated inputs were aligned to the human genome in a strand-sensitive manner. Inspection of absolute mismatch numbers in locations that exceeded a minimum of 10 overlapping reads at this unfiltered state revealed a range of mismatches that were roughly preserved in wildtype and NAT10−/− results (Fig. 5A). Importantly, C:T transitions were not the most abundant mismatch type in untreated inputs, establishing that downstream enrichment does not reflect a general bias in the RNA-sequencing results (Fig. 5A).We next examined the distribution of mismatch rates in wildtype and NAT10−/− HeLa input to determine type-specific basal error rates. To mitigate the occurrence of false mismatch detection in the NaBH4 treated results, mismatches with rates that fell below the 75th percentile observed in untreated input were omitted from further analysis (Fig. S3, red lines). This represented a range of 0.51–1.36% for specific mismatch types with C:T cutoff values falling centrally at 0.81 and 0.85% in wildtype and NAT10−/− results, respectively (Fig. S3). All mismatches that fell below these thresholds in the NaBH4-treated results were eliminated from further consideration. The remaining sites were next directly compared to wildtype and NAT10−/− HeLa input to remove locations that showed substantial evidence for mismatches in analogous untreated inputs. To account for intrinsic RNA features that increase error rate, we conservatively selected the median of the top quartile in input sequencing as the maximum tolerated error in input. This resulted in a range of 1.18–3.78% for specific mismatch types, or 1.95% and 2.09% for C:T mismatches in wildtype and NAT10−/− results (Fig. S3, green lines). Of note, Georgeson and Schwartz identified a single SNP that passed through our previous filters. Upon assessing for the root cause, we determined that this was due to an alternative haplotype sequence misalignment and represented an isolated case. We confirm that alternative haplotype misalignments have been corrected in the reanalysis presented here.Mismatches that persisted through these elimination filters and exceeded a minimal prevalence of >1.25% in the pooled NaBH4-treated results were next examined for their prevalence in wildtype versus NAT10−/− results. Basal examination of absolute abundance of error-corrected and thresholded sites confirms the marked bias towards increased C:T mismatch detection as compared to all other mismatch types (Fig. 5B). Importantly and contrary to the assertion by Georgeson and Schwartz that mismatches are globally elevated in NaBH4-treated wildtype sequencing results, detection of select mismatches was in fact increased in absolute abundance in the treated NAT10−/− results (Fig. 5B). However, G:T mismatches in the wildtype treated results remained high (Fig. 5B). This could reflect insufficient filtering given the lower basal stoichiometry and higher overall occurrence in input (Figs. 5A, S3), or bona fide NaBH4 reactivity with other modified residues in mRNA, as demonstrated for m7G (Fig. 4E). Though NAT10−/− cells were employed as a negative control to pinpoint ac4C sites, we recognize that outside this focused objective, NAT10−/− and wildtype HeLa cells exhibit numerous differences that could affect broader research aims3. Significantly, transcripts with CDS-ac4C, including several mRNAs encoding RNA modifying enzymes, are selectively depleted in NAT10−/− cells3. Thus, the statistical skew towards increased mismatches in wildtype RedaC:T-seq may be biologically driven, reflecting changes in the distribution of borohydride-sensitive mRNA modifications following NAT10 depletion.The results presented in Fig. 5B only examine numerical abundance and do not consider stoichiometry or statistical enrichment. To further assess for significance, Fisher’s exact test was performed and p-values were plotted against mismatch rate fold change at each location. Significant sites were determined based on the significance threshold (p-value) as well as magnitude (Fig. 5C). These results unambiguously demonstrate an overwhelming tendency towards increased C:T mismatch detection in wildtype relative to NAT10−/− results, and this trend is robust to both unadjusted and FDR adjusted p-values (Figs. 5D). To directly address the criticism by Georgeson and Schwartz that the bias is driven by a single wildtype replicate, we further examined whether the skew is observed if only considering locations with evidence of mismatches in both replicates. In so doing, we find the marked bias to increased C:T mismatch detection in wildtype relative to NAT10−/− RedaC:T-seq is preserved in the subset of replicated sites (Fig. 5E). Thus, in a detailed analysis that adheres to the rigorous error correction recommendations raised by Georgeson and Schwartz we reassert the presence of NAT10-dependent ac4C in HeLa mRNA.Abundant evidence supports the existence of ac4C outside of CCG motifsA major focus of the argument presented by Georgeson and Schwartz revolves around the existence of an ac4C ‘motif’. This assertion primarily rests on observations of cytidine acetylation principally occurring at the central cytidine within CCG sequences across eukaryotic and archaeal rRNA11,23–26. However, numerous studies, including those co-authored by the Schwartz group, have documented ac4C in various contexts outside of CCG motifs27,28. For example, in archaeal and bacterial tRNAs, ac4C is found in the wobble position within UCA, UCU, and UCG sequences, while rRNA acetylation occurs in CCG, UCG, GCU, and CCA contexts27,29–33. Similarly, ac4C has been validated in mRNA across several contexts after chemical reduction, including CCG, UCA, GCA, CCU, and ACC8–11,28. The versatility of NAT10 targeting to substrates is further evidenced by experiments from Schwartz and colleagues involving mutagenesis scanning of the CCG motif in BAZ2A mRNA, wherein they showed that one specific mRNA can receive ac4C at ACG, UCG, and CCA, albeit at reduced stoichiometry11,28. This collective body of work undermines the notion that ac4C is strictly confined to CCG motifs.Though the literature supports the presence of ac4C across a range of sequence contexts, Schwartz and colleagues found that overexpression of NAT10 and THUMPD1 in HEK293 cells produced mRNA acetylation that was predominantly restricted to CCG contexts11. To understand the basis of this discrepancy, it is important to consider NAT10 dependency on adapters for substrate targeting. Specifically, NAT10 interaction with THUMPD1 facilitates acetylation of tRNASer and tRNALeu in human cells, whereas its action on 18S rRNA is mediated by the Box C/D snoRNA SNORD1325. snoRNAs are particularly adept guides that target RNA-modifying enzymes to diverse substrates in a variety of sequence contexts34. Within this framework, preferential acetylation in CCG contexts following THUMPD1 overexpression likely reflects the adapter’s specific targeting preferences rather than a limitation of mRNA to undergo ac4C modification11.With these considerations in mind, we revisited our analysis to identify any overlooked sequence biases in a motif-centric evaluation. In specific, we mapped CDS ac4C sites to specific codons and examined for enrichment relative to codon frequency in acetylated transcripts. In so doing, we observed biased detection on specific codons, particularly in cases punctuated with a CG dinucleotide (Fig. 6A). Biased occurrence of ac4C within cellular mRNA is strongly indicative of biological regulation, rather than random technical error. Sources of inconsistencies in ac4C detection through reduction and sequencingMultiple factors, both biological and technical, could contribute to discrepancies in mismatch penetrance at ac4C sites. Biologically, mRNA acetylation may exhibit stochastic fluctuation, affecting the levels at specific substrates within cellular pools. Technically, variations in reduction efficiency, RNA fragmentation, and cDNA synthesis could all contribute to the differential occurrence of C:T mismatches at ac4C sites. In addition, general sequencing error can influence detection of ac4C in downstream data processing. A basic understanding of how these determinants are influenced by protocol specific parameters, including the choice of reducing agent, may aid the proper interpretation of divergent findings across studies. To begin to resolve inconsistencies between the RedaC:T-seq and ac4C-seq studies, we highlight a few key points here for consideration.Reduction efficiency.A primary limitation in detecting ac4C through existing chemical methods is associated with the efficiency of its reduction to tetrahydro-ac4C. As highlighted above, for protocols involving NaBH4, this capacity is restricted by off-target deacetylation in the basic reaction conditions12. In contrast, NaCNBH3 reduction occurs under acidic conditions, protecting ac4C from this off-target effect in ac4C-seq workflows11. However, there could be other factors that hinder conversion to tetrahydro-ac4C when using NaCNBH3. For instance, we were unable to achieve efficient NaCNBH3-mediated reduction of acetylated spike-ins under conditions that effectively reduced 18S rRNA (Fig. 3A). Given that ac4C facilitates the formation of structures and NaCNBH3 reduction is carried out at 20°C, inefficient denaturation of structures and consequent occlusion of ac4C represents a possible source of this discrepancy. Notably, a recent study pointed to abundance as an additional factor that could mitigate the utility of NaCNBH3 for ac4C mapping in cellular RNA: while abundant C:T mismatches were observed in a spike-in that was included at a 1:1 ratio, no mismatches were observed in the same sequence spiked-in at a 1:5000 ratio17. The authors thus concluded that ac4C-seq was insufficient for mapping ac4C in mRNA.Crucially, when analyzing C:T mismatch frequency in acetylated spike-ins used in the ac4C-seq study by Schwartz and colleagues, we noted significant variability in efficiency across replicates. The authors reported employing four synthetic RNA spike-ins, each containing a single ac4C within distinct sequence contexts (ACA, GCA, UCA, and ACU), and devoid of other cytidines11. Notably, among the four sequences, only reads associated with the ‘ACA’ context were recovered. Whether the inability to detect the remaining spike-ins relates to their selective loss during processing, or insufficient sequencing depth (expanded upon below) is unclear. However, based solely on the ‘ACA’ spike-in, we uncovered a stark variability in C:T mismatch detection, ranging from 0.69% to 32.45% across three wildtype replicates, and 1.83% to 36.25% across two NAT10−/− replicates (Figs. 3B, 3C). As these spike-ins were added to isolated RNA to control for reduction efficiency and sequencing quality, the expectation is that mismatch rates should be comparable between conditions.Together, these findings suggest that under conditions where borohydride efficiently reduces ac4C in abundant 18S rRNA, it may fail to reduce ac4C within less abundant mRNAs, highlighting the importance of exercising caution when drawing conclusions from negative results. Furthermore, considering the variability observed amongst the ac4C-seq study spike-ins, pooling reads, as in our RedaC:T-seq analysis, emerges as a justified approach to buffer against intra-sample variability in ac4C reduction efficiency.Fragmentation.An additional distinction between NaBH4 and NaCNBH3 reduction of RNA relates to their impacts on RNA integrity. Alkaline hydrolysis is a natural byproduct of the reaction conditions employed in NaBH4 reduction18, resulting in RNA that is fragmented to a size that is directly used for sequencing library construction with no perceptible loss in overall RNA levels (Fig. 3D). Sources of inconsistencies in ac4C detection through reduction and sequencing

Multiple factors, both biological and technical, could contribute to discrepancies in mismatch penetrance at ac4C sites. Biologically, mRNA acetylation may exhibit stochastic fluctuation, affecting the levels at specific substrates within cellular pools. Technically, variations in reduction efficiency, RNA fragmentation, and cDNA synthesis could all contribute to the differential occurrence of C:T mismatches at ac4C sites. In addition, general sequencing error can influence detection of ac4C in downstream data processing. A basic understanding of how these determinants are influenced by protocol specific parameters, including the choice of reducing agent, may aid the proper interpretation of divergent findings across studies. To begin to resolve inconsistencies between the RedaC:T-seq and ac4C-seq studies, we highlight a few key points here for consideration.

Reduction efficiency.A primary limitation in detecting ac4C through existing chemical methods is associated with the efficiency of its reduction to tetrahydro-ac4C. As highlighted above, for protocols involving NaBH4, this capacity is restricted by off-target deacetylation in the basic reaction conditions12. In contrast, NaCNBH3 reduction occurs under acidic conditions, protecting ac4C from this off-target effect in ac4C-seq workflows11. However, there could be other factors that hinder conversion to tetrahydro-ac4C when using NaCNBH3. For instance, we were unable to achieve efficient NaCNBH3-mediated reduction of acetylated spike-ins under conditions that effectively reduced 18S rRNA (Fig. 3A). Given that ac4C facilitates the formation of structures and NaCNBH3 reduction is carried out at 20°C, inefficient denaturation of structures and consequent occlusion of ac4C represents a possible source of this discrepancy. Notably, a recent study pointed to abundance as an additional factor that could mitigate the utility of NaCNBH3 for ac4C mapping in cellular RNA: while abundant C:T mismatches were observed in a spike-in that was included at a 1:1 ratio, no mismatches were observed in the same sequence spiked-in at a 1:5000 ratio17. General sequencing integrity.

Though C:T transitions represent the dominant mismatch type in our RedaC:T-seq results by a substantial margin, other mismatch types showed a statistical enrichment in wildtype as compared to NAT10−/− results (Fig. 1A)3. However, they do not substantiate this claim with evidence or provide insight into why such deamination would disproportionately affect wildtype over NAT10−/− RNA.

To distinguish the impact of general sequencing errors from specific effects in HeLa RedaC:T-seq and ac4C-seq analyses, we assessed per nucleotide error rates in sequences aligned to residual 18S and 28S rRNA. The advantage of this approach is two-fold: 1) sequencing coverage at rRNA is extensive, resulting in >100,000 reads per molecule, per condition and 2) locations and levels of modified RNA nucleotides are well-defined for both molecules (Tables S2 and S3). Given that various RNA modifications are sensitive to borohydride reduction, knowledge of their locations is particularly useful for discriminating general sequencing error from base-specific reduction19–22.

Focusing first on our RedaC:T-seq results and canonical nucleotides, mismatch levels were overall low and generally fell below the 1.25% cut-off threshold applied in our analytical pipeline (Figs. 4A, 4B). Rare exceptions are illustrated as outliers in the 18S rRNA results and are not restricted to either the wildtype or NAT10−/− condition (Fig. 4A). Direct comparison of aggregate data from the 18S and 28S rRNA results reinforces this observation: median mismatch rates were consistently below 0.2%, C:T mismatches included, and showed no particular increase in wildtype compared to NAT10−/− results (Fig. S2A). In brief, the authors expressed concern about whether intrinsic differences between wildtype and NAT10−/− HeLa and between specific mismatch types were adequately considered during basal error rate correction. Accordingly, we reevaluated our RedaC:T-seq results through a rigorous approach that calculates and corrects for intrinsic mismatch-specific error based on input RNA-sequencing results from wildtype and NAT10−/− HeLa. As the NAT10−/− cells were derived from our wildtype HeLa, we previously relied on wildtype input for SNP correction and established thresholds based on an average detected error rate of 0.8%3. However, inputs from both cell types were sequenced in parallel, allowing us to retroactively error correct in a cell-specific manner. In brief, trimmed reads from untreated inputs were aligned to the human genome in a strand-sensitive manner. Inspection of absolute mismatch numbers in locations that exceeded a minimum of 10 overlapping reads at this unfiltered state revealed a range of mismatches that were roughly preserved in wildtype and NAT10−/− results (Fig. 5A). Importantly, C:T transitions were not the most abundant mismatch type in untreated inputs, establishing that downstream enrichment does not reflect a general bias in the RNA-sequencing results (Fig. 5A).

We next examined the distribution of mismatch rates in wildtype and NAT10−/− HeLa input to determine type-specific basal error rates. To mitigate the occurrence of false mismatch detection in the NaBH4 treated results, mismatches with rates that fell below the 75th percentile observed in untreated input were omitted from further analysis (Fig. S3, red lines). This represented a range of 0.51–1.36% for specific mismatch types with C:T cutoff values falling centrally at 0.81 and 0.85% in wildtype and NAT10−/− results, respectively (Fig. S3). All mismatches that fell below these thresholds in the NaBH4-treated results were eliminated from further consideration. The remaining sites were next directly compared to wildtype and NAT10−/− HeLa input to remove locations that showed substantial evidence for mismatches in analogous untreated inputs. To account for intrinsic RNA features that increase error rate, we conservatively selected the median of the top quartile in input sequencing as the maximum tolerated error in input. This resulted in a range of 1.18–3.78% for specific mismatch types, or 1.95% and 2.09% for C:T mismatches in wildtype and NAT10−/− results (Fig. S3, green lines). Of note, Georgeson and Schwartz identified a single SNP that passed through our previous filters. Upon assessing for the root cause, we determined that this was due to an alternative haplotype sequence misalignment and represented an isolated case. We confirm that alternative haplotype misalignments have been corrected in the reanalysis presented here.

Mismatches that persisted through these elimination filters and exceeded a minimal prevalence of >1.25% in the pooled NaBH4-treated results were next examined for their prevalence in wildtype versus NAT10−/− results. Basal examination of absolute abundance of error-corrected and thresholded sites confirms the marked bias towards increased C:T mismatch detection as compared to all other mismatch types (Fig. 5B). Importantly and contrary to the assertion by Georgeson and Schwartz that mismatches are globally elevated in NaBH4-treated wildtype sequencing results, detection of select mismatches was in fact increased in absolute abundance in the treated NAT10−/− results (Fig. 5B). However, G:T mismatches in the wildtype treated results remained high (Fig. 5B). This could reflect insufficient filtering given the lower basal stoichiometry and higher overall occurrence in input (Figs. 5A, S3), or bona fide NaBH4 reactivity with other modified residues in mRNA, as demonstrated for m7G (Fig. 4E). Though NAT10−/− cells were employed as a negative control to pinpoint ac4C sites, we recognize that outside this focused objective, NAT10−/− and wildtype HeLa cells exhibit numerous differences that could affect broader research aims3. Significantly, transcripts with CDS-ac4C, including several mRNAs encoding RNA modifying enzymes, are selectively depleted in NAT10−/− cells3. Thus, the statistical skew towards increased mismatches in wildtype RedaC:T-seq may be biologically driven, reflecting changes in the distribution of borohydride-sensitive mRNA modifications following NAT10 depletion.

The results presented in Fig. 5B only examine numerical abundance and do not consider stoichiometry or statistical enrichment. To further assess for significance, Fisher’s exact test was performed and p-values were plotted against mismatch rate fold change at each location. Significant sites were determined based on the significance threshold (p-value) as well as magnitude (Fig. 5C). These results unambiguously demonstrate an overwhelming tendency towards increased C:T mismatch detection in wildtype relative to NAT10−/− results, and this trend is robust to both unadjusted and FDR adjusted p-values (Figs. 5D). To directly address the criticism by Georgeson and Schwartz that the bias is driven by a single wildtype replicate, we further examined whether the skew is observed if only considering locations with evidence of mismatches in both replicates. In so doing, we find the marked bias to increased C:T mismatch detection in wildtype relative to NAT10−/− RedaC:T-seq is preserved in the subset of replicated sites (Fig. 5E). Thus, in a detailed analysis that adheres to the rigorous error correction recommendations raised by Georgeson and Schwartz we reassert the presence of NAT10-dependent ac4C in HeLa mRNA.

Diverse sources of error. We show that the biased enrichment of C:T mismatches in wildtype HeLa mRNA persists in an error-sensitive RedaC:T-seq analysis defined through type-specific rates from untreated inputs. We additionally evaluate basal error after chemical reduction through analysis of 18S and 28S rRNA reads, and find exceedingly low rates at unmodified nucleotides for all mismatch types, including C:T, in HeLa RedaC:T-seq. These findings are inconsistent with a non-specific increase in C:T mismatches through deamination after NaBH4 treatment. In contrast, basal error analysis of the NaCNBH3-reduced ac4C-seq rRNA sequencing results reveals a pronounced predisposition towards mismatches involving thymine. Notably, Schwartz and colleagues did not apply specific error correction in the analysis of their ac4C-seq results.

Library complexity. High sequencing depth is required to comprehensively evaluate mRNA but can also result in reduced library complexity. As our goal in implementing RedaC:T was to localize ac4C to the 5’UTR versus CDS to complement acRIP-seq results, depth considerations were balanced against the overall objectives of the study, particularly considering the suboptimal efficiency of NaBH4 reduction. However, unique reads supporting C:T mismatches at ac4C sites are detected in both wildtype RedaC:T-seq replicates, and we do not detect a predilection for a higher incidence of identical reads in any particular sample.

Target mismatch rates. A RedaC:T-seq threshold of 1.25% was selected based on the suboptimal conversion efficiency at 18S rRNA, which failed to detect >75% of ac4C through C:T mismatches at fully acetylated sites. This threshold notably exceeds the median C:T mismatch rate of ~0.7% in the NaBH4-treated NAT10−/− RedaC:T-seq results and our functional analyses focused on mRNA sites with higher mismatch rates.

Balancing read depths. We are unaware of any statistical program that endorses the ad hoc removal of data. Notably, in both our HeLa RedaC:T-seq and the ac4C-seq published from Schwartz and colleagues, the treated wildtype samples produced substantially lower sequencing yields as compared to the treated NAT10−/− samples. This distinction suggests a biological basis that is potentially related to the presence of ac4C. We would further like to stress that though sequencing yields in the ac4C-seq study ranged from a minimum of 2.7M to a maximum of 14.5M reads, an effort was not made to balance the higher depth samples.

Sequence motifs. An abundance of evidence across evolution indicates that ac4C is not restricted to a specific motif. Thus, data fitting to a defined sequence is precluded. Instead, unbiased enrichment analysis reveals preferential contexts in mRNA that are enriched for CG dinucleotides, as we show for our RedaC:T-seq study, as well as a recent report that used ac4C-seq in hESCs.

Confirming mismatches. Our purpose for executing base-resolution mapping was to pinpoint ac4C to the 5’UTR or CDS, based on findings from an orthogonal method: acRIP-seq. We include the results here as Figure S5 as confirmatory evidence of the relationship between ac4C positioning in transcripts and mRNA translation. We also note that the statement by Georgeson and Schwartz that no studies in the literature have validated the occurrence of ac4C in mRNA through targeted amplification and sequencing is inaccurate. We cite several examples above.

Controls. Our study relied on NAT10−/− cells as a negative control to distinguish NAT10-dependent mismatches from other activities associated with NaBH4 reduction. Though Schwartz and colleagues achieved the same specificity through their NAT10−/− control, they endorse a chemically derived control that involves exposure of RNA to high pH to achieve ac4C deacetylation. In the absence of conclusive evidence that such conditions do not alter other modified or canonical RNA bases, we are unable to judge which method is superior. Georgeson and Schwartz further highlight the omission of NAT10−/− input sequencing in our RedaC:T-seq dataset. Though we did not use these inputs in our previous analysis, they were processed in parallel. We include the NAT10−/− inputs in our reanalysis presented here and find the results unchanged. Notably, despite stressing their importance, Schwartz and colleagues did not include NAT10−/− input sequencing in their own work.

In addition to the points raised by Georgeson and Schwartz, we would like to add the importance of considering biological variation. Our findings reveal that the wildtype HeLa cells cultured in our laboratory exhibit increased NAT10 expression and present genetic differences compared to those utilized by Schwartz and colleagues, offering a biological basis for discrepant ac4C detection. It is also conceivable that variations in cell culture conditions and other environmental factors will influence the distribution of the RNA acetylome.

In order to acquire the maximum sequence depth for putative SNP detection, input sample fastq files were merged in wildtype and NAT10−/− HeLa experiments. Sodium borohydride (NaBH4) treated replicates in both wildtype and NAT10−/− experiments, were processed separately. Adapters and Low-quality bases were removed using cutadapt based on settings previously described in Arango, et al., 202218. Trimmed reads were mapped to hg19 reference genome using STAR (2.7.9.a) with the following option settings: --outFilterMultimapNmax 10 --outFilterMultimapScoreRange 1 --outFilterScoreMin 10 --outFilterMismatchNmax 5 --alignEndsType Local --outSAMmapqUnique 60 --clip5pNbases 6,0. Strand-aware mismatch calling (library type=FR-FIRSTSTRAND) performed based on concordantly mapped fragments (Sam flags: 99, 147, 83 and 163) with minimum mapping quality of 10 (equivalent to ~10% error rate), using a custom python script. In case of overlapped fragment-mates, mismatch supported fragments (minimum base quality of 20) were counted once, and only mismatches supported on both mates were used in downstream analysis. Sequencing errors were identified based on the 75% quantile of mismatch-specific log2-based stoichiometry distributions in wildtype and NAT10−/− input samples. Mismatches in either NaBH4-treated and untreated input samples with higher stoichiometry than their defined thresholds in the previous step, were called valid mismatches and were used in downstream analysis. Valid mismatches with higher stoichiometry than their specific 50% quantile in wildtype and NAT10−/− input samples, were identified as SNPs and were removed from downstream analysis. Mismatches in NaBH4-treated samples from both wildtype and NAT10−/− experiments with minimum of a single supported fragment in all replicates and at least four supported fragments in one replicate, were used for enrichment analysis (Fisher’s exact test).
