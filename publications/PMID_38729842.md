Decades of work in developmental genetics has given us a deep mechanistic
understanding of the fundamental signaling pathways underlying animal
development. However, little is known about how these pathways emerged and
changed over evolutionary time. Here, we review our current understanding of the
evolutionary emergence of the Hippo pathway, a conserved signaling pathway that
regulates tissue size in animals. This pathway has deep evolutionary roots,
emerging piece by piece in the unicellular ancestors of animals, with a complete
core pathway predating the origin of animals. Recent functional studies in close
unicellular relatives of animals and early-branching animals suggest an
ancestral function of the Hippo pathway in cytoskeletal regulation, which was
subsequently co-opted to regulate proliferation and animal tissue size.

Thinking historically about signal transduction pathways

The computer scientist Gerald Weinberg, when describing the state of software, wrote that “things are the way they are because they got that way” [1]. This is true of all things, but is more profoundly true of biological phenomena such as signaling pathways controlling animal development, which have been shaped and changed over evolution through the forces of natural selection and genetic drift (see Glossary) [2]. Given that many developmental pathway components predate the emergence of animals [3], the evolutionary trajectory of these developmental pathways is likely complicated, with co-option of signaling modules into novel functions, involving contingencies that are not readily apparent without an understanding of these historical events. Thus, an understanding of the evolutionary emergence of such pathways could help explain, for instance, why and how signaling pathway architectures are arranged as they are, and thus may be of use when considering the best ways to target these pathways therapeutically.

In this review, we focus on the evolutionary emergence of the Hippo pathway, which was first elucidated in the fruit fly Drosophila due to its critical role in restricting tissue size (Figure 1) [4-6]. At its core, this pathway consists of the kinase Hippo (MST1/2 in mammals), which phosphorylates the kinase Warts (LATS1/2 in mammals), which phosphorylates and inactivates the transcriptional coactivator Yorkie (YAP/TAZ in mammals; for the sake of clarity and brevity, in this review, we use the Drosophila names of pathway components (i.e. Hippo, Warts, and Yorkie) when discussing this pathway in a general, not species-specific way, and use species-specific nomenclature when discussing specific orthologs.). Yorkie can form a complex with the transcription factor Sd (TEAD in mammals), which drives expression of genes that promote proliferation and inhibit apoptosis. Phosphorylation of Yorkie/YAP/TAZ by Warts/LATS causes cytoplasmic sequestration and/or degradation of the protein, thereby inhibiting its transcriptional activity and, in turn, limiting tissue growth. In the absence of Yorkie, Sd maintains pathway target genes in a state of default transcriptional repression in a manner that requires the transcriptional corepressor Tgi (VGLL4 in mammals) [7]. Although cytoskeletal tension [8-10], apical domain architecture [11], integrin-linked signaling [12, 13], and soluble signals [14] can regulate Hippo pathway activity, how Hippo signaling is physiologically regulated so as to generate a tissue of the appropriate characteristic size is still not well understood [15]. Upstream regulatory proteins such as Ex [16], Kibra [17], Mer/NF2 [18, 19], Fat [20], and AMOT [21] can regulate pathway activity, with some upstream regulators showing conservation between fly and mammals, and others with apparent regulatory activity in only one of these lineages [22, 23].

In the following review, we discuss recent work showing an ancient origin of the core kinase module of the Hippo pathway, which was subsequently incorporated into the canonical Hippo pathway with the evolutionary emergence of Yorkie. We describe new evidence indicating that the ancestral function of this pathway was not regulation of proliferation, but of cytoskeletal dynamics, and that this pathway function predated the emergence of animals. Finally, we speculate about how the Hippo pathway acquired the ability to regulate cell proliferation, where in the lineage leading to animals this co -option occurred, and the degree to which this involved changes in gene regulatory activity such as the emergence of transcriptional enhancers.

Ancient origin and deep conservation of the Hippo pathway kinase modulePhylogenomic analyses have revealed that, among the core Hippo pathway components, the Hippo kinase appears most ancient, and is present in amoebozoans such as Dictyostelium discoideum and Acanthamoeba castellanii [24,25] (Figure 2). Interestingly, a Warts-like kinase has not been detected in amoebozoans. Many fungi, which are more closely related to animals than amoebozoans, also possess a Hippo kinase ortholog. However, unlike the amoebozoans, the Hippo-like kinases in most fungal lineages do not contain a SARAH domain, which is known to mediate critical protein-protein interactions in animal Hippo signaling [26]. Thus, these fungal proteins have been suggested to not be “true” Hippo orthologs [25]. The earliest-branching organisms with a reported Warts kinase ortholog are fungi and the slime mold Fonticula alba, although a Hippo-like kinase has been lost in the latter [24].Despite the incompleteness of the Hippo pathway in these unicellular organisms, the Hippo- or Warts-like kinases in these species may show a remarkable ability to perform the same biochemical functions as their animal counterparts. For example, the nuclear localization of YAP/TAZ in MST1/2- or LATS1/2-deficient mammalian cells can be rescued by the Acanthamoeba Hippo ortholog or the Fonticula Warts ortholog, respectively [24]. Interestingly, the Fonticula Warts induces stronger phosphorylation of YAP than LATS1 does both in vivo and in vitro [24]. Although such heterologous expression experiments can be difficult to interpret given the different cellular milieu in which these two Warts orthologs normally exist, this observation hints that LATS activity is more repressed than Fonticula Warts, and that studying the differences of these kinases may reveal how this repression occurs. Altogether, these studies reveal ancient conservation of Hippo pathway kinase activity, showing that the ancestral Hippo pathway kinases were already competent to perform the biochemical activities that underlie the regulation of animal tissue size.What may have been the function of these kinases before the establishment of the canonical Hippo pathway? Some functional data is available in the fungi S. cerevisiae and S. pombe, with the caveat that the Hippo-like kinases in these organisms have lost the SARAH domain, and thus have likely lost some linked function or regulation (Figure 2). In S. pombe, the Hippo- and Warts-like kinases regulate the septation initiation network (SIN) pathway, which ensures that septation, the physical partitioning of a cell into two daughter cells, only occurs after the completion of mitosis (reviewed in [27]). In S. cerevisiae, these kinases are part of the mitotic exit network (MEN), which is required for the completion of mitosis [28]. The direct target of the MEN kinase module is the phosphorylation of the phosphatase Cdc14 [29]. In contrast to Yorkie, which is inactivated by phosphorylation, Cdc14 phosphorylation is an activating modification [29]. This activation of Cdc14 leads to dephosphorylation and inactivation of CDK1, facilitating mitotic exit. Of note, the MEN pathway does not appear to be conserved outside of fungi, as orthologs of the MEN kinase module and Cdc14 are dispensable for mitotic exit in Drosophila [30] and human cells [31]. Consistent with this notion, the S. cerevisiae MEN pathway components Tem1p (a GTPase) [32] and Nud1p (a scaffolding protein) [33] do not have clear mammalian orthologs, highlighting that a Hippo-like kinase module may be subjected to distinct regulators and fulfil distinct functions in different evolutionary lineages.

TEAD1/TEF-1, the first characterized TEAD protein, was identified as a regulator of simian virus 40 gene expression [35] and binds the canonical DNA motif 5’-CATTCC-3’ through the highly conserved TEA/ATTS domain [36]. Subsequently, TEAD family members were identified in Drosophila, the filamentous fungus A. nidulans, and S. cerevisiae (Figure 2), and were found to regulate wing development, [37], sporulation [38], and pseudohyphal growth [39], respectively. The fact that Sd/TEAD apparently predates Yorkie in evolutionary history suggests a model in which the Hippo-Warts kinase module and a TEAD transcription factor functioned in unlinked signaling modules, but with the evolutionary emergence of Yorkie these two modules were united, thus establishing a novel pathway linking upstream and downstream signaling components. Consistent with this model, we are aware of no common functionality of the Hippo-Warts kinase module and TEAD proteins in organisms lacking Yorkie.How may have Yorkie emerged in evolutionary history? A conserved Yorkie domain architecture across species consists of an N-terminal TEAD-binding domain (TBD) followed by tandem WW domains, which mediate Yorkie interaction with the Warts kinase [5] as well as chromatin-remodeling factors such as Ncoa6 [40]. The Nedd4 family ubiquitin ligase and the splicing factor Prp40 also contain tandem WW domains homologous to those found in Yorkie and are present in yeast, such as S. cerevisiae and S. pombe [41], and therefore predate Yorkie. Thus, a plausible model of the origin of Yorkie is duplication of a segment from an ancestral gene with tandem WW domains, with a de novo origin of the TBD within the new open reading frame. Yorkie appears to be restricted to holozoans, a clade of organisms comprising animals and their closest unicellular relatives but excluding fungi and other more basal unicellular eukaryotes [42] (Figure 2). Until recently, unicellular holozoans had been genetically intractable. However, parallel efforts in multiple unicellular holozoans have established techniques such as transgene expression, gene knockout, and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing [43-50]. As many of these organisms encode genes associated with animal developmental pathways, these technical advances open the door to understanding the evolutionary emergence of signaling modules such as the Hippo pathway. The earliest-branching holozoan possessing a reported Yorkie ortholog is Capsaspora owczarzaki, a close unicellular relative of animals with a round cell body covered with microvilli-like projections [51]. Capsaspora was originally identified as an endosymbiont of the snail B. glabrata [51] and can locomote with an actin-based amoeboid motility while adhered to a surface [46], having lost flagella and flagella-associated genes [52]. Capsaspora cells can aggregate into stable multicellular structures mediated by calcium-dependent adhesion [53, 54], suggesting that multicellular behaviors have been selected for in this lineage. An ortholog of the transcriptional corepressor Tgi/VGLL4, which interacts with Sd/TEAD and maintains Hippo pathway target genes in a state of default repression, is also present in the Capsaspora genome [7, 55] (Figure 2). In animals, the TBD of Yorkie/YAP/TAZ consists of two α-helical regions (α1 and α2) joined by a flexible linker [57]. Both of these helices show conservation in coYki [58], with the strongest conservation in residues found to be critical for YAP-TEAD interaction [57], although the presence of multiple helix-disrupting proline residues in the coYki α1 helix suggests that the predominant conserved binding interface may be the α2 helix [59]. Two choanoflagellates used as model organisms are Salpingoeca rosetta, which displays such multicellular behaviors, and Monosiga brevicollis, which has not been reported to display multicellularity [64]. Further, the M. brevicollis Hippo gene contains an insertion that is predicted to truncate the protein at the beginning of the kinase domain [65]. Thus M. brevicollis may be an example of an organism that encodes a Yorkie ortholog but has lost the capacity to be regulated by Hippo pathway signaling. Despite the fact that S. rosetta is more closely related to animals than Capsaspora, the srYki TBD shows less conservation with Yorkie/YAP/TAZ than coYki, with srYki lacking conserved residues within the α2 helix that are critical for YAP-TEAD interaction in mammals [57, 58]. The mbYki shows even less conservation, with an apparent absence of the α2 helix [58]. The degree to which choanoflagellate Yorkie orthologs interact with Sd/TEAD proteins is therefore currently unclear; interaction studies or functional studies of these proteins may clarify this possibility, as these organisms have recently been made genetically tractable [48, 49].

A putative ancestral function: Hippo pathway regulation of actin dynamics in early-branching animals and close unicellular relativesGiven that the evolutionary emergence of the Hippo pathway preceded the origin of animals, the ancestral function of this pathway clearly was not the regulation of the size of animal tissues. What then may have been the original ancestral function of this pathway, and how did it acquire the additional role of tissue size regulation? To address these questions, we have developed Capsaspora into a tractable genetic model and have examined Hippo pathway function in this organism. Targeted deletion of coYki does not affect cell proliferation under any condition examined [46], suggesting the surprising conclusion that the Hippo pathway, which is a key regulator of cell proliferation in animal development, does not significantly regulate cell proliferation in this unicellular organism. However, loss of coYki profoundly affects the cytoskeletal dynamics of Capsaspora cells: mutant cells undergo constant ectopic blebbing ([46], Figure 3A), indicating aberrant cytoskeleton organization at the cell cortex. Further, these cytoskeletal defects underlie changes in multicellular aggregate morphology: mutant aggregates are more flat and asymmetric than WT aggregates, and the cellular actomyosin network is required for this aberrant morphology [46]. Consistent with this result, targeted deletion of either Capsaspora Hippo (coHpo) or Capsaspora Warts (coWts) does not increase cell proliferation, but greatly increases the rate of occurrence of an actomyosin-mediated contractile behavior displayed by cells [66] (Figure 3A). Although it is important to keep in mind that Capsaspora is not the unicellular ancestor of animals, and is just as “evolved” as humans, these results suggest that the ancestral function of the Hippo pathway in unicellular organisms may have involved regulation of the cytoskeleton and not cell proliferation.Additional recent studies of the Hippo pathway, particularly in early-branching animals, have also provided evidence for a role for cytoskeletal regulation by the Hippo pathway. Hydra vulgaris is a freshwater cnidarian known for its remarkable regenerative abilities [67]. The hydra body plan consists of a cylindrical tube composed of an outer layer of ectodermal epithelial cells, an inner layer of endodermal epithelial cells, and a jelly-like matrix called mesoglea between these epithelia [68]. One end of this tube (the aboral end) can anchor to a substrate, while the other end (the oral end) terminates in a mouth surrounded by food-transporting tentacles. Epithelial cells generate contractile forces through actomyosin filamentous structures located at the basal end of epithelial cells called muscle processes (or myonemes) [69]. The Hydra genome encodes a Yorkie ortholog that is structurally homologous with human YAP and Drosophila Yorkie, including an N-terminal TEAD-binding domain, two WW domains, and three conserved HXRXXS Warts phosphorylation motifs [70] (Figure 2).shRNA-mediated genetic studies of the Hydra Yorkie/YAP and Warts/LATS orthologs (hyYAP and hyLATS) have revealed a critical role for the Hippo pathway in Hydra morphology and cytoskeletal architecture. A putative ancestral function: Hippo pathway regulation of actin dynamics in early-branching animals and close unicellular relatives

Given that the evolutionary emergence of the Hippo pathway preceded the origin of animals, the ancestral function of this pathway clearly was not the regulation of the size of animal tissues. What then may have been the original ancestral function of this pathway, and how did it acquire the additional role of tissue size regulation? To address these questions, we have developed Capsaspora into a tractable genetic model and have examined Hippo pathway function in this organism. Targeted deletion of coYki does not affect cell proliferation under any condition examined [46], suggesting the surprising conclusion that the Hippo pathway, which is a key regulator of cell proliferation in animal development, does not significantly regulate cell proliferation in this unicellular organism. However, loss of coYki profoundly affects the cytoskeletal dynamics of Capsaspora cells: mutant cells undergo constant ectopic blebbing ([46], Figure 3A), indicating aberrant cytoskeleton organization at the cell cortex. Further, these cytoskeletal defects underlie changes in multicellular aggregate morphology: mutant aggregates are more flat and asymmetric than WT aggregates, and the cellular actomyosin network is required for this aberrant morphology [46]. Consistent with this result, targeted deletion of either Capsaspora Hippo (coHpo) or Capsaspora Warts (coWts) does not increase cell proliferation, but greatly increases the rate of occurrence of an actomyosin-mediated contractile behavior displayed by cells [66] (Figure 3A). Although it is important to keep in mind that Capsaspora is not the unicellular ancestor of animals, and is just as “evolved” as humans, these results suggest that the ancestral function of the Hippo pathway in unicellular organisms may have involved regulation of the cytoskeleton and not cell proliferation.

Additional recent studies of the Hippo pathway, particularly in early-branching animals, have also provided evidence for a role for cytoskeletal regulation by the Hippo pathway. Hydra vulgaris is a freshwater cnidarian known for its remarkable regenerative abilities [67]. The hydra body plan consists of a cylindrical tube composed of an outer layer of ectodermal epithelial cells, an inner layer of endodermal epithelial cells, and a jelly-like matrix called mesoglea between these epithelia [68]. One end of this tube (the aboral end) can anchor to a substrate, while the other end (the oral end) terminates in a mouth surrounded by food-transporting tentacles. Epithelial cells generate contractile forces through actomyosin filamentous structures located at the basal end of epithelial cells called muscle processes (or myonemes) [69]. The Hydra genome encodes a Yorkie ortholog that is structurally homologous with human YAP and Drosophila Yorkie, including an N-terminal TEAD-binding domain, two WW domains, and three conserved HXRXXS Warts phosphorylation motifs [70] (Figure 2).

Co-option of Hippo signaling for proliferation inhibition and tissue size controlIf the ancestral function of the Hippo pathway was regulation of the actomyosin cytoskeleton, when and how did the pathway gain the ability to regulate proliferation and tissue size? Remarkably Yorkie seems to be absent from two species of ctenophores, the earliest-branching animal lineage [74], despite the presence of most upstream Hippo pathway components [75] (Figure 2). To our knowledge all other available animal genomes encode a Yorkie ortholog, so this finding indicates that ctenophores uniquely do not possess a complete core Hippo pathway. In the jellyfish Clytia hemisphaerica, immunostaining of a Yorkie ortholog (CheYki) shows nuclear CheYki localization in actively proliferating cell populations and cytoplasmic CheYki elsewhere [75], although no functional data for this protein is available. In Hydra, knockdown of hyYAP causes reduced proliferation, but knockdown of hyLATS does not increase proliferation despite causing an increase in nuclear hyYAP [70]. Thus whether upstream Hippo pathway components regulate proliferation in Hydra is unclear. Loss of function studies of a C. elegans Yorkie ortholog, Yap-1, report no effects on cell proliferation following either RNAi knockdown [76] or genetic mutation of Yap-1 [77], but indicate a role for the C. elegans Hippo pathway in apicobasal polarity [77] and thermotolerance [76]. A Yorkie ortholog (SMED-YKI) regulates proliferation in the planarian Schmidtea mediterranea, but in a manner opposite to what might be expected based on studies in other model systems, as SMED-YKI was reported to restrict proliferation during regeneration [78]. The adoption of proliferation control by the Hippo pathway could be an example of exaptation, or the taking on of a novel function by an already established structure or trait. The actomyosin cytoskeleton is not only a target but also a key upstream regulator of Hippo pathway in animals ranging from Drosophila to mammals [15, 79]. Particularly, actomyosin functions as a key mediator of Hippo regulation by mechanical signals. In an epithelium, cells are in constant mechanical interaction with their neighbors, being either stretched or compressed by surrounding cells. Epithelial cells accordingly remodel their cytoskeleton to adapt to their mechanical surroundings, becoming more flattened with a large apical surface when stretched or more columnar with a small apical surface when compressed. A large or small apical surface indicates a low or high local cell density, which correlates with higher or lower Yki/YAP/TAZ activity respectively [11, 80, 81] that could in turn further regulate cell proliferation as well as the cytoskeleton as a response to the mechanical surroundings. Thus, with the incorporation of the actomyosin cytoskeleton as an upstream Hippo pathway signal, coordination among cell density, cell shape, and cell proliferation can be maintained to ensure proper epithelial tissue size and integrity. It seems plausible that this regulatory mechanism may have emerged in early epithelia and played an important role in tissue size regulation in the animal lineage. Thus, we propose that the adoption of proliferation control by the Hippo pathway could be an example of exaptation. A related open question is whether cytoskeletal signals regulate Hippo pathway activity in unicellular organisms.Mechanistically how could the Hippo pathway gain the novel function of proliferation control? One possibility involves the evolutionary emergence of distal enhancer elements in the animal lineage. Distal enhancers are regulatory elements that can be located kilobases to megabases away from their regulatory targets and show characteristic chromatin marks such as H3K4me1 and H3K27ac [82]. Analyses in human cell lines reveal that the majority of YAP/TAZ binding sites are located in distal enhancers [83, 84]. In contrast, distal enhancers appear to be absent in Capsaspora, as cis-regulatory elements are overwhelmingly located close to transcriptional start sites and lack the distinguishing chromatin regulatory marks of enhancers [85]. Thus the evolutionary emergence of the Hippo pathway seems to have predated that of enhancer-mediated gene regulation. It may be the case that in the linage leading from unicellular organisms to Bilateria, the emergence of distal regulatory machinery opened up new gene regulatory opportunities for Yorkie, which provided a rich source of material for selection to act on and acquire a new function. Even in non-proliferative tissues, the Hippo pathway has been shown to regulate terminal differentiation [86] and innate immune response [87]. Thus, the Hippo pathway appears to have acquired diverse functions that can vary in different cell and tissue types. How such functional diversification has occurred is not well understood and remains an interesting and important question.

Concluding RemarksIt can be tempting to consider biological traits such as signaling pathways as being purpose-built for the functions that we observe them performing in biological systems. In this review, we have attempted to show that this is not the case: the Hippo pathway, which regulates tissue size and oncogenesis in animals, has deep roots in evolutionary history and appears to have been assembled piece by piece from components that performed functions unrelated to tissue size control. The core components of the pathway were brought together before the emergence of animals by linking two apparently unrelated signaling modules through the genesis of the transcriptional coactivator Yorkie (Figure 4A and B). Studies in close unicellular relatives suggest that this pathway may have functioned to regulate the cytoskeleton in unicellular ancestors of animals, a function it likely also performed in early animals as suggested by studies in early-branching animals such as Hydra. Subsequently the pathway was co-opted to regulate proliferation in the lineage leading to bilaterian animals while retaining its function in cytoskeletal regulation (Figure 4C).Two aspects of the evolutionary emergence of Hippo signaling remain unclear. First, to what degree are upstream regulators of the Hippo pathway functionally conserved? Many upstream regulators of the Hippo pathway in animals are also found in close unicellular relatives: NF2/Merlin, Kibra, and aPKC in Capsaspora, and Salvador and Fat in choanoflagellates [25]. Determination of whether these proteins also regulate Hippo signaling in unicellular organisms would not only further clarify the evolutionary history of this pathway but could establish relatively simple systems for studying these regulators, some of which are well-established tumor suppressors that can drive oncogenesis in humans when mutated. Second, what role may Hippo pathway-mediated cytoskeletal regulation have played in the morphology and morphogenesis of the earliest animals? Hippo signaling regulates actin dynamics and, in turn, the structure of simple multicellular Capsaspora aggregates [46]. In animals, Hippo signaling affects the morphology of the Hydra body plan through cytoskeletal effects [70], and in medaka fish, mutation of YAP causes a flattened body morphology due to actomyosin defects [88]. An intriguing possibility is that morphological control mediated by the cytoskeleton and regulated by the Hippo pathway is a conserved process in Capsaspora and animals, and thus that Hippo signaling may have shaped the morphology of the earliest animals. Alternatively, even if regulation of multicellular shape had independent origins in Capsaspora and animals, the fact that Hippo signaling can regulate multicellular shape in Capsaspora suggests that it may have been “poised” to regulate multicellular morphology during the evolutionary emergence of animals. A more complete understanding of the properties of the unicellular ancestors of animals and the earliest animals will help to evaluate these hypotheses (see Outstanding questions).
