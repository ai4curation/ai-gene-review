A key application of CRISPR/Cas9-based genomic editing is modification of genes
to introduce engineered sequences. However, the editing flexibility is severely
constrained by the requirement for targeting sites in proximity to the desired
modification site, which makes many modifications intractable. Here, we develop
a strategy that overcomes this key limitation to allow CRISPR-based editing at
any position with high efficiency. It relies on reconstructing the targeted gene
using Silently Mutate And Repair Template (SMART) where we mutate the gap
sequence in the repair template to prevent its base pairing with the target DNA
while maintaining the same amino acid coding. Using vertebrate retina as a
neuronal model system we document the application of SMART editing for labeling
endogenous proteins in vivo with high efficiency. We show that SMART editing
allows us to access numerous cell types in the retina and address fundamental
cell biological questions pertaining to its organization. We propose that this
approach will facilitate functional genomic studies in a wide range of systems
and increase the precision of corrective gene therapies.

Introduction

A vast number of proteins encoded in mammalian genomes remains poorly understood. An ability to modify endogenous proteins opens many avenues for interrogating their cellular roles in the native environment. In addition to mutations, the introduction of epitope tags greatly facilitates studies on cell-type specificity of expression and subcellular localization of proteins of interest. Antibodies are the most widely used tools for determining the expression and localization of proteins. However, their limited availability for unstudied proteins and poor performance with uncertain specificity amidst insufficient validation are endemic problems that hamper research progress. Having a convenient way to reliably label any endogenous protein in native tissues at any position with an epitope tag followed by their detection with validated universal antibodies will greatly aid cell biological studies and accelerate the understanding of understudied proteins at scale.

The advent of CRISPR-based technologies to gene modification revolutionized labeling endogenous proteins1–5. Several approaches have been developed that allow protein modification and the introduction of epitope tags6–10. These approaches utilize template-guided repair, relying on homology recombination following site-directed cleavage of DNA by the Cas9 enzyme. However, existing techniques have significant limitations when applied to labeling proteins in native neuronal tissues, including reliance on long-term genetic expression of Cas9, the need for delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 editing materials at embryonic stages of development, and low efficiency. Perhaps the most significant issue that detrimentally affects the efficiency of site-directed CRISPR/Cas9-based modifications is related to dependence on the location of the PAM site. Cas9 nuclease is most efficient when the gRNA is targeted in the vicinity of the PAM sequence1,11, and the efficiency of template-based editing drops precipitously as the DNA cut site is moved away from the insertion site12,13. Because the integration of sequences, such as epitope tags, is often inflexible in terms of their positioning within the protein, the selection of gRNAs is vastly limited, which substantially compromises the editing efficiency. Oftentimes, no effective gRNA could be designed to introduce modifications at the desired site, rendering powerful CRISPR technology useless.

In this report, we overcome this most significant limitation by innovating the principles of designing the repair template that makes it insensitive to the position of PAM sequences relative to the intervention site. We further leverage the use of RNP complexes, obviating the need to genetically express Cas9 and optimize conditions for their in vivo delivery in postnatal mice.

To validate our strategy, we perform genetic modifications in the mammalian retina, a sensory part of the central nervous system that mediates light reception. We demonstrate the ability to label various types of proteins in nearly all neuronal and non-neuronal cell types in the retina with high efficiency. We then use the technique to answer outstanding questions in retina organization, determine cell-type specificity of protein expression, and resolve the precise subcellular localization for a wide variety of proteins. We hope that the approach that we describe will prove widely useful for addressing many outstanding questions in neurobiology and beyond.

For example, epitope tags are usually introduced at the N- or C- termini of the protein to minimize the risks of disrupting its folding. This constrains the selection of gRNAs to be positioned in close proximity to the start or stop codons. This requires finding a nearby PAM site that determines the Cas9 cleavage site for the design of gRNA. The distance between the cleavage site and insertion site is critical for the recombination efficiency, and as it exceeds 10 base pairs, the knock-in efficiency decreases significantly12,17. We surveyed 145,442 putative modification sites randomly picked across the coding regions of the human genome to assess the availability of nearby PAM sites. Our analysis revealed that 8.2% of these sites lacked any PAM sites within 10 bp of the target location. On average, there are 3 PAM sites available per site, with two being the most common (Fig. 2a). Considering that for 75.7% of gRNAs on-target efficiency is lower than 60%18, our analysis suggests that ~50% of target sites lack efficient gRNAs (Fig. 2a). Using this pipeline, we optimized many parameters, including concentrations of each component, incubation temperature, and electroporation protocol. Under the most optimal conditions, we could achieve more than 40% delivery and almost 30% editing efficiencies (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). However, the use of high voltage compromised the viability of pups and was reduced to 105-volt level that still yielded 30% delivery efficiency and was well tolerated by mice.

Using this optimized protocol, we evaluated the timeline of construct expression and editing. We found that Lmnb1 can be correctly edited (Supplementary Fig. 1d) to express HA-Lamin-B1 at its expected nuclear localization (Supplementary Fig. 1e) without significantly affecting its expression level (Supplementary Fig. 1f) within ~1 day after surgery (Fig. 1d), indicating that the RNP approach enables fast genome editing in retinal progenitors. When retinas were harvested a week later (P8), a further increase in the efficiency was observed, suggesting that RNP particles persisted in the cells beyond one day and continued to edit more cells (Fig. 1e, g, h). However, we observed no significant increase in editing efficiency beyond this stage, and adult retinas displayed a similar number of edited cells (Fig. 1f–h), consistent with the quick degradation of RNP particles16. We found that there was a nearly perfect correlation between electroporation efficiency and knock-in efficiency (Fig. 1i), indicating that editing efficiency can be improved by optimizing the delivery of genetic material into cells.

Two in vitro assays were designed to evaluate the effectiveness of SMART strategies. To compare SMART-RC/CT with SMART, Nrxn3 was targeted by 8 different gRNAs with cut sites positioned 17–117 bp away from the stop codon (Fig. 4c). There are no closer PAM sites available for its C-terminus modification. An EcoRI site was integrated upstream of the stop codon using either SMART or SMART-RC/CT (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). The results show that SMART-RC/CT significantly improved the KI efficiency compared to SMART (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 5a). The highest KI efficiencies were observed with gRNA-17 and gRNA-85 when paired with SMART-RC/CT. NGS data further validated that SMART-RC/CT increased the proportion of desired editing among total edits (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). SMART-RC/CT enhanced the proportion of double-stranded breaks repaired by the HDR pathway likely by increasing the competition with the NHEJ/MMEJ pathways (Supplementary Fig. 6c) and improved the ratio of perfect KI to total KI of EcoRI sequence (Supplementary Fig. 6d). To explore whether SMART technology could handle large insertions, we inserted a 1,026-bp coding sequence for Cre recombinase, at the C terminus of Nrxn3 using gRNA-17. SMART and SMART-RC showed comparable editing efficiency (~14%), and double-stranded DNA templates were more efficient than single-stranded ones (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d).

To compare SMART-RC/NT to SMART, Nrl gene was targeted by 6 gRNAs with cut sites positioned 2–80 bp away from the start codon (Fig. 4d). An EcoRI sequence was added at the very N-terminus of Nrl using either SMART or SMART-RC/NT (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). The results show that SMART achieved higher KI efficiency than SMART-RC/NT when the cut site was within 28 bp of the start codon (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 5b). However, SMART-RC/NT provided higher editing efficiency when the distance exceeded 67 bp (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 5b). The regression analysis suggests that SMART and SMART-RC/NT have comparable editing efficiencies when the cut site is 37 bp from the start codon (Supplementary Fig. 6e), providing guidance on selecting the appropriate SMART design for use (Supplementary Fig. 7a). The highest KI efficiency, 50%, was achieved with gRNA-20 paired with SMART, suggesting that gRNAs farther from the modification site can be more effective for genome editing than those positioned closer.

To better guide the selection of gRNAs when using SMART, we performed a regression analysis studying how the predicted on-target score (Doench 2016 method23) and the cut-to-insert distance contribute to the editing efficiency when using SMART (Supplementary Fig. 6f, g). This leads us to suggest the following formula for gRNA selection: editing efficiency (%) = 7 + 0.36 * predicted on-target score − 0.1 * cut-to-insert distance. We further recommend trying at least two top-predicted gRNAs for achieving optimal results.

To further validate the SMART-RC/NT approach, we directly evaluated the P2A-mediated cleavage efficiency. For this purpose, double-tag repair templates were designed for Lmnb1, inserting the P2A sequence between Myc and HA tags (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). The results showed virtually no Myc tag presence following editing confirming efficient separation of the polypeptides by the P2A sequence and degradation of the Myc tag containing leader sequence (Supplementary Fig. 9c). This manipulation was specific as scrambling the P2A sequence in the repair template resulted in Myc and HA reactivity being simultaneously detected on nuclear Lamin-B1 (Supplementary Fig. 9d). Co-staining experiments revealed that protein labeling by SMART-RC/NT did not affect the localization and protein level of HA-tagged Lamin-B1 (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f).

In summary, we developed an approach that allows us to significantly increase the efficiency of the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-in at precise positions by eliminating the dependence on having a PAM site in the vicinity of the insertion site, thereby enabling a larger selection of gRNAs. Two versions of the SMART technology that we introduce offer further flexibility in designing repair templates depending on the distance between the gRNA cut site and sequence insertion site.

Several CRISPR-editing methods6–8,60 have been described including SLENDR6, which employs in utero electroporation to deliver single-stranded DNA repair templates and plasmids expressing Cas9 and gRNAs to the embryonic mouse brain for genome editing via the HDR pathway, as well as ORANGE60, which delivers plasmids or AAVs to cells for genome editing via the NHEJ pathway. While these methods differ in features and advantages (Supplementary Table 1), they are invariably limited by the availability of efficient gRNAs in the vicinity of the insertion site. SMART overcomes the limitation by silently mutating the sequence between cut and insertion sites in the repair template, thereby redesigning the targeted sequence. We devised several versions of the SMART design for various situations and needs and provided guidelines for the selection of a particular targeting strategy (Supplementary Table 1). These approaches should allow precise tailoring of sequence integration depending on the specifics of sequence features of the target gene.

A well-known limitation of CRISPR/Cas9-based knock-in is its application to post-mitotic cells, which is challenging due to the suppression of homologous recombination61–63 and nuclear envelope interference64,65. Previous solutions to circumvent it include targeting cells early in embryonic development, which oftentimes requires demanding in utero manipulations6 and the use of viruses to direct editing via NHEJ pathway60,66, which is prone to off-target labeling. Similarly, while SMART editing works very well in mitotic cells, we found it to be ineffective in post-mitotic neurons. To overcome these issues, we adopted a cell-fate reprogramming strategy in which we concurrently target transcription factors that determine cell fate, thereby converting the precursors of the cells in which editing had occurred into the cell type of choice. We applied this strategy to achieve efficient epitope tagging in traditional editing and gene delivery-resistant cone photoreceptors by converting rod precursors into cone-like neurons. In principle, this strategy should allow to expand the types of cells amenable to the manipulation as research on cell-fate determination and transcriptional programming advances. A caution needs to be exercised, however, with this approach as transcriptional reprogramming may not result in the neurons with the exact characteristic of the desired cell type, and significant characterization studies may need to be performed. Alternatively, we foresee a combination of SMART with Prime editing- a technique that does not rely on homology-directed repair67 or other Cas proteins, including Cas9 nickase68 and Cas12a69, to achieve efficient genome editing in post-mitotic cells.

In our study, we extensively demonstrated the utility of SMART editing for answering fundamental questions pertaining to the cell biological organization of the retina. For example, we documented the cell type of expression for an array of signaling proteins, determined precise subcellular localizations of signaling molecules, and resolved synaptic origins of trans-synaptic cell adhesion molecules. We expect that given its efficiency, SMART would become a mainstay tool for addressing similar issues across the nervous system and possibly in other tissues and organs. In addition, it is tempting to propose that SMART could be further adapted for the needs of gene therapy where precise and efficient manipulation with the target genes is required. As with protein tagging, targeted CRISPR/Cas gene therapy interventions aimed at correcting point mutations, deletions, or insertions70–72 are hinged on the availability of efficient gRNAs near the intervention site, which now can be solved with SMART editing, allowing a much broader range for gRNA selection. Thus, we envision the SMART approach to be a broadly applicable technique for addressing a wide range of genomic editing needs.

In silico analysisTo perform an in silico analysis assessing the frequency of cases with no effective gRNAs available within a 10 bp range of a site-specific modification, we first quantified the number of gRNAs available within this range for a given site. Setting the position of the target site as 0, we defined the cut site of a nearby gRNA to fall within the range of [−10, 10], where ‘−10’ represents 10 bp downstream of the target site, and ‘10’ represents 10 bp upstream.We observed that the number of nearby gRNAs corresponds to the number of ‘GG’ sites in the [−16, 6] range plus the number of ‘CC’ sites in the [−6, 16] range. Consequently, this analysis only requires the examination of a 32-nucleotide sequence flanking the target site. To facilitate high-throughput analysis, we randomly selected 32-nucleotide sequences from the human coding sequence dataset. Human coding sequences were obtained from NCBI, comprising a total of 145,504 sequences. From each sequence, a 32-nucleotide segment was randomly selected, while sequences shorter than 32 nucleotides were excluded. As a result, 145,442 sequences were included in the analysis, and the quantification of nearby gRNAs was performed on these sequences (Supplementary Data 2). Considering that for 75.7% of gRNAs on target, efficiency is lower than 60%, we calculated the percentage of the selected sites lacking any effective gRNAs. The results showed that approximately 50% of target sites lack effective gRNAs.

In silico analysis

To perform an in silico analysis assessing the frequency of cases with no effective gRNAs available within a 10 bp range of a site-specific modification, we first quantified the number of gRNAs available within this range for a given site. Setting the position of the target site as 0, we defined the cut site of a nearby gRNA to fall within the range of [−10, 10], where ‘−10’ represents 10 bp downstream of the target site, and ‘10’ represents 10 bp upstream.

Next-generation sequencing library preparation and analysis

The edited regions in target genes were amplified by PCR and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq. Amplification primers containing partial Illumina forward and reverse adapters (Supplementary Data 1) were used in the first round of PCR to amplify the edited regions. The first PCR reaction (25 µl) contained 12.5 µl of Q5 High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix (cat# M0492L), 0.5 µM forward and reverse primers, and 150 ng of genomic DNA. The PCR program was as follows: 98 °C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, optimized annealing temperature (Supplementary Data 1) for 30 s, and 72 °C for 15 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 2 min. PCR products were analyzed by gel electrophoresis, and concentrations were quantified.

In the second PCR, full-length Illumina adapters were added to each sample using NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (NEB, cat# E6440S). The second PCR reaction (50 µl) contained 25 µl of Q5 High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix, 0.125 µM index primers (higher concentrations resulted in amplification bias), and 1 ng of first-round PCR products. The program for the second PCR was as follows: 98 °C for 1.5 min; 12 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 72 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 20 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 2 min.

PCR products were analyzed by gel electrophoresis and purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-Up Kit. DNA concentration was measured using Qubit. All samples were pooled at an equimolar ratio and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform.

Sequencing reads were analyzed using Geneious software. Initially, the reads were trimmed with BBDuk, and reads with a quality score below 20 were discarded. Paired reads were then merged and mapped to a reference sequence. For quantification, the percentage of perfect HDR was calculated as the ratio of reads with the desired edit to the total number of reads. The percentages of imperfect HDR, indels, and unedited genes were quantified in the same manner.

Primers were designed to amplify the dsDNA repair templates, with one primer phosphorylated at the 5′ end and the other unphosphorylated. This resulted in PCR products that were dsDNA with a single phosphorylated 5′ end, which were subsequently digested by Lambda exonuclease (NEB, cat# M0262). The syringe used was a 10 µL Gastight Syringe from Hamilton (catalog #7653-01) with a 32-gauge, 10 mm needle (point style: 4, angle: 25 degrees; catalog #7803-04). After the injection, tweezer-shaped electrodes (7 mm Platinum Tweezertrodes, BTX450488) coated with electrode gel (Spectra 360, Parker Laboratories, Inc.) were gently positioned to clamp the pup’s head. Electroporation was initiated while moving the electrodes from dorsal to lateral direction in ~25° angle intervals every two squares. Ten square pulses (105 V, 60 ms duration, 1 s interval) were applied using a pulse generator (Electro Square Porator, ECM 830, BTX/Harvard Apparatus). Electroporated mice were warmed by a heat blanket until they awoke and then returned to their parents.
