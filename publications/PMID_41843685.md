Plasmalogens are a unique class of glycerophospholipids defined by a distinctive
vinyl ether bond. While these lipids are abundant in animals and important for
human health, their evolutionary history remains enigmatic, mostly due to their
absence in some major eukaryotic lineages like plants. Here, we resolve the
origin and evolution of the aerobic plasmalogen biosynthesis pathway in
eukaryotes. Through comprehensive phylogenomic analysis and experimental
validation of enzyme activity and plasmalogen presence, we demonstrate that the
essential desaturase plasmanylethanolamine desaturase 1 (PEDS1)-and likely the
fatty acyl-CoA reductase (FAR) and glycerone phosphate O-acyltransferase (GNPAT)
enzymes also critical in the pathway-were acquired by an early eukaryotic
ancestor through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) from myxobacteria. Our data show
that this bacterial pathway was retained in the Amorphea and Discoba supergroups
but lost or replaced in others. The findings yield insights into how HGT shaped
metabolic pathways in early eukaryotes.

## Full Text

Lipids are key components of cell membranes that determine their rigidity, fluidity, and function (1). Among these, plasmalogens are a unique class of glycerophospholipids (GPs) defined by a vinyl ether bond at the sn-1 position, distinct from the ester bond typically found in phospholipids (Fig. 1A) (1–3). This chemical feature endows plasmalogens with unique physical-chemical features that affect cellular membrane properties and likely underlie their roles in diverse biological functions (4–7). Plasmalogens can represent up to a fifth of the human phospholipidome and their altered levels are linked to serious conditions ranging from neurodegenerative diseases to cancer (4–10). Discovery of the enzyme responsible for the vinyl ether bond formation (Fig. 1B) (11–13) has enabled the identification of specific plasmalogen-mediated functions, which include a bacterial response to light (11), myeloid cell biology (10), and neurodevelopment (14). However, and despite their importance in animal biology, the evolutionary history of plasmalogens remains enigmatic.

Plasmalogens show a patchy distribution across the tree of life. They have been shown to be absent in archaea, plants, and fungi, yet present in animals, some protists, and certain bacteria (largely restricted among obligately aerobic bacteria to the phylum Myxococcota, and to some anaerobic and facultatively anaerobic bacteria) (5, 6, 11, 16, 17). Most strikingly, the biosynthesis of these lipids appears to have evolved twice (5, 18, 19). Anaerobic bacteria produce plasmalogens via a reductive pathway suited for an anoxic world, which involves conversion of an ester to a vinyl ether bond (5, 17, 18). In contrast, animals rely on a completely different, oxygen-dependent pathway to generate an alkyl ether bond and convert it into a vinyl ether one, involving four key enzymes (Fig. 1B): fatty acyl-CoA reductase (FAR), glycerone phosphate O-acyltransferase (GNPAT), alkylglycerone phosphate synthase (AGPS), and plasmanylethanolamine desaturase (PEDS1), the enzyme mentioned above that creates the vinyl ether bond (4–6, 11–13, 16).

The origin of this aerobic pathway in eukaryotes has been obscure. Indeed, the identity of PEDS1 was only recently unmasked, when it was demonstrated that M. xanthus CarF and its animal homologs, as well as those from the bacterial phylum Spirochaetota, all correspond to PEDS1; whereas proposed candidates in plants or Alphaproteobacteria were not functionally equivalent to PEDS1 (11). Moreover, and in addition to PEDS1/CarF, M. xanthus has potential FAR, GNPAT, and AGPS activities (6, 11, 15), mirroring the mammalian pathway (Fig. 1B). This resemblance raises a fundamental question: did eukaryotes evolve this pathway independently, or was it acquired from bacteria?

Besides the report mentioned above (11), few other studies have tried to shed light into the origin and evolution of the aerobic plasmalogen biosynthesis pathway in eukaryotes. A recent study (20) identified PEDS1 homologs in some members of the Holozoa, a clade that includes animals and their closest unicellular relatives (21, 22), in some genera of Amoebozoa and Excavata, and even in a few fungi. However, taxon sampling in these studies was limited, largely constraining the potential interpretations. Here, we investigate the origin of eukaryotic plasmalogen biosynthesis by mapping the distribution of the four key enzymes (FAR, GNPAT, AGPS, and PEDS1) across the tree of life. We combined comprehensive bioinformatic and phylogenetic analyses, using the broadest possible taxon sampling, with experimental validation of PEDS1 activity in diverse taxa. We identified a stark divide in biosynthetic capacity: the complete aerobic pathway is conserved in the Amorphea and Discoba supergroups but absent in others. Our phylogenetic reconstruction indicates that the genes for this pathway were acquired by an early eukaryotic ancestor via horizontal gene transfer (HGT) from myxobacteria. These findings reveal a bacterial origin for a hallmark animal biosynthesis pathway and highlight how HGT has shaped the metabolic versatility of eukaryotes.

ResultsDistribution of Plasmalogen Biosynthesis Pathway in Eukaryotes.To unravel the evolution of the eukaryotic plasmalogen biosynthesis pathway, we first need to map its distribution across the tree of eukaryotes. For this, we performed an extensive search for the four key enzymes (FAR, GNPAT, AGPS, and PEDS1) in a dataset of 376 high-quality genomes encompassing the whole breadth of the known eukaryotic diversity [(23); SI Appendix]. Detection of FAR, GNPAT, and AGPS would suggest an ability to produce plasmalogen precursors, and the additional presence of PEDS1 would suggest plasmalogen synthesis. For improved detection of distant homologs, we followed an iterative search algorithm that accounts for genome variations over evolutionary time and assigns higher rates to conserved positions when evaluating the hits. This allowed identification of functionally conserved regions even in proteins of low overall sequence similarity.Discoba.The plasmalogen biosynthesis pathway exhibited significant variation among the five Discoba groups analyzed (Fig. 2A and Datasets S1–S5). We identified PEDS1 in Heterolobosea and Kinetoplastea, whereas Jakobida, Euglenida, and Diplonemea lacked this desaturase. GNPAT hits in Heterolobosea, Kinetoplastea, and Euglenida were about twice the size of mammalian homologs due to a large ~650-residue N-terminal region not assigned to any known domain. Such an extension had been previously reported in the Trypanosomatidae family but with no precise function determined (24, 25). Since M. xanthus has a predicted GNPAT fused to an N-terminal FAR module (MXAN_1675 in Fig. 1B), and Tetrahymena thermophila (Ciliophora) GNPAT was experimentally shown to occur as a C-terminal fusion to FAR in a single bifunctional 1,140-residue FARAT protein (26), we explored the possibility that the large GNPAT hits were really FARAT. The presence of key biosynthetic enzymes, yet no plasmenyl or plasmanyl GPs, may stem from poor enzyme expression and/or activities, which can be addressed in future studies.Evolutionary Origin of PEDS1.Given the evident similarities between plasmalogen biosynthesis in eukaryotes and in bacteria from phylum Myxococcota, we studied the origin of this metabolic pathway in eukaryotes. The Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) (50), which provides a phylogenetically consistent and rank-normalized genome-based taxonomy for prokaryotes, was searched for PEDS1, AGPS, GNPAT, and FAR (or FARAT) using as queries the complete set of eukaryotic sequences identified in the previous section. Although individual genes were detected across several bacterial phyla, only six phyla contained at least one genome with the four enzymes. Distribution of Plasmalogen Biosynthesis Pathway in Eukaryotes.

To unravel the evolution of the eukaryotic plasmalogen biosynthesis pathway, we first need to map its distribution across the tree of eukaryotes. For this, we performed an extensive search for the four key enzymes (FAR, GNPAT, AGPS, and PEDS1) in a dataset of 376 high-quality genomes encompassing the whole breadth of the known eukaryotic diversity [(23); SI Appendix]. Detection of FAR, GNPAT, and AGPS would suggest an ability to produce plasmalogen precursors, and the additional presence of PEDS1 would suggest plasmalogen synthesis. For improved detection of distant homologs, we followed an iterative search algorithm that accounts for genome variations over evolutionary time and assigns higher rates to conserved positions when evaluating the hits. This allowed identification of functionally conserved regions even in proteins of low overall sequence similarity.

In several Diaphoretickes groups, absence of PEDS1 contrasts with presence of FAD4, the desaturase unrelated to plasmalogen biosynthesis retrieved in the search for PEDS1 homologs, as noted earlier (11, 37). A similar pattern observed for N. gruberi PEDS1 suggests its FARAT and PEDS1 were replaced through a secondary HGT event, possibly from an Amoebozoa. For Ciliophora, lack of PEDS1 (for direct comparison with FARAT) precluded a more definitive assessment of this scenario. Nonetheless, since Ciliophora is the only Diaphoretickes with FARAT (Fig. 2A), high UFB in the tree would support an HGT event among eukaryotes (SI Appendix, Fig. S8), possibly from an amoeba to either Ciliophora or Heterolobosea, which could have then acted as an intermediary for subsequent HGT to its sister group. As noted, while Amoebozoa, Discoba, and SAR possess FARAT, having separate GNPAT and FAR appears to be exclusive to Holozoa (with some exceptions). Additionally, FAR appears to have undergone expansion in Holozoa, forming a gene family, an aspect not explored further in this study.

Regarding the fungal sequences, their inclusion in the initial phylogenetic reconstructions significantly lowered UFB values across several key nodes, particularly those supporting the primary HGT event (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). These sequences introduced phylogenetic uncertainty and obscured the evolutionary signal within the broader eukaryotic tree. When included, fungal FARAT sequences form a monophyletic group that branches at an incongruent basal position relative to the rest of the eukaryotic sequences. The phylogenetic incongruence, coupled with the statistical instability they introduce, together with the fact that FARAT was identified only in a small closely related group of fungal species, suggests that its presence in Fungi could be the result of a secondary, independent HGT event, a scenario that mirrors the evolutionary pattern observed for PEDS1.

Beyond PEDS1, our analysis of the associated enzymes (FAR, GNPAT, AGPS) reveals a dynamic evolutionary history. In Metazoa, FAR and GNPAT function as separate enzymes (Fig. 1A). However, we demonstrate that this is the exception, not the rule. In most PEDS1-containing eukaryotes—including Amoebozoa, Discoba, Filasterea, and Ciliophora—these activities are fused into a single bifunctional protein, FARAT (26, 39). Whereas the FAR and GNPAT activities of FARAT localize to the peroxisome (26, 39), metazoan GNPAT occurs and acts in the peroxisome, but the peroxisomal membrane-located FAR acts on the cytosolic side (58). Physical separation of the FAR and GNPAT activities in Metazoa might ensure tighter spatial control, and the dual activity of FARAT in the peroxisome might enable more efficient coordinated regulation. Our analysis suggests that FARAT represents the ancestral eukaryotic state, inherited directly from a bacterial progenitor. The split system observed in animals likely results from a secondary gene fission event that occurred within the Holozoa. Thus, the mammalian pathway organization is a derived trait, underscoring the importance of studying diverse protists to reconstruct metabolic evolution.

A recent study proposed that PEDS1 was acquired by Filozoa, Excavata, Amoebozoa, and some small number of Fungi through four independent HGT events, each from an unknown donor (20). In contrast, our expanded taxon sampling and refined search strategies supports a more parsimonious scenario: a single HGT event from the phylum Myxococcota into an early eukaryotic ancestor. The topology of our trees (Figs. 4 and 5) shows the eukaryotic PEDS1 and FARAT sequences forming a monophyletic clade nested within, or sister to, the myxobacteria. This implies that the entire functional module was likely transferred en bloc. Indeed, HGT from Myxococcota to eukaryotes has also been proposed for key genes in the biosynthesis pathway of steroids, a class of fundamental eukaryotic membrane lipids (59–61). This pathway is also oxygen-dependent, as that for plasmalogens, and regulation of the two pathways is linked in mammals (62), with neither pathway reported in Archaea. Overall, these findings reinforce an essential relationship of early eukaryotes with aerobic myxobacteria in the evolution of eukaryotic membrane lipid complexity. Acquisition of the plasmalogen biosynthesis pathway, with consequences in membrane composition and function, may have conferred adaptive advantages.

The presence of PEDS1 in a handful of fungal species (e.g., Thozetella) appears to be the only exception to this single-origin model. If derived from the same ancestral HGT event, fungal PEDS1 would be expected to cluster near a PEDS1-like group, ideally closer to Holozoan sequences, but instead they lie apart and are more closely related to bacterial PEDS1. This suggests an independent more recent HGT event, also from Myxococcota, into this small group of Fungi, several of which are soil-dwelling, a trait shared with myxobacteria and known to promote HGT (63, 64). The apparent lack of plasmalogens in these handful of Fungi with PEDS1, AGPS, and FARAT may be because these recently acquired genes via HGT are not yet fully assimilated and optimized for function, consistent with the low levels of intron gain in the corresponding genes (Dataset S6), which reportedly correlates with low gene expression (63–65).

In our analysis, the patchy distribution of the pathway in eukaryotes is mainly explained by several independent events of secondary loss of the plasmalogen synthesis genes, an evolutionary process relevant and frequent in eukaryotic evolution (66, 67). A complete loss of the pathway could therefore account for its absence in anaerobic organisms like Metamonada, or Archamoebae, the sole anaerobic group within Amoebozoa. Secondary loss of the pathway is the most parsimonious evolutionary scenario in Archamoebae, whose aerobic sister groups do possess the pathway and is supported by our analysis reconstructing the evolutionary history of PEDS1 and FARAT genes. The alternative explanation that Metamonada never acquired the aerobic pathway is plausible if this group diverged prior to the common ancestor of Discoba and Amorphea. Future studies may build upon our findings on gene loss and gain to further elucidate the functional role of plasmalogens in eukaryotes.

It is important to acknowledge that, like previous studies, our work still grapples with the unresolved root of the eukaryotic tree (29). The relationships among the supergroups Metamonada, Discoba, Amorphea, and Diaphoretickes remain partially unresolved (29), which makes it challenging to draw further inferences. Several possibilities arise from the observation that the genes we studied are almost entirely restricted to Discoba and Amorphea. The simplest scenarios include either an HGT event to a common ancestor of both Amorphea and Discoba (if they are sister groups) or one involving the LECA followed by secondary loss in Diaphoretickes and Metamonada. We favor the latter scenario, drawing on recent phylogenomic analyses suggesting that excavate features represent the ancestral state of eukaryotes (30) (therefore Amorphea and Discoba would not be sister groups), and the accumulating evidence of HGT events from Myxococcota to LECA or pre-LECA (59, 68–70). While HGT events that introduce new functions are likely to be fixed by selection, these functions are often reversible and transient. Nonessential traits tend to be lost over evolutionary time when selective pressures shift (71). HGTs in eukaryotes are generally rare and short-lived, with many acquired genes providing only temporary benefits before being lost. However, the genes that are retained over long evolutionary periods, as observed in our study, are likely to be functionally significant or relevant (72). In sum, our work on the origin and evolution of aerobic plasmalogen biosynthesis points to a myxobacterial origin for this important eukaryotic membrane lipid biosynthetic pathway, explains its unusual evolutionary history, and underscores how HGT has shaped early eukaryotic metabolic capabilities.
