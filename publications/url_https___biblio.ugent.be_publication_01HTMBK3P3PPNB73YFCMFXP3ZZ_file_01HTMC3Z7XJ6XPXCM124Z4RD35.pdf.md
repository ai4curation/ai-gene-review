One Sentence Summary:  Biochemical, in planta  and structural analyses demonstrate that 24 
Arabidopsis ABCB19 is a brassinosteroid exporter.  25 

2 
 
Abstract: Brassinosteroids are steroidal phytohormones that regulate plant development  and 26 
physiology including adaptation to environmental stresses. Brassinosteroids are synthesized in 27 
the cell interior, but bind receptors at the cell surface necessitating yet to be identified export 28 
mechanism. Here we show that a member of the ATP -binding cassette (ABC) transporter 29 
superfamily, ABCB19, functions as a brassinosteroid exporter and present its structure in both 30 
the substrate-unbound and the brassinosteroid-bound states . Bioactive brassinosteroids are 31 
potent activators of ABCB19 ATP hydrolysis activity , and transport assays showed that 32 
ABCB19 transports brassinosteroids. In Arabidopsis thaliana , ABCB19 and its close 33 
homologue ABCB1 positively regulate brassinosteroid responses. Taken together, our results 34 
uncover a so far elusive export mechanism for bioactive brassinosteroids that is tightly 35 
coordinated with brassinosteroid signaling. 36

Main Text: Brassinosteroid hormones regulate a plethora of physiological and developmental 38 
processes in plants  and their adaptation to biotic and abiotic stresses  (1). Brassinosteroid 39 
mutants in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) display pleiotropic phenotypes such as epinastic 40 
leaf growth, reduced apical dominance and severe dwarfism (2-4). 41 
 Brassinosteroid signaling is initiated in apoplast by binding of brassinosteroid molecule 42 
to the extracellular domain of the plasma membrane -localized receptor kinase 43 
BRASSINOSTEROID INSENSITIVE1 (BRI1) and coreceptor (1, 5-12) to trigger downstream 44 
phosphorylation and dephosphorylation events that result in activation of the key transcription 45 
factors, BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT1 (BZR1) and BRI1 -EMS-SUPPRESSOR1 46 
(BES1)/BZR2 that regulate brassinosteroid-responsive genes (13, 14). While perceived at the 47 
cell surface, brassinosteroids are synthesized in the cell interior  (15-18), hence, posing the 48 
question of how brassinosteroids reach the apoplast to bind the ir receptors. As the bioactive 49 
brassinosteroids are polar molecules (19), they cannot easily cross the membrane due to their 50 
hydrophilicity. Reports that the G family ATP -binding cassette (ABC) transporters can 51 
transport steroids in human (20), and the identification of the ABC importer (21) and the solute 52 
carrier exporter of ecdysone in Drosophila (22) indicate that transporters may be required for 53 
brassinosteroid export in plants.  54 
 The ABCB transporters are membrane -bound proteins that utilize ATP to actively 55 
transport various substrates  (23). In mammalian cells, ABCB1 ( or P-glycoprotein/MDR1) 56 
mediates the efflux of sex steroid hormones  (24, 25). In Arabidopsis, 2 9 ABCBs have been 57 
identified (26), of which ABCB1 (or PGP1), ABCB4 and ABCB19 (or MDR1/PGP19) are the 58 
best characterized members with implicated functions in auxin transport (27-33). Of these, the 59 
abcb19 single mutant exhibits the most severe growth phenotypes (27-29, 32) that are further 60 
exaggerated in the abcb1 abcb19 double mutant, suggesting possible functional redundancy  61 
(27). The observations on interactions between ABCB1, ABCB19 and PIN  auxin exporters 62 

4 
 
transport system reinforce the involvement of ABCBs in polar auxin transport  (34, 35), while 63 
the contrasting phenotypes of abcbs and pin mutants imply that the two families play different 64 
roles in regulating auxin transport (36). The auxin transport activity of ABCB19, ABCB1 and 65 
ABCB4 is facilitated by a membrane -anchored immunophilin -like protein TWISTED 66 
DWARF1 (TWD1) or FK506 -binding protein (FKBP42), which acts as a chaperone for their 67 
plasma membrane targeting (37-40). Phenotypes of the twd1 mutant overlaps with those of the 68 
abcb1 abcb19 double mutant, further strengthening the regulatory role of TWD1 in ABCB1 69 
and ABCB19 functions (27, 41, 42 ). Additionally, twd1 shows reduced brassinosteroid 70 
sensitivity and TWD1 function is required for activation of the brassinosteroid receptor 71 
complex without affecting its plasma membrane targeting (42-44), thus raising the question of 72 
whether TWD1 along with ABCB1 and ABCB19 may have a role in brassinosteroid perception. 73 
 Here, we show that the Arabidopsis  ABCB19 functions as a brassinosteroid exporter. 74 
Stimulation of ABCB19  ATPase activity by brassinosteroid, in vitro and in planta transport 75 
assays, cryo -electron microscopy (cryo -EM)-based high -resolution structures  and genetic 76 
analysis, altogether confirmed that ABCB19 transports bioactive brassinosteroids.  77

Brassinosteroids trigger the ATPase activity of ABCB19 79 
Binding of substrates to ABC transporters typically stimulates their ATPase activities, 80 
especially for the P-glycoprotein/ABCB subfamily (45-47). To test the substrate specificity of 81 
ABCB19, we established an in vitro ATPase assay. While little protein was obtained from cells 82 
expressing ABCB19 alone ( fig. S1A), when co-expressed with TWD1, the protein yield was 83 
increased (fig. S1A), consistent with previous reports that TWD1 is needed for ABCB19 correct 84 
folding and localization (37, 48). 85 
 With the purified ABCB19 protein, we tested its ATPase activity in the presence of four 86 
phytohormones known to promote plant growth, namely two natural auxins, indole 3 -acetic 87 

5 
 
acid (IAA) and indole 3 -butyric acid (IBA), brassinolid e and gibberellic acid -3 (GA3). The 88 
basal ATPase activity of ABCB19 was approximately 29.6 ± 4.0 nmol min-1 mg-1 protein and 89 
remained unaffected in the presence of IAA, IBA or GA3 (Fig. 1 A). In contrast, its ATPase 90 
activity was stimulated by brassinolide in a dose-dependent manner, with an almost four-fold 91 
increase at a concentration of 10 µ M compared to the mock control (Fig. 1A). By fitting with 92 
the Hill equation, the stimulation showed a Vmax of 133.5 ±  4.0 nmol-1 min-1 mg-1 protein and a 93 
half maximal effective concentration (EC50) of 0.53 ±  0.11 µ M (Fig. 1A). The ATPase activity 94 
of ABCB19 was activated by other two bioactive brassinosteroids with similar kinetic  95 
parameters, 24 -epibrassinolide and 24 -epicastasterone, but not by the brassinosteroid-96 
biosynthesis precursors, campesterol and 22-hydroxycampesterol (17) (fig. S1, B and C, table 97 
S1), or mammalian steroids such as cholesterol, taurocholic acid and estrone sulfate (fig. S1, B 98 
and C). Addition of purified TWD1 protein had no effect to ABCB19 ATPase activity 99 
stimulated by brassinolide (fig. S1D). The ATPase activity of ABCB19 and its stimulation by 100 
brassinolide were abolished by a double -mutation introduced into the Walker -B motifs of the 101 
nucleotide-binding domains (NBDs) , which converts the glutamates to glutamines 102 
(E529Q/E1174Q, EQ mutant) to prevent ATP hydrolysis (fig. S1E), or the addition of vanadate 103 
which traps ABCB19 in a posthydrolytic transition-state (fig. S1F).  104 
 Moreover, a binding between brassinolide and ABCB19 was detected using isothermal 105 
titration calorimetry (ITC) with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 1.13 ± 0.54 µM and a n value of 106 
0.8, close to 1.0  (Fig. 1B). The Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) is -8.11 kcal mol-1, with the 107 
enthalpy change (ΔH) of 12.3 kcal mol-1 and the -TΔS (ΔS, entropy change) value of -20.5 kcal 108 
mol-1. The binding between ABCB19 and brassinolide is mainly driven by the entropy change, 109 
possibly due to the rearrangements of solvent molecules or protein conformation upon binding. 110 
Besides brassinolide, ABCB19 also bound 24-epibrassinolide and 24-epicastasterone with a Kd 111 
value of 0.14 ± 0.07 µM and 0.36 ± 0.16 µM, respectively (fig. S2A). No binding was detected 112

induce ATPase activity of ABCB19. 116

ABCB19 transports brassinosteroids 118 
To test whether ABCB19 transports brassinosteroids, we established a proteoliposome -based 119 
transport assay with 3H-labelled brassinolide. Purified wild-type or EQ mutant ABCB19 120 
proteins were reconstituted into proteoliposomes. Accumulation of [3H]-brassinolide in 121 
proteoliposomes was determined in the reaction buffer containing ATP and Mg2+, either in the 122 
presence or absence of vanadate. More brassinolide accumulated over time in proteoliposomes 123 
containing the wild -type ABCB19 without vanadate, compared to the same proteoliposomes 124 
with vanadate, or proteoliposomes containing the  EQ mutant with or without vanadate (Fig. 125 
1C). No differences were observed when ABCG25 was used as a control ( fig. S3A). In a 126 
separate assay, the accumulation of [3H]-brassinolide was determined with the reaction buffer 127 
containing either ATP and Mg 2+or ATP and EDTA, the latter serving as a negative control, 128 
where the lack of Mg2+ prevented ATP hydrolysis. Again, more [3H]-brassinolide accumulated 129 
in the presence of ATP and Mg 2+ (fig. S3B). We also tested the transport of IAA, cholesterol 130 
and estrone sulfate using 3H-labelled compounds. No active accumulation was observed for 131 
IAA, cholesterol or estrone sulfate in the proteoliposomes loaded with wild-type ABCB19 (fig. 132 
S3, C to E). However, in a human cell-based loading assay, cells co-expressing ABCB19 and 133 
TWD1 accumulated less IAA than control cells  expressing TWD1 alone, consistent with 134 
previous reports that ABCB19 exports auxin (fig. S3F) (29, 35, 50). One possible explanation 135 
is that ABCB19-mediated auxin efflux requires some endogenous factors that are missing in 136 
the in vitro system. 137

Structure of ABCB19 in the substrate-unbound state 159 
To uncover the molecular architecture and transport mechanism of ABCB19, we obtained a 160 
high-resolution structure of the transporter  (Fig. 2, fig. S4 -S7). Wild-type ABCB19 w as co-161 
expressed with TWD1 in HEK293F suspension cells to a large-scale (fig. S4A) and purified for 162 

8 
 
cryo-EM analysis. After data collecti on and processing  (fig. S4, B to G, fig . S5A), a  3D 163 
reconstruction map was obtained with an overall resolution of 3. 4 Å (Fig. 2A, fig. S4D, S6A, 164 
table S 2). The overall structure of ABCB19 exhibited an inward -facing conformation and 165 
adopted a conserved structural fold of the type IV ABC transporters, most of which are 166 
exporters (Fig. 2, B to D) (23). Structural alignments with the human ABCB1 in the substrate-167 
unbound state revealed a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 4.8 Å (over 1089 Cα atoms) 168 
(fig. S7A) (51). The transmembrane region contained two halves formed by transmembrane 169 
domain 1 (TMD1) and TMD2, of which each had six transmembrane segments (TMs) arranged 170 
in a domain-swapped manner (Fig. 2D). We observed a large cavity opening into the cytoplasm, 171 
which is predominantly hydrophobic on one side and weakly acidic on the other (fig. S7B). The 172 
absence of ATP and transport substrates separated the NBDs from each other (Fig. 2, A and B). 173 
Besides the canonical TMDs and NBDs of a typical ABC transporter, we identified extra EM 174 
densities between the intracellular parts of the two TMDs, adjacent to the half formed by TM1-175 
TM3, TM6, TM10 and TM11 (fig. S6A). This region corresponds to a linker sequence between 176 
NBD1 and TM7 of TMD2, designated regulatory (R) -domain hereafter (Fig. 2 , A to D). The 177 
R-domain contains a short helix and two β -sheets and packs to the surrounding helices, the 178 
cytosolic parts of TM10 and TM3 in TMD2 and TMD1, respectively (Fig. 2E). The R-domain 179 
sequence varies in length and residue compositions between ABCB1, ABCB4  and ABCB19 180 
(fig. S8). We generated a chimeric mutant in which the R -domain sequence of ABCB19 was 181 
replaced by that of ABCB4. The ATPase assay revealed that the mutant had increased activity 182 
compared to the wild-type ABCB19, either in the absence or the presence of brassinolide (Fig. 183 
2F). It indicates that this R-domain may play an inhibitory role in ABCB19 activity regulation. 184 
Whether it is involved in the regulation of ABCB19 by phosphorylation (52, 53) awaits further 185 
investigations. 186

Structure of ABCB19 in the brassinosteroid-bound state 188 
The biochemical analysis supports  that bioactive brassinosteroids, such as brassinolide or 189 
castasterone are transport substrates of ABCB19. To independently verify this result , we 190 
obtained the structure of ABCB19 in complex with brassinolide. After data collection and 191 
processing, an EM map of brassinolide-bound ABCB19 was generated with an overall 192 
resolution of 3.4 Å (Fig. 3A; fig. S4, H to M, fig. S5B and fig. S6B). The brassinolide-bound 193 
ABCB19 structure is almost identical to the unbound state, as revealed by structure alignments 194 
with a root -mean-square deviation of 0. 4 Å (over 1118 Cα atoms) ( fig. S7C). A clear EM 195 
density was visible in the transmembrane region that docked well with a brassinolide molecule 196 
(Fig. 3B) that lies at the bottom of the intracellular cavity (Fig. 3 C). The four ring moieties of 197 
brassinolide face the hydrophobic surface of the cavity formed by Y276 in TM5, F309, I312 198 
and M316 in TM6, and F704 in TM7 of ABCB19 (Fig. 3 D). The C26, C27 and C28 methyl 199 
groups protrude into a hydrophobic pocket formed by F59 and F62 in TM1, F953, V957, I958 200 
and N961 in TM12 (Fig. 3D). This pocket is large enough to accommodate both configurations 201 
of C24, allowing either brassinolide or 24-epibrassinolide binding. Besides the hydrophobic 202 
interactions, brassinolide forms hydrogen bonds with W283 in TM5 (Fig. 3D). 203 
 To verify the brassinolide binding sites, we generated a series of mutants and tested their 204 
ATPase activities. The protein yield and behavior of all generated mutants were similar to the 205 
wild-type (fig. S 9, A and B ). For most alanine mutants, the brassinosteroid concentration-206 
dependent ATPase activities were impaired, with decreased Vmax values and EC50 (Fig. 3E, fig. 207 
S10A, table S3). Particularly, the ATPase activities under 10 µM brassinolide were almost 208 
abolished for F62A, Y276A, W283A, F704A, F953A and I958A, and were reduced by at least 209 
50% for F 59A, I312A and M316A (Fig. 3 E, fig. S10A, table S3 ). The V957A mutant had 210 
similar Vmax and EC50 values compared to the wild-type ABCB19 (table S3). When mutated to 211 
less drastic residues, F59L, Y276T and F704L had similar Vmax and EC50 values to that of the 212 

10 
 
wild-type protein (fig. S10, B to D, table S3). For F309A or F309L mutant, which locates in 213 
TM6 and contributes to formation of the binding pocket for the brassinolide methyl groups, the 214 
Vmax was more than two-fold higher than that of the wild-type either in the presence or absence 215 
of brassinolide, but the EC50 was also increased , suggesting a reduced brassinolide binding 216 
affinity (Fig. 3E, table S3).  217 
 We also examined the brassinolide binding affinities of the representative mutants using 218 
ITC. Results showed that all tested mutants had reduced binding affinities towards brassinolide, 219 
with Kd values increased by more than 20 -fold compared to the wild-type, consistent with the 220 
reduced brassinolide-triggered ATPase activities except for F309A ( fig. S2C , t able S 4). It 221 
further supports that the elevated ATPase activity of F309A is not triggered by brassinolide. 222 
 We also tested the [3H]-brassinolide transport activities of some representative mutants. 223 
For the F59A, Y276A, I312A and F704A mutants, for which the ATPase activities had almost 224 
been abolished, the [3H]-brassinolide transport activities were reduced compared to that of the 225 
wild-type (fig. S10E). The V957A mutant, for which the ATPase activity remained similar to 226 
that of the wild-type, also retained [3H]-brassinolide transport activity (fig. S10E). For F309A, 227 
with an increased ATPase activity, [3H]-brassinolide transport activity was reduced by ~50% 228 
compared to that of the wild-type protein (fig. S10E). 229 
 Consistently, the export of castasterone -alkyne was reduced in Arabidopsis protoplasts 230 
expressing GFP-ABCB19 (F59A) mutant as compared with protoplasts expressing the wild-231 
type GFP-ABCB19 (Fig. 3, F and G) , but remained comparable to that of non-transfected 232 
protoplasts and protoplasts expressing GFP-ABCG25 (fig. S11, A and B).  In summary, our 233 
structural findings and mutant analyses further support that brassinolide is a transport substrate 234 
of ABCB19. 235

11 
 
As previously observed (27-29), the abcb19 mutant showed a smaller rosette  whereas the 238 
abcb1 abcb19 double mutant was severely dwarfed and resembled the brassinosteroid 239 
biosynthetic mutants, dwarf4 (dwf4) and constitutive photomorphogenesis and dwarfism (cpd) 240 
(Fig. 4A). The hypocotyls of the abcb19 and abcb1 abcb19 mutants were shorter than those of 241 
the wild-type, but the primary root length remained the same  (fig. S12, A to C). However, the 242 
mutant roots were wavy and had a slightly broader meristem with reduced length of the cortical 243 
meristem and mature cells (fig. S12, D -G), which is characteristic of brassinosteroid-related 244 
mutants (54, 55). Given the ability of ABCB19 to transport brassinosteroids, we examined if 245 
brassinosteroid signaling is affected in abcb19 and abcb1 abcb19 mutants. To that aim, we 246 
analyzed the accumulation of dephosphorylated BES1, which is used as a readout for 247 
brassinosteroid signaling activation (14). Compared to the wild-type, abcb19 and abcb1 abcb19 248 
mutants showed less dephosphorylated BES1, indicating a decreased brassinosteroid signaling 249 
(Fig. 4 , B and C). In agreement, the expression of the brassinosteroid biosynthetic gene 250 
BRASSINOSTEROID-6-OXIDASE1 (BR6OX1) and the brassinosteroid catabolic gene PHYB-4 251 
ACTIVATION-TAGGED SUPPRESSOR1 (BAS1)  were up- and down-regulated, respectively 252 
(Fig. 4D), reminiscent of mutants with impaired brassinosteroid signaling (54). Furthermore, 253 
overexpression of the gain-of-function mutant BES1-D (14) rescued completely the hypocotyl 254 
length but only partially the shoot phenotype of the abcb1 abcb19 mutant (fig. S12, H to J ). 255 
Similarly, application of bikinin, which can activate brassinosteroid signaling downstream of 256 
the BRI1 receptor  (56), restored the hypocotyl length of the abcb1 abcb19 mutant to almost 257 
that of the wild -type ( fig. S12, K and L ). We further tested whether the abcb19 and 258 
abcb1 abcb19 mutants can be rescued by feeding with the brassinolide precursor, 22-259 
hydroxycampesterol and with the bioactive brassinolide. 22-hydroxycampesterol is the product 260 
and the substrate of DWF4 and CPD enzymes, respectively, and it can rescue dwf4 but not cpd 261 
mutant by being converted to bioactive brassinosteroids (19) (Fig. 4A). Consistent with the 262 

12 
 
impaired brassinosteroid export in abcb19 and abcb1 abcb19 mutants, 22-hydroxycampesterol 263 
did not rescue the m (Fig. 4A) and the  abcb1 abcb19 mutant remained insensitive to the 264 
precursor in the BES1 dephosphorylation assay (Fig. 4 , E and F).  Non-trivially, while 265 
brassinolide recovered both dwf4 and cpd mutants, it failed to rescue abcb19 and abcb1 abcb19 266

Discussion 291 
Our results show that the Arabidopsis ABC transporter ABCB19 functions as a brassinosteroid 292 
exporter. This is consistently supported by induction of ATPase activities, binding and transport 293 
assays, structure as well as mutant phenotypes in Arabidopsis. How this verified function can 294 
be linked to its previously proposed role in auxin export remains currently unclear. However, 295 
previous research has shown that some ABC transporters are able to transport many structurally 296 
unrelated compounds  (60). In this case, the substrate preference might be determined by a 297 
phosphorylation switch or interaction with other factors. Through structural analysis  we 298 
resolved the 3D architectures of  ABCB19 in both substrate -unbound and brassinolide -bound 299 
states. A conceptualized transport process for type IV ABC exporters starts with an inward -300 
facing conformation prior to ATP binding, ready for substrate binding, followed by a transition 301 
to an outwar d-facing conformation upon ATP binding, and finally release of substrate and 302 
return to the inward -facing conformation after ATP hydrolysis (46). The two determined 303 
ABCB19 structures are in the inward -facing conformation, representing the first steps of the 304 
transport cycle. To gain more insights into the transport process, we also determined a structure 305 
of the wild -type ABCB19 bound to brassinolide and an ATP analogue, adenylyl -306 
imidodiphosphate (AMP-PNP), and a structure of the E529Q/E1174Q mutant ABCB19 bound 307 
with ATP (fig. S15, A to D, table S2 ). However, the two structures exhibit the same inward -308 
facing conformation and align well with the brassinolide -bound structure, with an RMSD of 309 
0.7 Å and 0.9 Å, respectively (fig. S7C). Thus, the mechanism of ABCB19-mediated transport 310 
remains elusive. Among the Arabidopsis ABCBs, ABCB1 and ABCB19 share high sequence 311 
similarity and cluster together in the phylogenetic tree  (fig. S16) (26), suggesting redundant 312 

14 
 
roles in brassinosteroid transporter, while  detailed functions of ABCB1 await  further 313 
investigations. 314 
 An extra R-domain was identified between the transmembrane domains of ABCB19. The 315 
corresponding sequence in ABCB1 was reported to be  a direct target of the protein kinase 316 
PINOID and is regulated by its interaction with TWD1 (42, 53). Such an linker domain, mostly 317 
disordered in structure, is present in ABC transporters in human and yeast (61-63) (fig. S7D) 318 
and it regulates the function of the transporter depending on its phosphorylation state (64). The 319 
activity of ABCB19 in auxin export is inhibited by phosphorylation by the photoreceptor kinase 320 
PHOTOTROPIN1 (52). Moreover, recently it has been shown that the substrate preference of 321 
some plant ABCB transporters can be switched by phosphorylation provided by an interacting 322 
receptor kinase  (65). Further investigation s are needed to determine w hether ABCB19 is 323 
regulated by phosphorylation of its R-domain and how this affects its structural dynamics and 324 
substrate specificity and whether this can explain the additional function of ABCB19 in auxin 325 
transport.  326

ATPase activity assay 456 
ATPase activities of the wild -type Arabidopsis ABCB19, chimeric ABCB19 and its mutants 457 
were measured by the ATPase Colorimetric Assay Kit (Innova Biosciences). To assess the 458 
ATPase activity of ABCB19 in the presence of different substrates, 3  µ g purified protein was 459 
added to the reaction buffer containing 25  mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.4), 150 mM KCl, 10 mM 460 
MgCl2, 0.01% (w/v) DDM. Each substrate was diluted to serial concentrations, added to the 461 
mixture and incubated for 20 min on ice. Afterwards, ATP was added to the solution at a final 462 
concentration of 3 mM to start the reaction at 37° C for 10 min, and the amount of Pi released 463 
was measured quantitatively with the ATPase Colorimetric Assay Kit by determining the light 464 
absorbance at 650 nm wavelength with the BioTek Synergy H1 Multimode Reader. 465 
Brassinolide, 24-epibrassinolide, 24 -epicastasterone, (22S) -22-hydroxycampesterol 466 
(22-OHCR), gibberellic acid 3, campesterol, taurocholic acid, estrone sulfate, indole -3-acetic 467 
acid (IAA) and 3 -indolebutyric acid (IBA) used fo r the measurement of the concentration -468 
dependent ATPase activity were dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 100  mM, 469 
respectively, and then serially diluted to the desired final concentrations in 1% DMSO. 470 
Cholesterol (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in ethyl alcohol at a concentration of 100 mM, and 471 
diluted to the desired final concentrations in 0.5% ethyl alcohol. Statistical analysis was done 472 
using GraphPad Prism 8. Kinetics of the ATPase activities of ABCB19 under serial 473 
concentrations of ligands were fitted with the Hill equation, with the Hill coefficient value fixed 474 
to 1. 475

Proteoliposome reconstitution 508 
Proteoliposomes were prepared as describe d (78). Briefly, soybean phospholipids (Sigma -509 
Aldrich) were dissolved in a mixture of methanol:chloroform 3:1 (v/v) (Sigma -Aldrich) at 510 
50 mg ml-1, then dried under nitrogen gas and washed in pentane (Sigma -Aldrich) to remove 511 
chloroform. Lipids were resuspended to 20 mg ml-1 in buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-KOH 512 
(pH 7.4) and 150 mM KCl, and frozen and thawed 10 times in liquid nitrogen. For subsequent 513 
reconstitution, liposomes were extruded 31 times through 0.4 mm membrane filters (Cytiva) 514 
and incubated with 1% (w/ v) DDM at 4° C for 1 hour to disrupt liposomes. The detergent -515 
solubilized protein was added into the DDM -destabilized liposomes at a protein:lipid ratio of 516 
1:100 (w/w) and incubated at 4° C for 1 hour. DDM was removed by adding 100 mg ml-1 Bio-517 
Beads (BioRad) in four batches overnight. The proteoliposomes were then homogenized by 518 
five freeze -thaw cycles followed by extrusion again. Proteoliposomes were harvested by 519 
ultracentrifugation at 160,000 g for 1  hour and resuspended in buffer containing 20  mM 520 
HEPES-KOH (pH 7.4) and 150 mM KCl, to a final lipid concentration of 100  mg ml-1 for the 521 
transport assay. 522

3H-brassinolide transport assay 524 
The transport assay was carried out by a rapid filtration method (49, 78 ). Briefly, for each 525 
experimental group, 2 μl proteoliposomes preloaded with wild-type or mutant ABCB19 protein 526 

23 
 
were added to 48 μl reaction buffer [20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.4), 150 mM KCl, 2 mM ATP, 527 
1 µ M brassinolide, 7 nM 3H-brassinolide (3H-BL) (specific activity 0.6  Ci mmol-1, American 528 
Radiolabeled Chemicals)] plus 5 mM MgCl 2 and incubated for the indicated time at room 529 
temperature. For the control group, the same 2 μl proteoliposomes were added to 48 μl reaction 530 
buffer plus 5 mM MgCl2 and 1 mM sodium orthovanadate (MedChemExpress) or 1 mM EDTA. 531 
Transport was stopped by transferring the samples to glass fibre filters (0.3 μm, Advantec), and 532 
then washed the filters rapidly with 8 ml ice-cold buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 533 
7.4) and 150 mM KCl at different time points. Radioactivity in the filter was measured by liquid 534 
scintillation counting (Tri -Carb 2910TR, Perkin -Elmer) before an incubation with 3  ml 535 
scintillation solution for 2 hours. For the time course assay, 3H-BL accumulation was calculated 536 
as the amounts of 3H-BL measured at different time points minus that at 0 min. For the ABCB19 537 
mutants, the accumulated 3H-BL quantity was represented by the differences between the 538 
amounts of 3H-BL measured in the experimental and control groups after a 45-minute reaction 539 
at room temperature, and the relative transport activity was determined as the 3H-BL transport 540 
quantity relative to the amount of 3H-BL in the control.  541
