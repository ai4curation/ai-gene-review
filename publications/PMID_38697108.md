In bony fishes, patterning of the vertebral column, or spine, is guided by a
metameric blueprint established in the notochord sheath. Notochord segmentation
begins days after somitogenesis concludes and can occur in its absence. However,
somite patterning defects lead to imprecise notochord segmentation, suggesting
that these processes are linked. Here, we identify that interactions between the
notochord and the axial musculature ensure precise spatiotemporal segmentation
of the zebrafish spine. We demonstrate that myoseptum-notochord linkages drive
notochord segment initiation by locally deforming the notochord extracellular
matrix and recruiting focal adhesion machinery at these contact points.
Irregular somite patterning alters this mechanical signaling, causing
non-sequential and dysmorphic notochord segmentation, leading to altered spine
development. Using a model that captures myoseptum-notochord interactions, we
find that a fixed spatial interval is critical for driving sequential segment
initiation. Thus, mechanical coupling of axial tissues facilitates
spatiotemporal spine patterning.

Introduction

In vertebrates, the skeletal muscles, connective tissues, and bony elements (vertebrae and arches) of the axial skeletal system exhibit a segmented pattern that is established from the anteroposterior (AP) division of the embryo into a series of functional units called somites.1 Somite segmentation is regulated through a highly conserved genetic network of oscillating signals, referred to as the segmentation clock.2 When the clock genes are disrupted, a wide range of axial musculoskeletal defects are observed.3–5 In amniote embryos (mammals, birds and reptiles), perturbations to the segmentation clock impact patterning of the skeletal muscles, tendons, and vertebral column, severely disrupting vertebral body and arch morphology.6,7 By contrast, in teleost fish, somite clock mutations cause defects in muscle and myoseptum segmentation as well as vertebral arch morphology, whereas vertebral body malformations are comparatively mild.8–10 Underlying these phenotypic differences between vertebrates are distinct developmental mechanisms that emerged in teleost fish and amniotes. Unlike amniotes, which predominantly rely on the re-segmentation of somite-derived sclerotome cells to pattern vertebral bodies, teleosts (modern bony fish) form vertebrae in a notochord-dependent manner via the production of metameric mineralized rings, known as chordacentra.10,11 Chordacentra then serve as template for osteoblast recruitment and vertebral body development.12 When the segmented template of the zebrafish notochord is missing, osteoblasts are still recruited to the notochord via the somite boundaries, producing hemi-vertebrae and diplospondyly (i.e., two vertebral elements per segment).13 This phenotype resembling the vertebral pattern of stem teleosts and primitive sarcopterygian lineages, the ancestors of teleosts and tetrapods, suggests that the morphogenetic program of the fish spine retains a core segmental program that is common to that of amniotes.

During vertebral body development in zebrafish, regions of chordacentra formation are first defined by segmentation of the notochord sheath, which patterns into cartilage-like and mineralizing domains in a sequential manner along the AP axis.8,12,14 Notochord segments that underlie chordacentra express mineralizing proteins such as Entpd5a and Cyp26b1, while proteins characteristic of cartilage formation, including collagen proteins Col9a2 and Col2a1, define the domains that eventually develop into the intervertebral discs (IVDs). Sheath cells transition from a uniform col9a2+ population to form alternating entpd5a+ domains in a sequential fashion, with one segment forming every ~8 hours in an A-to-P direction through continuously active metameric Notch signaling.12 This is in contrast to somitogenesis, which displays cyclic expression of Notch target genes.15 Furthermore, while segment formation during somitogenesis is strictly linear (i.e., always sequential in the A-to-P direction), gaps in the segmented pattern of the notochord can be repaired with some delay.8,12 Interestingly, previous work has also shown that mutations affecting somite boundary (SB) formation, such as fused somites (fss, a.k.a. tbx6), or localized disruptions of SBs during somitogenesis impact the fidelity of notochord and spine segmentation.8,12,16 For example, transient exposure of embryos to the Notch inhibitor DAPT during a short window of somitogenesis produces a gap in the pattern of SBs.17 These DAPT gaps also lead to localized regions of notochord segmentation defects that coincide with the affected SBs.12 In addition, transplantation experiments suggest that notochord-paraxial mesoderm interactions may also play a role in notochord patterning in amniotes.18 Together, these experiments strongly suggested that crosstalk between the notochord and the surrounding tissues has a role in regulating notochord segmentation in teleosts.

Here, we investigate the link between the spatial and temporal disorder that is observed during notochord segmentation in somite patterning mutants. We show that interactions between the notochord sheath and the SBs lead to focal adhesion clustering ahead of notochord segment initiation, with the activation of Notch and entpd5a at those sites. We find that when this spatial information is disrupted by genetic defects in SB formation or experimental perturbation of myosepta, notochord segmentation proceeds non-sequentially, indicating that locally provided mechanical cues at notochord-myoseptum contacts promote spatially precise and sequential segmentation of the notochord. We captured these dynamics in a theoretical model which suggests that a fixed length-scale of external cues is critical for driving sequential segment formation in the zebrafish notochord. This work uncovers a role for tissue-tissue interactions in sequential metameric patterning.

ResultsMyoseptum-notochord interactions define the site of notochord segment initiationNotochord segmentation still occurs in the absence of somite segmentation clock components8 or when SBs are mispatterned12, albeit imprecisely. This loss of precision in the pattern of notochord segments suggested that SBs somehow influence notochord segmentation. To explore this connection, we first examined in detail the developing axial musculoskeletal system (Figure 1A) using transgenic lines that specifically label the notochord sheath, its surrounding ECM, and the myosepta. To label the notochord ECM, we generated a functional knock-in (KI) fusion line for Calymmin, TgKI(cmn-tdTomato) using a CRISPR-based method we recently described.19 Calymmin is a teleost-specific extracellular matrix protein similar to Elastin.13 Calymmin-tdTomato localizes to the outermost layer of the notochord extracellular matrix (ECM) (Figure 1B), as previously shown using immuno-EM20, where it is arranged as circumferentially-oriented fibers (Figure 1C). Imaging of 5 days post-fertilization (dpf) TgKI(cmn-tdTomato) larvae expressing Tg(Bactin:pxna-eGFP), a transgene that labels the myotendinous junctions of myosepta21,22, revealed a close association of the two tissues at dorsal and ventral locations of the notochord (Figure 1C and 1D). The myosepta also appear to infiltrate the Calymmin ECM layer of the notochord at the ventral locations (Figure 1E).To examine the specific locations at which myoseptum connections occur in relation to notochord segment formation, we combined TgBAC(entpd5a:pkRed), which labels mineralizing segments8,12, with Tg(Bactin:pxna-eGFP). Notably, myoseptum-notochord connections were located at sites of notochord segment formation (Figure 1F–I). To investigate the relationship between these connections and notochord segment formation, we imaged cmn-tdTomato together with the Notch activity reporter TP1:VenusPest.23 Interestingly, time course imaging revealed that the surface of the ECM deforms at locations of segment formation marked by the Notch activation (Figure 1J). To quantitatively measure ECM deformation, we utilized the MATLAB toolbox, Image Surface Analysis Environment (ImSAnE).24 This image processing approach identifies and extracts a surface of interest (SOI) from three-dimensional datasets, thus enabling the examination of notochord surface geometry. Using our detected surface, we then mapped changes in curvature along the notochord sheath ECM (Materials and Methods, Figure S1A–E). In wild-type (WT) larvae, domains of active Notch signaling acquire a positive curvature, indicating these regions have bulged outward, while non-segmenting regions are mostly flat or display negative curvature values (Figure 1J, 1K, S1F, and S1G). Furthermore, quantifying changes in curvature and TP1:VenusPest expression along the AP axis (Figure S1H) revealed that curvature changes precede Notch activation in not yet segmented regions. At a later time point, we observed an increase in positive curvature that was concomitant with an increase in TP1 expression, suggesting that notochord-myoseptum contacts mature over time (Figure S1I–K).To test if the ECM deformation depends on myosepta attachments, we generated DAPT gaps (Figure 1L) as previously described.17 Time course imaging of 7 dpf larvae expressing cmn-tdTomato and TP1:VenuPest revealed that Notch activation was delayed within the DAPT gaps (Figure 1M and S1L) and that surface deformation occurred in irregular locations (Figure 1N, S1M, and S1N). When we imaged the same region at a later stage, we observed Notch activation and surface deformation still lagged within the DAPT gap, but was detected in unaffected, posterior regions (Figure 1O, 1P, and S1O–Q). Together, these data show that myosepta contact and deform the notochord surface at sites of subsequent notochord segment initiation.Myosepta provide a local mechanical signal for notochord segmentationThe delay in notochord deformation and Notch activation observed within DAPT gaps suggested that the myoseptum-notochord connections establish a spatial pattern for segment initiation. To characterize the impact of defects in myoseptum attachments on notochord segmentation, we examined the dynamics of segmentation in Bactin:pxna-eGFP and entpd5a:pkRed expressing larvae that harbor DAPT gaps. Based on the pattern of pxna-eGFP expression, DAPT gaps ranged from 2 to 6 segments and were flanked by correctly patterned regions on the anterior and posterior sides (Figure 2A), as previously reported.17 Plotting the mean intensities of Bactin:pxna-eGFP and entpd5a:pkRed along the AP axis revealed that notochord segmentation defects coincide with regions of disrupted myosepta (Figure 2B), further highlighting that disrupted notochord segmentation is impacted locally, as we previously observed.12 Time course imaging revealed that notochord segment formation is delayed and imprecise in regions with disrupted myosepta, resulting in alterations to segment morphology (Figure 2C). Next, to assess how the spatiotemporal segmentation dynamics are impacted within and outside the disrupted areas, we shortened our image acquisition interval to 24 hours to enable tracking of individual entpd5a+ segments in larvae from 5 to 12 dpf (Figure S2). We then quantified the patterning dynamics by assigning an ordering index to each segment. This index is a whole number which ranks each segment along the AP axis based on its initiation during segmentation (Materials and Methods, Figure 2D). To determine the ordering index for each larva, we compared the observed segmentation pattern against an expected sequential pattern. Additionally, to account for variations in DAPT gap sizes, we rescaled the segment positions so that the segments at the anterior and posterior boundaries of the disruption were labelled at −1 and 1 along the AP axis respectively. In both WT and undisrupted regions, we found that the ordering index showed little deviation from an expected sequential order (Figure 2E and 2F). However, in regions corresponding to disrupted myosepta, sequential segment initiation was lost and characterized by segments skipping ahead (positive mismatches) or filling in (negative mismatches) (Figure 2F). Collectively, these data indicate that the effect of gaps in myoseptum-notochord contacts are local.To directly test the role of myosepta in notochord segmentation, we adapted a laser ablation approach25,26 to disrupt myosepta without perturbing somite boundary formation or notochord patterning. For disrupting a single segment, we laser ablated the four connecting vertical myosepta (dorsal-left, dorsal-right, ventral-left, ventral-right) at 7 dpf (Figure 3A and 3D). Lesions made to the myosepta were performed in focal planes well above the notochord in Bactin:pxna-eGFP and entpd5a:pkRed expressing larvae, posterior to the last detectable notochord segment, as determined by fluorescent microscopy. We confirmed the tissue specificity of the laser cuts by using the vasculature reporter Tg(flk:ras-Cherry)27 and found that blood vessel morphology was unperturbed following cuts along the myosepta (Figure S3D–G). Immediately following laser ablation, we observed that cut myosepta recoiled and relaxed, indicating that tension along the myosepta was substantially reduced (Figure 3B and 3C). Laser cut larvae were then allowed to recover for 2 days before performing an imaging time course (Figure 3E). At 2 days post laser cutting (dplc), entpd5a+ expression was skipped in 15 out of 19 larvae in segments that directly neighbored the targeted myoseptum (Figure 3E). Between 4 and 6 dplc, the lesions began to heal allowing the notochord segment adjacent to the cut myoseptum to form (Figure 3E). To test the spatial specificity of the laser surgery, we also targeted only the ventral myosepta in a bilateral manner (ventral-left, ventral-right) (Figure S3A). Interestingly, when only ventral myosepta were cut, we observed a delay in ventral segment initiation, which is consistent with segmentation signals arising from both the dorsal and ventral sides (Figure S3B and S3C).To test whether forces loaded on myosepta by the axial musculature provide segment initiation cues, we targeted muscle fibers instead. To this end, we made laser cuts at the center of each surrounding muscle bundle, posterior to the last detectable entpd5a+ notochord segment (Figure 3F and 3I). Immediately following laser cutting, we observed that the two myosepta bordering the cut muscle segment displayed the same relaxed morphology, indicating that interconnected muscle fibers transfer tensile loading to the adjacent myosepta (Figure 3G and 3H). At 2 dplc, we observed that the two notochord segments that correspond to the affected myosepta bordering the cut muscle were skipped in 7 out of 8 larvae (Figure 3J). At 4 and 6 dplc, we also observed that segments filled in following the repair of muscle lesions (Figure 3J), which also extended to muscle-myoseptum attachment sites in some cases (Figure S3H). This suggested that mechanical coupling of the axial musculature via myoseptum-notochord interactions locally instructs notochord segment initiation.To test if muscle contractility is required for notochord segment formation, we generated a pool of CRISPR guides (gRNAs)28 to knockdown expression of Dystrophin, (dmd) (Figure 3K). Knockdown of Dystrophin produced mosaic disruptions to axial muscle morphology that progressively deteriorated (Figure S3I) as previously shown.29,30 Notably, when we compared either WT non-injected controls (NICs) or WT larvae expressing a GFP transgene injected with GFP-directed gRNA pool to dmd knockdowns of similar standard length (SL), we found that notochord segmentation was impaired. Specifically, we observed a reduction in the total number of complete segments, along with delays in segment initiation, which resulted in segment skipping similar to DAPT gaps or the effect of laser ablations (Figure 3L and 3M). Additionally, dmd knockdowns expressing TgKI(cmn-tdTomato);TP1:VenusPest exhibited reduced ECM deformation when visualizing changes in curvature (Figure S3J–M). Together, these findings along with our laser cut experiments demonstrate that forces loaded on the myosepta from the contracting axial musculature deform the notochord ECM at myoseptum attachment sites to precisely define notochord segment initiation positions.Focal adhesions transmit mechanical signals from the myoseptaOur imaging, laser cutting, and genetic perturbation experiments suggested that segment initiation cues are mechanically transmitted to the notochord via deformation of the ECM. We reasoned that cell-ECM complexes within the notochord sheath cells would be upregulated in response to these mechanical cues. To investigate this idea, we returned to our previously published RNA-sequencing dataset12 to identify candidate effectors of these interactions. We focused on the transitional or entpd5a+/col9a2+ cell population, which corresponds to cells that initiate segment formation (Figure 4A). We found a significant enrichment of transcripts for focal adhesion proteins including, vcla, pxna, zyx, tln2a, and ptk2ab (Figure 4B). We generated a TgKI(vclb-mScarlet) transgenic KI line and observed in cross-sections localization of Vinculin+ puncta on the basal membrane of cells expressing TP1:VenusPest, indicating the assembly of focal adhesions in new segments (Figure 4C). However, in regions lacking TP1:VenusPest expression, we observed fewer clusters along the basal membrane and noted more Vinculin+ puncta localized to cell-cell contacts, or adherens junctions (Figure 4D), indicating that focal adhesions are differentially upregulated in segmenting regions of the notochord sheath.Live-imaging approaches using the TgKI(vclb-mScarlet) proved challenging due to the widespread expression profile of Vinculin in the surrounding tissues, such as the axial musculature and vasculature. Therefore, to follow focal adhesions specifically in the notochord sheath, we injected RFP-vcla capped mRNA (cRNA) in single-cell embryos. Fortunately, RFP-Vinculin protein generated from these injections was particularly stable in the notochord sheath and could be readily detected until at least 9 dpf, well beyond the typical life span of most cRNA translated proteins (Figure 4E and 4F). In WT, RFP-Vinculin clustered at dorsal and ventral locations corresponding to myoseptum attachment sites (Figure 4E). Cells displaying RFP-Vinculin puncta also expressed TP1:VenusPest at a later developmental time point, and displayed increased clustering as segments matured (Figure 4F).Next, to assess if focal adhesion cluster assembly was dependent on myoseptum-notochord interactions, we used fss mutants, which fail to form well-defined somite boundaries and myosepta later in development.9 In fss mutants expressing TP1:VenusPest, RFP-Vinculin was only diffusely localized along the dorsal and ventral regions of the notochord (Figure 4G). At later time points, focal adhesion clustering was also diminished compared to WT (Figure 4H).To determine how the observed focal adhesion clustering relates to notochord segment initiation, we developed a pipeline (Materials and Methods) to analyze the spatial and temporal dynamics of RFP-Vinculin clusters with respect to TP1:VenusPest segmentation. In WT larvae, periodic clusters of RFP-Vinculin were readily detected at the dorsal and ventral portions of the notochord, which corresponded to TP1:VenusPest+ cells in the subsequent developmental time point (Figure 4I and 4K). By contrast, RFP-Vinculin clusters in fss mutants displayed decreased correlation with future TP1:VenusPest+ segments (Figure 4J and 4L). We also observed that some of the clusters were located laterally instead of being biased to the dorsal and ventral sides of the notochord (Figure 4J). These data indicate that myoseptum-notochord interactions lead to the formation of focal adhesions in sheath cells ahead of Notch activation and their differentiation into entpd5a+ cells.Next, we functionally tested whether focal adhesion assembly is required for notochord segment formation by treating 8 dpf larvae with the focal adhesion kinase (FAK) inhibitor PND-116831 for 60 hours. When we compared segmentation before and after treatment, we noted that the rate of new segment generation was significantly slowed in the treated larvae compared to the DMSO control (Figure 4M). To confirm that this effect was tissue-specific, we also injected embryos expressing the notochord sheath driver, col9a2:QF2, and TP1:VenusPest with a QUAS construct for a dominant-negative FAK32 (QUAS:ptk2abDN-mScarlet) or a QUAS:mCherry-NTR control (Figure 4N). When larvae were imaged at 10 dpf, those injected with QUAS:ptk2abDN-mScarlet showed a significant decrease in the number of notochord segments generated when compared to larvae injected with the QUAS:mCherry-NTR control (Figure 4O). Together, these data indicate that focal adhesion assembly relays notochord-myoseptum mechanical signals to precisely pattern new notochord segment formation.Disruptions in myosepta alter the spatial and temporal dynamics of notochord segmentationOur analyses of focal adhesion clustering in WT and fss mutants revealed that myosepta define the positions at which entpd5a segments form. To accurately determine the full dynamics of notochord segmentation in wild-type and fss mutants, we took high resolution time courses of entpd5a:pkRed expression and computationally unwrapped 3-D images to visualize the entire surface of the notochord using ImSAnE24 (Figure 5B and 5C, Figure S1). Surface projections revealed three phases of segmentation patterning in WT (Figure 5A). First, notochord segments begin as seeds located at regularly spaced intervals along the dorsal and ventral sides of the notochord. These seeds then grow and merge, forming a continuous ring, which later expands laterally to form mature segments (Figure 5B). By contrast, in fss mutants these seeds shift away from the dorsal and ventral portions of the notochord, often appearing on the lateral sides, and at irregular distances from adjacent seeds (Figure 5C). Computational extraction of these seed clusters and their positions around the notochord showed a shift in initiation sites from ventral locations in wild-type, to lateral ones in the fss mutants. (Materials and Methods, Figure 5D–F). These data are consistent with the position of focal adhesion clusters shown above (Figure 4E–L). As segmentation proceeds, we observed that the early seeding defects compound to produce errors in segment merging and maturation (Figure 5B and 5C), with some seeds failing to merge and generating partial segments or segments of varying widths (Figure 5C).To investigate how spatial defects impact the temporal dynamics of notochord segmentation (Figure 6A and 6B), we imaged entpd5a expression in WT and fss−/− larvae every 24 hours for several days. Using our previously developed computational routine, we calculated ordering indexes and the mismatches relative to an ideal sequential pattern (Figure 2D). We found that deviations in segment formation mostly clustered around 0 for WT, whereas fss mutants lost sequential segmentation (Figure 6C). We also noted that on average it took WT larvae 11 days to fully segment, whereas fss mutants took 14 days, indicating that the patterning dynamics are significantly slowed when SBs/myosepta are disrupted. Using our ordering indexes and ranked sequence of segment formation, we also characterized the emergence of new segments over time by tracking inter-segment distance between two consecutively formed segments (Figure S4A). A value of 1 indicated segments were sequentially and proximately created. We found that WT values cluster around 1, whereas the fss mutants display a broad spread in the formation of subsequent segments (Figure 6D). Interestingly, monitoring the average distance between unranked segments in fss−/− mutants shows that segments fill in to eventually achieve a periodic inter-segment distance comparable to that of WT (Figure 6E). These data reveal that the loss of regular spatial information from myosepta leads to slower, non-sequential segmentation dynamics within the notochord sheath. Our data also suggest the existence of a robust error correction process that fixes early patterning defects to produce a relatively well-segmented pattern even when the external inputs are highly disorganized.A theoretical model that captures the dynamics of notochord segmentationEven when spatial cues are completely compromised as in fss mutants, the unsegmented notochord still manages to move toward a final segmented pattern (Figure 7A). However, the time evolution of this process shifts from a predominantly sequential mode to non-sequential when the spatial information is disorganized. We therefore investigated if the emergence of periodic segments in the notochord and their assembly dynamics could be understood from minimal models which do not make any assumptions on the underlying molecular mechanisms a priori. Theoretically, several mechanisms including Turing’s reaction-diffusion system of morphogens33,34, cell-cell interaction-based models35, and mechanical instabilities36 could generate periodic patterns. All these mechanisms can be understood within a unified framework of model systems with competing short-range attraction and long-range repulsion.37 The phase transition associated with such competing interactions gives rise to modulated phases in a variety of physical systems38 and in the context of biological systems these can be applied as models of non-equilibrium pattern formation.33,39,40 The simplest model system obeying the requisite criterion can be captured by an effective free energy functional41 with dynamics governed by the corresponding Swift-Hohenberg equation.42,43 We adapted these ideas to formulate a coarse-grained expression that describes the notochord system using a spatially anisotropic functional (F) for a scalar order parameter Φ that captures whether a cell has been activated by Notch Φ>0 or not Φ<0 (Supplementary Text). At sufficiently low noise t, this model favors the formation of spatially modulated striped domains of length scale λ=2π/k0, which represent the alternate domains expressing either entpd5a or col9a2 along the AP x axis of the notochord. The effective model contains a term that favors interfaces perpendicular to the x-axis and one that inhibits domain formation perpendicular to the y-axis. The inherent anisotropy of the tubular shape of the notochord makes such assumptions plausible. At low fluctuations, the functional is minimized by the creation of interfaces between cell domains of scale λ. By incorporating these parameters, our model shows that for fixed k0, the system dynamics transitions from a nucleation (i.e., random segment formation) to a front propagation (i.e., sequential segment formation) regime as t diminishes, with larger λ favoring front propagation (Figure 7B). These data suggest that changes in the temporal order of segment formation can occur in our simple model.To obtain experimental values for k0 we extracted fluorescent signals from WT and fss−/− larvae expressing Bactin:pxna-eGFP and entpd5a:pkRed (Figure 7C–F). We then performed a Fourier transform (FT) of each signal and calculated the standard deviation (σ) and mean k0 from the ensemble averaged distribution (n=6 for WT and fss−/−) (Materials and Methods, Figure 7G–J). Plotting σ from the entpd5a and pxna FT signals from WT images, we found that their variance is relatively small and of similar range. By contrast, fss−/− larvae present a much higher variance for both markers (Figure 7K). Interestingly, we found that the calculated σ from the pxna signal displays more variation compared to entpd5a, indicating that the pattern of myosepta is much more disorganized than the final segmented pattern of the notochord (Figure 7K).We next used our model to analyze how the dynamics of the system changes with σ. By computing the nucleation rates and the front velocity V (Supplementary Text), we found that for a given τ, the system undergoes a crossover from front propagation to nucleation as σ increases (Figure 7L). Simulations of the temporal dynamics of the system for high and low values of σ produce front propagation and nucleation regimes, respectively (Figure 7M and 7N, Videos S1–3). For σ values obtained from WT, we observe that the dynamics is dominated by a sequential propagation (Video S1). However, in some of the simulation runs, a small loss in order was also observed (Video S2). Interestingly, similar defects are experimentally observed for WT fish in the rare occasions where a small loss of sequential order is detected (Figure S4B), indicating that in WT the system is close to the crossover point between front propagation and nucleation. In contrast, σ values derived from fss mutants show a much stronger loss in temporal order (Video S3, Figure S4C). We also compared the experimental values for the temporal ordering of segments and domain width with the simulation results and found them to be in good agreement (Figure S4D–G). Intuitively, this behavior can be understood as the following: fluctuations in the parameter k0, lead to fluctuations in the energy barrier that must be overcome for nucleation, which in turn leads to disorder in patterning, as segments occasionally form at random locations.Together, our phase-ordering model suggests that when inputs are provided at a fixed length scale, such as regularly spaced myosepta, sequential domain formation should be observed. However, fluctuations in the position of these inputs, as in the DAPT manipulations or in fss mutants, introduce variability in the timescale of nucleation, resulting in segments forming with spatial and temporal disorder. Consistent with this physical picture, the increase in spatial noise of external inputs found in the fss mutants delays patterning at notochord-myoseptum interaction sites with weak or diffuse signals, producing non-sequential segment initiation. Altogether, our study reveals that regularly spaced interactions with myosepta from the axial musculature contributes to spatially precise and sequential segmentation in the notochord sheath.

Disruptions in myosepta alter the spatial and temporal dynamics of notochord segmentation

A theoretical model that captures the dynamics of notochord segmentationEven when spatial cues are completely compromised as in fss mutants, the unsegmented notochord still manages to move toward a final segmented pattern (Figure 7A). However, the time evolution of this process shifts from a predominantly sequential mode to non-sequential when the spatial information is disorganized. We therefore investigated if the emergence of periodic segments in the notochord and their assembly dynamics could be understood from minimal models which do not make any assumptions on the underlying molecular mechanisms a priori. Theoretically, several mechanisms including Turing’s reaction-diffusion system of morphogens33,34, cell-cell interaction-based models35, and mechanical instabilities36 could generate periodic patterns. All these mechanisms can be understood within a unified framework of model systems with competing short-range attraction and long-range repulsion.37 The phase transition associated with such competing interactions gives rise to modulated phases in a variety of physical systems38 and in the context of biological systems these can be applied as models of non-equilibrium pattern formation.33,39,40 The simplest model system obeying the requisite criterion can be captured by an effective free energy functional41 with dynamics governed by the corresponding Swift-Hohenberg equation.42,43 We adapted these ideas to formulate a coarse-grained expression that describes the notochord system using a spatially anisotropic functional (F) for a scalar order parameter Φ that captures whether a cell has been activated by Notch Φ>0 or not Φ<0 (Supplementary Text). At sufficiently low noise t, this model favors the formation of spatially modulated striped domains of length scale λ=2π/k0, which represent the alternate domains expressing either entpd5a or col9a2 along the AP x axis of the notochord. The effective model contains a term that favors interfaces perpendicular to the x-axis and one that inhibits domain formation perpendicular to the y-axis. The inherent anisotropy of the tubular shape of the notochord makes such assumptions plausible. At low fluctuations, the functional is minimized by the creation of interfaces between cell domains of scale λ. By incorporating these parameters, our model shows that for fixed k0, the system dynamics transitions from a nucleation (i.e., random segment formation) to a front propagation (i.e., sequential segment formation) regime as t diminishes, with larger λ favoring front propagation (Figure 7B). Furthermore, it seems possible that coordination between these patterning processes may provide a conserved mechanism for ensuring efficient musculoskeletal connectivity during development.

Using a combination of genetic, pharmacological, and laser ablation experimental approaches, we find that these myoseptum-notochord interactions provide mechanical cues for segment initiation. Furthermore, there may be additional mechanisms that reinforce signaling at these attachment sites over time, such as progressive changes to the ECM at the interface of the myoseptum and notochord sheath, which has been observed previously47 and is further supported by the ECM deformations we observe ahead of Notch activation (Figures 1, 3, and S3). An increase in rigidity at the dorsal and ventral myoseptum contact sites may locally impart mechanical cues, from the growing muscle or maturing myosepta21,46, to induce an increase in focal adhesion clustering and assembly. When the external signals from the myosepta are weak or irregular, as in fss mutants or DAPT-treated conditions, ECM deformation is delayed, focal adhesions fail to cluster efficiently, and notochord segmentation loses spatiotemporal precision.49

Exactly how mechanical signals feed into the transcriptional changes associated with segment formation, such as Notch, or BMP signaling, which precedes Notch activation and the mineralization program49,50, remains unclear. Blocking BMP signaling by nog3 overexpression impairs segment growth, but not seed formation50, suggesting that the segment initiation sites defined by the notochord-myosepta interactions and segment growth mediated by BMP are separable.

Although the attachments between the myosepta and notochord evidently increase spatiotemporal precision of notochord segmentation, we find that even in WT larvae, segment initiation is not always purely sequential. Our simulations indicate that temporal fluctuations in segment ordering and variance in the periodicity of myoseptum signals place the wild-type notochord close to the crossover point between sequential and non-sequential patterning regimes (Figure 7B). This may allow the system to fill gaps in the pattern by switching segmentation regime, even when tissue patterning during earlier developmental processes, such as somitogenesis, fails. In fss−/− and DAPT-treated larvae, where spatial cues become especially noisy, the autonomous notochord segmentation program becomes dominant. While this program is less accurate and produces defects that arise as early as the first initial seeding events, the system can eventually fill in skipped regions, or gaps lacking segments. This robust autonomous program also ensures that filled-in segments manage to move toward more uniform inter-segment spacing and produce similar inter-segmental distances as those of WT notochords (Figure 6E). Furthermore, the reduction in disorder between the pattern of myosepta (pxna-eGFP) and entpd5a (Figure 7L) suggests that the notochord segmentation program is able to filter out noise from poorly patterned myosepta to produce a relatively regular pattern. Together, these results suggest that an axial tissue-coupling mechanism dominates segment initiation or segment seeding, whereas autonomous notochord patterning mechanisms can drive segment growth and correct defects by back-filling when spatial signals are poorly defined.

Our phase-ordering model suggests that when myoseptum cues are provided at discrete locations (single length-scale), spatial and temporal dynamics of segment initiation are preserved. By contrast, when external positional cues are irregular, temporal regulation of segment initiation is lost. Therefore, the temporal order imparted by regular, external landmarks may represent an emerging property of the system that adds precision to the patterning cues provided by tissue-autonomous signaling gradients. Dissecting the molecular mechanisms that regulate the signaling dynamics of the notochord segmentation program and phase ordering dynamics predicted by the model will be a major goal of future research.

To account for this change, the new segment- to- segment distances were summed until the results were similar. The number of new pairwise distances that needed to be added to match the previous pairwise distance identified the number of new segments at that time point. Finally, any leftover pairwise distances indicated segments formed to the right of the last known segment. Following our pairwise comparison, these new segments were again ranked according to their area and added to the running list. Once these regions were determined, segment areas and positions were no longer updated in the subsequent time points.
