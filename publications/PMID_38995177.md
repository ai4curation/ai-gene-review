TLRs are the most thoroughly studied group of pattern-recognition receptors that
play a central role in innate immunity. Among them, TLR10 (CD290) remains the
only TLR family member without a known ligand and clearly defined functions. One
major impediment to studying TLR10 is its absence in mice. A recent study on
TLR10 knock-in mice demonstrated its intrinsic inhibitory role in B cells,
indicating that TLR10 is a potential drug target in autoimmune diseases. In this
study, we interrogated the expression and function of TLR10 in human
plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs). We have seen that primary human pDCs, B
cells, and monocytes constitutively express TLR10. Upon preincubation with an
anti-TLR10 Ab, production of cytokines in pDCs was downregulated in response to
stimulation with DNA and RNA viruses. Upon further investigation into the
possible mechanism, we documented phosphorylation of STAT3 upon Ab-mediated
engagement of TLR10. This leads to the induction of inhibitory molecule
suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3) expression. We have also documented
the inhibition of nuclear translocation of transcription factor IFN regulatory
factor 7 (IRF7) in pDCs following TLR10 engagement. Our data provide the (to our
knowledge) first evidence that TLR10 is constitutively expressed on the surface
of human pDCs and works as a regulator of their innate response. Our findings
indicate the potential of harnessing the function of pDCs by Ab-mediated
targeting of TLR10 that may open a new therapeutic avenue for autoimmune
disorders.

Introduction:

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are the most thoroughly studied group of pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) that play a central role in innate immunity. TLRs help host immune cells to recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and activate a plethora of innate responses including innate cell activation and cytokine production. A total of 13 mammalian functional TLRs have been recognized thus far: 10 in humans and 12 in mice. Although the extracellular domains of TLRs comprise many leucine-rich repeats (LRRs), they show notable plasticity regarding ligand recognition (1). Human TLRs 1–9 have been widely studied, and all of their ligands have been characterized. However, TLR10 remains the only orphan receptor in the human TLR family.

TLR10 was first cloned in 2001 (2) and was later reported to be a functional gene in humans (3, 4) and many mammalian species. In mice, however, TLR10 was found to be a pseudo-gene, hence a genetically-deficient mouse model to study TLR10 could not be produced. In fact, TLR10 is disrupted by retroviral insertion in mice but conserved in all other rodents. A knock-in mouse model of TLR10 has recently been made, indicating an immunosuppressive role of TLR10 (5). Genetic studies have shown correlation between genetic variation of TLR10 and a myriad of immune disorders including asthma (6–8), rheumatoid arthritis (9), childhood IgA nephropathy (10), Crohn’s disease (11), sarcoidosis (12), autoimmune thyroid disease (13), and Graves’ disease (14). These findings suggest that TLR10 may have an immune-regulatory role.

TLR10 shares a common locus with TLR1 and TLR6 in the short arm of chromosome 4. Similar to TLR1 and TLR6, TLR10 is capable of forming a heterodimer with TLR2 by recognizing PAM3CSK4 but is incapable of generating a pro-inflammatory signal (15). Additionally, it is unknown if the TLR2/10 heterodimer is physiologically relevant since TLR2/1 has a higher affinity for PAM3CSK4, and, unlike TLRs 1,2 and 6, TLR10 is capable of forming a homodimer (15, 35). A recent study has suggested that TLR10 may compete with TLR3 for recognition of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) in monocytic cell lines and play an inhibitory role in innate immunity (15). However, a crystal structure of TLR10 with dsRNA or its synthetic analog is not available.

Similar to other members of the TLR family, the expression of TLR10 is cell-specific. TLR10 has been shown to be expressed in plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) and B cells (4, 16), monocytes, primary macrophages, and FoxP3+ regulatory T cells (17–19). The function of TLR10 has been explored in monocytic cell lines, primary monocytes and primary B cells but not in pDCs. In primary human monocytes, the monocytic cell line THP1, B cells, as well as other cellular constructs, TLR10 showed immunosuppressive function by inhibiting proinflammatory cytokine synthesis and suppressing B cell proliferation (5, 15, 19–21). On the other hand, TLR10 was also indicated to be playing a positive role in influenza-mediated cytokine expression in the THP1 cell line (18), H. pylori-mediated cytokine and chemokine expression in constructed cell lines (22), and Listeria-mediated cytokine production in an epithelial cell line (23). The role that TLR10 might play in pDCs is still currently unknown and is the basis of this study.

pDCs are specialized innate immune cells known for their ability to produce an abundant amount of type-I and type-III interferons (IFNs), as well as other cytokines in response to pathogenic stimuli (24–27). Both TLR7 and TLR9 pathways are highly active in pDCs, mediating their triggering of cytokine production in response to single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) and double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) (27). Apart from these intracellular TLRs, pDCs also express some of the extracellular TLRs; human pDCs have been reported to express TLR4 although to a much-subdued level (16, 28). Expression of TLR10 mRNA was reported in tonsil-derived pDCs constitutively as well as following treatment with interleukin 3 (IL3) and CD40L (4), yet the expression pattern, regulation, and function of TLR10 in pDCs have not been explored.

Here, we have explored the expression of TLR10 in pDCs at steady state as well as with different stimuli both at the mRNA and protein level. We have tested how antibody-mediated engagement of TLR10 in pDCs affects the function of this cell type in terms of cytokine production and activation. We confirmed that antibody-mediated engagement of TLR10 subdues the cytokine expression in monocytes as reported earlier. In pDCs, antibody-mediated engagement of TLR10 showed even more pronounced inhibition of virus-mediated cytokine production than that seen in monocytes without affecting the virus uptake by pDCs. Unlike monocyte-derived DCs, the capacity of pDCs to stimulate T cells was not affected by TLR10-engagement. We have also gathered evidence that the inhibitory effect upon TLR10-engagement in pDCs is possibly mediated by phosphorylation of STAT3 and induction of SOCS3 expression associated with inhibition of nuclear translocation of the transcription factor interferon regulatory factor 7 (IRF7).
