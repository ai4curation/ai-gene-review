The unfolded protein response (UPR) was discovered in budding yeast as a
mechanism that allows cells to adapt to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stressors.
Although the UPR is not thought to be necessary for cellular fitness of
wild-type cells in the absence of stress, we found that UPR deficiency led to
poor growth in cycling mitotic yeast cells. This led to pervasive adaptive
aneuploidy of specific chromosomes that was seen in divergent strain
backgrounds, indicating an important basal role for this pathway that was missed
by studies of the most common laboratory-derived strains. Aneuploid
UPR-deficient cells grew better than euploid UPR-deficient cells but exhibited
heightened general proteostatic stress, a hallmark of aneuploidy in wild-type
cells. Modulation of key genes involved in ER proteostasis that were encoded on
aneuploid chromosomes could phenocopy the effects of aneuploidy, indicating that
the reason UPR-deficient cells become aneuploid is to counteract protein folding
stress in the ER. Proteomic analyses indicate that expression of a small subset
of stress-induced UPR targets is supported by basal UPR activity, including the
chaperone Kar2/BiP. Together, our results reveal an unexpected role for the UPR
in baseline ER folding that is important enough to safeguard cellular fitness
that cells tolerate the substantial proteostatic costs that result from
aneuploidy to counteract its loss.

## Full Text

The unfolded protein response (UPR) is a conserved pathway that cells activate in response to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, helping them adapt to and cope with the stress. In metazoans, there are three main branches of the UPR, one of which is conserved in budding yeast. The ER transmembrane protein Ire1 can be activated by several cues, including those that result from a buildup of misfolded proteins in the ER, subsequently driving splicing of the cytoplasmically retained intron in the mRNA encoding Hac1 (XBP-1 in metazoans) (Sidrauski et al. 1996). Removal of the intron allows translation of the resultant spliced mRNA (termed sHAC1) (Chapman et al. 1998). Hac1 protein acts as a transcription factor to activate synthesis of mRNAs, including those encoding ER-localized chaperones (such as Kar2, known as BiP or GRP78 in mammals) (Cox et al. 1993; Cox and Walter 1996; Mori et al. 1996, 1998). Targets like Kar2 increase the capacity of the ER to fold proteins and allow the cell to inactivate the UPR and return to homeostasis.

Most studies of the UPR mechanism have relied on harsh external triggers, including drugs such as dithiothreitol (DTT) or tunicamycin. The UPR is also known to be important under physiological conditions in metazoans, including during development and B-cell differentiation (Reimold et al. 2000, 2001). Roles for the UPR in cytokinesis and haploid tolerance in yeast have been reported (Lee et al. 2003; Bicknell et al. 2007), but no conditions have been reported in which UPR deficiency leads to a reduced mitotic growth rate in wild-type (WT) cells, supporting the longtime characterization of the UPR as a stress pathway. Consistently, UPR deficiency has not been reported to result in aneuploidy. By adaptive aneuploidy, fitness of slow-growing cells is improved by gain or loss of specific chromosomes, leading to population-wide loss of euploidy. It is now known that this can occur efficiently in response to specific genetic or environmental conditions in budding yeast, an organism that is typically studied in its stable euploid state (Rancati et al. 2008; Chen et al. 2012; Yona et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2015; Beaupere et al. 2018). Some of the best-characterized cases of adaptive aneuploidy are in response to deletion of one of two paralogs encoding ribosomal proteins, which often results in a severe growth defect and aneuploidy, leading to gain of the chromosome encoding the other paralog (Steffen et al. 2012; Cheng et al. 2019). Population-level aneuploidy can arise extremely efficiently in such instances, likely owing to the strong selective pressure to restore translation capacity. Adaptive aneuploidy arising in unstressed cells would not be expected in response to disruption of a pathway without important basal homeostatic function.

Organisms rely on the proper balance of gene expression for optimal fitness, a balance that is maintained by stable euploid chromosome content. Aneuploidy results in a widespread imbalance in gene expression and has been shown to generally lead to poor cellular fitness compared with euploidy (Hughes et al. 2000; Niwa et al. 2006; Torres et al. 2007; Williams et al. 2008). This is thought to occur at least in part because protein complexes depend on precise stoichiometry of their protein components. Because transcription and translation generally occur in proportion to genomic copy number in aneuploid cells, degradation of oversynthesized protein complex components is required to prevent aggregation of orphan subunits (Springer et al. 2010; Torres et al. 2010; Tang et al. 2011). In aneuploid cells, these excess subunits tax the cellular machinery that promotes protein folding and degradation (Oromendia et al. 2012). Aneuploidy may be best studied for its association with cancer, as most solid tumor and hemopoietic cancer cells are aneuploid (Boveri 2008). The origins of aneuploidy in most cancers are unknown, but it has been proposed that the type of adaptive aneuploidy observed in yeast may also be relevant in a human cancer context (Pavelka et al. 2010a; Holland and Cleveland 2012; Ben-David and Amon 2020).

In this study, we unexpectedly identified the consistent and robust emergence of specific aneuploidies in UPR-deficient yeast cell populations. Remarkably, the aneuploidies that we observed in UPR-deficient haploid and diploid yeast cells were beneficial for growth, even in the absence of ER stressors. This benefit of aneuploidy occurs despite the presence of general proteostatic stress that is typical of aneuploidy in UPR-proficient cells. We found that maintenance of euploid cell populations under mitotic conditions requires continuous presence of the UPR, which we propose reflects a previously unrecognized requirement for basal UPR activity for cell fitness. These aneuploidies arose so rapidly that euploid populations following traditional gene deletion approaches could not be isolated, equivalent to the situation following loss of ribosomal protein-encoding genes (Steffen et al. 2012; Cheng et al. 2019) and indicating remarkably strong selective pressure to restore the function provided by basal UPR activity. Genes that lead to improved ER proteostasis seem to drive selection for these aneuploidies, which can also be prevented by increased levels of ER chaperones. Our findings indicate that baseline ER functions in protein folding require UPR activity and that loss of this activity is sufficiently costly to cells that aneuploidy is adaptive at the expense of general proteostasis.

Aneuploid UPR-deficient cells exhibit the characteristic proteostatic cost of chromosomal imbalance

Prior studies focusing on aneuploidy have reported a cellular cost to chromosome imbalances that results at least in part from increased flux through the ubiquitin proteasome system due to the need to degrade the excess subunits of protein complexes that are synthesized in improper stoichiometry (Pavelka et al. 2010b; Springer et al. 2010; Torres et al. 2010; Tang et al. 2011; Oromendia et al. 2012; Muenzner et al. 2024). Consistent with prior work in haploid cells containing one additional chromosome (Torres et al. 2007), both diploid ire1Δ and hac1Δ cells lacking one copy of chromosome V express transcripts in proportion to chromosome copy number. Specifically, mRNA-seq analysis revealed that mRNAs expressed from chromosome V were present in aneuploid strains at roughly half of the abundance seen in WT strains (Fig. 3A,B). Mass spectrometry analysis (Supplemental Table S2) also found protein levels for diploid UPR-deficient cells lacking one chromosome V and haploid UPR-deficient cells with an extra chromosome X to be generally proportional to gene stoichiometry (Fig. 3C,D). A small subset of proteins on chromosome X in haploid aneuploids was not expressed more highly than euploid controls, and their analysis revealed strong enrichment for membership in protein complexes (shaded in Fig. 3D), consistent with findings in studies of disomic wild-type yeast cells (Dephoure et al. 2014). No such buffered population of proteins was seen for chromosome V-encoded proteins in diploid cells, consistent with the need to lower the levels of partner proteins encoded by other chromosomes to achieve matched stoichiometry of protein complex components in this case. Consistently, several cases in which a protein encoded by another chromosome must be degraded in diploid −V cells can also be observed (e.g., see Fig. 3E,F), but in fact, the burden of degrading excess protein partners is even greater in monosomic diploid cells than in disomic haploid cells, owing to multisubunit complexes in which many partners are synthesized superstoichiometrically. Prime examples of this are the cytosolic and mitochondrial ribosomes, of which six and two components, respectively, are encoded from genes on chromosome V, necessitating degradation of dozens of proteins encoded by other chromosomes to achieve matched stoichiometry (as described by Cheng et al. 2019; Powers et al. 2022). Consistently, we observed lower levels of both classes of proteins in monosomic diploid cells regardless of the chromosome they are encoded from (Fig. 3G; Supplemental Fig. S3A).

DiscussionWe have found that UPR-deficient budding yeast cell populations reliably become aneuploid. These aneuploidies occur in both diploid and haploid cells and are highly specific and characteristic. Haploid UPR-deficient cells typically gain a copy of chromosome X, and diploid UPR-deficient cells most often lose one copy of chromosome V. The fact that UPR-deficient cell populations become predominantly aneuploid rapidly (e.g., in the time it takes to either make stable mutants or within 3 days of withdrawing Hac1 expression) suggests strong selective pressure for loss of euploidy even in the absence of stressors known to be associated with ER folding defects. Our results are intriguing for at least two major reasons. First, the UPR has not previously been thought to be needed to support mitotic growth of otherwise WT cells. Second, adaptive aneuploidy in this instance pits ER proteostasis against general cytosolic proteostasis and indicates that the former is of particularly strong importance to cellular fitness.What are the factors that lead to aneuploidy in UPR-deficient cells? It has been seen that yeast cells lacking the UPR display defects in chromosome segregation (Henry et al. 2010). Using a strategy to follow segregation of an individual chromosome in live cells (Straight et al. 1996; Marston et al. 2004), we did not observe a significant difference between WT and UPR-deficient SK1 cells (Supplemental Fig. S5A). It is possible that a modest effect on chromosome segregation from loss of UPR activity exists that was not detected by our assay. However, this would not be necessary for the type of adaptive aneuploidy that we observed, which would only require three factors: (1) a small pool of simple aneuploidies present in the population, (2) poor mitotic growth of cells with a mutation or WT cells exposed to environmental pressure, and (3) the existence of an aneuploid cell in the population for which the growth advantage relative to euploid cells offered by the altered chromosome complement is greater than the growth disadvantage conferred by the aneuploidy. Together, the balance of these factors determines whether adaptive aneuploidy will occur, how quickly it will occur, how pervasive it will be, and how many ways it may occur. The first requirement, the existence of some aneuploid cells in the population, is likely to be inevitable as a result of the low natural rate of chromosome segregation errors. The other two requirements vary profoundly by case. The loss of most genes impacts mitotic growth of yeast cells to some degree (Breslow et al. 2008). However, a recent study suggests that <9% of these would result in adaptive aneuploidy. The central insight of our study is that UPR deficiency invariably and rapidly leads to adaptive aneuploidy in multiple yeast strain backgrounds. This is surprising because it indicates (and we directly show) that the second requirement above is met despite the reported dispensability of this pathway for mitotic growth. The efficiency with which this occurs is akin to the strongest selective pressures reported to lead to adaptive aneuploidy (based on analysis of the yeast deletion collection), which are a result of the loss of translation capacity in ribosomal protein gene mutants (Steffen et al. 2012). A study from our laboratory using the same growth conditions and strain background as in this study analyzed ribosomal protein genes (Cheng et al. 2019) and observed invariable population-level aneuploidy arising in mutants, mirroring what we report here for cells lacking HAC1 or IRE1. It is also surprising that the third requirement can be met in the case of UPR deficiency, given the complexity of the target complement of this signaling pathway (Travers et al. 2000). In contrast to the case of ribosomal protein gene mutants, which reliably result in gain of the chromosome encoding the paralogous gene (Steffen et al. 2012; Cheng et al. 2019), we observed multiple independent strategies by which UPR-deficient cells can regain fitness through aneuploidy, providing insight into the basis of the selective pressures that underlie this adaptation. Our analyses of two of these suggest that the critical disrupted balance in mitotic cells lacking UPR activity is between ER chaperones and ER client content.Haploid cells disomic for chromosome X or diploid cells monosomic for chromosome V divide significantly more rapidly than euploid controls lacking the UPR. This is the opposite of the pattern seen for otherwise wild-type haploid cells disomic for chromosome X, which divide more slowly than control euploid cells (Torres et al. 2007; Pavelka et al. 2010b). The characteristic slow growth of aneuploid yeast cells has led to the conclusion that aneuploidy comes at a cost to cellular fitness. The mitotic growth defect observed in aneuploid cells has been attributed in part to the proteostatic cost of synthesizing excess protein complex members that must be degraded (Pavelka et al. 2010b; Springer et al. 2010; Torres et al. 2010; Tang et al. 2011; Oromendia et al. 2012; Muenzner et al. 2024). This conclusion is based on a number of observations, including expression of proteins proportional to their genomic copy number, the increased dependence of aneuploid cells on proteasome activity for their survival, and the increased presence of Hsp104 foci in aneuploid relative to wild-type cells (Pavelka et al. 2010b; Springer et al. 2010; Torres et al. 2010; Tang et al. 2011; Oromendia et al. 2012; Muenzner et al. 2024). We observed evidence of similar costs in aneuploid UPR-deficient cells, with transcript and protein abundance proportional to genomic copy number, and Hsp104 foci elevated in aneuploid versus wild-type cells. These data show that there is a trade-off occurring in the absence of UPR activity that favors support for ER protein folding at the expense of general proteostasis. A recent large-scale study of wild and laboratory yeast strains found upregulation of the proteasome as a unifying response to aneuploidy (Muenzner et al. 2024). Standard PCR-based homologous recombination cassette-swapping strategies in yeast were used to generate deletions of genes for this study (Longtine et al. 1998; Powers et al. 2022).
