## Full Text

Abstract

Plant CCCH proteins participate in the control of multiple developmental and adaptive processes, but the regulatory mechanisms underlying these processes are not well known. In this study, we showed that the Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ) CCCH protein C3H15 negatively regulates cell elongation by inhibiting brassinosteroid (BR) signaling. Genetic and biochemical evidence showed that C3H15 functions downstream of the receptor BR INSENSITIVE 1 (BRI1) as a negative regulator in the BR pathway. C3H15 is phosphorylated by the GLYCOGEN SYNTHASE KINASE 3 -like kinase BR-INSENSITIVE 2 (BIN2) at Ser111 in the cytoplasm in the absence of BRs. Upon BR perception, C3H15 transcription is enhanced, and the phosphorylation of C3H15 by BIN2 is reduced. The dephosphorylated C3H15 protein accumulates in the nucleus, where C3H15 regulates transcription via G-rich elements (typically GGGAGA). C3H15 and BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT 1 (BZR1)/BRI1-EMS-SUPPRESSOR 1 (BES1), two central transcriptional regulators of BR signaling, directly suppress each other and share a number of BR-responsive target genes. Moreover, C3H15 antagonizes BZR1 and BES1 to regulate the expression of their shared cell elongation-associated target gene, SMALL AUXIN-UP RNA 15 ( SAUR15 ). This study demonstrates that C3H15-mediated BR signaling may be parallel to, or even attenuate, the dominant BZR1 and BES1 signaling pathways to control cell elongation. This finding expands our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying BR-induced cell elongation in plants.

The CCCH transcription factor C3H15 antagonizes BZR1 and BES1 to inhibit BR signaling.

Introduction

The CCCH zinc finger family contains one or several motif(s) with three cysteine residues and one histidine residue, and members of this family have been widely identified in eukaryotes ( Bogamuwa and Jang, 2014 ; Fu and Blackshear, 2017 ). In animals, these CCCH proteins function in development, differentiation, metabolism, autophagy, and cancer progression through the regulation of multiple steps of RNA metabolism ( Fu and Blackshear, 2017 ). The best known CCCH protein is human tristetraprolin ( hTTP /ZFP36), which often binds to class II AU-rich elements (two or more overlapping UUAUUUA(U/A)(U/A) nonamers) in the 3′-untranslated region of target genes and recruits ARE mRNAs to cytoplasmic foci for silencing via RNA decay ( Lai et al., 1999 ). In contrast, only a small number of CCCHs have been functionally characterized in plants. These genes play critical roles in plant growth, development, and adaptive responses to the environment ( Bogamuwa and Jang, 2014 ). Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ) C3H14 and C3H15, a pair of CCCH paralogs, contain an hTTP -like CCCH motif and have potential posttranscriptional and transcriptional regulatory capabilities ( Kim et al., 2014 ; Chai et al., 2015 ). C3H14 and C3H15 redundantly control stem elongation and other developmental and adaptive processes ( Kim et al., 2014 ; Lu et al., 2014 ; Chai et al., 2015 ; Wang et al., 2020 ). However, the molecular mechanisms by which C3H14 and C3H15 control stem elongation remain unclear.

Brassinosteroids (BRs) are a group of hormones that regulate plant cell elongation ( Tong et al., 2014 ; Wei and Li, 2016 ; Planas-Riverola et al., 2019 ; Nolan et al., 2020 ). Mutants deficient in BR biosynthesis or signaling display a dwarf stature. Extensive studies in Arabidopsis have identified a BR signaling pathway that runs from the membrane-localized BR INSENSITIVE 1 (BRI1) receptor to two central transcription factors, BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT 1 (BZR1) and BRI1-EMS-SUPPRESSOR 1 (BES1), which activate or suppress the expression of many BR-responsive genes ( Kim and Wang, 2010 ; Yang et al., 2011 ; Wang et al., 2014 ; Nolan et al., 2020 ). In the absence of BRs, BZR1 or BES1 is phosphorylated by BR-INSENSITIVE 2 (BIN2; He et al., 2002 ; Li and Nam, 2002 ) and consequently degraded by the 26S proteasome in the cytoplasm ( He et al., 2002 ; Gampala et al., 2007 ). Upon perception of BRs, BIN2 kinase activity is inhibited; then, BZR1 and BES1 are dephosphorylated by phosphatase 2A and move from the cytoplasm into the nucleus to regulate the expression of downstream genes ( Yin et al., 2002 ; Tang et al., 2011 ).

BZR1 and BES1 share 88% protein sequence identity and exhibit distinct yet overlapping roles in controlling cell elongation. The gain-of-function mutants bzr1-1D and bes1-D display reduced stature and repression of the phenotype observed in BR-deficient and BR-insensitive mutants ( Wang et al., 2002 ; Yin et al., 2002 ). At the molecular level, BZR1 binds to the BR-responsive element (BRRE; CGTGT/CG) and mainly represses gene expression ( He et al., 2005 ), while BES1 binds to the E-box (CANNTG) by interacting with BES1-INTERACTING MYC-LIKE 1 or MYB30 to preferentially promote target gene expression ( Yin et al., 2002 ; Li et al., 2008 ). BZR1 can also bind to the E-box and BES1 to BRRE, both of which account for the coordinated and direct regulation of the expression of multiple target genes, such as the BR signaling marker gene SMALL AUXIN-UP RNA 15 ( SAUR15 ; He et al., 2005 ; Yu et al., 2011 ). Currently, the components of the BZR1/BES1-independent BR signaling pathways are less well known.

In this study, we have shown that C3H15 performs transcriptional regulation by preferentially binding to G-rich elements (typically GGGAGA) in the promoters of target genes. C3H15 negatively regulates cell elongation by inhibiting BR signaling. BR treatment promotes C3H15 transcription and reduces BIN2 phosphorylation of C3H15, which corresponds to the translocation of C3H15 from the cytoplasm to the nucleus. We also found that the Ser111 residue of C3H15 is a phosphorylation site for BIN2. More importantly, C3H15 was shown to antagonize BZR1 and BES1 to regulate the expression of SAUR15 , a common cell elongation-associated target gene. Thus, it is proposed that the fine regulation of BR signaling by C3H15 and BZR1/BES1 may contribute to cell elongation in Arabidopsis.

Results

C3H15 negatively regulates cell elongation by inhibiting BR signaling

Arabidopsis C3H14 and C3H15 have been shown to redundantly and negatively control cell elongation in stems ( Kim et al., 2014 ; Chai et al., 2015 ). In this study, we investigated the hypocotyl elongation phenotype of C3H14OE ( 35S:C3H14 ), C3H15OE ( 35S:C3H15 ), and c3h14c3h15 seedlings. The c3h14c3h15 plants exhibited significantly longer hypocotyls than wild-type (WT) plants, whereas the hypocotyls of C3H14OE and C3H15OE plants were shorter in darkness or dim light ( Figure 1A; Supplemental Figure S1, A and B ). To determine the association of C3H14/15-repressed cell elongation with the BR response, we detected the sensitivities of WT (control), C3H14/15 overexpression and c3h14c3h15 plants to propiconazole (PCZ, a BR biosynthesis inhibitor) and 2,4-eBL. As expected, hypocotyl elongation of all the genotypes was inhibited with PCZ treatment and promoted with BR treatment. In the presence of PCZ, the largest reduction was in C3H14OE and C3H15OE plants, followed by WT, and the smallest reduction was in c3h14c3h15 plants ( Figure 1A; Supplemental Figure S1A ). In contrast, the promotion of hypocotyl elongation by BR was decreased in C3H14OE and C3H15OE plants but was increased in c3h14c3h15 plants, compared with WT plants ( Supplemental Figure S1B ). C3H15 and C3H14 redundantly inhibited hypocotyl elongation, and C3H15 was strongly expressed in the elongated hypocotyls of etiolated seedlings in darkness ( Supplemental Figure S2 ). Therefore, C3H15 was used for subsequent investigations. Transgenic lines overexpressing C3H15 driven by its own promoter, such as 35S:C3H15 plants, displayed shorter hypocotyls and increased PCZ sensitivity relative to WT plants ( Supplemental Figure S1, C and D ), further confirming that C3H15 inhibits hypocotyl elongation likely via BR signaling.

Figure 1.

To determine whether there is an interaction between C3H15 and BRI1 at the genetic level, we generated c3h14c3h15bri1-5 and C3H15OE/bri1-6 plants. Simultaneous mutations in C3H14 and C3H15 repressed the dwarf phenotype of bri1-5 , a BRI1 mutant ( Figure 1B ). Dark-grown c3h14c3h15bri1-5 seedlings were less sensitive to PCZ than bri1-5 seedlings ( Figure 1C ). Similar to c3h14c3h15 ( Chai et al., 2015 ), c3h14c3h15bri1-5 plants were completely sterile and flowered earlier than bri1-5 plants. Additionally, the defective phenotype of bri1-6 (another loss-of-function allele of bri1 ) was markedly increased when C3H15 was ectopically expressed ( Supplemental Figure S3 ). These results suggested that C3H15 may function downstream of BRI1 as a negative regulator in the BR signaling pathway.

C3H15 regulates the expression of many BR-responsive genes

Our previous microarray data identified 1,322 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the stems of C3H15OE and/or c3h14c3h15 versus WT plants ( Chai et al., 2015 ). These DEGs were defined as C3H15-regulated genes ( Supplemental Table S1A ). To examine the association of C3H15 regulation with BR signaling, the 1,322 C3H15-regulated genes were compared to genes that are affected by BR treatment or BR mutation ( Supplemental Table S1B ; Sun et al., 2010 ). A total of 274 (20.7%) and 170 (12.9%) of the 1,322 C3H15-regulated genes overlapped with 1,562 BR-activated genes and 1,434 BR-repressed genes, respectively ( Figure 1D; Supplemental Table S2 ). Notably, nearly 1/3 (438/1,322) of the C3H15-regulated genes were responsive to BR. Of these 438 genes, C3H15 repressed the expression of more (>2 times or 173/81) BR-activated genes than BR-repressed genes ( Figure 1D ). These results suggested that C3H15 may function by mediating the regulation of these BR-responsive genes.

Functional classification of the 438 C3H15-regulated BR-responsive genes based on Gene Ontology (GO; P < 0.05) categories showed that C3H15 controlled a range of biological processes and cell activities and mediated multiple hormone signaling, which might be associated with BRs ( Figure 1E; Supplemental Table S2C ). In particular, BR activated or repressed the expression of a number of C3H15-regulated enzymes associated with cell wall modification, such as expansins, extensins, pectin lyases, xyloglucan endotransglycosylases, and cellulose synthases, which are likely to mediate the promotion of cell elongation by BR. Combined with the above genetic data ( Figure 1, B and C ), it is concluded that C3H15 may be a negative component of the BR signaling pathway.

C3H15 is a phosphorylation substrate of BIN2 in BR signaling

To identify the C3H15-interacting components of BR signaling, we performed yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays to screen an Arabidopsis hypocotyl library that was constructed using the hypocotyls of 5-d-old dark-grown WT plants in our lab. Of the positive clones, the GLYCOGEN SYNTHASE KINASE 3 (GSK3)-like kinase BIN2, which phosphorylates BZR1 and BES1 in the BR pathway ( Li and Nam, 2002 ), was found ( Figure 2A ). Pull-down assays revealed that Glutathione S transferase (GST)-BIN2, but not GST, pulled down a substantial amount of MBP-C3H15 protein ( Figure 2B ), lending support to the C3H15–BIN2 interaction observed by Y2H assays. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) results showed that the protein complexes pulled down by anti-HA agarose were recognized by anti-Myc antibody in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves cotransformed with 35S:Myc-C3H15 and 35S:BIN2-HA but not with 35S:Myc-GFP (control) and 35S:BIN2-HA ( Figure 2C ). All these results confirmed the physical interaction between C3H15 and BIN2 both in vitro and in vivo.

Figure 2.

To determine whether C3H15 is phosphorylated by BIN2, we performed western blot analysis of the total proteins derived from 35S:Myc-C3H15 plants by using an antibody against Myc. Two different forms of C3H15 protein were detected ( Figure 2D ), similar to those observed for BZR1 and BES1 ( Wang et al., 2002 ; Yin et al., 2002 ). The intensity of the slow migrating band was drastically enhanced when BIN2 was included, but this intensity was antagonized after the addition of λ-phosphatase (λ-PPase; Figure 2D ). This result indicated that the slow band represents phosphorylated Myc-C3H15 and the fast band represents dephosphorylated Myc-C3H15. Furthermore, BIN2 may mediate the phosphorylation of C3H15.

To examine whether BIN2 phosphorylates C3H15 in planta, we treated WT (control) and 35S:Myc-C3H15 seedlings with bikinin, a BIN2 inhibitor, as the application of bikinin causes the activation of BR signaling ( De Rybel et al., 2009 ). Hypocotyl elongation was enhanced in the two genotypes under two different bikinin concentrations, but this induction was more dramatic in WT plants than in 35S:Myc-C3H15 plants ( Supplemental Figure S4A ). Consistently, bikinin treatment increased the accumulation of dephosphorylated C3H15 and reduced the amount of the phosphorylated form ( Supplemental Figure S4B ). Furthermore, BIN2-mediated phosphorylation of C3H15 was reduced with BR treatment ( Supplemental Figure S4C ). The severe dwarf phenotype of bin2-1 (+/−) plants with enhanced BIN2 activity and reduced BR sensitivity ( Li et al., 2001 ) was partially rescued in the c3h14c3h15 background ( Figure 2E ). Collectively, these results indicated that C3H15 may be phosphorylated by BIN2 in the absence of BR.

Ser111 of C3H15 is a phosphorylation target site for BIN2

To characterize the BIN2 phosphorylation sites on C3H15, we performed liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis of the phosphorylated Myc-C3H15 peptides from 35S:Myc-C3H15 plants by incubating them with BIN2. The most abundant phosphopeptide contained Ser111 as the phosphorylation site ( Figure 3A ). To clarify whether BIN2-mediated phosphorylation at Ser111 is required for C3H15 function, we generated transgenic Arabidopsis lines expressing the native form ( C3H15 WT ) or phosphodeficient form ( C3H15 S111A ; Ser111 mutated to Ala). Two representative C3H15 variant lines were selected because in these lines, the transcript levels of C3H15 were comparable and within 80-fold that of WT plants detected by reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) ( Figure 3B ). This ensures that the growth differences between these lines are likely not due to differences in C3H15 expression. The protoplasts of these C3H15 WT and C3H15 S111A lines were extracted for transformation with 35S:BIN2 . Western blotting data showed that in the presence of BIN2, the S111A mutation in C3H15 resulted in reduced accumulation of phosphorylated C3H15 protein but increased accumulation of dephosphorylated C3H15 in comparison to the WT form ( Figure 3C ). Consistently, C3H15 S111A plants displayed shorter hypocotyls, a reduced response to bikinin and a lower expression level of SAUR15 , a cell elongation-associated gene ( Yin et al., 2002 ), compared with C3H15 WT plants ( Figure 3, D and E ). Moreover, bikinin-mediated induction of SAUR15 expression was attenuated in C3H15 S111A plants relative to C3H15 WT plants ( Figure 3E ). These results suggested that BIN2-mediated phosphorylation of C3H15 may occur at Ser111 during BR signaling.

Figure 3.

BR promotes the accumulation of dephosphorylated C3H15 in the nucleus

BZR1 and BES1 function as nucleocytoplasmic signaling mediators by shuttling between the cytoplasm and the nucleus in response to BRs ( Wang et al. 2002 ; Yin et al. 2002 ). To determine how BR treatment affects the translocation of C3H15 between the cytoplasm and the nucleus, we performed subcellular fractionation experiments using 35S:Myc-C3H15 seedlings. Under normal conditions, both phosphorylated and dephosphorylated forms of C3H15 accumulated in the cytoplasmic fraction, and only the dephosphorylated form was observed in the nucleus ( Supplemental Figure S5A ). When plants were treated with BR, the dephosphorylated C3H15 protein level was increased 1.9-fold in the nucleus, while the phosphorylated form was reduced in the cytoplasm ( Supplemental Figure S5B ). We further investigated the effect of BR treatment on C3H15 subcellular localization in tobacco leaves. With BR treatment, the GFP-C3H15 signal was rapidly enhanced in the nucleus within 1 h but weakened in the cytoplasm ( Supplemental Figure S5C ). Digital fluorescence signals revealed that the ratio of GFP-tagged C3H15 in the nucleus compared to the cytoplasm was increased more than five-fold with BR treatment. These results suggested that BRs might promote the transfer of C3H15 from the cytoplasm to the nucleus for transcriptional regulation, similar to the effect of BRs on BZR1 and BES1.

C3H15 has a BR-responsive pattern that is similar to but distinct from that of BZR1 or BES1

To investigate the BR-responsive pattern of C3H15 (BZR1 and BES1 as the controls), we first detected C3H15 , BZR1 , and BES1 transcription in WT seedlings that were treated with different concentrations (0, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 0.2, 1, and 10 µM) of 2,4-eBL. The RT-qPCR results revealed that the expression level of C3H15 was substantially increased at 0.01 µM (19.1-fold) or 0.1 µM (>6.2-fold), and slightly reduced (∼0.8-fold) at >1 µM BL compared with untreated controls ( Supplemental Figure S6A ). BZR1 and BES1 mRNA levels were less altered at 0.01 µM and weakly repressed (∼0.6-fold) at >0.1 µM BL. To examine the effects of BR on C3H15, BZR1, and BES1 proteins, we treated 35S:Myc-C3H15 , 35S:BZR1-HA , and 35S:Myc-BES1 plants with two contrasting concentrations of BL (low, 10 nM; high, 1 µM). Western blotting results revealed that these two BL treatments caused a gradual increase in the ratio of dephosphorylated C3H15/BZR1 to their corresponding phosphorylated forms until 12 h ( Figure 4, A and B ; Supplemental Figure S6, B and C ). Dephosphorylation of BES1 was induced at 1 µM but not at 10-nM BL treatment ( Figure 4C; Supplemental Figure S6D ). It is noted that under 1-µM BL treatment C3H15 accumulation was gradually increased until 12 h, but BZR1 and BES1 proteins retained at a high level from 0.5 h ( Figure 4 ).

Figure 4.

To determine the stability of C3H15 protein in the presence of BR, we treated 35S:Myc-C3H15 plants using cycloheximide (CHX), which blocks de novo protein biosynthesis, followed by BR treatment. Similar to the induction effect of BR on the BZR1 and BES1 proteins ( He et al., 2002 ; Gampala et al., 2007 ), more accumulation of dephosphorylated C3H15 was observed with BR treatment. This accumulation was more obvious without CHX treatment than with CHX treatment ( Supplemental Figure S7 ), indicating that BR induction stabilizes dephosphorylated C3H15. Collectively, our results indicated that BR may have some overlapping and some distinct effects on C3H15, BZR1, and BES1.

Identification of the DNA-binding motifs of C3H15

Our recent study showed that C3H15 has both DNA- and RNA-binding abilities ( Chai et al., 2015 ). However, the binding motifs of C3H15 are still unknown. Here, we combined bioinformatic analysis and electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) to identify the putative DNA binding motifs of C3H15. The 1-kb promoter sequences of 1853 DEGs (greater than two-fold) from comparisons of microarray data from C3H14OE , C3H15OE , or c3h14c3h15 versus WT plants ( Chai et al., 2015 ) were extracted to locate the recurrent motifs using the online software MEME ( Bailey et al., 2009 ). Potential C3H15-binding sequences were found in the promoters of the DEGs ( Supplemental Figure S8A ). Three putative core sequences, GGGAGA (P1), TGGAGA (P2), and AGAAGA (P3), were selected to test C3H15 binding ability using EMSA. Recombinant C3H15 protein bound to P1 but not to P1m (AAAAAA), P2, or P3 ( Figure 5A ). Unlabeled P1 competed with labeled P1 for the binding site, indicating binding specificity. To determine the optimal DNA sequence to which C3H15 binds, the 6-bp core sequence GGGAGA (P1) was mutated by substituting one nucleotide at a time with all three other nucleotides to create probes for EMSA ( Figure 5B; Supplemental Figure S8B ). Mutating individual residues in P1 visibly reduced C3H15 binding, except for M4-1 (GGGGGA) and M6-1 (GGGAGG), which were recognized by C3H15 more significantly. It should be noted that mutation of P1 to M1-2 (AGGAGA) or M1-3 (TGGAGA) completely stopped C3H15 binding, implying that the first residue in P1 must be a G or C for C3H15 to bind. These results indicated that the G-rich elements (named CHRE, typically GGGAGA) may be C3H15-responsive cis-regulatory motifs.

Figure 5.

C3H15 and BZR1 or BES1 directly repress each other at the transcriptional level

The promoters of BZR1 and BES1 contain the CHRE sites GGGAG/CA ( Figure 5C ), which prompted us to analyze whether C3H15 directly regulates BZR1 and BES1 expression. EMSA results validated that C3H15 bound to the BZR1 and BES1 promoter fragments covering the CHRE sites in vitro ( Supplemental Figure S9A ). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR results revealed that the amount of BZR1 or BES1 promoter DNA immunoprecipitated from 35S:Myc-C3H15 plants was more than two-fold higher than that precipitated from WT plants ( Figure 5C ). These results, together with other findings that the expression levels of BZR1 and BES1 were upregulated in c3h14c3h15 plants but downregulated in C3H15OE plants ( Figure 5D ), indicated that C3H15 directly suppresses BZR1 and BES1 transcription by binding to CHRE elements in the promoters of BZR1 and BES1 .

BZR1 and BES1 function as master regulators of BR signaling through activation and suppression of the expression of many target genes ( Sun et al., 2010 ; Yu et al., 2011 ). To test whether C3H15 is a target of BZR1 and BES1, we first detected the expression levels of C3H15 in the gain-of-function mutants bzr1-1D and bes1-D . C3H15 expression was suppressed significantly in the two gain-of-function mutants compared to WT plants ( Figure 5E ). β-glucuronidase (GUS) activities in Arabidopsis leaf protoplasts cotransformed with proC3H15:GUS and 35S:BZR1 or 35S:BES1 showed a significant reduction compared with those cotransformed with proC3H15:GUS and the empty vector (EV) ( Supplemental Figure S10 ), confirming the negative regulation of C3H15 by BZR1 and BES1. Promoter sequence analysis showed that the promoter region of C3H15 contains an E-box CACTTG that is a cis-acting element bound by BZR1 or BES1 ( Sun et al., 2010 ). Thus, we used the promoter fragment containing the E-box as a probe in EMSA to assess the binding ability of BZR1 or BES1 in vitro. Both BZR1 and BES1 proteins specifically bound to the E-box in the C3H15 promoter ( Supplemental Figure S9B ). ChIP-qPCR assays on WT, pBZR1:mBZR1-CFP , and 35S:Myc-BES1 seedlings revealed that BZR1 or BES1 was significantly enriched at −983 bp but not at −274 bp (a negative control) ( Figure 5F ), indicating the binding of BZR1 or BES1 to the C3H15 promoter fragment at position −983 bp in vivo. All these results indicated that BZR1 and BES1 directly suppress the expression of C3H15 .

To explore the genetic relationship between C3H15 and BZR1 or BES1, we overexpressed C3H15 in the bzr1-1D or bes1-D background. The C3H15OE / bzr1-1D and C3H15OE / bes1-D seedlings had shorter hypocotyls and displayed observably enhanced PCZ sensitivity compared with bzr1-1D and bes1-D seedlings in darkness, respectively ( Figure 5G ). Further, the inhibition of hypocotyl elongation in C3H15OE seedlings was weakened in bzr1-1D or bes1-D ( Figure 5G; Supplemental Figure S1B ). All these results suggested that C3H15 might offset the dominant BZR1/BES1 signaling during cell elongation.

C3H15 represses the expression of some BZR1 and BES1 target genes

The BZR1/BES1-centered regulatory network coordinates cell elongation ( Nolan et al., 2020 ). To investigate how C3H15 affects the transcriptional regulation of BZR1 or BES1 during cell elongation, we compared DEGs from C3H15OE , c3h14c3h15 , bzr1-1D , and bes1-D plants versus WT plants ( Yu et al., 2011 ; Oh et al., 2012 ; Chai et al., 2015 ). A total of 252 (19%) and 209 (15.8%) of the 1,322 C3H15-regulated genes overlapped with 2,151 BZR1- and 3,835 BES1-regulated genes, respectively; 48 of these were coregulated genes ( Figure 6A; Supplemental Table S3 ). A comparison of these overlapping genes and BR-responsive genes ( Supplemental Table S1 ) showed that 46.0% (116/252) of C3H15- and BZR1-coregulated genes and 49.3% (103/209) of C3H15- and BES1-coregulated genes were affected by BRs ( Figure 6A ). Moreover, C3H15-activated, BR-repressed, and BZR1/BES1-repressed genes (34.5%) and C3H15-repressed, BR-activated, and BZR1/BES1-activated genes (24.1%) accounted for the majority of the 29 C3H15-, BZR1-, and BES1-coregulated BR-responsive genes ( Figure 6A; Supplemental Table S3C ).

Figure 6.

Eleven BR-responsive targets of BZR1 and/or BES1 ( Supplemental Table S3 ) were selected to investigate how C3H15 affects the BZR1 or BES1 cascade. Among these genes, XYLOGLUCAN ENDOTRANSGLUCOSYLASE-HYDROLASE 6 ( XTH6 ), XTH9 , XTH33 , SAUR15 , and SAUR63 mediate cell elongation ( Yu et al., 2011 ); CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENESIS AND DWARFISM ( CPD ), DWARF4 ( DWF4 ), BR-6-OXIDASE ( BR6OX ), and ROTUNDFOLIA 3 ( ROT3 ) suppress and BR-ENHANCED EXPRESSION 1 ( BEE1 ) induces BR biosynthesis ( He et al., 2005 ; Poppenberger et al., 2011 ; Yu et al., 2011 ); but AT3G51400 is not functionally annotated . The RT-qPCR results showed that BR promoted C3H15-driven suppression of four BR- and BZR1/BES1-induced genes ( XTH33 , BEE1 , SAUR15 , and SAUR63 ), one BR-induced and BZR1/BES1-repressed gene ( XTH9 ), and six BR- and BZR1/BES1-repressed genes ( XTH6 , CPD , DWF4 , BR6OX , ROT3 , and AT3G51400 ) ( Figure 6B ). Promoter sequence analysis revealed that the 2,000-bp promoter fragments of XTH33 , SAUR15 , DWF4 , BR6OX , ROT3 , BEE1 , and AT3G51400 individually contain the CHRE and G/E-box sites bound by C3H15 and BZR1/BES1, respectively, at different regions ( Figure 6C ). EMSA data showed that C3H15 specifically bound to the CHRE sites in the promoters of the seven genes in vitro ( Supplemental Figure S9A ). ChIP-qPCR results confirmed that BR treatment increased the binding of C3H15 to the CHRE sites in each gene in vivo ( Figure 6D ). Thus, C3H15 may control cell elongation and BR biosynthesis through direct and indirect regulation of these BR-responsive BZR1 and/or BES1 targets.

C3H15 antagonizes BZR1 or BES1 to regulate the expression of SAUR15 , a common cell elongation-associated target gene

To evaluate the potential antagonistic functions of C3H15 and BZR1/BES1 in controlling cell elongation, we first performed transcriptional activation assays in Arabidopsis leaf protoplasts to analyze the expression pattern of SAUR15 , a common cell elongation-associated target gene. As illustrated in Figure 7, A and B , the expression of proSAUR15:GUS was repressed by C3H15 but activated by BZR1 and BES1 in the protoplasts. However, the inhibition effect of SAUR15 by C3H15 was significantly weakened by coexpression of BZR1 or BES1 , that was associated with reduced binding of C3H15 to the SAUR15 promoter by overexpressing BZR1 or BES1 in leaf protoplasts ( Figure 7C ). EMSA was further performed to examine whether BZR1 and BES1 affect the binding of C3H15 to CHRE promoter regions. The SAUR15 promoter fragment (from −344 bp to −272 bp, Figure 6C ) covering two CHREs and one E-box was used as the probe. As shown in Figure 7D , the binding of C3H15 to CHRE in the promoter region of SAUR15 was observably inhibited by the addition of equal BZR1 or BES1. These results indicated that C3H15 may antagonize BZR1 and BES1 when regulating SAUR15 expression.

Figure 7.

To examine how BR treatment affects the antagonism between C3H15 and BZR1/BES1 during transcription, we detected SAUR15 expression in WT, C3H15OE , bzr1-1D , bes1-D , C3H15OE/bzr1-1D , and C3H15OE/bes1-D seedlings under low (0.01 µM) or high (1 µM) concentrations of 2,4e-BL. The expression levels of SAUR15 in bzr1-1D and bes1-D seedlings were significantly attenuated in the C3H15OE background ( Figure 7, E and F ), similar to the results obtained from transcriptional activation assays ( Figure 7, A and B ). Altered expression of SAUR15 was detected in BL (0.01 µM or 1 µM)-treated WT, C3H15OE , bzr1-1D , and C3H15OE/bzr1-1D plants but only in 1-µM BL-treated bes1-D and C3H15OE/bes1-D plants compared to their corresponding untreated controls ( Figure 7, E and F ). These results indicated that a high level of BR induction may enhance the antagonizing effect of C3H15 on BZR1 and BES1 when regulating SAUR15 expression.

Discussion

Cell elongation in plants is controlled by intrinsic growth regulators or hormones and environmental cues through interconnected signal transduction pathways ( Depuydt and Hardtke, 2011 ; Oh et al., 2014 ; McKim, 2019 ). Currently, the regulatory mechanisms underlying cell elongation have not been fully characterized. In this study, we have demonstrated that the Arabidopsis CCCH protein C3H15 acts as a transcriptional repressor and inhibits cell elongation by binding to the CHRE sites in the promoters of some cell elongation-associated target genes, including SAUR15 and XTH33 . This result also confirms our previous finding that C3H15 has DNA-binding ability ( Chai et al., 2015 ).

C3H15 is a negative BR signaling protein that functions downstream of BIN2

Although the BR signaling pathway has been investigated extensively in plants, the BZR1/BES1-independent signaling pathway is poorly understood. Recently, three studies have shown that BR signaling negatively regulates the NAC transcription factor RESPONSIVE TO DESICCATION 26 (RD26) and AP2/ERF transcription factor TINY through BIN2 phosphorylation ( Ye et al., 2017 ; Jiang et al., 2019 ; Xie et al., 2019 ). Here, we identified C3H15 as another phosphorylation substrate of BIN2 and it inhibited BR signaling. The c3h14c3h15 double mutants, such as bzr1-1D and bes1-D ( Wang et al., 2002 ; Yin et al., 2002 ), exhibited less sensitivity to PCZ treatment than WT plants, while 35S:C3H15 and proC3H15:C3H15 plants displayed an increase in PCZ sensitivity in darkness ( Figure 1A; Supplemental Figure S1 ). In the c3h14c3h15 background, the dwarf phenotype of bri1-5 was partially rescued, and its sensitivity to PCZ was decreased ( Figure 1, B and C ). Of 438 C3H15-regulated BR-responsive genes, C3H15 downregulates 173 BR-activated genes and upregulates 89 BR-repressed genes. Further, C3H15-regulated BR-responsive genes are involved in a variety of cellular, developmental, and regulatory processes ( Figure 1E ). Consistent with this finding, C3H15 controls vascular formation, fertility, and immune responses, in addition to cell elongation ( Kim et al., 2014 ; Lu et al., 2014 ; Chai et al., 2015 ; Wang et al., 2020 ). It will be interesting to investigate whether C3H15 regulates these processes by mediating BR signaling. Our transcript data revealed that 33.1% of the C3H15-regulated genes were associated with the BR response ( Supplemental Table S2 ), which was much lower than the ratio (∼80%) of BZR1- or BES1-regulated BR-responsive genes to their downstream genes ( Sun et al., 2010 ; Yu et al., 2011 ). One possible explanation for this result is that C3H15 is not simply a downstream component of the BR signaling pathway. Indeed, c3h14c3h15bri1-5 plants displayed a completely sterile phenotype, which is different from bri1-5 plants ( Figure 1B ). It is speculated that C3H15 signaling functions as a branch of the BR signaling pathway.

Similar to BZR1 and BES1, C3H15 directly interacted with BIN2 kinase and could be phosphorylated in the absence of BR ( Figure 2 ). With BR or bikinin treatment, the phosphorylation of C3H15 by BIN2 was rapidly decreased ( Supplemental Figure S4 ). Ser111 of C3H15 was identified as a phosphorylation target site of BIN2 ( Figure 3 ). Compared with C3H15 WT plants, S111A mutants caused C3H15 dephosphorylation in the presence of BIN2 and attenuated the bikinin-mediated induction of SAUR15 expression. Considering that BIN2 is apparently localized in the cytoplasm ( Yin et al., 2002 ) and BR induced the dephosphorylation of C3H15 and subsequent translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus ( Supplemental Figure S5 ), it is speculated that BIN2-mediated phosphorylation of C3H15 might take place in the cytoplasm, as seen with BZR1 and BES1 ( Gampala et al., 2007 ; Ryu et al., 2010 ). Therefore, C3H15 is another regulator mediating signaling from BIN2.

C3H15 antagonizes BZR1 or BES1 during transcription to repress cell elongation

BZR1 and BES1 are important transcription factors that activate BR signaling and promote cell elongation ( Sun et al., 2010 ; Yu et al., 2011 ). Here, we have shown that C3H15 inhibits dominant BZR1 and BES1 signaling to regulate hypocotyl elongation. C3H15 was a negative target of BZR1 and BES1, and C3H15 overexpression inhibited the hypocotyl elongation of bzr1-1D and bes1-D plants and weakened their PCZ insensitivity ( Figure 5 ). C3H15 suppressed the expression of BZR1 and BES1 and their BR-responsive target genes, including cell elongation-associated genes ( XTH6 , XTH9 , XTH33 , SAUR15 , and SAUR63 ) and BR biosynthetic genes ( DWF4 , CPD , BR6OX , ROT3 , and BEE1 ) ( Figure 6 ). We cannot exclude the possibility that C3H15 and BZR1/BES1 affect each other at the posttranslational level. Importantly, BZR1/BES1 attenuated C3H15-mediated inhibition of the expression of SAUR15 , their shared target gene ( Figure 7 ). Therefore, BR-induced cell elongation may require the coordinated action of BZR1/BES1- and C3H15-mediated signaling.

C3H15 and BZR1/BES1 function antagonistically in the regulation of cell elongation-associated genes, as is consistent with their overlapping BR-responsive patterns. The dephosphorylation of C3H15, BZR1, and BES1 was induced by BR at high (typically 1 µM) concentrations, and the C3H15 and BZR1 dephosphorylation was induced at low concentrations (10 nM) of BL ( Figure 4 ; Supplemental Figure S6 ). This result is in agreement with a previous physiological study showing that bzr1-1D , but not bes1-D , is hypersensitive to 10 nM BL ( He et al., 2005 ). In rice ( Oryza sativa ), the CCCH protein LEAF AND TILLER ANGLE INCREASED CONTROLLER (LIC) antagonizes BZR1 to prevent intense activation of the BR cascade only at high BR levels ( Zhang et al., 2012 ). The overlapping roles of C3H15 and LIC in BR pathways may result from the relatively distant (14% protein sequence identity, also see Wang et al., 2008 ) evolutionary relationship between C3H15 and AT1G75340, a putative ortholog of LIC in Arabidopsis, or intrinsic differences in the BR pathways between Arabidopsis and rice.

Our results support a model for C3H15 offsetting BZR1/BES1-dependent BR signaling to control cell elongation ( Figure 8 ). In the absence of BRs, C3H15, as well as BZR1 and BES1, is phosphorylated by the negative regulator BIN2 in the cytoplasm. Upon perception of BRs, C3H15 transcription is significantly enhanced, and the accumulation of dephosphorylated C3H15, BZR1, and BES1 proteins increased in the nucleus, where C3H15 antagonizes BZR1 and BES1 to regulate the expression of downstream cell elongation-associated genes. Interestingly, our western blot results revealed that C3H15 accumulation lagged behind BES1/BZR1 accumulation with 1 µM BL treatment ( Figure 4 ). It is possible that C3H15 is a part of feedback inhibition mechanisms that shut down BZR1/BES1 functions in BR signaling. BR induces the accumulation of C3H15 protein ( Figure 4A ), which inhibits BZR1/BES1 signaling through multiple mechanisms ( Figures 5–7 ). Our finding is similar to the regulatory mechanism of RD26- and TINY-mediated BR signaling pathways. RD26 and TINY function downstream of BIN2 and they interact with and antagonize BES1 in the regulation of plant growth and drought-responsive genes ( Ye et al., 2017 ; Jiang et al., 2019 ; Xie et al., 2019 ). Together, the interaction between C3H15 and BZR1 in the BR signaling pathway reported here presents a layer of control that modulates gene expression and finetunes BR-mediated cell elongation.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis ( A. thaliana ) ecotype Col-0 was used as the WT for genetic transformation and phenotype comparison. Plants were grown in a greenhouse under a 16-h light/8-h dark cycle at 20–23°C. To analyze the response of hypocotyl length to BR, seeds were plated on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium with or without the indicated concentrations of PCZ (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA; Hartwig et al., 2012 ) or 2,4-eBL (Sigma-Aldrich). After 2 d of incubation at 4°C to promote germination, seedlings were grown in darkness for 4 d or under dim light for 7 d.

Plasmid construction and plant transformation

The coding region of BES1 was ligated downstream of the 35S promoter in the modified pBI221-Myc vector, and the same was done for coding region of BZR1 in the modified pCambia1301-HA vector. C3H15 WT and C3H15 S111A were generated using a site-directed mutagenesis kit (Takara, Kyoto, Japan) and confirmed by sequencing. All resulting constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, and then transformed into WT plants using the floral-dip procedure. C3H15 was overexpressed in bri1-6 ( Noguchi et al., 1999 ), bzr1-1D ( Wang et al., 2002 ), or bes1-D ( Yin et al., 2002 ) to generate C3H15OE/bri1-6 , C3H15OE/bzr1-1D , and C3H15OE/bes1-D plants. The c3h14c3h15bri1-5 and c3h14c3h15/bin2-1 (+/−) plants were obtained by crossing c3h14c3h15 ( Chai et al., 2015 ) and bri1-5 ( Noguchi et al., 1999 ) or bin2-1 (+/−) plants. Homozygous plants were used to examine phenotypes.

Expression analysis

For RT-qPCR analysis, total RNA isolation and first-strand cDNA synthesis were performed as described previously ( Chai et al., 2015 ). qPCR assays were conducted on a LightCycler 480 Detection System (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) with TransStart Green qPCR superMix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). ACTIN2 was used as an internal control, and data represent the average of 10 samples from three biological replicates. Primers of the detected genes are listed in Supplemental Table S4 .

A promoter fragment 1.5-kb upstream of the ATG codon of C3H15 was cloned into the pKGWFS7 vector (Invitrogen) to create the GUS reporter construct. T 2 transgenic plants were visualized by staining for GUS activity.

Protein interaction analysis

Y2H and pull-down assays were conducted to test the interaction of C3H15 and BIN2 in vitro. For Y2H assays, the coding regions of C3H15 and BIN2 were fused into the GAL4 binding domain (BD) vector pGBKT7 and the GAL4 activation domain (AD) vector pGADT7 (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA), respectively. Yeast ( Saccharomyces cerevisiae ) strain AH109 was cotransformed with these two constructs and grown on the selective medium SD-LTHA (L, Leu; T, Trp; H, His; A, adenine)+ 0 or 5-mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT) to detect its growth status. For pull-down assays, the BIN2 coding region was cloned into the pGEX-4T-1 vector to generate the expression construct. GST-BIN2 or GST protein purified with glutathione sepharose 4B (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) was immobilized with glutathione sepharose beads (GE Healthcare) and then incubated with the purified MBP-C3H15 protein. Subsequently, the bead-retained proteins were eluted, analyzed by 12% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE), and then visualized by western blot analysis or autoradiography.

To determine the C3H15-BIN2 interaction in vivo, Co-IP assays were performed as described ( Kim et al., 2006 ) with some modifications. Nicotiana benthamiana leaves coexpressing Myc-C3H15 / Myc-GFP (control) and BIN2-HA were ground in immunoprecipitation buffer and centrifuged at 20,000 g for 20 min. The supernatant (1.5 mL) was incubated with anti-HA coupled to protein G Sepharose beads overnight under gentle rotation. Beads were washed four times with wash buffer, and the proteins were eluted by boiling in 2 × SDS loading buffer for 5 min. Samples were analyzed by SDS–PAGE and immunoblotted with anti-HA (Roche; Cat: 11666606001) and anti-Myc (Roche; Cat: 11667149001) antibodies.

Transient gene expression assays

Arabidopsis transient expression assays were performed following the procedure described previously ( Chai et al., 2014 ). The BZR1 , BES1 , or C3H15 coding region was ligated between the 35S promoter and the NOS terminator after removing GUS from the pBI221 vector to create the effector construct. A 1,188-bp C3H15 promoter was ligated upstream of the GUS reporter after removing the 35S promoter in pBI221 to create the proC3H15:GUS reporter construct. To determine how C3H15 affects BZR1- or BES1-driven activation of SAUR15 expression, proSAUR15:GUS reporter genes were constructed. The reporter and effectors with different combinations of constructs as well as the 35S:LUC vector (internal control) were cotransfected into Arabidopsis leaf protoplasts. Samples were incubated overnight in darkness, and then LUC luminescence and MU fluorescence (4-methylumbelliferone, products of GUS activity) were measured by a luminometer (Promega). The expression levels of the reporter genes were determined as the relative ratio of GUS activity to luciferase activity. Data are the means of nine independent samples from three biological replications.

EMSA

EMSA was performed as described ( Chai et al., 2014 ). The C3H15 , BZR1 , or BES1 coding region was fused in frame with MBP in the pMAL-c2x vector. Recombinant proteins were expressed and purified from Escherichia coli using amylose resin (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). Biotin-labeled synthetic oligonucleotides (Beijing Genomics Institution, Supplemental Table S4 ) were annealed with unlabeled oligonucleotides and then used as probes. A DNA–protein binding reaction was conducted with a LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Pierce, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The protein-bound DNA fragments were separated from the unbound fragments by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The DNA was electroblotted onto a nitrocellulose membrane and detected by chemiluminescence. To test whether BZR1 or BES1 is involved in the interference of the CHRE binding activity by C3H15, a similar set of EMSAs was performed with MBP-BZR1 or MBP-BES1 included ( Figure 7D ). The experiments were performed three times independently.

ChIP-qPCR

ChIP-qPCR assays were conducted on 2-week-old WT (control), 35S:Myc-C3H15 ( Wang et al., 2020 ), pBZR1:mBZR1-CFP ( Wang et al., 2002 ) and 35S:Myc-BES1 seedlings to validate that C3H15 and BZR1/BES1 target each other. Leaf protoplasts were isolated from 35S:Myc-C3H15 plants and transformed with the EV (control), 35S:BZR1 or 35S:BES1 for ChIP-qPCR analysis to examine whether coexpression of BZR1 or BES1 affects the binding of C3H15 to the SAUR15 promoter. Immunoprecipitation was performed with the anti-Myc (Roche; Cat: 11667149001) or anti-GFP (Clontech; Cat: 632381) antibody, and ChIP products were analyzed by qPCR with the appropriate primers ( Supplemental Table S4 ) following the instructions in the EZ CHIP Kit (Millipore). Enrichment was calculated as the ratio of transgenic to WT samples. For each ChIP experiment, nine independent samples from three biological replications were used and standard deviations were calculated.

Nucleus and cytoplasm fraction isolation

Cytoplasmic and nuclear protein fractions were extracted from 2-week-old 35S:Myc-C3H15 seedlings with or without 1 µM 2,4-eBL (Sigma-Aldrich) following the method described previously ( Ryu et al., 2007 ). Briefly, samples were lysed in a buffer (20-mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.0, 250-mM sucrose, 20-mM KCl, 2.5-mM MgCl 2 , 30-mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1/1,000 protease inhibitor cocktail (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), 2-mM EDTA, 0.7% (w/v) Triton X-100, and 25% (v/v) glycerol) and fractionated by centrifugation at 3,000 g . The supernatant was taken as the cytosolic fraction. The pellet was further washed with a resuspension buffer (20-mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.0, 30-mM β-mercaptoethanol, 2.5-mM MgCl 2 , and 25% (v/v) glycerol) and reconstituted as the nuclear fraction. All proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, and probed with an antibody against the Myc tag (Roche; Cat: 11667149001).

Confocal microscopy and quantification of protein fluorescent signals

The C3H15 coding region was fused with GFP in the pK7WGF2 vector, and the resulting construct was infiltrated into tobacco ( N. benthamiana ) leaves as described previously ( Sparkes et al., 2006 ). For the analysis of BR induction, the transfected leaves were incubated with 1 µM 2,4-eBL for 1 h. The leaves transformed with GFP-C3H15 were visualized under a Leica TCS SP5 II confocal microscope with an objective lens (HC PL APO 20 × 0.7 IMM). GFP was excited with the argon laser line at 488 nm and the emission detected at 500–550 nm. Images of GFP-C3H15 with or without BR treatment were acquired with identical settings (smart gain value: 720 V; smart offset value: −0.3%). Quantification of the fluorescent protein signal was conducted using ImageJ ( http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij ). To determine the ratio of nuclear to cytoplasmic signals (N/C ratio) for GFP-C3H15 in each cell, integrated densities were measured from representative areas within the nucleus, cytoplasm, and background of each cell. The average N/C ratio from 120 cells in three independent experiments was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance among samples was determined by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparison post-hoc test at P < 0.01. Data were expressed as means ± standard deviation of the mean. Significant differences are indicated by different lowercase letters.
