Bacterial biofilms are resilient multicellular communities that underlie
persistent infections and environmental survival. Dispersal from biofilms is a
pivotal event for transmission and pathogenesis, yet the host signals and
bacterial mechanisms orchestrating this transition remain poorly understood.
Here, we show that nitric oxide (NO), a ubiquitous host-derived signaling
molecule, acts as a rapid trigger for biofilm dispersal in Vibrio cholerae, a
highly motile gram-negative bacterium and the etiologic agent of cholera, by
promoting the development of motility. NO exposure induces broad upregulation of
flagellar biosynthesis genes, increases flagellin production, and reduces
intracellular cyclic-di-GMP levels, thereby priming aflagellated
biofilm-associated cells for active swimming and dispersion. Using single-cell
imaging in custom microfluidic devices, we directly visualize NO-stimulated
biofilm detachment and development of robust swimming motility within minutes.
In vivo, biofilm-derived V. cholerae colonize more efficiently in NO-rich
environments, and NO produced by epithelial cells enhances bacterial detachment
from epithelial surfaces. Our findings reveal a host-pathogen interface in which
NO serves as a morphogenetic cue, orchestrating the rapid transition from
sessility to motility.

## Full Text

The ability of bacteria to reversibly attach to and detach from surfaces is a fundamental physiological strategy that supports microbial survival, adaptation, and ecological success. Surface association enables formation of biofilms—multicellular communities encased in a self-produced matrix—that protect cells from environmental stresses, enhance resource access, and promote genetic exchange (1). In pathogens, surface attachment often occurs at the onset of host colonization and infection, while timely detachment enables dissemination to new niches or escape from hostile environments (2, 3). This dynamic balance between attachment and detachment is tightly regulated, allowing bacteria to optimize their fitness in response to fluctuating environmental and host-derived cues (4). Central to this regulation is the second messenger c-di-GMP, which integrates diverse signals to control transitions between sessile and motile states (5, 6). High intracellular c-di-GMP levels promote the production of biofilm matrix components and suppress motility, whereas reduced c-di-GMP concentrations favor motility and biofilm dispersal (7).

Among the diverse environmental and host-derived signals influencing biofilm dynamics, nitric oxide (NO) is a conserved and potent modulator of biofilm dynamics across multiple bacterial species (8). As a key signaling and immune defense molecule, NO exerts selective pressure on bacteria, which have evolved diverse sensory pathways to detect and respond to it (9, 10). Diverse sensory pathways exist across bacterial species, and certain bacteria respond differently to NO stress. NO can induce widespread transcriptomic changes associated with nitrosative and oxidative stress. However, bacterial responses vary: NO promotes biofilm formation in Shewanella oneidensis, dispersal in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and inhibits colonization-associated biofilms in Vibrio fischeri (8, 11, 12). However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which NO regulates biofilm dynamics and its physiological consequences during host colonization remain incompletely understood, particularly in the context of major human pathogens such as Vibrio cholerae.

V cholerae, the causative agent of cholera, alternates between aquatic reservoirs and the human gastrointestinal tract, relying on biofilms for environmental persistence and transmission (13, 14). Biofilm-derived V. cholerae cells exhibit enhanced infectivity and colonization efficiency, and effective dispersal from biofilms is also crucial for pathogenesis (15–17). While the complex regulatory networks governing V. cholerae biofilm development have been extensively studied [see the comprehensive recent review (18)], how NO influences this process remains unclear. During infection, V. cholerae encounters NO in the human intestine, where NO levels increase due to elevated inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) activity (19). Several NO-sensing pathways—including NorR, NosP, H-NOX, and the virulence regulator AphB—have been implicated in regulating quorum sensing and virulence in response to NO (11, 20–22), yet the mechanisms by which NO modulates V. cholerae biofilms are not known.

In this study, we investigate how NO modulates V. cholerae biofilm dispersal and contributes to host colonization. We show that NO induces the expression of flagellar biosynthesis genes, promoting dispersal from biofilm structures. Motility is a key virulence factor in many pathogens, including V. cholerae (14, 23), which shows reduced colonization ability when certain motility components are deleted. Motility is inversely regulated by c-di-GMP (24). c-di-GMP directly binds to the flagellar master regulator FlrA as a repressor, blocking class II flagellar gene promoters (25), and therefore stopping transcription of classes III and IV of the flagellar transcriptional hierarchy as well. Our results demonstrate that NO reduces intracellular c-di-GMP levels, likely facilitating increased flagellar production. In vivo, we find that biofilm-derived V. cholerae colonizes more efficiently when host-produced NO is present and that NO promotes bacterial detachment from epithelial surfaces. Together, these findings provide insight into the interplay between host signals and bacterial regulatory networks that govern biofilm dispersal and pathogenesis in V. cholerae.

Next, we analyzed RNA-seq data for changes in expression of 53 genes encoding proteins with GGDEF, EAL, or HD-GYP domains—key regulators of intracellular c-di-GMP levels (38, 44, 45) (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Of these, 40 were differentially expressed in response to NO, with 15 showing changes greater than twofold. Among the 29 GGDEF-domain-encoding genes, 6 were upregulated more than twofold (VC1029, VC1370, VC0217, VCA0848, VCA0939, and VCA0965) and 3 were downregulated (VC1367, VC1593, and VCA0697). VCA0697 encodes CdgD, a diguanylate cyclase that serves as a negative regulator of motility (46); repression of this gene’s expression may contribute to the stimulation of flagellar biosynthesis and motility by NO. Of the 14 phosphodiesterase-domain-encoding genes, 8 were upregulated (VC1086, VC1211, VC1641, VC1710, VC1851, VC2340, VCA0210, and VCA0681) and 2 were downregulated (VCA0101 and VCA0536). Only one of the 10 genes encoding dual-domain proteins was upregulated >2-fold (VC0658). Included among the genes with altered expression is VC0658, encoding the enzyme CdgI, which has been shown to be involved in dispersal (29) and was upregulated in response to NO. In addition, the aforementioned hnoB (VS1086) was upregulated, even though we determined that HnoXK does not mediate NO-induced motility and dispersal. This potentially indicates the presence of alternative pathways involving HnoB. In addition to these changes in transcription, certain DGCs and PDEs may be repressed or activated posttranslationally. Overall, these results suggest that NO triggers a coordinated transcriptional response involving multiple diguanylate cyclases and phosphodiesterases, likely contributing to the observed reduction in c-di-GMP levels.

We next investigated whether NO-mediated reduction of c-di-GMP contributes to flagellum-dependent biofilm dispersal. Specifically, we asked whether disrupting the effects c-di-GMP on motility would abrogate NO-induced motility activation and dispersal. To this end, we constructed a point mutation in the flagellar master regulator FlrA, FlrAR176H, which has been shown to render motility insensitive to changes in c-di-GMP level (25). Consistent with this regulatory role, our RNAseq analysis (Fig. 2A) showed that flrA was one of the few flagellar genes not induced by NO, suggesting posttranscriptional control. When we compared NO-induced biofilm dispersal between the wild-type and FlrAR176H mutant strains, we found that the dispersal response was markedly attenuated in the mutant (Fig. 4C), indicating that NO-regulated c-di-GMP reduction plays an important role in promoting flagellar production and subsequent dispersal. However, a measurable reduction in biofilm biomass (ΔA595>0) was still observed in the mutant following NO treatment, suggesting that additional regulatory pathways also contribute to NO-induced dispersal.

DiscussionThe dynamic interplay between V. cholerae and its host environment is defined by the bacterium’s remarkable ability to switch between sessile attachment and motile planktonic lifestyles. Our study shows that NO, a host-derived signaling molecule, rapidly triggers biofilm dispersal by inducing a flagellar gene expression program and promoting de novo flagellar synthesis. It is also possible that NO may additionally enhance flagellar activity. In its aquatic reservoirs, V. cholerae often resides on chitinous surfaces and is more infectious in this biofilm-associated state due to enhanced acid resistance and transcriptional reprogramming (15, 17, 47, 50). These traits support the idea that biofilms represent a natural infection route. Upon entry into the small intestine, however, effective dispersal from biofilms and subsequent epithelial attachment are critical for colonization; failure to do so reduces infectivity (17). As infection progresses and host environments change, epithelial-associated bacteria likely undergo further transcriptional shifts to facilitate exit from the host and prepare for environmental survival (51, 52). We propose a dual role for NO: It triggers dispersal from environmental biofilms during host entry and promotes detachment from epithelial surfaces during later stages of infection (Fig. 5F). Of note, the 50 µM NO donor used here does not inhibit wild-type V. cholerae growth (21). Since NO was added postbiofilm with a short incubation, growth effects are minimal. These include induction of MSHA Type IV pilus retraction (54), quorum sensing, and detection of environmental polyamines (17, 55, 56). In the host intestine, bile salts such as taurocholate, which serve as key signals to activate virulence genes (57), promote dispersal independent of protein synthesis (58). Each of these mechanisms targets the biofilm matrix or cell-surface adhesins, facilitating detachment by weakening the physical connections that anchor cells within the biofilm. Rather than primarily targeting the matrix, NO acts as a developmental cue that rapidly reprograms biofilm-associated cells toward motility. Within minutes of NO exposure, we observed a dramatic upregulation of nearly all flagellar and chemotaxis genes, a surge in flagellin synthesis, and the emergence of actively swimming cells from previously sessile biofilms. This transition is coupled to a drop in intracellular c-di-GMP (Fig. 4), consistent with the established role of this second messenger in repressing flagellar gene expression via FlrA. After incubation, 250 ml of each culture was harvested by centrifugation. The remaining 500 ml of culture was split into two 250 ml cultures, one set of which received 50 µM DEA NONOate. Cultures were grown at 37 °C for 30 additional minutes. OD600 of each culture was measured, and cultures were normalized. Harvesting was repeated. Cells were resuspended in 10 ml extraction solvent (40% acetonitrile, 40% methanol, 20% H2O) and incubated at -20 °C overnight. Samples were pelleted, and supernatants were dried down by SpeedVac and lyophilization. Dried samples were resuspended in H2O and assayed using a fluorescence-based c-di-GMP assay kit (Lucerna Technologies) following kit instructions.
