1-Aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate synthase (ACCS) catalyzes the conversion of
S-adenosyl-methionine to 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC), a
rate-limiting step in ethylene biosynthesis. A gene encoding a putative ACCS
protein was identified in the human genome two decades ago. It has been shown to
not exhibit any canonical ACC synthase activity and its true function remains
obscure. In this study, through a biochemical profiling approach, we demonstrate
that human ACCS possesses cysteine conjugate sulfoxide β-lyase activity. This
function is unexpected but reasonable, as it somewhat parallels the activity of
ACCS proteins found in non-seed plants. Structure-function relationship study of
human ACCS, guided by an AlphaFold2 model, allowed us to identify key active
site residues that are important for its β-lyase activity. Our biochemical study
of human ACCS also provided insights into the function of other mammalian ACCS
homologs.

Introduction

While tremendous progress has been made in understanding protein functions in well-studied model organisms [1, 2], at least 6% of human proteins remain unassigned with any molecular function [3, 4]. These proteins, often referred to as proteins of unknown functions (PUFs), represent a substantial gap in our knowledge of human biology [5]. The lack of functional annotation for these PUFs is concerning because they may play important yet-unrecognized roles in human health [6]. Uncovering the functions of these PUFs is therefore significant, not only for filling in the gaps in our understanding of human biochemistry, but also for advancing biomedical research. By identifying and characterizing the roles of these proteins, we can gain new insights into fundamental biological processes and identify novel biomarkers or drug targets. This could lead to the development of new diagnostics, treatments, and preventive strategies for a wide range of diseases [7].

Our group has launched a research program focused on deorphanizing human PUFs. In particular, we are interested in uncovering the previously unknown roles of pyridoxal 5’-phosphate (PLP)-dependent proteins. This family of enzymes is remarkable for catalyzing a diverse range of chemical transformations, accounting for at least 4% of all enzymatic activities [8]. PLP-dependent proteins play critical roles in metabolism and are involved in numerous essential cellular processes, including the biosynthesis of amino acid, lipids, and natural products [9, 10]. Due to their significant biological roles, disruptions in the function of PLP-dependent proteins have been linked to many diseases. For instance, mutations in serine palmitoyltransferase are associated with hereditary sensory neuropathy type 1 (HSAN1) and early onset amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [11]; while mutations in alanine-glyoxylate amino transferase lead to primary hyperoxaluria type I [12]. Additionally, PLP-dependent enzymes are common targets in drug development, and several inhibitors of these enzymes are currently used in clinical settings to treat various diseases. Examples include vigabatrin, a γ-aminobutyric acid transaminase inhibitor to treat epilepsy [13]; eflornithine, an ornithine decarboxylase inhibitor for treatment of African trypanosomiasis [14]; and D-cycloserine, an alanine racemase inhibitor for treating tuberculosis [15]. Therefore, identifying and characterizing the previously unknown functions of human PLP-dependent proteins could unlock new opportunities for therapeutic development.

Two decades ago, a human gene encoding a putative 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate synthase (ACCS) was cloned, and its protein product (Uniprot accession number Q96QU6) was biochemically characterized [16, 17]. Plant ACCS is known to convert S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM) to 5’-methylthioadenosine and 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC), which is the rate-limiting step in the biosynthesis of the phytohormone ethylene.[18] Despite having an amino acid sequence most similar to plant ACCS (e.g., < 32% sequence identity), human ACCS (hereafter referred to as hACCS) does not exhibit any ACC synthase activity. Instead, hACCS was found to possess weak vinylglycine deaminase activity in vitro [17]. However, since L-vinylglycine is not a physiologically relevant substrate, and the catalytic efficiency for this transformation is low, the true molecular function of hACCS remains unclear.

According to the Human Protein Atalas database (www.proteinatlas.org) [19], hACCS has ubiquitous cytoplasmic expression and hACCS proteins are most abundant in lymphoid tissues. Furthermore, a P421L mutation in hACCS was associated with lung cancer susceptibility, suggesting hACCS may play an important yet unknown physiological role [20]. Driven by the potentially important biological function of hACCS, and the unmet need to elucidate human proteins of unknown function, we embarked on a study to unravel the enigmatic function of hACCS. In this work, we report the discovery of cysteine-S-conjugate sulfoxide β-lyase activity of hACCS. Our structure-function relationship study of hACCS, enabled by an AlphaFold2 model, has also provided molecular insights into other ACCS homologues.

ResultsBioinformatics analysis of human ACCSTo gain insights into the biological function of hACCS, we began by analyzing the genomic neighborhood of the hACCS gene. Human ACCS gene is located on chromosome 11 and is implicated in the 11p11.2 copy number variation syndrome (see the human gene database www.genecards.org [21]). Interestingly, hACCS gene is located adjacent to another gene encoding a PLP-dependent protein (Figure 1a), annotated as the ACCS-like protein (ACCSL, Uniprot accession number Q4AC99) based on its high amino acid sequence identity (50%) with hACCS. While hACCS orthologues are widespread in vertebrates, ACCSL orthologues are only found in mammals (Figure 1b), with their genomic context being strictly conserved (Figure 1a). We propose that ACCSL may have evolved from mammalian ACCS through gene duplication.A detailed examination of the human ACCSL (hereafter referred to as hACCSL) protein sequence revealed that a key active site arginine residue is replaced by cysteine (Figure 2). This arginine typically forms a salt bridge with amino acid substrate’s α-carboxylate group, an interaction essential for substrate recognition.[10, 22] Interestingly, this arginine-to-cysteine substitution is unique to hACCSL, as the arginine residue is conserved in other non-human ACCSL orthologues (Figure 2). The absence of this catalytic arginine in hACCSL raises the question of whether human ACCSL is catalytically inactive.[23] Nevertheless, our bioinformatics analysis clearly indicates that animal ACCS homologues are more prevalent and complex than previously anticipated. Biochemical characterization of these animal homologues is therefore warranted to help us understand their functions.Discovery of cysteine-S-conjugate sulfoxide β-lyase activity of hACCSGiven that our bioinformatics analysis did not provide direct clue about the enzymatic function of hACCS, we decided to use a biochemical approach to identify the hidden enzymatic activity of hACCS. We designed a competition assay to screen amino acid ligands that can inhibit hACCS’s vinylglycine deaminase activity. We hypothesized that a substrate analogue sharing structural similarity with hACCS’s native substrate might outcompete vinylglycine for binding at the same active site pocket and, therefore, act as an inhibitor. To perform this in vitro competition assay, we first prepared the recombinant hACCS protein through heterologous expression in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and purified it homogeneity using Ni-affinity chromatography (Figure 3a). As-isolated hACCS is yellow and exhibited an absorbance peak at ~330 nm (Figure 3b). When hACCS was treated with NaOH, this peak shifted to ~390 nm, suggesting that the absorbance at ~330 nm exhibited by the native hACCS protein is due to the covalently bound PLP cofactor [24]. Furthermore, the absorption peak wavelength (~330 nm) also suggests that the hACCS-bound PLP cofactor rests predominantly in the enolimine state.We next confirmed its vinylglycine deaminase activity and determined the steady-state kinetics parameters (Figure 3c). With L-vinylglycine as the substrate, hACCS exhibited an overall turnover number (kcat) of 1.4 min−1 and a Michaelis constant Km of 3.6 mM. The catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km = 6.5 M−1s−1) measured here is about 6-fold less than the previously reported result (kcat/Km = 44 M−1s−1) [17]. It is not clear if this small disparity is due to kcat or Km since these parameters alone were not reported in the previous study. Nevertheless, with active hACCS protein in hand, we next set out to measure its vinylglycine deaminase activity in the presence of common proteinogenic amino acids. As shown in Figure 3d, L-cysteine clearly emerged as an inhibitor among all the proteinogenic amino acids tested. Although L-cysteine was not consumed by hACCS, the fact that it can outcompete vinylglycine for hACCS binding suggests that the native substrate of hACCS might be structurally similar to L-cysteine, such as cystine, cystathionine, and many other common cysteine derivatives. Compared to cysteine, these S-substituted cysteine conjugates have better leaving groups at the β-position, making them favorable for β-elimination/replacement reactions.Accordingly, we set out to screen for potential Cβ-S lyase activity of hACCS against a panel of cysteine S-conjugates (Figure 4), including S-alkyl/aryl-cysteines (1-5), selenocysteine derivatives (6, 7), S-alkyl/aryl-cysteine sulfoxides (8-10), and cysteine sulfenic acid (11). Since pyruvate is the universal product of Cβ-S lyase reactions, we evaluated hACCS activity by measuring the formation rate of pyruvate using an LC-MS based method. Interestingly, several cysteine S-conjugate substrates showed significant Cβ-S lyase activity, whereas no activity was observed with serine derivative 12, despite it being a common substrate for PLP-dependent β-elimination/substitution reactions. Overall, cysteine S-conjugate sulfoxides (8-10) are better substrates. For example, S-phenyl-L-cysteine sulfoxide 10 is 2.5-fold more active than its near-isosteric thioether analogue S-phenyl-L-cysteine 4 (Figure 4).To understand the reaction specificity of hACCS, we conducted steady-state kinetic analysis using 4 and 10 as representatives for cysteine-S-conjugate substrates and cysteine-S-conjugate sulfoxide substrates, respectively. Comparison between these two substrates would reveal the catalytic specificity between sulfoxide and non-sulfoxides, since these two substrates only differ by a sulfoxide functional group. The catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) for 4 and 10 are approximately the same (~700 M−1s−1), and both exceed that of the vinylglycine deamination reaction by two orders of magnitude (Figure 5). However, reaction with substrate 4 shows saturation kinetics with an apparent Km of 0.5 mM, whereas substrate 10 does not reach saturation up to 16 mM. The turnover number (kcat) for 10 (14 s−1) is 38-fold higher than 4 (0.4 s−1), consistent with the results shown above that sulfoxide substrates are more reactive than non-sulfoxide cysteine conjugate. Because the sulfoxide substrate 10 used in our study is a mixture of two stereoisomers, to investigate whether hACCS shows any diastereoselectivity between the two sulfoxide diastereomers, we then conducted steady-state kinetic analysis with L-(+)-alliin, a single stereoisomer and naturally existing cysteine sulfoxide conjugate,[25] and compared the parameters with the diastereomeric mixtures L-(±)-alliin (9). As shown in Figure 5, although the reaction was barely saturated with 9, its catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) is about 4-fold higher than that of L-(+)-alliin. These results indicate that hACCS is active with both alliin diastereomers and slightly prefers the non-natural L-(−)-alliin. This substrate preference is similar to some bacterial alliinases reported recently [26].We also explored the potential γ-elimination activity of hACCS by testing related γ-sulfoxide substrate analogues (e.g. methionine sulfoxide) and other common γ-lyase substrates (e.g. O-acetyl-L-homoserine and SAM). No γ-lyase product (i.e. α-ketobutyrate) was detected, indicating that hACCS is specific to β-elimination reaction. Since β-elimination is also a prerequisite step for β-replacement reaction, we also investigated potential β-replacement activity by including various nucleophilic co-substrates in hACCS-catalyzed reactions (e.g. sodium sulfide, phenol, etc.). However, we were unable to detect any corresponding β-substituted amino acid products.Collectively, our results above suggest that hACCS can function as a cysteine-conjugate sulfoxide β-lyase, although the catalytic activity of hACCS is 10-fold lower than the activity of well-studied garlic alliinase (e.g. kcat = 193 s−1 with L-(+)-alliin) [27]. We propose that either the substrates tested here do not well resemble the physiological substrates of hACCS, or the activity of hACCS needs to be further activated by a yet-unknown mechanism. Nevertheless, this cysteine-conjugate sulfoxide β-lyase activity discovered above is previously unknown for hACCS, and this new insight will facilitate future study focusing on unveiling the biological function of hACCS.Structural-function relationship study of hACCS guided by an AlphaFold2 modelTo understand the factors that determine hACCS’s catalytic specificity, we generated a homology model of hACCS homodimer by using AlphaFold2 [28], and docked the representative cysteine-S-conjugate substrate 4 in the PLP-bound external aldimine form into the active site of hACCS using Autodock Vina (Figure 6a) [29]. We also docked substrate 10 (i.e. both diastereomers) to understand the diastereoselectivity for the sulfoxide stereoisomers (Figure 6b, c). The overall fold of hACCS is highly similar to plant ACCS (e.g. PDB entry 1MY7) with an r.m.s.d. of 1.4 Å for 322 Cα atoms [30]. The docked PLP cofactor makes canonical interactions with key active site residues of hACCS, including salt bridges with D286 and R331, hydrogen bonds with N258 and Y132(B), and a π-π stacking interaction with Y195. The PLP-bound substrate 4 is oriented by R457, while its side chain extends into a side pocket primarily defined by residues from the other monomer within the homodimer, including W135(B), Y132(B), and R351(B). Similar interactions were also observed for the phenyl group in substrate 10, but no direct interaction was observed between the sulfoxide oxygens and hACCS in either diastereomer. We hypothesize that the sulfoxide oxygen could potentially interact with H201 or Y195 through a water-mediated hydrogen bond if the sulfoxide of 10 adopts the S-configuration (Figure 6c), and such an interaction is precluded by the opposite orientation of R-configured sulfoxide (Figure 6b). This proposal is supported by the observation that hACCS slightly prefers L-(–)-alliin, whose relative configuration is the same as S-configured sulfoxide shown in Figure 6c. We also docked SAM to understand the loss of ACCS activity (Figure S2). Apparent steric clash was observed between the side chain of SAM and residue Y132(B), which would suggest that binding of SAM is impeded in hACCS due to steric hinderance. Therefore, it would be very challenging for hACCS to catalyze the cyclization reaction of SAM if it could not reach the productive conformation.To further validate our docking model, we performed a mutagenesis study targeting the implicated sites and assayed the activity of each mutant using substrates 4 and 10 (Figure 6d). We first mutated the highly conserved PLP-binding lysine residue K323. As expected, the K323A mutant completely abolished enzyme activity, confirming the importance of K323 in catalysis and also demonstrating the observed cysteine S-conjugate sulfoxide β-lyase activity is specific to hACCS. Next, we targeted residues implicated in substrate recognition. Alanine substitutions at residue W135(B) and residue R351(B) resulted in an over 10-fold loss of activity, highlighting the importance of both residues in catalysis. Notably, both W135 and R351 are highly conserved in mammalian ACCS orthologues (Figure 7). Furthermore, the catalytic function of R351 appears to be associated with its positive charge, as replacing R351 with other two cationic residues (i.e. His and Lys) successfully rescued enzyme activity. In contrast, the function of W135 could not be simply mimicked by another aromatic residue; both W135Y and W135A mutants exhibited similarly compromised activity, approximately 5.5-fold lower than that of the wild-type enzyme. We also substituted Y195 with its isosteric counterpart, phenylalanine, and observed that the resulting Y195F mutant exhibited a roughly 3.5-fold decrease in activity. This result suggests that while Y195 is important for catalysis, it is not essential. This finding is in line with previous observations in other PLP-dependent Cβ-S lyase; for instance, the Y119F mutant in Streptococcus anginosus Cβ-S lyase retains 16% of activity compared to the wild-type enzyme.[31] However, it contrast sharply with plant ACCS, where the equivalent tyrosine-to-phenylalanine mutation retains only 1.7% of its ACC synthase activity.[32] We hence hypothesize that the role of Y195 in hACCS is similar to that in Cβ-S lyases, rather than plant ACCS.Taken together, we propose a plausible catalytic mechanism for hACCS (Figure 6e). The essential K323 may act as a general base to initiate the α, β-elimination. Meanwhile, residues R351 and W135 may be responsible for recognizing the side-chain of cysteine sulfoxide conjugates. Residue Y195 may play as a general acid to stabilize the leaving group, potentially by either forming a water-mediated hydrogen bond network or directly interacting with the leaving group. The latter interaction would, however, require Y195 to adopt a rotamer conformation different from the current model predicted by AlphaFold2. To fully understand the catalytic role of Y195, a co-crystal structure of hACCS in complex with a substrate analogue would be required.Molecular insights for hACCSL functionBuilding on our enhanced understanding of the structure-function relationship of hACCS, we shifted our focus to human ACCSL to test the hypothesis whether it is a pseudoenzyme due to the arginine-to-cysteine substitution as indicated in Figure 1. We first prepared a R457C mutant of hACCS and assayed its activity. This mutant was designed to replicate the natural cysteine substitution found in hACCSL, and was shown to decrease hACCS activity by a 100-fold (Figure 6d). This finding confirmed the catalytic importance of this highly conserved arginine for hACCS.To gain further insights, we generated an AlphaFold2 model of hACCSL. As depicted in Figure 8, the overall active site structure of hACCSL is very similar to hACCS with only three residues differing: C529, Y423, and S273. Since Y423 in hACCSL corresponds to R351 in hACCS, a residue already shown to be important for catalysis in hACCS, we hypothesized that the arginine-to-tyrosine substitution may also affects the activity of hACCSL. Indeed, substituting R351 with tyrosine in hACCS led to a significant (~40-fold) decrease in activity (Figure 6d). In light of these two “loss-of-function” substitutions (i.e. C529 and Y423) naturally found in hACCSL, we hypothesize that hACCSL is either a pseudoenzyme as predicted, or it possess a totally different function and recognizes substrates different from what we used in this study, a phenomenon known for some psuedokinases.[33] Direct biochemical characterization of hACCSL in vitro would answer this question in the future;

Discovery of cysteine-S-conjugate sulfoxide β-lyase activity of hACCS

Given that our bioinformatics analysis did not provide direct clue about the enzymatic function of hACCS, we decided to use a biochemical approach to identify the hidden enzymatic activity of hACCS. We designed a competition assay to screen amino acid ligands that can inhibit hACCS’s vinylglycine deaminase activity. We hypothesized that a substrate analogue sharing structural similarity with hACCS’s native substrate might outcompete vinylglycine for binding at the same active site pocket and, therefore, act as an inhibitor. To perform this in vitro competition assay, we first prepared the recombinant hACCS protein through heterologous expression in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and purified it homogeneity using Ni-affinity chromatography (Figure 3a). As-isolated hACCS is yellow and exhibited an absorbance peak at ~330 nm (Figure 3b). When hACCS was treated with NaOH, this peak shifted to ~390 nm, suggesting that the absorbance at ~330 nm exhibited by the native hACCS protein is due to the covalently bound PLP cofactor [24]. Furthermore, the absorption peak wavelength (~330 nm) also suggests that the hACCS-bound PLP cofactor rests predominantly in the enolimine state.

We next confirmed its vinylglycine deaminase activity and determined the steady-state kinetics parameters (Figure 3c). With L-vinylglycine as the substrate, hACCS exhibited an overall turnover number (kcat) of 1.4 min−1 and a Michaelis constant Km of 3.6 mM. The catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km = 6.5 M−1s−1) measured here is about 6-fold less than the previously reported result (kcat/Km = 44 M−1s−1) [17]. It is not clear if this small disparity is due to kcat or Km since these parameters alone were not reported in the previous study. Nevertheless, with active hACCS protein in hand, we next set out to measure its vinylglycine deaminase activity in the presence of common proteinogenic amino acids. As shown in Figure 3d, L-cysteine clearly emerged as an inhibitor among all the proteinogenic amino acids tested. Although L-cysteine was not consumed by hACCS, the fact that it can outcompete vinylglycine for hACCS binding suggests that the native substrate of hACCS might be structurally similar to L-cysteine, such as cystine, cystathionine, and many other common cysteine derivatives. Compared to cysteine, these S-substituted cysteine conjugates have better leaving groups at the β-position, making them favorable for β-elimination/replacement reactions.

Accordingly, we set out to screen for potential Cβ-S lyase activity of hACCS against a panel of cysteine S-conjugates (Figure 4), including S-alkyl/aryl-cysteines (1-5), selenocysteine derivatives (6, 7), S-alkyl/aryl-cysteine sulfoxides (8-10), and cysteine sulfenic acid (11). Since pyruvate is the universal product of Cβ-S lyase reactions, we evaluated hACCS activity by measuring the formation rate of pyruvate using an LC-MS based method. Interestingly, several cysteine S-conjugate substrates showed significant Cβ-S lyase activity, whereas no activity was observed with serine derivative 12, despite it being a common substrate for PLP-dependent β-elimination/substitution reactions. Overall, cysteine S-conjugate sulfoxides (8-10) are better substrates. For example, S-phenyl-L-cysteine sulfoxide 10 is 2.5-fold more active than its near-isosteric thioether analogue S-phenyl-L-cysteine 4 (Figure 4).

To understand the reaction specificity of hACCS, we conducted steady-state kinetic analysis using 4 and 10 as representatives for cysteine-S-conjugate substrates and cysteine-S-conjugate sulfoxide substrates, respectively. Comparison between these two substrates would reveal the catalytic specificity between sulfoxide and non-sulfoxides, since these two substrates only differ by a sulfoxide functional group. The catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) for 4 and 10 are approximately the same (~700 M−1s−1), and both exceed that of the vinylglycine deamination reaction by two orders of magnitude (Figure 5). However, reaction with substrate 4 shows saturation kinetics with an apparent Km of 0.5 mM, whereas substrate 10 does not reach saturation up to 16 mM. The turnover number (kcat) for 10 (14 s−1) is 38-fold higher than 4 (0.4 s−1), consistent with the results shown above that sulfoxide substrates are more reactive than non-sulfoxide cysteine conjugate. Because the sulfoxide substrate 10 used in our study is a mixture of two stereoisomers, to investigate whether hACCS shows any diastereoselectivity between the two sulfoxide diastereomers, we then conducted steady-state kinetic analysis with L-(+)-alliin, a single stereoisomer and naturally existing cysteine sulfoxide conjugate,[25] and compared the parameters with the diastereomeric mixtures L-(±)-alliin (9). As shown in Figure 5, although the reaction was barely saturated with 9, its catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) is about 4-fold higher than that of L-(+)-alliin. These results indicate that hACCS is active with both alliin diastereomers and slightly prefers the non-natural L-(−)-alliin. This substrate preference is similar to some bacterial alliinases reported recently [26].

We also explored the potential γ-elimination activity of hACCS by testing related γ-sulfoxide substrate analogues (e.g. methionine sulfoxide) and other common γ-lyase substrates (e.g. O-acetyl-L-homoserine and SAM). No γ-lyase product (i.e. α-ketobutyrate) was detected, indicating that hACCS is specific to β-elimination reaction. Since β-elimination is also a prerequisite step for β-replacement reaction, we also investigated potential β-replacement activity by including various nucleophilic co-substrates in hACCS-catalyzed reactions (e.g. sodium sulfide, phenol, etc.). However, we were unable to detect any corresponding β-substituted amino acid products.

Collectively, our results above suggest that hACCS can function as a cysteine-conjugate sulfoxide β-lyase, although the catalytic activity of hACCS is 10-fold lower than the activity of well-studied garlic alliinase (e.g. kcat = 193 s−1 with L-(+)-alliin) [27]. We propose that either the substrates tested here do not well resemble the physiological substrates of hACCS, or the activity of hACCS needs to be further activated by a yet-unknown mechanism. Nevertheless, this cysteine-conjugate sulfoxide β-lyase activity discovered above is previously unknown for hACCS, and this new insight will facilitate future study focusing on unveiling the biological function of hACCS.

Cysteine S-conjugates, derived from the degradation of glutathione S-conjugates, are recognized as playing a crucial role in the detoxication of xenobiotics.[34] Typically, cysteine S-conjugates are excreted, but they can also be further broken down by PLP-dependent β-lyases, or by flavin-dependent monooxygenases (FMO) to produce cysteine S-conjugate sulfoxides.[35] A number of PLP-dependent β-lyases have thus far been identified with cysteine S-conjugates β-lyase activity, but all these enzymes also possess other functions, such as kynureninase and kynureinine aminotransferase.[35] As a result, cysteine conjugate β-lyase activity has been considered a moonlighting function for these PLP dependent enzymes.[36] Furthermore, although cysteine S-conjugate sulfoxides are known to be produced by FMO,[34] the metabolic fate of these sulfoxide amino acids is well understood, and mammalian β-lyases specific to cysteine S-conjugate sulfoxides have not been identified.

In this study, we showed that hACCS does exhibit Cβ-S lyase activity. Although the catalytic efficiency determined from our assay is still low (<1,000 M−1s−1) as compared to some other metabolic enzymes in human, it is still the highest activity reported for hACCS so far. We reason that the modest activity of hACCS is because the cysteine S-conjugate (sulfoxides) substrates we used are likely not the natural substrates for hACCS. Future systematic study of different cysteine S-conjugates bearing different substitution groups is necessary to identify the preferred substrate.

Nevertheless, our results demonstrate that hACCS is specific to cysteine S-aryl/alkyl sulfoxides and exhibits lower activity on cysteine S-aryl conjugates. This substrate preference aligns with the substrate specificity reported nearly four decades ago for a human hepatic cysteine-conjugate β-lyase, whose molecular identity remains unknown.[37] We speculate that hACCS may be the elusive hepatic cysteine-conjugate β-lyase. Indeed, hACCS is expressed in liver and has wide tissue distribution.[38] Our discovery that hACCS preferentially catalyze the Cβ-S lyase reaction on S-alkyl/aryl cysteine sulfoxides suggests that these sulfoxide amino acids can undergo enzymatic β-elimination to produce pyruvate, ammonia, and a sulfenic acid-containing molecule. Although understanding the biological function of hACCS still awaits in vivo study, our discovery of hACCS’s cysteine-conjugate sulfoxide Cβ-S lyase activity in vitro suggests hACCS is the potential missing link in the glutathione-based xenobiotic metabolic pathways.

Although the cysteine S-conjugate sulfoxide lyase activity discovered in hACCS is reminiscent of alliinase activity, hACCS is distant from alliinase in terms of either sequence (<20% sequence identity between hACCS and garlic alliinase) or structure (e.g. garlic alliinase is known to harbor an additional epidermal growth factor-like domain [39]). In fact, the discovered Cβ-S lyase activity in hACCS is unexpected but parallels with some non-seed plant ACCS proteins, which have also been shown to lack the canonical ACC synthase activity and possess only Cβ-S lyase activity.[40] Consequently, plant ACCS proteins have been classified into two types: the genuine ACCS type, which catalyzes ACC biosynthesis, and the non-seed plant type, which catalyze β-elimination reactions. Although phylogenetic analysis revealed that the plant and animal ACCS proteins diverged from their last common ancestor early in evolution, mammalian ACCS proteins appear to have functionally converged with non-seed plant ACCS proteins (Figure 1b). Because both human ACCS and non-seed plant ACCS proteins are demonstrated to be β-lyases instead of ACC synthases, we suggest these proteins should be renamed as β-lyases in order to accurately reflect their functions.

In summary, by using a biochemical profiling strategy, we discovered that hACCS exhibits previously unknown S-alkyl/aryl sulfoxide β-lyase activity, which is reminiscent of the ACCS proteins found in non-seed plants, emphasizing that the Cβ-S lyase activity and ACC-synthase activity are evolutionarily related. Our discovery of hACCS’s β-lyase activity in vitro also suggests that hACCS is the potential missing link in glutathione-based xenobiotic metabolism.
