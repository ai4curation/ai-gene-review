Gram-negative bacteria have an elaborate envelope that is composed of an outer
membrane, the cell wall, and the inner membrane, which collectively encase the
cytoplasm. The outer membrane is covalently anchored to the peptidoglycan, the
major constituent of the cell wall. We document that the gene product of PA2854
is the catalyst that performs this transformation between the cell wall and the
outer-membrane lipoprotein OprI in live Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Furthermore, we
reconstitute this reaction in vitro with the use of purified recombinant PA2854,
OprI, and synthetic samples of the cell-wall peptidoglycan, in each case
documenting the attachment of the side-chain ε-amino group of Lys83 of OprI to
the peptide stem of the peptidoglycan. OprI forms a trimeric structure. The
X-ray structure of the trimeric state of OprI was solved to 2.1 Å resolution,
which reveals it as an extended 82 Å helix bundle. The X-ray structure of PA2854
was also solved at 2.63 Å resolution. The enzyme is composed of three domains,
which were documented to bind to the synthetic peptidoglycan and to OprI. The
enzyme turns over both non-cross-linked and cross-linked peptidoglycan as its
substrate. A model for the ternary assembly of the complex of
PA2854-OprI-peptidoglycan is proposed based on the collective evidence. We
document that anchoring of the outer membrane to the cell wall catalyzed by
PA2854 does not appear to be a redundant reaction (product of more than one
enzyme), in the absence of which the bacterium shows a weakened envelope, prone
to disruption.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONCharacterization of the Function of the Gene Product of PA2854We studied the functional role of PA2854 in performing the chemical anchoring of the outer membrane by comparing the PG composition of the wild-type P. aeruginosa MPAO1 and that of the transposon mutant MPAO1 PA2854::Tn, which we obtained from the Manoil library (Figure 2).14 The sacculus, i.e., the purified intact cell wall, was prepared from each strain. As depicted in Figure 2A, processing of the wild-type OprI-linked cell wall by the V8 protease (from Staphylococcus aureus V8 strain; red arrow) leaves a remnant of the C-terminus of OprI (the peptide sequence KASRK) linked to the cell wall, which served for the diagnostic identification by mass spectrometry. Subsequently, the sacculus was digested with mutanolysin (purple arrows) and the reducing ends of the sugars were reduced with NaBH4. The sequential reactions of mutanolysin and borohydride produced fragments that could readily be identified by LC/MS analysis.As shown for the sacculi prepared from the wild-type (Figure 2B) and the transposon mutant (Figure 2C) strains, the total-ion-chromatogram (TIC) traces display the expected abundant PG monomer and dimer species (Tetra, TetraTetra, TetraTetra*). In this nomenclature, Tetra and Tri refer to N-acetylglucosamine-β-1,4-N-acetylmuramic acid with a tetra- or tripeptide stem (GlcNAc-MurNAc-Tetra/Tri). Similarly, TetraTetra represents two Tetra units joined by a peptide cross-link, and TetraTetra* contains one disaccharide borohydride-reduced MurNAc (MurNAcr) replaced by 1,6-anhydro-N-acetylmuramic acid (anhMurNAc; found at the termini of the individual strands of PG), as depicted in Figures 2A and S1. Furthermore, the extracted-ion chromatograms (EICs) of the samples from the wild-type MPAO1 show three additional peaks corresponding to OprI-linked muropeptides 1–3 (Tri-KASRK, TetraTri-KASRK, and TetraTri-KASRK*; the underline K designates the lysine attached to the peptidoglycan at its side chain), which were entirely absent from the MPAO1 PA2854::Tn mutant samples. The absence of OprI-linked PG in the sacculus of the mutant strain reveals that PA2854 cross-links OprI to the peptidoglycan. OprI-linked muropeptides from the wild-type sacculus show both non-cross-linked (e.g., 1) and cross-linked (e.g., 2 and 3) PG species. The important conclusions are that OprI can be attached to both the cross-linked and non-cross-linked stem peptides and that there is no apparent redundancy in the P. aeruginosa genome for the function of PA2854. If indeed there exists another enzyme in P. aeruginosa that performs this reaction, that of PA2854 dominates.High-resolution accurate-mass data for compounds 1–3 agreed with the calculated masses for the proposed structures (Figures 2D and S1). Compound 1 was further analyzed by collision-induced dissociation (CID) mass for the purpose of confirmation of the assignments, which produced the expected series of b/y ions from peptide-bond cleavage and B/Y ions from glycosidic-bond cleavage, as annotated in Figures 2E and S1C. The fragment ions highlighted in bold (Y1, y1, y2, y3, y4) all retain the DAP–ε–Lys linkage, confirming that the KASRK peptide is covalently attached to the peptidoglycan stem.Consequences of the In Vivo Loss of Functional PA2854The functional loss of the PG-outer-membrane anchoring is not lethal, as evidenced by the growth of MPAO1 PA2854::Tn. One consequence of losing PG-outer-membrane anchoring is a more fragile outer membrane.13 With our biochemical positioning of PA2854 as the sole catalyst for PG anchoring to the outer membrane protein OprI, we sought to investigate its role in preserving outer membrane integrity. We conducted scanning electron microscopy experiments to compare the morphological changes of strain MPAO1 under stress in the presence and absence of PA2854 function. We exposed log-phase wild-type MPAO1 and MPAO1 PA2854::Tn to either isotonic or hypotonic conditions. The hypotonic shock was induced by brief exposure to 1 mM EDTA in water. Addition of EDTA depletes divalent cations, which normally coordinate lipid A to stabilize the outer membrane; therefore, the differences in morphology between the two strains are clearer. The cells were then imaged by scanning electron microscopy.Both the wild-type (Figure 3A) and mutant (Figure 3B) bacteria exhibited similar morphology under isotonic conditions. The cell shape, size, and surface structure did not change with the loss of the PA2854 activity. However, we observed some disrupted bacteria in the case of the mutant strain (Figure 3B). A more drastic difference was observed under divalent ion depletion conditions in the presence of EDTA. The wild-type strain showed intact bacteria but also some that appeared deflated as they had burst under hypotonic conditions (Figure 3C). The mutant strain displayed multiple prominent protrusions on the surface with a spider-web appearance due to leaky membranes (Figure 3D). The cross-linking of the PG to OprI by PA2854 is not critical for bacterial survival but would appear to play an important role in maintaining the outer-membrane stability/integrity.13OprI Exists in a Monomer-Trimer EquilibriumHaving documented the reaction of PA2854 in live P. aeruginosa, we turned our attention to reconstituting the reaction in vitro with recombinant purified proteins and synthetic PG samples. We expressed the coding sequences and purified recombinant PA2854 and OprI to homogeneity (Figure S2A). We confirmed the molecular mass of PA2854 by mass spectrometry (Figure S2C). In the case of OprI, mass spectrometry under nondenaturing conditions showed both monomeric (6504 Da) and trimeric (19,513 Da) species (Figure S2B). Significant trimerization was seen at concentrations of OprI exceeding 20 μM, which allowed for estimation of a KD at ~20 μM. No dimeric species were detected in our mass spectrometry experiment, indicating that trimerization occurs over a single kinetic transition. OprI accounts for approximately 20% of the outer-membrane proteins in P. aeruginosa.15PA2854 Interacts with Its Substrates In VitroNext, we attempted thermal-shift assays to demonstrate that indeed PA2854 interacts with its substrates in vitro. This experiment was important for the synthetic PG samples, since the actual PG, a polymer, is being approximated by the synthetic samples. For this assessment, we prepared the synthetic PG samples 6 and 7 (structures of compounds 4-7 are given in Figure S3A), each with a tetrapeptide stem, by methods that we have reported previously.16-19 The native OprI is a lipoprotein; however, the anchor for the membrane is absent in the recombinant sample. These experiments were possible in the light of the presence of multiple tyrosines and phenylalanines in PA2854 and only one tyrosine per OprI monomer. This gave a fluorescence signature with reliable inflection temperatures for PA2854, and a total absence of fluorescence for OprI. We observed a clear temperature shift for PA2854 when it was incubated with the synthetic GlcNAc-anhMurNAc-Tetra (6), GlcNAc-MurNAc-Tetra (7), OprI, or OprI plus either 6 or 7 (Figure 4A). The “Tetra” in these compounds is for tetrapeptide as the stem. These experiments indicated that recombinant PA2854 interacts with both the synthetic peptidoglycan substrate(s) and OprI in vitro.In Vitro Reconstitution of the Catalytic Reaction of PA2854To document the reaction of PA2854, we incubated the enzyme with OprI and with the aforementioned synthetic PG samples individually (compounds 4-7; Figures 4B and S3). We used non-nucleophilic HEPES buffer and performed all reactions at 4 °C. Products for the coupling of substrates 4–7 with OprI, compounds 4p–7p, respectively, were detected by intact-mass analysis; the quantities of each species incidentally increased as a function of time (Figures 4B and S4A). The peak at 6504 Da corresponds to unmodified OprI, whereas the additional peaks (4p–7p; “p” denotes product) represent OprI modified by a single PG sample with a tripeptide stem (Tri–OprI). The difference in mass of each product reflects the mass of the corresponding appended PG, as illustrated in Figure 4B. The transformation clearly accounts for the ligation of OprI to the synthetic PG sample(s) in a process that uses the tetrapeptide stem in the substrate PG for the linkage to the side chain of lysine in OprI. This ligation of the two substrates entails the departure of the terminal d-Ala from the tetrapeptide in the PG substrate(s), which is accounted for in the mass spectrometry data.Cross-linked PG substrates containing a TetraTetra stem motif were also tested to determine whether TetraTri–OprI adducts would result (Figure S3). These substrates were generated by digestion of the sacculus with the lytic transglycosylase MltA, which produced GlcNAc-anhMurNAc-containing species 6 (Figure 4B) and TetraTetra 8 (Figure S3) as the two most abundant species, or with lysozyme, which produced GlcNAc-MurNAc-containing species 7 (Figure 4B) and TetraTetra 9 (Figure S3). The resultant mixtures were subjected to the PA2854/OprI ligation. Under these conditions, PA2854 performed the ligation reaction (to OprI) with the non-cross-linked donors 6 and 7, but also with the cross-linked donors 8 and 9 (Figure S3B,C).Covalent Inhibition of PA2854 by MeropenemSequence analysis of PA2854 revealed it to have a YkuD domain, found in ld-transpeptidases. ld-Transpeptidases are typically inhibited by carbapenems.20 Under the conditions that we documented the reaction of PA2854, we also observed the formation of the covalent PA2854–Meropenem adduct, which increased with longer incubation time (Figures 4C and S4B). As shown in Figure 4C, apo-PA2854 appears as two closely spaced species corresponding to loss of the N-terminal Met (34,988 Da) and loss of Met plus gluconoylation (35,166 Da). Nα-Gluconoylation on the His-tag N-terminus is a well-known transformation.21 Meropenem modifies each form to give a covalent adduct (PA2854–MEM), which is shifted by +383 Da in the deconvoluted mass spectrum (Figure 4C). Time-course analysis under these conditions yielded an observed reaction rate kobs = 0.066 ± 0.004 h−1 with a half-life of t1/2 = 10.5 ± 0.6 h (Figure 4C). These kinetic parameters are reflective of a slow acylation process, consistent with the slow cross-linking reaction catalyzed by PA2854 in the presence of its substrates. Despite the slow nature of this reaction, the transformation is nearing quantitative with fraction modified (Fmax) at 95.4 ± 1.9%.X-ray Structure of PA2854We turned our attention to the determination of the structures of PA2854 and OprI. The sequence of PA2854 is composed of a predicted signal peptide (amino acids 1–23), an N-terminal PG-binding LysM domain (amino acids 48–84), a YkuD catalytic domain (amino acids 96–231),22,23 and a C-terminal extension (residues 232–323). Structural determination of PA2854 was achieved by the construct that spanned amino acids 24–323 (see the Experimental Procedures Section and Table S1). An N-terminal histidine tag was incorporated, consisting of the sequence MGSSHHHHHHSSGEN-LYFQGHM, which proved essential in preventing protein aggregation upon TEV cleavage. This was consistent with previously reported solubility issues that hindered production of soluble PA2854.8 The highest-quality crystals allowed structure determination at 2.63 Å resolution (Figure 5). Notably, the crystals exhibited 72% solvent content—significantly higher than the typical protein (47–50%) among the PDB-deposited structures24,25—likely contributing to the moderate resolution limit and marked anisotropy. The electron-density map clearly resolved the stretch of amino acids 24–305, whereas no density was observed for the final C-terminal segment (amino acids 306–323) or for the initial 14 amino acids from the His-tag, indicating inherent high flexibility in this C-terminal segment of the protein (amino acids 306–323; Figure S5).In the crystal structure of PA2854, the N-terminal purification tag of a symmetry-related molecule was inserted into the active site of PA2854 (Figure 7A). The tag establishes different interactions with the PA2854 active site: salt-bridge (Lys180), hydrogen bonds (Trp147, Pro150, Thr182, and Thr139), and van der Waals interactions (Pro150, Leu176, and Leu189). This ensconced tag abrogated all of our soaking experiments with carbapenems or with synthetic peptidoglycan analogues. A truncated version of the protein (amino acids 24–304) bearing the His-tag at the C-terminus was prepared to circumvent this problem, but it failed to crystallize in more than 900 tested conditions. These included trials with antibiotics and seeding from crystals of the full-length constructs. These results underscore the importance of the N-terminal region in promoting crystal formation and protein stability.The PA2854 structure revealed a three-domain architecture (Figures 5A and S6A), composed of a LysM domain (amino acids 40–84), a catalytic YkuD domain (amino acids 96–231) and a C-terminal domain (amino acids 235–303). The LysM domain adopts the typical βααβ fold (Figure 5A) that has been reported to recognize the GlcNAc-X-GlcNAc motif in different polysaccharides such as the peptidoglycan.26 The LysM domain of the B. subtilis YkuD (PDB: 4A1J)27 is the closest structural homologue (Figure S6B). The catalytic domain presents a YkuD-like fold27 consisting of a β-sandwich formed by two mixed β-sheets (β7-β10-β4-β5-β6 and β7-β8-β9) and a single α-helix between β9 and β10 strands (Figure 5B). The conserved l,d-transpeptidase fingerprint motif28 (Hxx14–17(S/T)HGCh(R/N) with h for any hydrophobic amino acid) is present in PA2854 (amino acids 190–207; Figure S7). Cys207, the invariant catalytic residue in other l,d-transpeptidases29 exists in this motif (Figure 5B). His191 is the base that promotes it for the active-site acylation by the substrate. The l,d-transpeptidase motif forms a partially disordered loop in the structure, denoted as LC (residues 191–207) (Figure 5B).An Inactive-to-Active Structural Transition of PA2854Structural homologues of the catalytic domain were identified using DALI server30 and Foldseek,31 including l,d-transpeptidases that perform the 3,3-cross-linking of the cell wall in various organisms (Figure S6), the closest one being the B. subtilis YkuD (PDB: 4A1J, RMSD of 0.96 Å for 53 Cα atoms superimposition).27 Other structurally related l,d-transpeptidases are Enterococcus faecium Ldtfm (PDB: 6FJ1)32 and Escherichia coli YcbB (PDB: 6NTW)33 (Figure S6). Although the global YkuD fold is conserved, there are important differences in that of PA2854. The catalytic cysteine in PA2854 is not exposed, but buried and somewhat remote from the side-chain imidazole of the catalytic histidine (distance Nε2-Sγ of 6.0 Å versus 4.2 Å in B. subtilis YukD) (Figure 5C). Differences are also observed in loops L122–133 and Lc surrounding the active-site cavity and in the substrate cap (residues 144–178) (Figure 5C). This cap has been implicated in regulating substrate access and product release in homologues.33,34 Its conformation in PA2854 is stabilized by a hydrophobic patch (Trp147, Pro149, Pro150, Ile153, Leu164, Pro175, Leu176), and a salt-bridge between His205 (Ldt motif) and Glu156 in the cap (Figure 5D). Notably, this region is proline-rich (10 Pro, >28% of residues), suggesting a structural role and/or a potential interface for protein–protein interactions. The C-terminal domain consists of a β-sheet with three β-strands and two α-helices (Figure 5A). This domain is unique to PA2854, as it was not identified by Prosite,35 Pfam,36 nor found in homologues.The three domains of the protein establish many polar contacts among themselves. The LysM domain contains an extra β-strand (β1) packed with the β6 of the catalytic domain (Figure 5A). Also, an extensive network of salt-bridge interactions connects the three domains at the protein core (Figure 5E), conferring rigidity to this region, whereas the peripheral regions of the domains exhibit high conformational mobility (Figure S5B).Comparison of the crystal structure of PA2854 with the model predicted by AlphaFold237 (Figure S8A) reveals that the overall fold is maintained (RMSD of 0.998 Å, 214 Cα atoms). Changes in the relative positions of the peripheral regions of the three domains are observed (Figure S8B), consistent with the mobility detected in the crystal (Figure S5B). Notably, significant conformational differences are detected in loops proximal to the catalytic center (LC, L122–133, and L249–263). The predicted structure displays a fully competent catalytic dyad, as observed in other enzymes with the YkuD domain, such as B. subtilis (Figure 5C), with the active-site cysteine thiol positioned for promotion by the histidine imidazole. Transition between the two conformations requires the concerted backbone rearrangements of these three loops (LC, L122–133, and L249–263) of up to ~9 Å (Figure S8C). The enzyme therefore appears to exist in two conformations; an inactive one, where there is spatial separation between His191 and the thiol of Cys207, and an active conformation, where these groups are in close proximity. The active conformation shows an active-site architecture poised for catalysis. We reconstituted this transition from the inactive conformation to the active conformation using targeted molecular dynamics (Movie S1). In this simulation, we observed the rearrangement of all three loops over the course of 11 ns of simulation time. These motions both organize the binding surface for OprI and properly poise the active site His191 and Cys207 for catalysis. The thiol of Cys207 and the lone pair of electrons in His191 imidazole sample distances between ~4.5 and 5.0 Å. This simulation argues that the PA2854 structure can transition between two states, one suitable for catalysis and another as a latent form, which might exist for regulatory functions of its catalytic process.OprI Forms a Triple Helical StructureA soluble version of OprI (spanning amino acids 25–83) crystallized in the cubic space group P213 with one chain per asymmetric unit (see the Experimental Procedures Section and Table S1). The OprI structure, solved at 2.1 Å resolution, comprises a long α-helix (residues 25–80) (Figure 6A) with the final three C-terminal residues in a more flexible conformation, resulting in a less-defined electron density (Figure S5C,D). OprI organizes as an 82-Å-long α-helical trimeric coiled-coil generating the symmetry of the crystal. The X-ray structure is consistent with the trimeric assembly, revealed by what was observed in solution by mass spectrometry (Figure S2B). The coiled-coil organization of three parallel α-helices varies its cross-sectional arrangement along the structure, with an N-terminal triangular cross-sectional side width of ~11.6 Å to a more compact cross-sectional side width at the C-terminal end (8.7 Å) (Figure 6B-E).The OprI chain contains a high percentage of alanine residues (28.1%; 18 alanines). These alanine residues are all sequestered at the interfaces of the trimer, conforming to what can be construed as an “alanine zipper” (Figure 6D,E), essential for the formation of the coiled-coil. The alanine side chains participate in extensive van der Waals interactions along the zipper length, contributing to its hydrophobic core. In addition, polar interchain interactions are observed all along the length of the structure that contribute to trimer stabilization. In the N-terminal region, Arg27 establishes two salt bridges and a hydrogen bond with amino acids of the adjacent chain (Glu28, Glu35, and Thr32, respectively) (Figure 6A). In the central region (Figure 6A), another interchain salt-bridge interaction is formed between Arg44 and Asp46. In the C-terminal region, two interchain hydrogen bonds are formed between Thr65 and Gln63 and between Arg72 and Asn70 (Figure 6A). As these interactions are repeated three times in the oligomer, the global contribution of both the alanine-zipper and the polar interactions makes a stable trimer. This feature supports the notion that OprI behaves physiologically as a trimer, similarly to the lipoprotein Lpp protein in E. coli,38,39 with which it shares structural homology (RMSD = 1.36 Å for 158 Cα atoms superimposition) (Figure S9). It is worth mentioning that most of the hydrophobic amino acids in OprI are alanine residues (capped by a Leu at the N-terminus and a Met at the C-terminus) (Figure S9B), in Lpp the stretch of alanine residues is shorter and most of the hydrophobic amino acids are bulkier (Leu, Ile and Val), thus occupying half of the length of the lipoprotein (Figure S9C). OprI and Lpp display comparable electrostatic potential profiles (Figure S9D). Both show a basic N-terminus promoting outer-membrane lipid interactions, a largely acidic central region in the exposed helical trimer, and a basic C-terminal segment containing the Ser–Arg–Lys motif required for peptidoglycan binding.In Silico Reconstruction of the Outer Membrane–Peptidoglycan Cross-Linking ComplexStructural comparison of PA2854 with related enzymes, such as Meropenem-acylated E. coli YcbB33 (Figure S6D) and Mycobacterium tuberculosis LdtMT2 bound to Meropenem and faropenem34,40 revealed that their ligands and the N-terminal symmetry mate in PA2854 all occupy the same acyl-donor binding site. In order to further explore the relevance of the observed amino acids in the interaction with the acyl-donor stem of PG, a computational model of the acylated PA2854 intermediate with the peptidoglycan fragment GlcNAc-MurNAc-l-Ala-g-d-Glu-m-DAP was built. This complex was constructed in the model of the AlphaFold prediction of the structure for PA2854 (Figure S8). The model was generated by cofolding the PA2854 sequence with GlcNAc-MurNAc-Tetra using RoseTTAFold All-Atom.41 The tetrapeptide stem inserts into the active site of PA2854, while the glycan units remain at the entrance. The peptide stem was poised for nucleophilic attack by Cys207 for the formation of the thioester intermediate with the attendant displacement of d-Ala. Lys180 makes a salt-bridge interaction with the carboxylate group of the γ-d-Glu and His204 with the carboxylate of m-DAP, while residues Trp147, Ser152, His191, and Pro186 establish hydrogen-bond interactions with the peptide stem (Figure 7B). The van der Waals interactions by Leu189 and Pro150, among others, contribute to stabilization of the intermediate state. Incidentally, all these amino acids interact with the N-terminal tag in the crystal structure of PA2854 in the inactive state (Figure 7A).The second step of the transpeptidation reaction, leading to anchoring of PG to OprI, differs from the canonical l,d-transpeptidases, which perform the 3,3-cross-linking reaction. AlphaFold-Multimer extension,42 provides a high-confidence model of trimeric OprI in complex with PA2854 (Figures 8A and S10). Complexes were modeled with a 1:3 PA2854:OprI stoichiometry, based on trimeric OprI arrangement observed both in solution and in the crystal structure. The model positioned the C-termini of the OprI trimer near the catalytic cavity of PA2854, specifically in a pocket opposite the acyl-donor site—consistent with the expected location of the acceptor site. Notably, acidic surface patches on PA2854—formed by acidic residues located in the substrate cap (Asp160, Asp162, and Asp173) and in the LysM domain (Glu47, Asp52, and Glu55)—appear to stabilize this interaction (Figure 8B). These acidic features are unique to PA2854 and absent in 3,3-l,d-transpeptidases such as B. subtilis YkuD, E. coli YcbB, E. faecium Ldtfm217 (Figure S11), or the hydrolytic enzymes LdtF or DpaA (data not shown), suggesting a specialized role in outer membrane lipoprotein recognition. Importantly, the computational model for the PA2854:OprI-trimer complex inserts the terminal Lys83 from one of the OprI chains in a very close distance of the PA2854 catalytic dyad (Figure 8C).The crystallographic coordinates used to generate the complex of OprI and PA2854 (in complex with GlcNAc-MurNAc-Tri) were aligned over the AlphaFold-Multimer model of PA2854:OprI-trimer (see the Experimental Procedures Section). A POPE membrane section was constructed and covalently anchored to the N-terminal cysteine of all three OprI monomers to mimic the environment of membrane-linked OprI. The dynamics of the full complex (PA2854:Glc-NAc-MurNAc-Tri:OprI-trimer:membrane) was simulated for 100 ns (Movie S2). Throughout the simulation, the C-terminal ends of all three OprI monomers were in contact with PA2854 through electrostatic complementarity between OprI and PA2854 (Figure 8D). The C-terminus of OprI is dominated by basic residues, which are complemented by acidic residues at the entrance of the PA2854 active site. This arrangement allows for penetration of the ε-amine of Lys83 of one of the OprI monomers into the active site. The simulation revealed a stable arrangement of the catalytic components required for OprI-peptidoglycan cross-linking. We observed stabilization of the thioester oxygen of acylated PA2854 in an oxyanion hole formed by hydrogen bonding to the amides of Gly206 and Cys207. The ε-amine of Lys83 of the OprI monomer inserted into the active site frequently sampling conformations and distances to the thioester carbonyl that allow the nucleophilic attack to occur (Figure 8E,F and Movie S2). The distance (d) and the angle of nucleophilic attack (θ) approach ~3 Å and 100°, respectively. Our simulations support the double-displacement mechanism for PA2854. A double-displacement mechanism is one in which the first substrate produces a covalent complex with the enzyme. The second substrate then binds and reacts to produce the final product.

Consequences of the In Vivo Loss of Functional PA2854The functional loss of the PG-outer-membrane anchoring is not lethal, as evidenced by the growth of MPAO1 PA2854::Tn. One consequence of losing PG-outer-membrane anchoring is a more fragile outer membrane.13 With our biochemical positioning of PA2854 as the sole catalyst for PG anchoring to the outer membrane protein OprI, we sought to investigate its role in preserving outer membrane integrity. We conducted scanning electron microscopy experiments to compare the morphological changes of strain MPAO1 under stress in the presence and absence of PA2854 function. We exposed log-phase wild-type MPAO1 and MPAO1 PA2854::Tn to either isotonic or hypotonic conditions. The hypotonic shock was induced by brief exposure to 1 mM EDTA in water. Addition of EDTA depletes divalent cations, which normally coordinate lipid A to stabilize the outer membrane; therefore, the differences in morphology between the two strains are clearer. The cells were then imaged by scanning electron microscopy.Both the wild-type (Figure 3A) and mutant (Figure 3B) bacteria exhibited similar morphology under isotonic conditions. The cell shape, size, and surface structure did not change with the loss of the PA2854 activity. However, we observed some disrupted bacteria in the case of the mutant strain (Figure 3B). A more drastic difference was observed under divalent ion depletion conditions in the presence of EDTA. The wild-type strain showed intact bacteria but also some that appeared deflated as they had burst under hypotonic conditions (Figure 3C). The mutant strain displayed multiple prominent protrusions on the surface with a spider-web appearance due to leaky membranes (Figure 3D). The cross-linking of the PG to OprI by PA2854 is not critical for bacterial survival but would appear to play an important role in maintaining the outer-membrane stability/integrity.13

Covalent Inhibition of PA2854 by MeropenemSequence analysis of PA2854 revealed it to have a YkuD domain, found in ld-transpeptidases. ld-Transpeptidases are typically inhibited by carbapenems.20 Under the conditions that we documented the reaction of PA2854, we also observed the formation of the covalent PA2854–Meropenem adduct, which increased with longer incubation time (Figures 4C and S4B). As shown in Figure 4C, apo-PA2854 appears as two closely spaced species corresponding to loss of the N-terminal Met (34,988 Da) and loss of Met plus gluconoylation (35,166 Da). Nα-Gluconoylation on the His-tag N-terminus is a well-known transformation.21 Meropenem modifies each form to give a covalent adduct (PA2854–MEM), which is shifted by +383 Da in the deconvoluted mass spectrum (Figure 4C). Time-course analysis under these conditions yielded an observed reaction rate kobs = 0.066 ± 0.004 h−1 with a half-life of t1/2 = 10.5 ± 0.6 h (Figure 4C). These kinetic parameters are reflective of a slow acylation process, consistent with the slow cross-linking reaction catalyzed by PA2854 in the presence of its substrates. Despite the slow nature of this reaction, the transformation is nearing quantitative with fraction modified (Fmax) at 95.4 ± 1.9%.

An Inactive-to-Active Structural Transition of PA2854Structural homologues of the catalytic domain were identified using DALI server30 and Foldseek,31 including l,d-transpeptidases that perform the 3,3-cross-linking of the cell wall in various organisms (Figure S6), the closest one being the B. subtilis YkuD (PDB: 4A1J, RMSD of 0.96 Å for 53 Cα atoms superimposition).27 Other structurally related l,d-transpeptidases are Enterococcus faecium Ldtfm (PDB: 6FJ1)32 and Escherichia coli YcbB (PDB: 6NTW)33 (Figure S6). Although the global YkuD fold is conserved, there are important differences in that of PA2854. The OprI structure, solved at 2.1 Å resolution, comprises a long α-helix (residues 25–80) (Figure 6A) with the final three C-terminal residues in a more flexible conformation, resulting in a less-defined electron density (Figure S5C,D). The X-ray structure is consistent with the trimeric assembly, revealed by what was observed in solution by mass spectrometry (Figure S2B). The coiled-coil organization of three parallel α-helices varies its cross-sectional arrangement along the structure, with an N-terminal triangular cross-sectional side width of ~11.6 Å to a more compact cross-sectional side width at the C-terminal end (8.7 Å) (Figure 6B-E).The OprI chain contains a high percentage of alanine residues (28.1%; 18 alanines). These alanine residues are all sequestered at the interfaces of the trimer, conforming to what can be construed as an “alanine zipper” (Figure 6D,E), essential for the formation of the coiled-coil. The alanine side chains participate in extensive van der Waals interactions along the zipper length, contributing to its hydrophobic core. In addition, polar interchain interactions are observed all along the length of the structure that contribute to trimer stabilization. In the N-terminal region, Arg27 establishes two salt bridges and a hydrogen bond with amino acids of the adjacent chain (Glu28, Glu35, and Thr32, respectively) (Figure 6A). In the central region (Figure 6A), another interchain salt-bridge interaction is formed between Arg44 and Asp46. In the C-terminal region, two interchain hydrogen bonds are formed between Thr65 and Gln63 and between Arg72 and Asn70 (Figure 6A). As these interactions are repeated three times in the oligomer, the global contribution of both the alanine-zipper and the polar interactions makes a stable trimer. This feature supports the notion that OprI behaves physiologically as a trimer, similarly to the lipoprotein Lpp protein in E. coli,38,39 with which it shares structural homology (RMSD = 1.36 Å for 158 Cα atoms superimposition) (Figure S9). It is worth mentioning that most of the hydrophobic amino acids in OprI are alanine residues (capped by a Leu at the N-terminus and a Met at the C-terminus) (Figure S9B), in Lpp the stretch of alanine residues is shorter and most of the hydrophobic amino acids are bulkier (Leu, Ile and Val), thus occupying half of the length of the lipoprotein (Figure S9C). OprI and Lpp display comparable electrostatic potential profiles (Figure S9D). Both show a basic N-terminus promoting outer-membrane lipid interactions, a largely acidic central region in the exposed helical trimer, and a basic C-terminal segment containing the Ser–Arg–Lys motif required for peptidoglycan binding.

CONCLUSIONOprI, abundant at approximately 20% of the outer-membrane proteins,43 exists as a homotrimer as evidenced by mass spectrometry and X-ray crystallography. The trimeric OprI as a lipoprotein is anchored to the inner leaflet of the Gram-negative outer membrane. The membrane environment imposes mechanical and structural constraints on the assembly and orientation of OprI. We estimate the KD of trimerization at ~20 μM for the recombinant OprI in solution. However, one might expect a different value in the native membrane environment. The natural abundance of OprI in the outer membrane argues that an evolutionary pressure might not exist for significant enhancement of the KD value.Meanwhile, the tetrapeptide stem of the peptidoglycan, the most abundant form of the peptidoglycan during homeostasis,16 undergoes a reaction with the active site cysteine of PA2854 to generate a thioacyl intermediate. The amide carbonyl of m-3DAP in the tetrapeptide stem sequesters within the active-site oxyanion hole for this transformation, which leads to the nucleophilic attack of the carbonyl by the cysteine side chain with the attendant displacement of the terminal d-4Ala (Figure 9, top). The thioester carbonyl is promoted for the second step of the double-displacement mechanism by the same oxyanion hole, which is poised for nucleophilic capture of the side chain of Lys83 from trimeric OprI (Figure 9, bottom). We documented this process in both live bacteria and a reconstituted in vitro system with recombinant proteins and synthetic peptidoglycan surrogates. Incidentally, these experiments revealed that the PA2854 can catalyze the ligation reaction with both the non-cross-linked and cross-linked peptidoglycan.

The in vitro-reconstituted system exhibits slow reaction, which is also true for the modification of PA2854 by Meropenem. These slow kinetics hint at the requirements of the native environment of the outer membrane and periplasmic space for proper enzymatic function, which has not been duplicated in vitro. Notwithstanding the slow kinetics, we successfully demonstrated the enzymatic reaction both in vitro and in live bacteria.

We proposed in this report a three-dimensional model of how this assembly is formed. The membrane-anchored OprI binds PA2854 by electrostatic surface interactions that showed stability in dynamics simulations and was supported by nanoDSF measurements. Assessment of PA2854 Reaction Products from the SacculiV8-treated P. aeruginosa sacculi were isolated from the wild-type MPAO1 and PA2854::Tn MPAO1 strains. This cell suspension was added dropwise to 50 mL hot 8% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and boiled with stirring for 3 h. The SDS solution was removed by repeated (5×) centrifugation at 36,000 rpm at 30 °C in an F37L −8 × 100 rotor (ThermoScientific), followed by resuspension in 80 mL water. After the final wash, the pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, supplemented with 2.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.5 mM CaCl2. X-ray Data Collection and Structural Determination of PA2854 and OprIX-ray diffraction data were collected for PA2854 at the XALOC beamline of the ALBA Synchrotron (Barcelona, Spain), using a wavelength of 0.979 Å and a Pilatus 6 M detector. The diffraction pattern exhibited pronounced anisotropy, reflected in varying diffraction quality along different reciprocal lattice directions. Data processing was performed using XDS,50 followed by ellipsoidal truncation with the STARANISO server.51 The diffraction-limit surface was defined using a CC1/2 > 0.3 threshold. The resulting resolution limits along the principal axes of the ellipsoid cutoff were: a* = 3.84 Å, b* = 3.84 Å, and c* = 2.44 Å, providing an overall resolution of 2.63 Å. Phase determination was achieved by molecular replacement, using an AlphaFold2-predicted model37 as the search template. Model building and refinement were conducted manually in Coot, Phenix,52 and Refmac5.53 PA2854 crystallized in space group P43212, with one molecule per asymmetric unit and a solvent content of 72%. Targeted Molecular Dynamics Simulation of PA2854The transition of PA2854 from the latent crystallographic conformation to the active conformation shown in the AlphaFold model was studied by targeted molecular dynamics. These simulations were performed using Amber 24.0.60,61 The structures of the latent and active conformations of PA2854 were treated as the initial and target conformations, respectively. Both structure files in PDB format were prepared using tLEaP using an FF19SB force field, neutralized, and solvated in a ~15 Å octahedral TIP3P water box. Each structure was then energy-minimized over three stages. In the first stage, only water and ions were allowed to move. In the second stage, the entire system was mobile, but a 0 kcal·mol−1·Å−2 harmonic restraint force constant was applied to the protein. Finally, in the third stage, the entire system was energy-minimized with no restraint force. Each system is then equilibrated over five stages. In the first stage, the system is heated to 300 K, and only solvent and water movement took place. Second, the system is equilibrated with no protein movement. In the third to final equilibration stages, the system is equilibrated in NPT with sequentially decreasing the harmonic restraint force constant from 50 to 20 to 5 kcal·mol−1·Å−2. These preparation steps yield the simulation-ready system.Targeted molecular dynamics was completed over three consecutive simulations totaling 11 ns. In the first simulation, a mild harmonic restraint force of 0.25 kcal·mol−1·Å−2 was applied to the backbone of loops LC, L122–133, and L249–263 for 5 ns. Next, the system was simulated for 3 ns with a moderate restraint force of 0.5 kcal·mol−1·Å−2 applied only to all heavy atoms of Lc. Finally, all heavy atoms of all three loops were once again subjected to a restraint of 0.5 kcal·mol−1·Å−2 for 3 ns of simulation time. All three simulations were combined using CPPTRAJ and visualized using PyMOL.62-64 The full targeted molecular dynamics simulation is shown in Movie S1 made with PyMOL.
