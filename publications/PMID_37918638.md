Myoregulin (MLN) is a protein that regulates the activity of the sarcoplasmic
reticulum Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA) without affecting its affinity for Ca2+. MLN's
residue Lys27 is located at a site where other SERCA regulators control Ca2+
affinity. Therefore, we conducted atomistic simulations and ATPase activity
experiments to determine whether replacing Lys27 with asparagine, a conserved
residue found in various muscle SERCA regulators, would enable MLN to modulate
both the Ca2+ affinity and catalytic activity of SERCA. Our findings indicate
that replacing Lys27 with Asn significantly enhances the inhibitory potency of
MLN, but it does not affect SERCA's affinity for Ca2+. We suggest that the SERCA
site modulating Ca2+ affinity also acts as a catalytic activity switch.
Therefore, this site is a key element contributing to the functional divergence
among homologous SERCA regulators. This study paves the way for future
investigations to explore how biological function diverges during the evolution
of the SERCA regulator family.

## Full Text

Myoregulin (MLN) is a 46-residue transmembrane protein that regulates the activity of the skeletal muscle calcium pump (sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase, SERCA1a) [1]. MLN shares functional homology with regulators sarcolipin (SLN), a 31-residue protein that is expressed in skeletal muscle, and with phospholamban (PLN), a 52-residue protein found primarily in cardiac muscle to modulate Ca2+ transport in muscle cells [2, 3]. MLN binds SERCA1a in a heterodimeric complex [4], and it is formed by a cytosolic domain that is unstructured and oriented perpendicularly to the membrane normal, a transmembrane helix, and a short luminal domain (Fig. 1A).

Despite the structural and functional homology between MLN and PLN/SLN [5], a significant difference between these proteins is that MLN inhibits SERCA1a’s maximal activity (Vmax) without changing the relative affinity of the pump for Ca2+ (KCa) [5, 6]. Sequence alignment shows that PLN and SLN have a conserved Asn residue, but MLN has a basic residue, Lys27, at this position (Fig. 1B). This observation is interesting because residue Asn11/Asn34 in SLN and PLN is a primary molecular switch modulating the KCa of SERCA1a [7, 8], but we have recently shown that wild-type MLN cannot modulate KCa [6]. Therefore, we ask why Asn, a functionally conserved residue across muscle regulins [5, 9], is substituted for Lys in MLN. We hypothesize that Lys27 confers MLN with functional divergence among the family of SERCA regulators by allowing it to control SERCA1a’s function without altering its Ca2+ affinity. Therefore, we expect that the replacement of Lys27 by Asn will yield an MLN peptide that modulates both Vmax and KCa of SERCA1a. We tested this hypothesis by using atomistic molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and ATPase activity assays of SERCA1a–MLN co-reconstitutions; detailed methods are in the supplementary materials.

We simulated SERCA1a bound to wild-type MLN (MLNWT) and the MLN mutant Lys27Asn (MLNK27N) starting from a structural model published recently by us [5] and Fig. S1 of the supplementary materials. Briefly, this model was built based on the SERCA-PLN structure (PDB: 4kyt) as a template, a consensus-modeled structure of MLN generated using extensive microsecond-long atomistic simulations of the unbound MLN protein embedded in a lipid bilayer, and the sequence alignments shown in Fig. 1B. We performed five independent microsecond-long trajectories of each SERCA1a‒MLN complex. The formation of stable SERCA1a–MLNWT and SERCA1a–MLNK27N complexes is illustrated by the root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis showing that compared to the MLNWT, the peptide MLNK27N does not have a measurable effect on the mobility of the transmembrane domain (RMSF<1.3 Å, Fig. 1C). Importantly, we found Lys27Asn occupies a position in the SERCA1a–MLN interface that is very similar to that observed in the crystal structures of SERCA1a bound to PLN and SLN (Fig. 1D). In this position, Lys27Asn remains near SERCA1a residues Gly801 and Thr805 in all five trajectories, in agreement with x-ray crystallography studies [10–12]. These findings validate the structure of the SERCA1a–MLNK27N and allow us to establish whether Asn at position Lys27 of MLN mimics the KCa modulating function as observed in PLN and SLN [7, 8].

We next analyzed the MD trajectories to find evidence that the mutation Lys27Asn induces structural changes in the transport sites that have been proposed to modulate SERCA1a’s KCa. Previous crystallography and molecular dynamics simulation studies have shown that Asn11 of SLN and Asn34 of PLN decrease SERCA1a’s apparent affinity for Ca2+ by inducing a structural change in the transport sites that is incompetent for Ca2+ binding to SERCA1a [10–14]. Here, changes in KCa are controlled by the interaction of Asn11 of SLN and Asn34 of PLN with the backbone oxygen of SERCA1a residue Gly801, which controls the location of Asp800 in the Ca2+ transport sites, represented here as the distances between Glu771 and Asp800 (Fig. 1D). Plotting of these distances from published trajectories of SERCA1a bound to SLN and PLN [13] yields a map that represents the structural space associated with decreased (Fig. 1E, red box) and unaltered (Fig. 1E, green box) Ca2+ affinity. We found that the map representing the effects of MLNK27N on SERCA1a is qualitatively similar to that of the non-inhibited SERCA1a–PLN complex (Fig. 1E), indicating that the replacement of Lys27 to Asn is insufficient to induce the structural changes in the transport sites of SERCA1a associated with modulation of KCa. We tested these predictions by using ATPase activity assays of skeletal muscle SERCA1a from rabbit and synthetic MLNWT (control) or MLNK27N co-reconstituted in proteoliposomes. We found that both MLNWT and MLNK27N inhibit SERCA1a’s Vmax in a Ca2+ concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1F). We found that neither MLNWT nor MLNK27N has a significant effect on SERCA1a’s KCa (Fig 1G). These experiments validate the MD-based results and show that MLNK27N is insufficient to produce an MLN peptide that is capable of modulating both the KCa and Vmax of SERCA1a. MLNWT, our positive control in this study, significantly decreases the Vmax of SERCA1a by 20% (Fig. 1G) but MLNK27N inhibits SERCA1a more potently than MLNWT, decreasing the pump’s Vmax by 32% (Fig. 1G).

Our findings are unexpected and show that instead of controlling SERCA1a’s KCa, MLNK27N is a gain-of-function protein with enhanced inhibitory potency on SERCA1a. By what mechanism does MLNK27N enhance its inhibitory effect on SERCA1a? Analysis of the SERCA1a–MLNWT and SERCA1a–MLNK27N complexes show that they are both stable in the microsecond timescale. While the transmembrane and luminal domains of MLNK27N are less mobile than MLNWT in the complex (Fig. 1C), there are no differences in the structure of the transmembrane domains of either SERCA1a or MLN. In addition to interacting with SERCA1a residue Gly801, Asn of MLNK27N interacts with SERCA1a residue Trp107, a residue that does not play a role in the catalytic function of the pump [15]. Independent MD simulations of the unbound peptides show that the transmembrane domain of MLNK27N, but not MLNWT, populates a tilt angle distribution that is distinct from MLNWT and virtually identical to that of MLN bound to SERCA1a (Fig. 1H). We have shown that Lys27 interacts with the lipid headgroups via ‘snorkeling’ [6], and mutation of this residue to Asn completely abolishes this effect. Indeed, we found that the number of contacts of this residue with the lipid headgroups drops from an average number of contacts of 135 ± 36 for MLNWT (mean ± SEM, n=5) to zero contacts for MLNK27N. Therefore, this structural change induced by mutation Lys27Asn likely affects the tilt angle of the transmembrane helix through changes in ‘snorkeling’ at this position. We also showed that the inhibitory activity of MLN mutants correlates with their ability to populate bound-like conformations in the lipid bilayer environment [6]. We propose that the gain in the inhibitory function of MLNK27N arises from a combination of the ability of MLNK27N to populate a range of conformations that match those of MLN bound to SERCA1a, and an improved stability of the SERCA1a–MLN complex induced by MLNK27N.

In summary, we tested the hypothesis that Lys instead of the conserved Asn at position 27 of MLN is the result of evolutionary divergence to abolish MLN’s ability to control Ca2+ affinity that characterizes Asn-containing regulators PLN and SLN. Our combined computational and experimental approach showed that this is not the case. Instead, the replacement of Lys27 by Asn yields an MLN peptide with significantly increased inhibitory potency but without the ability to shift SERCA1a’s KCa. Based on these findings, we propose that position 27 of MLN is an activity switch that modulates the maximal activity of SERCA1a. These findings also suggest that the functional role of the conserved Asn residue in the family of muscle SERCA regulators is more complex than previously thought and may serve either as a Ca2+ affinity modulator or as a catalytic activity switch. This study directs future investigations to address the question of how biological function diverges in the evolution of regulins to differentially modulate SERCA-mediated Ca2+ signaling in muscle cells.
