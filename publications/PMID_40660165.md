BACKGROUND: Adenosine deaminase acting on RNA (Adar) is a critical enzyme
involved in post-transcriptional epigenetic regulation through
adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) RNA editing. However, the biological role and
regulatory mechanisms of Adar remain largely unknown.
RESULTS: Using Drosophila as a model, we found that loss of Adar leads to
spontaneous genome instability characterized by DNA damage and mitotic defects.
Genome-wide ssDRIP-seq revealed global R-loop accumulation in Adar mutants,
particularly at gene promoters, introns, and repetitive regions including
telomeric retrotransposons. Notably, overexpression of RNase H1 (RNH1)
suppressed R-loop accumulation and rescued genome instability in Adar-deficient
flies. Strikingly, a catalytically inactive Adar mutant (E374A), which lacks
A-to-I editing activity, retained its ability to suppress R-loop accumulation
and preserve genome integrity.
CONCLUSIONS: Our findings identify a novel editing-independent role of Adar in
maintaining genome stability via regulation of R-loop homeostasis. This work
highlights the evolutionarily conserved functions of Adar beyond RNA editing and
establishes Drosophila as a valuable model to study R-loop-mediated genomic
instability.

ADAR2 is also indispensable, as null-mutant mice develop epileptic seizures and die several weeks after birth [10]. However, the relationship between ADAR genes and R-loops remains poorly understood, particularly regarding the importance of ADAR proteins in maintaining R-loop homeostasis and regulating genome stability in animal models.

In this present study, we utilized a Drosophila model to investigate the biological functions of Adar in maintaining R-loop homeostasis and genome stability. Using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, we generate an Adar knockout strain (Adar−/−) and subsequently produced a deaminase-inactivation mutant (AdarE374A). Adar−/− mutants displayed genomic damage and abnormal R-loops accumulation, whereas AdarE374A did not manifest these phenotypes. Adar prevents endogenous R-loop-induced DNA-replication defectPrevious studies have shown that the formation of endogenous R-loops during transcription often interferes with the DNA replication machinery [46]. To test whether excessive accumulation of R-loops induced by Adar depletion leads to a replication defect, a 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) colorimetric assay was performed to probe the effect of Adar on cell viability. Indeed, we found that Adar-knockdown cells showed a decrease in absorbance compared to the control (Additional file 1: Fig. S4a), suggesting that Adar may assist cell proliferation. DNA analog incorporation methods were then used to explore whether Adar modulates DNA synthesis. In this experiment, 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) was introduced during DNA synthesis. Likewise, Adar-knockdown cells also exhibited reduced EdU incorporation into DNA (Additional file 1: Fig. S4b), reflecting reduction in DNA replication. To explore whether the reduced replication during Adar knockdown was caused by R-loop accumulation, RNH1 was overexpressed in S2 cells. It was found that EdU incorporation remained essentially unchanged when comparing the control and Adar-knockdown cells when RNH1 was overexpressed (Additional file 1: Fig. S4b). Consequently, these results suggest a role of Adar in preventing R-loop-induced deficiency in DNA replication.

In this study, we have demonstrated that Drosophila Adar plays a crucial role in maintaining genome stability by regulating R-loop homeostasis. Our findings provide novel insights into the biological significance of Adar and underscore its importance in preserving genome integrity at the organismal level.

Aberrant accumulation of R-loops has been linked to genomic instability and various diseases, such as Aicardi-Goutières syndrome (AGS), where mutations in RNase H2 and SAMHD1 genes have been implicated [36, 44]. However, the lack of suitable animal models has impeded the investigation of the relationship between ADAR and R-loop-mediated genomic instability. Our study addresses this gap by using Drosophila as a model organism. We observed abnormal R-loop accumulation in both Adar-depleted S2 cells and the brains of Adar knockout flies. Genome-wide profiling of R-loops by ssDRIP-seq revealed a global increase in R-loop levels upon Adar depletion, particularly at promoters, gene bodies, and transcription termination regions. The high enrichment of R-loops in these transcriptionally active regions is likely to contribute to genomic instability, as evidenced by the increased DNA damage observed in Adar knockout flies. Notably, transgenic expression of RNH1, the Drosophila homolog of RNase H, not only reduced R-loop accumulation but also rescued the DNA damage phenotype, establishing a direct link between R-loop accumulation and genomic instability in the absence of Adar.

Our findings raise important questions about the mechanism by which Adar regulates R-loop homeostasis. Previous studies have identified two main classes of proteins involved in R-loop regulation: the RNase H family, which directly degrades the RNA moiety of R-loops, and RNA helicases, such as SETX and DHX9, which unwind R-loop structures [49]. The observed interaction between Adar and DHX9 suggests that Adar may influence R-loop homeostasis through its association with R-loop-resolving factors [50]. Additionally, Adar has been implicated in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and fragile X syndrome (FXS), where R-loop accumulation and genomic instability have been reported [51, 52]. The interactions between Adar and proteins involved in these disorders, such as TDP-43 and FMR [53, 54], further support the notion that Adar may regulate R-loop homeostasis through a complex network of protein interactions. Moreover, given the role of ADAR proteins in regulating gene expression [55], it is conceivable that ADAR depletion may lead to inefficient RNA processing and export, which could facilitate R-loop formation [24, 56].

Our study also sheds light on the distribution and potential consequences of R-loops in the Drosophila genome. Similar to the patterns observed in mammalian genomes [22, 57], we found that R-loops are widely distributed throughout the Drosophila genome, with enrichment at conserved genetic hotspots such as promoters and transcription terminators. The presence of R-loops in intronic regions of gene bodies is consistent with previous findings from single-molecule R-loop footprinting (SMRF-seq) [58]. Interestingly, we also observed a significant enrichment of R-loops in repetitive regions, including telomeric retrotransposons, in Adar knockout flies. The aberrant accumulation of R-loops in these regions led to increased transcriptional activity of telomeric retrotransposons, which may further contribute to genomic instability. These findings highlight the evolutionarily conserved nature of R-loop distribution patterns and their potential impact on genome stability.

The discovery that Adar maintains genome integrity independently of its A-to-I editing activity raises intriguing questions about the underlying mechanism. While our study does not provide a definitive answer, it opens up new avenues for future research. It is possible that Adar interacts with other proteins involved in R-loop regulation or that it influences R-loop homeostasis through its effects on gene expression and RNA processing[59]. Further studies are needed to elucidate the precise mechanism by which Adar regulates R-loop levels and maintains genome stability.

ConclusionsOur study establishes Drosophila as a valuable model system for investigating the role of Adar in maintaining genome stability through the regulation of R-loop homeostasis. The findings presented here provide a foundation for future research aimed at understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying Adar-mediated R-loop regulation and its implications for human diseases associated with genomic instability, such as AGS and neurodegenerative disorders. By shedding light on the evolutionarily conserved function of Adar in preserving genome integrity, our work highlights the importance of investigating the biological roles of ADAR proteins beyond their well-established function in A-to-I RNA editing.

Alkaline comet electrophoresisDrosophila tissue samples were dissected in cold PBS and incubated with collagenase to obtain single-cell preparations. Frosted or positively charged pretreated slides were immersed in boiling 1% agarose and carefully removed. The liquid on one side of each slide was wiped off so that the agarose solidified evenly on the other side. A solution of 1.5% low melting point agarose was boiled and kept warm at 37 °C. An 80-µl aliquot of each cell or tissue sample containing single cells was mixed 1:1 with the solution of low melting point agarose, transferred immediately to the bottom gel, and covered with a cover slip so that bottom gel was evenly covered with the mixture of cells and agarose while avoiding bubble formation. The low melting point agarose was allowed to solidify at 4 °C for 10 min. The cover slip was pried off with a blade, and the slides were placed in fresh, pre-cooled cell lysate (2.5 M NaCl, 0.1 M EDTA, 10 mM Tris–HCl, 1% Triton X-100), and the cells were lysed for 1–2 h. Genomic DNA was dispersed with alkaline electrophoresis solution (0.3 M NaOH, 1 mM EDTA) three times for 10 min each. The slides were carefully placed in a horizontal electrophoresis apparatus, followed by electrophoresis at 300 mA for 30 min at 4 °C. The slides were incubated for 5 min in neutralizing solution (0.4 M Tris–HCl, pH 7.5), washed twice with PBS and once with ddH2O, while carefully preventing the agarose from separating. The agarose was allowed to dry completely, followed by the spotting of 150 µl EB solution on each slide and covering each with a cover slip to distribute the EB solution evenly. The slides were sealed with nail polish and stored in the dark at 4 °C. The slides were examined by Zeiss Z2, with 100–200 comet images collected for each sample. Images were analyzed and statistical analyses performed using CASP comet analysis software.

CUT&Tag and analysis

Approximately 50 pairs of brain were dissected for each sample preparation. The CUT&Tag experiment was conducted using the NovoNGS® CUT&Tag 4.0 High-Sensitivity Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China, catalog number N259-YH01), following the manufacturer’s protocol and employing the γH2Av antibodies (UNC93-5.2.1, DSHB). Finally, Qubit was utilized to measure the concentration. Concentrations are determined using Qubit assays. The library fragments are then assessed with the Agilent Technologies 2100 Bioanalyzer and sequencing with Illumina.

CUT&Tag reads were aligned to the Drosophila genome with Bowtie2 version 2.3.5, allowing only uniquely mapping reads with up to two mismatches. The aligned reads were normalized to total reads aligned (reads per million). The track files were made with the bamCoverage command from deepTools 3.3.0. CUT&Tag peaks were called using MACS (model-based analysis of ChIP-seq) version 2.2.7.1 using default parameters and q value cutoff of 1 × 10−4. The distribution of CUT&Tag peaks was annotated with the R package ChIPseeker.

RNA sequencing and analysisTotal RNA was extracted using a TRIzol kit (Invitrogen, USA), followed by quality assessment through three methods: agarose gel electrophoresis, NanoDrop micro spectrophotometry, and Agilent 2100 analysis. RNA fragments were enriched and then processed with the Hieff NGS® Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit for reverse transcription into cDNA. Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co.’s Illumina was used to sequence the obtained cDNA library.Paired-end fastq reads were quality-profiled, adapter-trimmed, and filtered using fastp v0.22. The resulting output files were aligned to the dm6 genome using STAR v2.7.10a. Samtools v1.6 was then used to sort the aligned BAM files, fix the paired option, reorder the BAM file, and deduplicate the reads using the markup option. RNA editing sites were detected using REDiscover (https://github.com/lpryszcz/REDiscover) with the parameters set as -q 15 -Q 20, indicating that putative editing sites with a mapping quality of more than 15 and a base call quality of more than 20 were retained. After running the main function of REDiscover, duplicates and low-quality reads were automatically filtered. The reads were matched against the reference Drosophila melanogaster genome, and editing sites with more than one alternative allele and located at the same location of SNPs of fruit fly (downloaded from dbSNP) were excluded. Furthermore, sites with an average coverage of all samples over 50 were retained. For strand-specific data, “A > G + ” and “T > C − ” were considered A-to-I editing, and these editing sites were selected as Adar editing sites.
