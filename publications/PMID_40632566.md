Peptidoglycan (PG), a defining feature of the bacterial cell envelope, is
crucial for cell integrity and morphology. PG is a macromolecular mesh
consisting of glycan polymers crosslinked by short peptides encasing the
cytoplasmic membrane. PG peptides contain two to five amino acids of both L- and
D-configuration, with a conserved L-alanine residue at the first position in
most bacteria. We previously identified a β-lactam hypersensitive mutant of
Escherichia coli lacking yfiH (renamed pgeF) that shows incorporation of
L-serine or glycine instead of L-alanine. Here, we demonstrate that PgeF is an
editing enzyme that imparts fidelity to the biosynthesis of PG peptides across
bacterial genera. Using extensive mass spectrometry, we find accumulation of
misincorporated cytoplasmic PG precursors in the absence of pgeF. Detailed
biochemical analysis of several bacterial PgeF homologs reveals that PgeF
specifically cleaves serine or glycine but not alanine from the PG precursors.
Additionally, expression of heterologous ligases that incorporate L-serine or
glycine is lethal in the absence of pgeF, indicating that L-alanine at the first
position is crucial for wall integrity. Interestingly, PgeF is selectively
conserved in bacteria and vertebrates; however, we find that the PG editing
activity is exclusive to bacterial homologs. Furthermore, homologs from both
taxa were previously characterized as purine nucleoside phosphorylases (PNP).
Here, we find that they indeed have a weak PNP activity, but with no
physiological relevance in bacterial systems. Overall, our study demonstrates
the existence of a conserved proofreading pathway that is fundamental to the
integrity of the bacterial cell wall.

## Full Text

Bacteria inhabit diverse and extreme environments, largely enabled by the presence of a robust cell envelope of which the cell wall or peptidoglycan (PG) is an integral component (1). PG is a large mesh-like polymer that surrounds the cytoplasmic membrane and protects the cell from lysis due to turgor. Structurally, it is made up of several long glycan strands crosslinked by short peptide chains. The glycan strands consist of alternating N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) and N-acetylmuramic acid (MurNAc) sugars, with the peptides attached to the lactyl side chain of each MurNAc residue. The peptides are of two to five residues in length, comprising both L- and D-amino acids, with the most commonly found pentapeptide sequence being L-alanine1 (L-Ala)–D-glutamate2 (D-Glu)–mesodiaminopimelic acid (mDAP)3/L-lysine3 (L-Lys)–D-Ala4–D-Ala5. Peptides from the neighboring glycan strands are crosslinked to each other, either directly or through an additional peptide crossbridge, forming a strong yet flexible mesh around the cell. In Escherichia coli and many related bacteria, approximately 50% of the peptides are crosslinked to each other through D-Ala4–mDAP3 residues (2–4).

PG biosynthesis is a complex process that occurs in two different cellular compartments, with the formation of the disaccharide-pentapeptide monomers in the cytoplasm, and their polymerization in the periplasm (Fig. 1) (5–8). In the cytoplasm, the nucleotide-activated sugar UDP-GlcNAc is converted to UDP-MurNAc by MurA and MurB marking the first committed step of PG synthesis. Subsequently, the peptide stem is synthesized by the sequential addition of amino acids onto UDP-MurNAc, by dedicated aminoacyl ligases in a ribosome-independent manner. The first amino acid, L-Ala, is added onto UDP-MurNAc by MurC, followed by D-Glu by MurD, mDAP by MurE, and a D-Ala−D-Ala dimer by MurF. The resultant UDP-MurNAc-pentapeptide is then loaded onto a lipid carrier C55-P at the cytoplasmic membrane by MraY, where it receives a molecule of GlcNAc by MurG, and is transported across the membrane through the flippase MurJ. Subsequently, the disaccharide-pentapeptide unit is polymerized into the PG sacculus through linkage of disaccharides by glycosyltransferases, and crosslinking of peptides by transpeptidases (Fig. 1) (5–8).

Schematic of the peptidoglycan biosynthetic pathway. PG precursor synthesis begins in the cytoplasm with the conversion of UDP-GlcNAc to UDP-MurNAc, followed by the sequential addition of amino acids onto MurNAc. The UDP-MurNAc-pentapeptide is added onto a lipid carrier in the cytoplasmic membrane, where it receives GlcNAc, and the lipidated disaccharide pentapeptide (lipid-II) is flipped into the periplasm to be polymerized by the action of glycosyltransferases (GTase) and transpeptidases (TPase). D-cycloserine depletes D-ala−D-ala by inhibiting alanine racemase and D-Ala ligase. TPase activity is the target of β-lactam antibiotics.

L-Ala at position 1 is a highly conserved residue in most bacterial cell walls, with few rare genera containing L-serine (L-Ser) or glycine (Gly) instead; and no other variations are known to exist (3). In addition, within a given species, amino acid variation at the first position has not been observed so far. We previously identified a β-lactam sensitive mutant of E. coli lacking an ORF of unknown function yfiH, which interestingly showed L-Ser or Gly instead of L-Ala in the peptides of their PG sacculi. This phenotype allowed us to propose a role for YfiH in the maintenance of PG peptide composition, for which it was renamed PgeF (Peptidoglycan editing factor) (9). In addition, we observed a putative binding site for UDP-MurNAc in the crystal structure of PgeF and subsequently showed that it has activity on synthetic derivatives of UDP-MurNAc-L-Ala or UDP-MurNAc-L-Ser (10). Taken together, these studies suggested that PgeF functions to maintain PG peptide composition in E. coli (9, 10).

PgeF belongs to a multicopper polyphenol oxidoreductase/laccase superfamily with a well-conserved catalytic triad of His-Cys-His (11) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Interestingly, PgeF is uniquely conserved in bacteria and vertebrates, but absent in other opisthokonts, plants, and archaea (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The human homolog of PgeF, LACC1 is of significant interest as single nucleotide polymorphisms in the LACC1 gene (including C284R involving the putative catalytic cysteine), are strongly associated with various autoinflammatory disorders such as Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, and increased susceptibility to leprosy (12–14). Recently, LACC1 was shown to be a multifunctional enzyme of the purine salvage pathway, catalyzing several reactions including the phosphorolysis of purine nucleosides (15). Interestingly, the purine nucleoside phosphorylase (PNP) activity was also shown for two of the bacterial homologs, from E. coli and Geobacillus stearothermophilus (15), raising an important question regarding the physiological function of PgeF in bacteria. Meanwhile, an additional unrelated enzymatic activity has also been shown for human LACC1 in conversion of L-citrulline to L-ornithine (16), further confounding the understanding of the biochemical activity of PgeF homologs.

Here, we show that bacterial PgeF is a conserved proofreading enzyme that edits misincorporated L-Ser or Gly residues from the cytoplasmic precursors during PG synthesis, to preserve cell wall composition. Using extensive mass spectrometry, we find that the pgeF mutant accumulates precursors containing misincorporated L-Ser or Gly instead of L-Ala. Interestingly, expression of MurC orthologs from rare organisms that contain L-Ser or Gly at the first position in the PG is lethal in the E. coli pgeF mutant, emphasizing the need for an editing mechanism to maintain cell wall integrity. Further, we demonstrate that purified PgeF homologs from different Gram-negative and -positive bacterial species specifically cleave L-Ser or Gly-containing peptides from UDP-MurNAc. Moreover, we find that the PG editing activity is absent in vertebrate PgeF homologs despite the active site being well conserved. Importantly, we also examine the significance of the PNP activity of bacterial PgeF homologs and find that the PNP and PG editing activities are catalytically independent, with the PNP activity showing no physiological significance in E. coli at least under standard laboratory conditions. Overall, this study demonstrates that PgeF is an editing enzyme that maintains cell wall composition and integrity in bacteria.

DiscussionOur study demonstrates the existence of an editing pathway that functions to preserve the composition and thereby integrity of the PG sacculus across bacteria. We find that during PG precursor biogenesis, L-Ser or Gly residues get misincorporated onto UDP-MurNAc through promiscuous activity of the ligase MurC. PgeF functions to mitigate the misligation by specifically cleaving the L-Ser or Gly containing peptides from the UDP-MurNAc moieties, thereby allowing L-Ala containing precursors to proceed through the PG biosynthetic pathway. Substitution of L-Ala is poorly tolerated by the cells, with excess misligation being lethal, emphasizing the importance of conservation of L-Ala residue for the wall integrity. Furthermore, PgeF homologs from several bacterial species exhibit identical catalytic activity, suggesting a critical role for the editing process in the maintenance of L-Ala at the first position in the bacterial cell wall. Interestingly, PgeF is conserved in bacteria and vertebrates but not in other organisms, and a PNP activity has been shown previously for homologs from both the classes. Here, we find that the bacterial PgeF homologs indeed show a weak PNP activity; however, the catalytic mechanisms involved in PG editing and PNP activities are distinct and independent with the PNP activity seemingly having no physiological relevance in E. coli. Moreover, despite the demonstrated ability of E. coli MurC to incorporate L-Ser or Gly onto UDP-MurNAc (22–24), these residues have not been observed in the PG sacculi (4, 25). Our study shows that L-Ser or Gly misligation by MurC is a physiological process, and there exists a mechanism to actively eliminate these residues during PG biogenesis, which is governed by PgeF. We find noncanonical PG intermediates containing L-Ser or Gly in the cytoplasm of the pgeF mutant but not the WT cells, suggesting that proofreading by PgeF occurs in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3). In support of this, we find that PgeF is a soluble cytoplasmic protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), and not periplasmic as previously shown by us (9). Further, PgeF shows a clear discriminatory enzymatic activity, wherein it cleaves and eliminates the L-Ser or Gly-containing UDP-MurNAc derivatives but not the cognate L-Ala-containing derivatives from the PG precursor pool (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Moreover, the absence of any detectable misligation in WT cells, even in the presence of heterologous MurC orthologs capable of extreme misligation, demonstrates the efficacy of PgeF in editing the noncognate precursors (Fig. 4).PgeF function may be especially crucial in amino acid–rich environmental niches. In the absence of pgeF, even the marginal misligation observed in LB is lethal when PG recycling is blocked (9), suggesting that accumulation of misligated peptides is only transiently tolerated in the PG sacculus. Moreover, excess misligation severely impairs cell viability, demonstrating that the presence of L-Ala residue at position one is crucial for a robust cell wall (Fig. 4). Why its substitution by L-Ser or Gly has such a drastically adverse effect is not clear. It could be due to a limited availability of functional precursors resulting from the enzymes downstream of MurC having reduced affinity for the misincorporated substrates, as seen by the decrease in the relative amounts of the L-Ser/Gly containing UM-Di or -Tri compared to the UM-Mono (Fig. 3E). Alternatively, the conformational change induced by the substitution in the first residue may affect the efficiency of the PG synthases. Poor discrimination between L-Ala/L-Ser/Gly residues could be typical of alanine-adding enzymes—as it is also seen for alanine tRNA synthetase, which relies on additional editing factors to remove the mischarged L-Ser or Gly to avoid pathological consequences (37). Likewise, our study shows that error-prone MurC activity requires editing by PgeF, forming the basis for the maintenance of L-Ala in the PG sacculi of most bacteria. However, the mechanism of discrimination between the three residues is not clearly understood. The pgeF gene is conserved in most genera, with few exceptions. Intriguingly, pgeF is absent in bacterial species reported to contain L-Ser or Gly in the PG, including E. larvae and M. oryzae (SI Appendix, Fig. S12), indicating an evolutionary incompatibility between PgeF and a non-ala residue at the first position of the peptide. In this context, it is interesting to note that a few genera belonging to Lactobacillales lack a PgeF homolog although they have L-Ala in the first position in the PG sacculi.Is the PNP Activity of PgeF Physiological?This study unambiguously demonstrates the PG editing function of PgeF, which does not intuitively correlate with its previously reported activity as a PNP. Therefore, we examined whether PgeF has an additional moonlighting role and tested the significance of the PNP activity of PgeF using biochemical and genetic approaches (Fig. 6 and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). PgeF can indeed phosphorolyze inosine, guanosine, and possibly several other purine nucleosides; however, the following observations call into question the physiological relevance of the PNP activity in bacteria: i) PNP activity requires >500-fold higher protein concentration as compared to the precursor editing activity (Fig. 6), ii) PgeF fails to compensate for the absence of PNP activity in E. coli (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), with no phenotypes indicative of a role in purine metabolism being observed for pgeF so far, and, most importantly iii) the conserved catalytic triad of PgeF is dispensable for PNP activity (Fig. 7), leaving its mechanism unclear at this juncture. Whether the role of PgeF in purine metabolism is significant in other bacteria and/or under specific environmental conditions is an open question. It is likely that the PNP activity has evolved to play a significant physiological role in vertebrate systems (15). Adding to the enigma of the multiple biochemical activities of this family of proteins, LACC1 has also been shown to catalyze the conversion of L-citrulline to L-ornithine and isocyanic acid (16). How and why PgeF has evolved to perform multiple functions in vertebrates is an interesting question. Finally, as PG is a conserved bacterial signature for which the vertebrate immune system has evolved several recognition mechanisms (38, 39), including many that have originated from bacteria-to-vertebrate lateral transfer of genes encoding PG enzymes (40), there exists a possibility of LACC1 having a PG-associated role in vertebrates.

Role of PgeF as a Proofreading Amidase in PG Biosynthesis.

Although the PG peptide composition shows considerable variation across bacterial genera, L-Ala at the first position is among the most conserved residues. Moreover, despite the demonstrated ability of E. coli MurC to incorporate L-Ser or Gly onto UDP-MurNAc (22–24), these residues have not been observed in the PG sacculi (4, 25). Our study shows that L-Ser or Gly misligation by MurC is a physiological process, and there exists a mechanism to actively eliminate these residues during PG biogenesis, which is governed by PgeF. We find noncanonical PG intermediates containing L-Ser or Gly in the cytoplasm of the pgeF mutant but not the WT cells, suggesting that proofreading by PgeF occurs in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3). In support of this, we find that PgeF is a soluble cytoplasmic protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), and not periplasmic as previously shown by us (9). Further, PgeF shows a clear discriminatory enzymatic activity, wherein it cleaves and eliminates the L-Ser or Gly-containing UDP-MurNAc derivatives but not the cognate L-Ala-containing derivatives from the PG precursor pool (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Moreover, the absence of any detectable misligation in WT cells, even in the presence of heterologous MurC orthologs capable of extreme misligation, demonstrates the efficacy of PgeF in editing the noncognate precursors (Fig. 4).

PgeF function may be especially crucial in amino acid–rich environmental niches. In the absence of pgeF, even the marginal misligation observed in LB is lethal when PG recycling is blocked (9), suggesting that accumulation of misligated peptides is only transiently tolerated in the PG sacculus. Moreover, excess misligation severely impairs cell viability, demonstrating that the presence of L-Ala residue at position one is crucial for a robust cell wall (Fig. 4). Why its substitution by L-Ser or Gly has such a drastically adverse effect is not clear. It could be due to a limited availability of functional precursors resulting from the enzymes downstream of MurC having reduced affinity for the misincorporated substrates, as seen by the decrease in the relative amounts of the L-Ser/Gly containing UM-Di or -Tri compared to the UM-Mono (Fig. 3E). Alternatively, the conformational change induced by the substitution in the first residue may affect the efficiency of the PG synthases. Interestingly, the misligation leading to filamentation and bulging at the midcell indicates a larger effect on cell division. As the cell septum is a multilayered wall, the altered peptide conformation may be particularly detrimental to the local architecture of the septal PG (Fig. 4C).

Meanwhile, we cannot rule out the possibility of L-Ser/Gly misincorporation offering a fitness advantage under certain environmental conditions. Excess intracellular L-Ser is known to be toxic as it leads to inhibition of isoleucine and threonine biosynthetic pathways (33), errors in translation due to mischarging on noncognate aminoacyl tRNAs (34), and perturbation of one-carbon metabolism (35, 36). Therefore, temporarily channeling L-Ser into the PG pathway may sequester it away to reduce toxicity on other metabolic processes. However, whether PgeF expression or activity is regulated under specific conditions remains to be seen.

Is the PNP Activity of PgeF Physiological?This study unambiguously demonstrates the PG editing function of PgeF, which does not intuitively correlate with its previously reported activity as a PNP. Therefore, we examined whether PgeF has an additional moonlighting role and tested the significance of the PNP activity of PgeF using biochemical and genetic approaches (Fig. 6 and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). PgeF can indeed phosphorolyze inosine, guanosine, and possibly several other purine nucleosides; however, the following observations call into question the physiological relevance of the PNP activity in bacteria: i) PNP activity requires >500-fold higher protein concentration as compared to the precursor editing activity (Fig. 6), ii) PgeF fails to compensate for the absence of PNP activity in E. coli (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), with no phenotypes indicative of a role in purine metabolism being observed for pgeF so far, and, most importantly iii) the conserved catalytic triad of PgeF is dispensable for PNP activity (Fig. 7), leaving its mechanism unclear at this juncture. Whether the role of PgeF in purine metabolism is significant in other bacteria and/or under specific environmental conditions is an open question. It is likely that the PNP activity has evolved to play a significant physiological role in vertebrate systems (15). Adding to the enigma of the multiple biochemical activities of this family of proteins, LACC1 has also been shown to catalyze the conversion of L-citrulline to L-ornithine and isocyanic acid (16). How and why PgeF has evolved to perform multiple functions in vertebrates is an interesting question. Finally, as PG is a conserved bacterial signature for which the vertebrate immune system has evolved several recognition mechanisms (38, 39), including many that have originated from bacteria-to-vertebrate lateral transfer of genes encoding PG enzymes (40), there exists a possibility of LACC1 having a PG-associated role in vertebrates.
