## Full Text

Abstract

Flowering at the right time is crucial to ensure successful plant reproduction and seed yield and is dependent on both environmental and endogenous parameters. Among the different pathways that impinge on flowering, the autonomous pathway promotes floral transition independently of day length through the repression of the central flowering repressor FLOWERING LOCUS C ( FLC ). FLC blocks floral transition by repressing flowering time integrators such as FLOWERING LOCUS T ( FT ). MSI4/FVE is a key regulator of the autonomous pathway that reduces FLC expression. Here we report that the MSI4 protein is a DDB1 and CUL4-associated factor that represses FLC expression through its association with a CLF-Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) in Arabidopsis . Thus, the lack of MSI4 or decreased CUL4 activity reduces H3K27 trimethylation on FLC, but also on its downstream target FT, resulting in increased expression of both genes. Moreover, CUL4 interacts with FLC chromatin in an MSI4-dependant manner, and the interaction between MSI4 and CUL4–DDB1 is necessary for the epigenetic repression of FLC . Overall our work provides evidence for a unique functional interaction between the cullin-RING ubiquitin ligase (CUL4–DDB1 MSI4 ) and a CLF–PRC2 complex in the regulation of flowering timing in Arabidopsis .

In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana , floral transition is regulated by changes in day length and exposure of plants to long periods of low temperature by two well-defined genetic pathways, called photoperiod and vernalization, respectively (reviewed in refs. 1 – 3 ). In addition to environmentally controlled pathways, the gibberellic acid-dependent and the autonomous pathways are controlled by internal cues, ensuring flowering independently of external signals. However, input signals that might control the activities of these pathways are currently unknown. These different pathways converge to regulate several target genes at the transcriptional level, resulting in a complex network of flowering-time regulation. Among target genes, FLOWERING LOCUS C ( FLC ) encodes a MADS-box transcription factor, which quantitatively blocks the floral transition by repressing the expression of the floral integrator genes SUPPRESSOR OF OVEREXPRESSION OF CONSTANS 1/AGAMOUS-LIKE 20 ( SOC1/AGL20 ) and FLOWERING LOCUS T ( FT ). FLC transcriptional regulation is at the convergence of the vernalization pathway that represses FLC after exposure to low temperatures and the autonomous pathway, which constitutively represses FLC ( 2 , 3 ). In turn, FLC repression results in floral integrator (i.e., FT and SOC1 / AGL20 ) activation, leading to the induction of expression of floral-primordium-identity genes such as APETALA 1 ( AP1 ) and LEAFY ( LFY ) and floral-organ-identity genes such as AGAMOUS ( AG ) and AP3 , promoting the transition from a vegetative to an inflorescence meristem ( 1 – 3 ).

Mutants in the autonomous pathway flower very late when grown under either long-day (LD) or short-day (SD) photoperiods ( 4 ). Cloning of the autonomous pathway gene FVE ( 5 , 6 ) revealed a WD40 protein, hereafter called MSI4, which belongs to an evolutionarily conserved protein family ( 7 ) whose founding member is MULTI-COPY suppressor of IRA1 (MSI1) from yeast ( 8 ). In both metazoans and plants, MSI-like proteins are part of protein complexes that are involved in chromatin functions ( 7 ). MSI4 promotes flowering through the autonomous pathway by repressing FLC expression, probably as part of a multiprotein complex including a histone deacetylase activity ( 5 ).

Results and Discussion

We noticed that the MSI4 protein sequence contains a conserved WDxR motif ( Fig. S1 A ), a typical feature of the recently identified WD40-containing DDB1 and CUL4-associated factors (DCAFs) ( 9 , 10 ). DCAFs are thought to act as substrate receptors of CUL4-based E3 ubiquitin ligases. The Arabidopsis genome encodes two DDB1-related proteins, DDB1A and DDB1B. By a yeast two-hybrid assay, we found that MSI4 interacts with both Arabidopsis DDB1-related proteins ( Fig. 1 A ). To provide further evidence for a physical interaction between MSI4 and DDB1A/B proteins in plant cells, we carried out bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) experiments. Plasmids YN-MSI4 and YC-DDB1A were cobombarded into etiolated mustard hypocotyls. A strong YFP signal was observed in the nucleus of 77% of examined cells ( Fig. 1 B ). Next we tested whether both proteins can be pulled down from plant protein extracts. No antibody specific for MSI4 is currently available. Thus, we generated transgenic Arabidopsis plants expressing constitutively the MSI4 protein fused to a C-terminal Flag epitope ( Fig. S1 ). In agreements with our BiFC assays, immunoprecipitation of Arabidopsis DDB1A/B coprecipitated MSI4 ( Fig. 1 C ).

Fig. 1.

Next we tested whether the WDxR motif in MSI4 is required for DDB1A/B binding. Because the arginine (R) residue in the WDxR motif is critical for interaction of DCAFs with DDB1 ( 11 ), we replaced this residue in MSI4 by a histidine (H) residue (MSI4 WDxH ; Fig. S1 A ). Although the WDxH mutation did not eliminate the interaction between MSI4 and DDB1A or DDB1B in yeast, the interaction was strongly reduced, especially with DDB1B ( Fig. S1 B ). Therefore, we tested whether this motif is required for MSI4 function in the control of flowering time. Loss of function of MSI4 , like other autonomous pathway mutations, results in late flowering in all photoperiods due to elevated FLC expression ( 5 ). Although constitutive expression of Flag-tagged native MSI4 suppressed the msi4 late-flowering phenotype and even led to slight earlier flowering of some transgenic lines, this was not observed with the mutated MSI4 WDxH form of the protein, despite the selection of transgenic lines exhibiting similar levels of protein expression ( Fig. S1 C–E ). Collectively, these results support the existence of a physical interaction between DDB1A/B and MSI4 and demonstrate the requirement of the WDxR motif for MSI4 function in the regulation of the flowering time in Arabidopsis .

It was recently reported that under SD photoperiod an Arabidopsis cul4 cosuppression line flowers earlier than wild type ( 12 ). Hence, CUL4–DDB1 may act in the photoperiod pathway by interacting with the SPA–COP1 complex. Because COP1 controls CONSTANS (CO) protein degradation mainly in the dark ( 13 , 14 ), the cul4 mutation might affect CO protein content, which in turn would lead to a higher accumulation of FT transcript levels ( 12 ) and thus to early flowering. Intriguingly, MSI4 acts in the autonomous pathway, and its mutation leads to a late flowering phenotype associated with increased level of FLC mRNAs ( 5 ). Therefore, we examined the flowering phenotype of cul4 and ddb1a/b mutants under both LD ( Fig. 2 ) and SD conditions ( Fig. S2 ). We used the previously identified cul4-1 mutant and CUL4 antisense (hereafter called AScul4 ) lines, which both exhibit a strong reduction of CUL4 protein content, although the AScul4 line produces the strongest phenotype in adult plants ( 15 ). For Arabidopsis DDB1A/B, we analyzed ddb1a-2 ( 16 ) and ddb1b-1 single mutants (SALK 061944). Whereas ddb1b flowered similarly to wild-type plants, AScul4 and to a lesser extent cul4-1 and ddb1a-2 plants flowered earlier than Col0 both under LD ( Fig. 2 A and B ) and SD ( Fig. S2 ) photoperiods. A lower expression level of DDB1B with respect to DDB1A could explain the absence of a flowering phenotype for the ddb1b mutation, because functional redundancy between the genes is expected.

Fig. 2.

Strikingly, despite its early-flowering phenotype, cul4-1 enhanced the late flowering phenotype of msi4 when both mutations were combined ( Fig. 2 A and B and Fig. S2 ). To better understand the molecular basis of this phenotype, we monitored the expression levels of different genes involved in flowering time regulation ( Fig. 2 C ). The higher expression level of FLC in the msi4 mutant is consistent with the proposed role of MSI4 as a repressor of FLC in the autonomous pathway ( 5 ). Accordingly, in the msi4 mutant, the transcript levels of FT and SOC1 were maintained low as FLC represses both genes. Interestingly, in cul4 mutants, the transcript level of FLC was also higher than in wild-type plants, although not as much as in msi4 , and this despite the early flowering phenotype of these mutants ( Fig. 2 B and C ). Moreover, CUL4 knockdown enhanced both FLC transcript accumulation and flowering time in an msi4 mutant background. Thus, decreased CUL4 function results in the activation of both positive ( FT and SOC1 ) and negative ( FLC ) regulators of flowering. On the basis of our data, we conclude that the CUL4–DDB1 complex not only acts in the photoperiod pathway via COP1 to repress FT expression ( 12 , 13 ), but also plays a role in the autonomous pathway to repress FLC and FT , most likely via MSI4 and eventually other MSI-like proteins.

Previous studies reported that, in winter annuals, FLC is repressed during vernalization through the deposition of the repressive H3K27me3 mark by a PRC2-like complex containing the histone methyltransferase CLF ( 17 ). Similarly, in the rapid-cycling Arabidopsis Col0 ecotype, it was recently suggested that a putative CLF–PRC2 complex, acting in concert with the autonomous pathway, deposits the repressive H3K27me3 mark on both FLC and FT chromatin to repress their expression ( 18 ). Thus, CLF mutation leads to early flowering despite elevated FLC expression because FT expression is also affected (ref. 18 and Fig. S3 ), a phenotype reminiscent of the cul4 mutants. CLF acts redundantly with a closely related gene, SWINGER ( SWN ) in the regulation of flowering time ( 19 ). Interestingly, when combining cul4 and clf mutations, the transcript levels of both FLC and FT were synergistically increased ( Fig. S3 C ), as expected from the combination of two weak alleles acting in the same pathway ( 20 ). This suggests that CUL4 and CLF (most likely as part of a PRC2 complex) act together on these loci. It is noteworthy that clf-29 cul4-1 double mutants show a later flowering than the clf-29 single mutants despite higher FT expression, suggesting the implication of other flowering genes in this regulation. Hence, at least MADS AFFECTING FLOWERING 5 ( MAF5 ), a gene closely related to FLC , was also up-regulated in the cul4 mutant background ( Fig. S3 D ).

Although MSI-like proteins are part of PRC2 complexes in mammals, Drosophila , and Arabidopsis ( 7 ), the presence and/or identity of the MSI protein involved in PRC2-dependant FLC and FT repression is currently unknown. Thus, we examined by ChIP the H3K27me3 level on both FLC and FT chromatin in cul4 and msi4 mutant plants still at the vegetative stage ( Fig. 2 D and Fig. S4 A ). Strikingly, the msi4 mutant exhibited an almost complete loss of repressive H3K27me3 all over the FLC locus, whereas we observed a higher level of histone H3 acetylation at this locus, as previously reported ( 5 ). For both cul4 mutant lines we also observed a significant decrease in the deposition of the repressive H3K27me3 mark on FLC and to a lesser extent on FT chromatin, which is consistent with the higher expression level observed for both genes. Conversely, we also noticed a slight enrichment of histone H3 acetylation marks on both FLC and FT chromatin ( Fig. 2 D ). It is noteworthy that CUL4 knockdown has no global effect on H3Ac or H3K27me3 levels in Arabidopsis ( Fig. S4 B ). Taken together, these results are supportive of the appealing hypothesis that MSI4 acts at the level of a repressive PRC2-like complex in addition to playing a role in a histone deacetylation mechanism as previously suggested ( 5 ). Moreover, Arabidopsis CUL4–DDB1A/B is also required for the establishment and/or maintenance of PRC2-mediated repression of FLC and possibly FT .

As a next step, we decided to uncouple the effect of the photoperiod pathway mediated by CO protein from the FT repression by the CLF–PRC2 complexes. CLF mutation leads to early flowering in LD photoperiod with both higher FLC and FT transcript levels (ref. 18 and Fig. S5 ), whereas co-10 flowered very late, as previously reported. Interestingly, the clf-29 co-10 double mutant entered flowering only slightly later than the clf-29 single mutant ( Fig. S5 A and B ) and showed a clear up-regulation of FT in the absence of CO under LD conditions ( Fig. S5 C ). Thus, PRC2-dependant repression of FT , at least in LD photoperiod conditions, plays a predominant role in the control of flowering time, most likely to avoid premature and unscheduled FT expression. Consistent with our observation that CUL4 is necessary to maintain FT and FLC epigenetic repression, the cul4 co-10 double mutant also flowered at a time intermediate between both single mutants and, in contrast to co-10 , the double mutant clearly exhibited FT mRNA accumulation ( Fig. S5 C ). Therefore, CUL4 controls FT expression, possibly by negatively regulating CO protein abundance ( 12 ), but it is also necessary to maintain FT and FLC epigenetic repression.

Because CUL4–DDB1 physically interacts with MSI4 and because both CUL4 and MSI4 are required to maintain the repressive H3K27me3 mark on FLC and FT chromatin, we examined whether the CUL4–DDB1 MSI4 complex physically associates with CLF and FERTILIZATION INDEPENDENT ENDOSPERM (FIE), two subunits of the CLF–PRC2-like complex previously identified as involved in FLC repression in concert with autonomous-pathway repressors ( 18 ). Although MSI1 has been shown to interact with FIE ( 21 ), no interaction between MSI4 and FIE was detected in yeast. However, we detected an interaction between MSI4 and CLF in the yeast two-hybrid assay ( Fig. S6 A ), which we further confirmed in planta using BiFC assays ( Fig. S6 B ). Next we tested whether both CUL4–DDB1 MSI4 and CLF–PRC2 complexes coimmunoprecipitate in plant extracts. To perform these assays we produced functional 35S:CLF-Flag and 35S:GFP-MSI4 transgenic lines ( Fig. S7 ). Coimmunoprecipitation assays revealed that CLF coprecipitated with DDB1A/B from Arabidopsis extracts ( Fig. 3 A ). Consistently, we also found that GFP-MSI4 as well as CUL4 coprecipitated with Flag-tagged CLF from plant extracts coexpressing both CLF-Flag and GFP-MSI4 proteins ( Fig. 3 B ). Together, our results support the existence of a physical interaction between CUL4–DDB1 MSI4 and a CLF–PRC2 complex in Arabidopsis .

Fig. 3.

On the basis of these findings, we tested whether such a protein complex directly associates with FLC and FT chromatin. By ChIP experiments we first examined the MSI4 interaction with these loci. Thus, DNA precipitated with the GFP antibody from 35S:GFP-MSI4 transgenic plant extracts was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR ( Fig. 3 C ). We found that all three regions examined in FLC , but also two regions in FT , were significantly enriched in these assays. This finding is in agreement with the binding of CLF at the same loci ( 18 ) and supports a model in which a PRC2-like complex containing CLF–MSI4 represses the floral genes FLC and FT . Interestingly, MSI4 binding at the FLC locus requires CUL4 activity ( Fig. 3 C ). Finally, we investigated whether CUL4 also binds FLC and FT chromatin. ChIP assays using the anti-CUL4 antibody revealed a clear enrichment at both loci ( Fig. 3 D ). Moreover, CUL4 binding to both FLC and FT chromatin was abolished in an msi4 mutant background, indicating that CUL4–DDB1 is recruited on FLC and FT chromatin in a process dependant on MSI4.

In mammalian cells, it is well established that the polycomb group repressive complex 1 (PRC1) contains E3 ubiquitin ligase activity ( 22 ). Hence, one of its component Ring1B is a RING finger E3 ubiquitin ligase that monoubiquitylates histone H2A ( 23 ). Histone H2A monoubiquitylation represses transcription by inhibiting RNA polymerase II elongation ( 24 , 25 ). Arabidopsis also contains RING domain proteins that, together with LHP1, may fulfill PRC1-like functions ( 26 ), most likely via histone H2A ubiquitylation ( 27 ). Here we provide genetic and functional evidence for an interaction between a cullin-RING ubiquitin ligase (CUL4–DDB1 MSI4 ) and a PRC2 complex.

On the basis of our data, we propose a model in which CUL4 is involved at two levels in the control of flowering time ( Fig. 4 ). First, CUL4–DDB1, through its association with the COP1–SPA complexes, may trigger the turnover of the CO protein ( 12 ). Second, CUL4 as part of the CUL4–DDB1 MSI4 complex is required to repress FLC and possibly FT gene expression. This could be achieved by a direct regulation of CLF–PRC2 activity by the CUL4–DDB1 MSI4 E3 ubiquitin ligase. Such a scenario is supported by the facts that both protein complexes physically interact in Arabidopsis and that both bind to FLC and FT loci (ref. 18 and this work). Nevertheless, we do not favor the hypothesis that CUL4–DDB1 MSI4 regulates the stability of components of the PRC2 such as CLF, because in this case CUL4 knockdown would be expected to increase CLF–PRC2 activity, which is the opposite to what we have observed. Thus, CUL4–DDB1 MSI4 may rather positively regulate the CLF–PRC2 complex or modify chromatin proteins whose ubiquitylation is required for CLF–PRC2 function. Interestingly, recent work has revealed that CUL4–DDB1 DDB2 monoubiquitylates histone H2A ( 28 ) and histones H3 and H4 ( 29 ) in response to DNA damage to facilitate recruitment of the DNA repair machinery. In addition, CUL4 has been shown to interact with methyltransferases to influence the methylation status of histones ( 30 , 31 ).

Fig. 4.

Components of the autonomous pathway have been shown to act at the levels of RNA processing and chromatin modification (reviewed in refs. 32 – 34 ). Thus, FCA and FPA, two proteins with RRM-type RNA-binding domains, control 3′-end formation and alternative polyadenylation of antisense RNAs at the FLC locus ( 35 , 36 ). FCA regulation of FLC requires FLD, a histone demethylase, linking RNA processing to chromatin changes ( 37 ). FCA also interacts with the mRNA 3′-end processing factor FY, and this interaction is required for FLC down-regulation ( 38 ). Intriguingly, FY, like MSI4, belongs to the DCAF protein family and was previously shown to interact with DDB1 in yeast two-hybrid and coimmunoprecipitation assays ( 39 ). Future work should reveal whether the chromatin functions of CUL4–DDB1 MSI4 at the FLC locus also require RNA-based pathways.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material.

All Arabidopsis plants used in this study were in the Columbia Col-0 background. The cul4-1 (At5g46210) GABI T-DNA insertion and AS cul4 antisense hypomorphic mutants ( 15 ), the ddb1a-1 (At4g05420, SALK 055584) and ddb1b (At4g21100, SALK 061944) T-DNA insertion mutants ( 16 ), and the clf-29 (AT2G23380, SALK N521003) ( 26 ) and co-10 (At5g15840) SAIL T-DNA insertion mutants ( 13 ) have been previously described. The msi4 (At2g19520) T-DNA insertion mutant (SAIL 1167-E05) was obtained from the Nottingham Arabidopsis Seed Stock Center (NASC). Double cul4-msi4 , cul4-clf , cul4-co , clf-co , and msi4-co mutants were created by crossing the indicated single mutants. Homozygous double mutants were selected by PCR genotyping, and their F3 progenies were used to analyze flowering time and in quantitative real-time PCR and ChIP experiments. MSI4 and CLF cDNAs were cloned in Gateway-based pMDC43 (GFP in Nt), pGWB12 (Flag in Nt), and pGWB11 (Flag in Ct) vectors under control of the CaMV 35S promoter. Wild-type plants as well as simple and double mutants were agro-transformed with these vectors and analyzed.

Analysis of Flowering Time.

Plants were grown on soil directly or on selective Murashige and Skoog medium (transgenic lines) and then transferred on soil in controlled-environment growth chambers under LDs (16 h light/8 h dark) or SDs (8 h light/16 h dark). Flowering time was measured by scoring the number of days and the number of rosette leaves at bolting time. Means and SDs were calculated on a minimum of 24 individuals for each genotype. The experiment was repeated three times, and the results of one experiment have been presented.

Yeast Two-Hybrid Test.

The DDB1A and DDB1B cDNAs were cloned as fusions to the GAL4 binding domain, in the Gateway-compatible pGBT9 vector (Ghent plasmids collection, http://bccm.belspo.be/index.php ), and the MSI4 cDNA was fused to the GAL4 activation domain in the pGADT7 vector (Clontech). The cDNA of CLF in pGBKT7 (Clontech) was fused to the GAL4 binding domain. The yeast strain AH109 (Clontech) was double-transformed with the appropriate combinations of bait and prey vectors. Transformants were selected on synthetic (SD)/-Leu/-Trp (-LW) medium for 2 d at 30 °C. Weak and strong interactions were tested by transferring transformants on SD/-Leu/-Trp/-His (-LWH) and SD/-Leu/-Trp/-Ade/-His (-LWAH) media, respectively, allowing growth for 4 d at 30 °C.

Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation.

The DDB1A , DDB1B , MSI4 , and CLF cDNAs were cloned into the split YFP destination vectors by recombination (Invitrogen) to obtain YC-DDB1A, YC-DDB1B, YC-MSI4, YN-MSI4, and YN-CLF constructs. YN-, YC-, and 35S:CPRF2–CFP are described elsewhere ( 40 ). Empty vectors bearing YN or YC were used as negative controls. The different constructs were transformed into 3-d-old, dark-grown mustard seedlings by particle bombardment ( 40 ). Images were recorded 20 h after bombardment with a Nikon fluorescent stereomicroscope E800 equipped with 40× water immersion optics and using CFP- and YFP-specific filters.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR Analyses.

RNA was extracted from 17-d-old Arabidopsis using a Tri-Reagent (TRIzol; Molecular Research Center) method. Total RNA was precipitated with 1 volume isopropanol. After treating with RNase-free DNase (Qiagen kit), cDNA was synthesized from 2 μg total RNA, using the Applied Biosystems reverse transcription kit. Quantitative real-time PCR reactions (10 μL) were performed in 384-well optical plates on a BioRad i-cycler apparatus using PCR master 2× mix (Roche) containing 480 SYBER Green I fluorescein reporter with gene-specific primers ( Table S1 ). Quantification was performed in triplicate. ACTIN2 was used as an internal standard for equalization of RNA levels. GAPDH was tested as a second standard gene to validate the ACTIN2 gene. Quantifications were then normalized to WT Col plantlets. Error bars represent the SD from three replicates. Experiments were repeated three times in a biologically independent manner.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation.

ChIP experiments were performed as previously described ( 18 ) using 17-d-old plants. Five milliliters of mixed tissue powder was prepared after cross-linking DNA with proteins by formaldehyde. Preparation of chromatin, sonication, and immunoprecipitation using the rabbit polyclonal anti-trimethyl H3K27 (07-449, Millipore) or anti-acetyl H3 (06-599, Millipore) were carried out using Millipore Magna ChIP protein A kit (16-661, Millipore) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The immunoprecipitated DNA was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR. FUSCA and Tub2 primers were used as internal standard for normalization. FUSCA was used for normalization because neither GFP-MSI4 nor CUL4 were found enriched at this locus ( Fig. S8 ). Data analysis was done as previously described ( 41 ). Experiments were performed three times using independent biological samples.

Immunoprecipitation Experiment.

Total soluble proteins were extracted from msi4 35S:MSI4-Flag transgenic plants using buffer A [100 mM NaHPO 4 (pH 8.0), 1% Triton X-100, and protease inhibitor mix (Complete; Roche Molecular Biochemical)]. The DDB1–MSI4 immunoprecipitation assay was performed using anti-DDB1A/B polyclonal antibody coupled to ProteinA-Sepharose beads. The Flag-tagged MSI4 protein was detected using anti-Flag monoclonal antibody (F9291 Sigma). The MSI4-CLF coimmmunoprecipitation assay was performed using the progeny of crossed clf 35S: CLF -Flag with msi4 35S:GFP- MSI4 transgenic plants. Anti-Flag monoclonal coupled to ProteinA-Sepharose beads and anti-GFP polyclonal antibodies were used in these experiments.

Protein Extraction and Immunoblotting.

Total proteins were extracted from pools of 2-wk-old plantlets using denaturating buffer previously described ( 42 ). Total protein extracts were separated on SDS/PAGE gels and blotted onto Immobilon-P membrane (Millipore). The MSI4-Flag and CLF-Flag proteins were detected by using the anti-Flag monoclonal antibody (F9291, Sigma). The GFP-MSI4 and GFP-CLF proteins were detected by anti-GFP-HPR (130-091-833, Miltenyi Biotec). The anti-DDB1A/B and anti-CULLIN4 polyclonal antibodies were used to detect the respective proteins.
