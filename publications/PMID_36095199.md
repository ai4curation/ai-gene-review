Muscle cell fusion is a multistep process where the final step of the reaction
drives progression beyond early hemifusion events to complete fusion. This step
requires activity of the muscle-specific fusogen Myomerger, a single-pass
transmembrane protein containing 84 amino acids with an ectodomain that includes
two α-helices. Previous studies have demonstrated that Myomerger acts by
destabilizing membranes through generation of elastic stresses in the outer
leaflet of the plasma membrane. An obvious question is how such destabilizing
activity might be regulated to avoid membrane and cellular damage, and how the
two juxtaposed helices cooperate in fusion. Using cellular fusion assays and
in vitro liposome assays, we report that the two helices possess unique
characteristics, both of which are needed for full activity of the protein. We
demonstrate that externalized phosphatidylserine (PS), a lipid previously
implicated in myoblast fusion, has a determinant role in the regulation of
Myomerger activity. The membrane-proximal, amphipathic Helix-1 is normally
disordered and its α-helical structure is induced by PS, making membrane
interactions more efficacious. The distal, more hydrophobic Helix-2 is
intrinsically ordered, possesses an ability to insert into membranes, and
augments the membrane-stressing effects of Helix-1. These data reveal that
Myomerger fusogenic activity is an exquisitely orchestrated event involving its
two ectodomain helices, which are controlled by membrane lipid composition,
providing an explanation as to how its membrane-stressing activity is spatially
and temporally regulated during the final step of myoblast fusion.

## Full Text

Skeletal muscle requires cell fusion events for development, regeneration, and adaptation after exercise (1, 2). Fusion of muscle progenitor cells is controlled by numerous proteins and lipids that help prepare the cells to fuse (3–5). Once ready to fuse, the membrane-remodeling component of the myoblast fusion reaction is driven by the muscle-specific proteins Myomaker and Myomerger (6–9). These transmembrane proteins function to independently modify membranes to drive fusion, where Myomaker functions at or before the outer leaflets of the two membranes mix (hemifusion) and Myomerger controls pore formation and thus fusion completion (10). While Myomaker and Myomerger represent a unique bipartite fusion pathway, it is unclear how the activities of these proteins are regulated to precisely control fusion. This is an especially important question as Myomerger possesses an activity to destabilize membranes, which in theory would need to be regulated to restrict function to the proper site and minimize effects on lipids throughout the cell membrane.

Myomerger is a single-pass transmembrane protein with 84 amino acids with an extracellular C terminus (ectodomain). This primary structure is divergent from most fusogens that have long extracellular domains (11). Myomerger is similar to fusion-associated small-transmembrane (FAST) proteins, which are encoded by a family of nonenveloped reoviruses that induce syncytium formation through membrane effector properties of the FAST proteins (12). Myomerger contains two regions within its ectodomain that are predicted to harbor α-helical character (Helix-1 and Helix-2). Theoretical modeling and functional assays showed that the Myomerger membrane-stressing activity encompasses generation of positive membrane curvature (13).

Throughout biology, proteins insert in membranes and impact curvature of the membrane to control cellular processes such as endocytosis and exocytosis, cell division, and cell fusion (14, 15). Since membranes do not spontaneously fuse, specialized proteins referred to as “fusogens” act to lower the energy barrier for the required lipid rearrangements (15). Different classes of fusogens utilize either short peptide regions or longer peptide loops for membrane insertion, along with conformational changes within the protein to prime membrane bilayers for fusion and to bring them in close proximity (15, 16). Protein regions that drive fusion are diverse, including those known for viral fusogens (fusion peptides, fusion loops, and the membrane-proximal stem region) (16, 17). Most fusogens require the ability to induce negative membrane curvature for hemifusion and positive membrane curvature for pore formation. In contrast, Myomerger functions only at the pore formation stage, acting locally after outer-leaflet lipid mixing (hemifusion) connections have been formed (10). The ectodomain of Myomerger generates positive membrane curvature in the outer leaflets of the plasma membrane but how this is achieved in a spatial and temporal manner and which regions of Myomerger are directly fusion-activating are not understood.

A potential problem with proteins that generate membrane curvature and destabilize membranes is that they can disrupt membrane homeostasis. Indeed, some antimicrobial peptides can work in this manner to kill bacteria (18). For useful membrane destabilizations leading to fusion, the system would need to be controlled to allow positive membrane curvature at the right time and place. Given that Myomerger functions to stress membranes leading to fusion, regulation of this activity should be needed to avoid ectopic membrane disruptions. Activities of fusogens are often regulated by pH, cofactors such as Ca2+, oligomerization, protein cleavage, and lipid moieties present in the plasma membrane (11). It is unclear if some or all of these mechanisms regulate Myomerger but phosphatidylserine (PS) has a well-documented role in myoblast fusion and various other fusion systems (19, 20). PS on the outer leaflet is important for early stages of myoblast fusion through activation of PS receptors, Bai proteins (21, 22), and Stabilin-2 (23), leading to activation of the actin cytoskeleton. Moreover, there is an interaction between PS, transiently exposed at the surface of differentiating myoblasts through the activity of TMEM16 scramblases (24), and annexins during myoblast fusion (25). While PS has been implicated in the membrane-remodeling stages of the fusion process for virus–cell and osteoclast fusions (26–28) and SARS-CoV-2 spike-induced syncytium formation (29), the exact role for PS in myoblast fusion is not fully understood.

In this report, we show that Myomerger structure and function are controlled by the lipid composition of the outer leaflet of plasma membranes. The data indicate that PS acts as a trigger for Myomerger activity by allowing ordering of the amphipathic Helix-1, which is the main driver of the fusion process through its insertion into lipid head groups. Helix-1 is obligatory for Myomerger activity, and Helix-2 helps amplify the effects of Helix-1 activity. These data reveal a mechanism by which myoblast fusion is controlled by regulating the ability of Myomerger to generate positive curvature stresses that drive fusion pore formation.

Thus, movement of PS to the extracellular leaflet, a phenomenon proposed to regulate myoblast fusion, could be a primary determinant for Myomerger activity. To test if the presence of PS specifically modulates Myomerger activity, we manipulated external PS in both cellular and biochemical assays.High positive curvature of lipid monolayers lowers the edge energy of the pores in membranes and, thus, facilitates their formation (34). The ability of Myomerger to generate positive curvature and promote pore formation has been linked to an elevated sensitivity of myoblast plasma membranes to hypotonic osmotic shock (10), which increases tension in the membrane. When this tension reaches a critical threshold, the continuity of the membrane lipid bilayer is disrupted by opening of the pore(s) allowing probes that do not normally cross the bilayer to enter the cells. Proteins and lipids that facilitate pore formation by generating membrane stresses lower critical tension and promote membrane permeabilization. Indeed, osmotic shock resulted in permeabilized membranes in WT myoblasts but Myomerger-deficient myoblasts were largely refractory to permeabilization (10). To explore whether PS externalization influences Myomerger interactions with the plasma membrane, we utilized the osmotic shock assay in Myomerger−/− myoblasts. The cells were differentiated for 3 days and incubated with WT ectodomain in the presence or absence of compounds that alter PS externalization, and then the cells were placed into water to induce osmotic shock (Fig. 4A). Effects on the membrane were evaluated by the ability of the membrane-impermeable propidium iodide (PI) to label nuclei. Addition of PS liposomes increased PS on the cell surface, while an inhibitor (A01) of the Tmem16 family of scramblases reduced surface levels of PS (35) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Myomerger-null myoblasts are resistant to this level of osmotic shock as indicated by the absence of PI+ nuclei in the control (no protein), and PS alone was not sufficient to induce permeability (Fig. 4B). Two different concentrations of WT ectodomain increased the number of PI+ nuclei, and application of PS further increased this effect (Fig. 4B). In a similar experimental setup, reduction of PS on the outer leaflet with A01 reduced the activity of WT ectodomain, and reapplication of PS with liposomes restored Myomerger activity (Fig. 4C). Thus, movement of PS to the extracellular leaflet, a phenomenon proposed to regulate myoblast fusion, could be a primary determinant for Myomerger activity. To test if the presence of PS specifically modulates Myomerger activity, we manipulated external PS in both cellular and biochemical assays.High positive curvature of lipid monolayers lowers the edge energy of the pores in membranes and, thus, facilitates their formation (34). The ability of Myomerger to generate positive curvature and promote pore formation has been linked to an elevated sensitivity of myoblast plasma membranes to hypotonic osmotic shock (10), which increases tension in the membrane. When this tension reaches a critical threshold, the continuity of the membrane lipid bilayer is disrupted by opening of the pore(s) allowing probes that do not normally cross the bilayer to enter the cells. Proteins and lipids that facilitate pore formation by generating membrane stresses lower critical tension and promote membrane permeabilization. Indeed, osmotic shock resulted in permeabilized membranes in WT myoblasts but Myomerger-deficient myoblasts were largely refractory to permeabilization (10). To explore whether PS externalization influences Myomerger interactions with the plasma membrane, we utilized the osmotic shock assay in Myomerger−/− myoblasts. The cells were differentiated for 3 days and incubated with WT ectodomain in the presence or absence of compounds that alter PS externalization, and then the cells were placed into water to induce osmotic shock (Fig. 4A). Myomerger Activity Is Regulated by Cell-Surface PS.

PS-mediated induction of Myomerger ectodomain structure and membrane interactions may represent a mechanism to control Myomerger function given that PS is usually contained on the inner leaflet of mammalian cell membranes. Thus, movement of PS to the extracellular leaflet, a phenomenon proposed to regulate myoblast fusion, could be a primary determinant for Myomerger activity. To test if the presence of PS specifically modulates Myomerger activity, we manipulated external PS in both cellular and biochemical assays.

High positive curvature of lipid monolayers lowers the edge energy of the pores in membranes and, thus, facilitates their formation (34). The ability of Myomerger to generate positive curvature and promote pore formation has been linked to an elevated sensitivity of myoblast plasma membranes to hypotonic osmotic shock (10), which increases tension in the membrane. When this tension reaches a critical threshold, the continuity of the membrane lipid bilayer is disrupted by opening of the pore(s) allowing probes that do not normally cross the bilayer to enter the cells. Proteins and lipids that facilitate pore formation by generating membrane stresses lower critical tension and promote membrane permeabilization. Indeed, osmotic shock resulted in permeabilized membranes in WT myoblasts but Myomerger-deficient myoblasts were largely refractory to permeabilization (10). To explore whether PS externalization influences Myomerger interactions with the plasma membrane, we utilized the osmotic shock assay in Myomerger−/− myoblasts. The cells were differentiated for 3 days and incubated with WT ectodomain in the presence or absence of compounds that alter PS externalization, and then the cells were placed into water to induce osmotic shock (Fig. 4A). Effects on the membrane were evaluated by the ability of the membrane-impermeable propidium iodide (PI) to label nuclei. Addition of PS liposomes increased PS on the cell surface, while an inhibitor (A01) of the Tmem16 family of scramblases reduced surface levels of PS (35) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Myomerger-null myoblasts are resistant to this level of osmotic shock as indicated by the absence of PI+ nuclei in the control (no protein), and PS alone was not sufficient to induce permeability (Fig. 4B). Two different concentrations of WT ectodomain increased the number of PI+ nuclei, and application of PS further increased this effect (Fig. 4B). In a similar experimental setup, reduction of PS on the outer leaflet with A01 reduced the activity of WT ectodomain, and reapplication of PS with liposomes restored Myomerger activity (Fig. 4C). We also quantified binding of a fluorescent Myomerger ectodomain to the surface of Myomerger-deficient myoblasts. Suppressing PS exposure by treating the cells with A01 lowered the amount of cell surface–associated TAMRA-labeled WT ectodomain (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). These data confirm that cell-surface PS augments both the membrane-binding and membrane-stressing activities of the Myomerger ectodomain.

We also tested if PS increases Myomerger-mediated myoblast fusion by treating Myomerger-null myoblasts that contain a doxycycline (Dox)-inducible Myomerger construct with PC or PC:PS liposomes (70:30) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). As expected, Myomerger-null cells do not fuse in the absence of Dox-induced Myomerger expression, and addition of PC:PS liposomes did not overcome the requirement for Myomerger (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). After Dox treatment and Myomerger expression, PC:PS liposomes enhanced the activity of Myomerger and led to more fusion compared with cells that were not treated with lipid (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). An unexpected finding here is that PC alone inhibited fusion, which could be explained by a concomitant reduction in mobility of PS to the outer leaflet or that PC can result in membrane flattening and thus antagonize curvature needed for fusion (36). Overall, these data demonstrate that the Myomerger activity in cells for fusion is controlled by cell-surface concentrations of PS.

DiscussionThe final step of the myoblast fusion reaction is driven by one of the skeletal muscle-specific fusogens, Myomerger, which stresses membranes generating positive curvature and promoting the transition from hemifusion to fusion pore formation. Despite knowledge of the general activity for Myomerger, questions existed about how the two juxtaposed helices in the ectodomain cooperate to interact with membranes and associated regulatory mechanisms that control its activity. We found that the two helices in the Myomerger ectodomain possess unique characteristics, both of which are needed for full activity of the protein. In addition to both helices, the level of membrane-destabilizing activity of Myomerger leading to fusion completion is achieved in the presence of PS. Since PS is usually found in the inner leaflet of mammalian membranes, the work reveals a mechanism by which cells control the Myomerger membrane-destabilizing activity to avoid collateral cellular membrane damage and focus the activity at sites of PS externalization at the time of fusion.The data presented here indicate that Myomerger experiences several levels of membrane interactions, but only one of which results in the necessary energy for formation of a fusion pore. The amphipathic Helix-1 is disordered in solution but still possesses intrinsic membrane-binding activity independent of Helix-2 and PS, suggesting that Helix-1 may cause a low level of membrane stress in cells. Helix-2 is ordered and can insert into the acyl-tail region of membranes, enhancing the effects of Helix-1 on the membrane to an intermediate level. PS is required for the fusion-inducing level of membrane interactions, where it increases phospholipid displacement by the Myomerger ectodomain. Based on these data, we propose a model (Fig. 6D) where prior to fusion, Myomerger is stored in a relatively inactive conformation within a membrane environment lacking PS where Helix-1 is disordered and weakly interacting with the membrane, and Helix-2 is inserted into the lipid acyl chains. Movement of PS to the extracellular leaflet triggers the activity of Myomerger by allowing Helix-1 to become structured and causing further displacement of the phospholipid head groups and Helix-2 enhances the effects of Helix-1 by deepening the interaction with the membrane, overall causing the membrane stresses needed to drive pore formation. These data reveal a regulatory mechanism by which the activity of Myomerger for fusion is spatially controlled to complete the reaction at the proper time.We propose that Helix-1 of Myomerger is the direct fusion-activating domain driving positive curvature because of multiple reasons. There is a change in activity of Helix-1 from a baseline state by PS, and Helix-1 alone possesses some fusogenic activity. In response to a trigger (PS), Helix-1 is structured and inserts into membranes. Moreover, mutation of the Arg residues in Helix-1 renders the protein nonfunctional, indicating an obligatory requirement for this region of Myomerger. While loss of the leucine residues in Helix-2 also results in a reduction of cellular fusion, indicating it is an important domain for Myomerger activity, there is some residual activity since Helix-1 is present. Based on data presented here, it is difficult to know if Helix-2 of Myomerger, which contains a highly conserved AxLyCxL motif proposed to act as a fusion peptide (30), directly and independently contributes to the induction of positive curvature. Given that Helix-2 is hydrophobic, we predict that even in a nonfusion state, this helix would be incorporated into the acyl chains of the membrane bilayer, consistent with the finding that the presence of Helix-2 reduces FRET efficiency between the lipid tails. This would also suggest that this deeper insertion facilitated by Helix-2 is not independently sufficient to drive membrane disruptions or lead to fusion pore formation. The deeper insertion facilitated by Helix-2 is also unlikely to directly contribute to the development of positive curvature because splaying of phospholipid tails would not on its own generate positive membrane curvature, and can have the opposite effect of generating negative curvature. It is also noteworthy that interactions with PS, which we have shown to be essential to drive the fusion reaction and apparently activate the fusion-inducing function of Myomerger, have no impact on the ability of Helix-2 to insert into the acyl-chain region of the membrane. Nonetheless, Helix-2 at a minimum indirectly impacts positive curvature as insertion into phospholipid heads is severely reduced in the absence of Helix-2 or mutation of leucine residues in Helix-2. A critical function for Helix-2 is consistent with human myopathies caused by a truncated Myomerger protein that lacks Helix-2 (37). The order of the helices within the protein is also likely important if a function of Helix-2 is to tether Helix-1 close to the membrane. We favor the interpretation that the critical role for Helix-2 in Myomerger’s role of driving fusion beyond hemifusion to completion is to amplify the lipid-displacing effects of Helix-1 with PS, causing the requisite stress and positive curvature induction necessary for fusion pore formation.Our work also highlights the importance of a trigger for membrane remodeling needed for cell fusion and more broadly informs mechanisms for how cells control the activity of proteins that perturb membranes. PS is a known positive regulator of cell fusion in myoblasts, osteoclasts, and trophoblasts and HIV virus–cell fusion (19). It is interesting to consider that PS flipping to the outer leaflet is a signal for fusion but also apoptosis. In fact, apoptotic cells can stimulate myoblast fusion (21). We propose that negatively charged PS regulates cell fusion by interacting with positively charged residues on Myomerger, causing increased structure and membrane insertions. This mechanism could be similar to what occurs to the fusogenic envelope protein on HIV, increasing fusion and implicating a conserved pathway for activation of fusogens. The unique regulation and activity of Myomerger further demonstrate that fusion of membranes can be accomplished by proteins with divergent fusion-activating domains. While we demonstrate that PS is a determinant of Myomerger’s fusogenic activity, this interaction need not be confined to the actual fusion site. Since Myomerger functions to remodel lipids in the cell membrane to drive fusion, its targeted localization to a fusion synapse is not needed. Indeed, imaging of Myomerger during zebrafish myoblast fusion revealed localization throughout the plasma membrane and no specific targeting to the fusion synapse (38). The data presented here that Myomerger is always moderately active are consistent with the model that its effects lead to fusion when those effects on the membrane are enhanced by PS and overlap with the effects of Myomaker.

Myomerger Insertion Assay.We followed the protocol described previously (13), with small modifications. In brief, LUVs containing DOPC:DOPS:TopFluor PE:RhPE (89:10:0.2:0.5 mol %) were used to measure changes in FRET efficiency for probes placed in the lipid head-group region. LUVs containing DOPC:DOPS:TopFluor PC:TopFluor TMRPC (89:10:0.2:0.5 mol %) were used to measure changes in FRET efficiency for probes placed in the lipid tail region. LUVs were prepared by extrusion in a buffer containing 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM Hepes, and 5 mM EDTA (pH 7.0) according to the previously described protocol (13). Fluorescence emission spectra were recorded on a FluoroMax 4 spectrofluorometer (Horiba) in the range of 490 to 650 nm with excitation and emission bandwidths set to 5 and 2 nm, respectively, excitation wavelength set to 480 nm, and integration time set to 0.1 s. LUVs were added to 2 mL of liposome buffer to a final lipid concentration of 2.5 µM. Peptides were added to the cuvette in small increments and fluorescence spectra were recorded after each addition. FRET efficiency at each peptide concentration was calculated from emission spectra (13).

CD.CD measurements were performed using a Jasco J-815 CD spectropolarimeter in CD buffer containing 10 mM Na2HPO4 and 50 mM NaF. An average of three scans were recorded per measurement at 100 nm/min at room temperature (∼23 °C) between 200 and 250 nm, using a 1-mm optical bandwidth. Myomerger ectodomain (34 residues), Helix-1 (13 residues), or Helix-2 (11 residues) was diluted into CD buffer to a final concentration of 40 µM and either added to a quartz cuvette for reading or supplemented with 2.67 mM either 100% PC or 70% PC/30% PS liposome mixture prior to reading. Baseline measurements were performed to reduce background scattering interference by using either CD buffer alone or supplemented with the appropriate concentration of liposomes in the absence of peptide.
