Extracellular perception of auxin, an essential phytohormone in plants, has been
debated for decades. Auxin-binding protein 1 (ABP1) physically interacts with
quintessential transmembrane kinases (TMKs) and was proposed to act as an
extracellular auxin receptor, but its role was disputed because abp1 knockout
mutants lack obvious morphological phenotypes. Here, we identified two new
auxin-binding proteins, ABL1 and ABL2, that are localized to the apoplast and
directly interact with the extracellular domain of TMKs in an auxin-dependent
manner. Furthermore, functionally redundant ABL1 and ABL2 genetically interact
with TMKs and exhibit functions that overlap with those of ABP1 as well as being
independent of ABP1. Importantly, the extracellular domain of TMK1 itself binds
auxin and synergizes with either ABP1 or ABL1 in auxin binding. Thus, our
findings discovered auxin receptors ABL1 and ABL2 having functions overlapping
with but distinct from ABP1 and acting together with TMKs as co-receptors for
extracellular auxin.

Introduction

Auxin regulates nearly every aspect of plant growth, development, and responses to the environment, and has been shown to control transcription-independent rapid responses occurring in seconds as well as slower transcriptional responses 1–7. The latter requires the intracellular receptors TRANSPORT INHIBITOR RESPONSE 1 (TIR1)/AUXIN-SIGNALING F-BOX proteins (AFBs) that promote the degradation of their co-receptors AUX/IAA transcriptional repressors and act as adenylate cyclases 8–12. Increasing studies show that many auxin-induced effects such as plasma-membrane hyperpolarization, cytosolic Ca2+-transients, activation of ROP GTPases, RAF-like kinase-dependent cytoplasmic streaming, and protoplast swelling, are too rapid to depend on transcriptional regulation 5,7,13–21. Most of these rapid auxin responses are evidently regulated by a class of auxin receptors distinct from the nuclear TIR1/AFB receptors and presumably localized to the cell surface 7,14,22,23.

A key hallmark of auxin action lies in auxin dynamic flux across the plasma membrane and the cell wall of adjacent cells and also its long-distance flow across tissues and organs, which relies on the intercellular flux 24–29. Thus, it is imperative that apoplastic auxin levels are tightly monitored and controlled. Furthermore, cell surface auxin signaling is needed to rapidly regulate cytoplasmic responses when extracellular auxin levels change. Indeed, the transmembrane kinase (TMK) family receptor-like kinases are the essential plasma membrane-localized auxin signaling components required for many auxin responses including rapid responses such as cell wall acidifications, ROP GTPase activation, and protein phosphorylation 3,14,15,30–32. Auxin activates TMK phosphorylation, and the activated TMK kinase domain directly phosphorylates a series of effectors such as IAA32/34, AHA, MKK4/5-MPK3/6, TAA1, and ABI1/2 to regulate multiple developmental processes 14,31–35. However, the molecular mechanism for the perception of auxin signals that activate TMK-dependent auxin responses remains enigmatic. Auxin-binding protein 1 (ABP1) forms a complex with the extracellular domain of TMK1 in an auxin-dependent manner, suggesting that it may sense auxin to activate TMK-dependent auxin responses 14. However, the role of ABP1 in auxin perception/signaling was debated, because knocking out the single-copy ABP1 gene did not produce visible phenotypes in Arabidopsis
36. In contrast, knocking out all four functionally redundant TMKs, as in the tmk1;tmk2;tmk3;tmk4 mutant, induces a wide range of phenotypes including embryo and seedling lethality in Arabidopsis
14,37. A recent study shows that both abp1 and tmk1 null mutants exhibit similar defects in auxin-induced rapid global phospho-responses and canalization in Arabidopsis and that auxin binding to ABP1 is required for these auxin responses, corroborating the function of ABP1 in TMK1-dependent auxin perception/signaling 3,23. Yet this study fails to explain why many TMK-dependent developmental processes and auxin responses are unaffected in the abp1 null mutants. Importantly whether and how the quintessential TMKs perceive extracellular auxin remains unresolved.

Given the lack of obvious phenotypes in abp1 knockout mutants and the predominant localization of ABP1 to ER 3,36,38,39, we speculated the existence of other extracellular auxin-binding proteins that might act together with TMKs to perceive extracellular auxin. To search for such proteins, we conducted an immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry analysis from a TMK1-Flag Arabidopsis line and identified two highly related germin-like proteins (GLPs) that contain an auxin-binding box-like motif similar to A-box found in ABP1. Here we demonstrate that these ABP1-like (ABL) proteins and TMKs act as co-receptors for sensing extracellular auxin.

ResultsThe auxin-binding mutant protein ABP1–5 suppresses auxin responsesTo assess the existence of other auxin binding proteins acting with TMKs, we first investigated the effect of ABP1–5, harboring H94Y mutation, which compromises auxin-binding and auxin-induced interaction with TMK1 14. Expressing ABP1–5 in vivo is anticipated to cause a dominant negative effect on other auxin-binding proteins interacting with TMKs. We introduced pABP1::ABP1–5 into the abp1-TD1 mutant 36 and analyzed the phenotypes of pABP1::ABP1–5;abp1-TD1 (ABP1–5;abp1) in comparison with pABP1::ABP1;abp1-TD1 (ABP1;abp1). ABP1 and ABP1–5 protein levels in these lines were comparable (Figure S1A). ABP1–5;abp1 showed a series of growth and developmental phenotypes and was compromised in auxin responses, which were not observed in either ABP1;abp1, or wild type. As in the tmk1;tmk2;tmk3;tmk4 mutant 14, the auxin-induced lobing of pavement cells in cotyledons was compromised in ABP1–5;abp1 but not in ABP1;abp1 (Figures 1A–1C). We previously showed that auxin promotes lobe and indentation formation by activating ROP2 and ROP6, respectively 2. Consistently, the auxin-promoted activation of both ROP2 and ROP6 was partially abolished in ABP1–5;abp1 but not in ABP1;abp1 (Figures 1D–1F). Like the tmk1;tmk4 mutant 14,15,34, ABP1–5;abp1 exhibited reduction in auxin-promotion of hypocotyl elongation and cotyledon epinasty (Figures 1G–1I), PIN1 protein polarization in root cells (Figures S1B and S1C), root elongation and gravitropic responses (Figures S1D-S1G). ABP1–5;abp1 lines displayed single and triple cotyledons with abnormal pavement cell morphology, as in pin1
40 and tmk1;tmk2;tmk3;tmk4
14 (Figures S1H-S1J). ABP1–5;abp1 rosette leaves also exhibited abnormal shapes as found in the pin1 mutant (Figure S1K). None of the phenotypes described above were observed in the abp1 mutants or the ABP1;abp1 lines (Figures S1D-S1H and S1K). Altogether, our results show that the accumulation of the ABP1–5 protein impacts TMK-modulated auxin responses likely by causing a dominant negative effect on other auxin-binding proteins that functionally compensate for the loss of ABP1.ABP1-like proteins (ABLs) form auxin sensing complex with TMKs in the apoplastBecause we failed to identify clear ABP1-homologous genes in the Arabidopsis genome by amino acid sequence homology search, we explored potential auxin binding proteins that compensate for ABP1 in TMK-mediated auxin signaling by immunoprecipitating the TMK complex from the pTMK1::TMK1-Flag transgenic line. The immunoprecipitants contained a member of the GLP protein family where ABP1 belongs, which we termed ABP1-like protein 1 (ABL1) (Table S1). ABL1 (AT1G72610) and its close homolog ABL2 (AT5G20630) encode polypeptides with 208 and 211 residues respectively, sharing high amino acid sequence similarity with each other (64.42% identity). ABL1 and ABL2 exhibit low sequence similarity to ABP1 (26.26% and 18.44% identity, respectively), but contain a conserved metal ion binding motif with invariable amino acid residues predicted to be involved in auxin binding 41–43 (Figure S2A). AlphaFold-based structure simulations of ABL1 and ABL2 predicted a three-dimensional structure that highly resembles that of ABP1. Structure-based blind docking of ABL1 and ABL2 protein predicted a conserved auxin binding motif as in ABP1 42, suggesting a potential role for ABL1 and ABL2 as new auxin binding proteins (Figure S2B and Table S2).To assess whether both ABL1 and ABL2 form auxin sensing complexes with TMKs, we generated anti-ABL1 and anti-ABL2 antibodies that specifically detected ABL1 and ABL2 proteins, respectively (Figure S2C). ABL1 and ABL2 are predicted to be secreted as they contain signal peptides and lack other targeting signals. We analyzed the subcellular localization of ABL1 in Arabidopsis by both immuno-fluorescence staining and immuno-gold labeling. Both assays showed that ABL1 is almost exclusively localized to the cell surface (Figures 2A, 2B and S2D-S2H), contrasting to ABP1, which contains an ER retention signal and is primarily localized to ER with a small fraction (10–15%) found in the apoplast 3,38. Immunogold labeling further demonstrated that ABL1 is localized to the apoplast consistent with the lack of ER retention signals (Figure 2A).The localization of ABL1 to the apoplast provides the spatial possibility to form a complex with TMKs on the cell surface. Co-immunoprecipitation assays showed that both ABL1 and ABL2 form protein complexes with TMK1 in plant cells, respectively. Both ABL proteins were immunoprecipitated by anti-GFP antibodies from pTMK1::TMK1-GFP (TMK1-GFP) seedlings but not with BRI1 from BRI1-GFP seedlings (Figures 2C and 2D). The interaction between TMK1 with ABL1/ABL2 was enhanced by auxin in a concentration-dependent manner, and NAA treatment induced the interaction within 1 min, suggesting that auxin rapidly promotes ABL1/ABL2–TMK1 interactions in plant cells (Figures 2C–2F, S2I and S2J).We next used fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) analysis to assess whether ABL1 and TMK1 directly interact with each other and via TMK1’s extracellular domain. We transiently co-expressed ABL1-GFP with either mCherry-TMK1-ex (extracellular domain of TMK1) or mCherry-TMK1 (K616E) (a full-length kinase-dead version) in tobacco leaves. This analysis suggests a direct interaction between ABL1-GFP and mCherry-TMK1-ex or mCherry-TMK1 (K616E), and this interaction was strongly promoted by treatments with 100 nM NAA (Figures 2G, 2H, S2K and S2L). This auxin-induced interaction was completely abolished when two histidine residues (H100 and H102) in the conserved metal binding site of ABL1 were mutated to Ala (A) (ABL1-M2) (Figures 2G, 2H, S2K and S2L). These mutations eliminated ABL1’s ability to bind auxin (see below). Taken together, our results indicate that secreted ABL1 directly interact with the extracellular domain of TMK1 and that this interaction is promoted by auxin but is independent of TMK1’s kinase activity. Hence, we hypothesize that the ABLs and TMKs form auxin receptor complexes on the cell surface to perceive extracellular auxin.Functionally redundant ABL1 and ABL2 show distinct and overlapping functions with ABP1Because both ABLs and ABP1 form complexes with TMK1 in an auxin-dependent manner (Figure 2) 14, we asked whether ABLs and ABP1 are functionally redundant or distinct. We obtained both abl1–1 (abl1) and abl2–1 (abl2) single knockout mutants in the A. thaliana Col-0 background generated by either T-DNA insertion or CRISPR-based method (Figures S3A and S3B), but neither exhibited obvious defects in growth and development (Figure 3A). However, abl1–1;abl2–1 (abl1/2) double mutant showed obvious morphological defects including reduced seedling size, curling leaves, and altered shapes of pavement cells (Figures 3A–3D and S3C), suggesting functional redundancy between ABL1 and ABL2.To assess whether ABP1 is functionally redundant with ABL1 and/or ABL2, we generated abp1-TD1;abl1–1 (abp1;abl1), abp1-TD1;abl2–1 (abp1;abl2) double mutants and a abp1-TD1;abl1–1;abl2–1 (abp1;abl1/2) triple mutant. The abp1;abl1 mutant did not exhibit an obvious phenotype, while the abp1;abl2 double mutant showed a slight reduction in seedling growth, much weaker than that in the abl1/2 double mutant (Figures 3A and 3B). The abp1;abl1/2 triple mutant showed greatly reduced plant sizes and curling leaves, much more severe growth and developmental defect than either abl1/2, or abp1;abl1, or abp1;abl2 (Figures 3A and 3B), suggesting that ABP1 contributes to the ABL1/2-mediated processes. Growth defects such as small plant sizes in the triple mutant were nearly fully complemented by pABL1::ABL1 but partially complemented by pABP1::ABP1 or pABL1::ABP1 (Figures 3A and 3B). Furthermore, some phenotypes such as leaf curling were complemented neither by pABP1::ABP1 nor by pABL1::ABP1 but were fully complemented by pABL1::ABL1 (Figure 3A). These results show that the redundant ABL1 and ABL2 functionally overlap with ABP1 but also have ABP1-independent physiological functions. Importantly, ABL1 and ABL2 are the major contributors to the overlapping functions compared to ABP1. The differential effects of ABP1 and ABL1/2 on growth and development (Figures 3A–3D) are consistent with the differences in their subcellular distribution (Figures 2A and S2D-S2H), spatial expression patterns, and co-expression with TMK1 and TMK4 (Figure S3D).The abp1;abl1/2 triple mutant exhibited defects in pavement cell morphogenesis as well, which were partially complemented by pABL1::ABP1 (Figures 3C and 3D). This triple mutant also displayed PIN1 localization defects as in ABP1–5;abp1 (Figures S3E and S3F) or tmk1;tmk2;tmk3;tmk4 mutants 14. The triple mutant did not exhibit an obvious gravitropism defect (Figure S3G). Other ABLs functionally overlapping with ABP1 may be involved in the regulation of gravitropic response. Consistent with the defects of pavement cell morphogenesis and PIN localization, the activation of both ROP2 and ROP6 by auxin was partially abolished in the abp1;abl1/2 mutant, which was partially restored by pABP1::ABP1 and pABL1::ABL1 (Figures 4A and 4B). Furthermore, the abp1;abl1/2 mutant showed insensitivity to the auxin promotion of hypocotyl elongation like both gABP1–5;abp1 and tmk1;tmk4, and this abp1;abl1/2 defect was rescued by pABP1::ABP1 or pABL1::ABL1 (Figures 4C and 4D). Phosphoproteomics analysis showed that the auxin-induced fast global protein phosphorylation within 1 min (89.9%) and 5 min (82%) was abolished in the abp1;abl1/2 mutant (Figures 4E, 4F, S3H and Table S3), suggesting that ABP1 and ABL1/2 are required for the rapid auxin responses in regulating protein phosphorylation. These results confirm the function of ABP1 in auxin responses as previously reported 2, and further support its overlapping function with ABL1 and ABL2 in both plant development and auxin signaling.ABL1 and ABP1 functionally interact with TMKsGiven the auxin-dependent physical interactions between ABP1/ABLs and TMKs, we were interested in their functional relationship in the regulation of auxin responses. We analyzed this relationship by respectively crossing abp1, abl1, and abp1;abl1 with tmk1–1−/+;tmk4–1 (tmk1−/+;tmk4) heterozygous double mutant, which exhibited mild growth defects (Figures 5A and 5B). Neither abp1 nor abl1 enhanced the growth defects in tmk1−/+;tmk4, but the abp1;abl1;tmk1−/+;tmk4 quadruple mutants showed a series of severe growth and developmental defects, much stronger than abp1;abl1 or tmk1−/+;tmk4, including young seedling size (Figures 5A and 5B), plant architecture (Figure S4A), and inflorescence architecture (Figure S4B). All these developmental defects were restored by either ABP1 or ABL1 (Figures 5A, 5B and S4). More importantly, auxin promotion of hypocotyl elongation (Figures 5C and 5D), pavement cell morphogenesis (Figures 5E, 5F and S4C), and ROP activation (Figures 5G and 5H) were abolished in this quadruple mutant, and all these defects were rescued either by ABP1 or ABL1 (Figures 5C–5H). Finally, we analyzed PIN1 localization in these mutants by immuno-staining, and found that PIN1 was depolarized in the abp1;abl1;tmk1−/+;tmk4 mutant (Figures S4D and S4E). Moreover, abp1;abl1;tmk1−/+;tmk4 showed reduced primary root length compared with the double or triple mutants (Figures S4F and S4G). These data show that functionally overlapping ABP1 and ABL1 genetically interact with TMK1 and TMK4 in regulating both plant development and auxin responses. Together with the auxin-dependent physical interactions between ABLs/ABP1 and TMK1, our findings conclusively demonstrate that ABLs/ABP1 and TMKs act in the same pathway in the regulation of auxin responses.ABL1/ABP1 and TMK1 act as co-receptors for extracellular auxinBecause of auxin-dependent physical and functional interactions between TMKs and the putative auxin binding proteins ABL1/ABL2, we hypothesize ABLs and TMKs form auxin receptor complexes on the cell surface. To test this hypothesis, we first determined whether ABL1 indeed directly binds auxin as predicted (Figures S2A and S2B). Flag-tagged ABP1 and ABL1 fusion proteins, transiently expressed in and purified from Arabidopsis protoplasts, were used for auxin binding assays using the microscale thermophoresis (MST) method. ABP1 proteins bound IAA with a KD of 4.71 μM, whereas ABP1–5 proteins barely bound IAA as reported 3,44 (Figures 6A–6C). ABL1 proteins bound IAA with a KD of 1.73 μM, whereas the ABL1-M2 mutant protein harboring mutations in the auxin binding motif (Figures S2A and S2B) showed no binding with IAA (Figures 6A–6C). Importantly, neither pABP1::ABP1–5 nor pABL1::ABL1-M2 were able to complement abp1;abl1/2 triple mutant phenotype (Figures 3A and 3B), and pABL1::ABL1-M2 failed to complement the morphological defects in the abl1/2 mutant (Figure 6D), indicating that auxin binding to ABP1 and ABL1 is essential for their physiological functions. Furthermore, like ABP1 45, ABL1 bound IBA (Indole-3-butyric acid) and bound PEO-IAA (α-Phenylethyl-2-oxo-IAA) weakly, but did not bind BA (benzoic acid), L-Trp (L-tryptophan), IPA (Indole-3-propionic acid) and PIC (Picloram) at all (Figures S5A-S5D) and barely bound 2-NAA (2-Naphthylacetic acid, an inactive NAA analog) (Figure S5D) 3,45,46.ABL1 showed a stronger binding affinity with NAA (KD, 365 nM) than IAA as previously reported for ABP1 47,48 (Figure 6A). As expected, both ABP1–5 and ABL1-M2 showed dramatically reduced binding with NAA (Figure S5D), supporting direct interactions between ABL1/ABP1 and auxin. To further confirm that ABL1 and ABP1 directly bind auxin, we used mammalian cell-expressed proteins for MST assays. His-tagged ABP1 and ABL1, expressed in and purified from HEK293T cells, bound NAA with a KD of 11.9 μM and 17.1 μM, respectively. His-tagged ABP1–5 and ABL1-M2 failed to bind NAA (Figure S5E). Taken together, our results show that like ABP1, ABL1 is a bona fide auxin-binding protein that binds active auxin but not inactive analogs.Given the auxin-dependent interaction between TMKs and ABLs/ABP1, we speculated that the extracellular domains of TMK proteins could also participate in auxin perception. We found that HA-tagged TMK1-ex fusion protein transiently expressed in and purified from Arabidopsis protoplasts was also able to bind IAA with a KD of 1.82 μM, NAA with a KD of 7.21 μM and other auxin analogues such as IBA (KD, 6.14 μM), PEO-IAA (KD, 40.86 μM) that were capable of binding ABP1, but was unable to bind auxin analogues such as BA, L-Trp, IPA, and PIC that also failed to bind ABP1 and ABL1 (Figures 6A and S5A-S5D). HEK293T cells-expressed His-tagged TMK1-ex bound NAA with a KD of 71.4 μM (Figure S5E). As a negative control, FLS2-ex did not bind auxin (Figure S5). Thus, we demonstrated that the extracellular domain of TMK1 also directly binds auxin specifically as both ABP1 and ABL1.Based on their capacity to bind auxin and to interact with each other in an auxin-dependent manner, we propose that TMK1 and ABLs/ABP1 act as co-receptors for the apoplastic auxin. ABL1/ABP1 and TMK1 act as co-receptors for extracellular auxin

Because of auxin-dependent physical and functional interactions between TMKs and the putative auxin binding proteins ABL1/ABL2, we hypothesize ABLs and TMKs form auxin receptor complexes on the cell surface. To test this hypothesis, we first determined whether ABL1 indeed directly binds auxin as predicted (Figures S2A and S2B). Flag-tagged ABP1 and ABL1 fusion proteins, transiently expressed in and purified from Arabidopsis protoplasts, were used for auxin binding assays using the microscale thermophoresis (MST) method. ABP1 proteins bound IAA with a KD of 4.71 μM, whereas ABP1–5 proteins barely bound IAA as reported 3,44 (Figures 6A–6C). ABL1 proteins bound IAA with a KD of 1.73 μM, whereas the ABL1-M2 mutant protein harboring mutations in the auxin binding motif (Figures S2A and S2B) showed no binding with IAA (Figures 6A–6C). Importantly, neither pABP1::ABP1–5 nor pABL1::ABL1-M2 were able to complement abp1;abl1/2 triple mutant phenotype (Figures 3A and 3B), and pABL1::ABL1-M2 failed to complement the morphological defects in the abl1/2 mutant (Figure 6D), indicating that auxin binding to ABP1 and ABL1 is essential for their physiological functions. Furthermore, like ABP1 45, ABL1 bound IBA (Indole-3-butyric acid) and bound PEO-IAA (α-Phenylethyl-2-oxo-IAA) weakly, but did not bind BA (benzoic acid), L-Trp (L-tryptophan), IPA (Indole-3-propionic acid) and PIC (Picloram) at all (Figures S5A-S5D) and barely bound 2-NAA (2-Naphthylacetic acid, an inactive NAA analog) (Figure S5D) 3,45,46.

ABL1 showed a stronger binding affinity with NAA (KD, 365 nM) than IAA as previously reported for ABP1 47,48 (Figure 6A). As expected, both ABP1–5 and ABL1-M2 showed dramatically reduced binding with NAA (Figure S5D), supporting direct interactions between ABL1/ABP1 and auxin. To further confirm that ABL1 and ABP1 directly bind auxin, we used mammalian cell-expressed proteins for MST assays. His-tagged ABP1 and ABL1, expressed in and purified from HEK293T cells, bound NAA with a KD of 11.9 μM and 17.1 μM, respectively. His-tagged ABP1–5 and ABL1-M2 failed to bind NAA (Figure S5E). Taken together, our results show that like ABP1, ABL1 is a bona fide auxin-binding protein that binds active auxin but not inactive analogs.

Given the auxin-dependent interaction between TMKs and ABLs/ABP1, we speculated that the extracellular domains of TMK proteins could also participate in auxin perception. We found that HA-tagged TMK1-ex fusion protein transiently expressed in and purified from Arabidopsis protoplasts was also able to bind IAA with a KD of 1.82 μM, NAA with a KD of 7.21 μM and other auxin analogues such as IBA (KD, 6.14 μM), PEO-IAA (KD, 40.86 μM) that were capable of binding ABP1, but was unable to bind auxin analogues such as BA, L-Trp, IPA, and PIC that also failed to bind ABP1 and ABL1 (Figures 6A and S5A-S5D). HEK293T cells-expressed His-tagged TMK1-ex bound NAA with a KD of 71.4 μM (Figure S5E). As a negative control, FLS2-ex did not bind auxin (Figure S5). Thus, we demonstrated that the extracellular domain of TMK1 also directly binds auxin specifically as both ABP1 and ABL1.

Based on their capacity to bind auxin and to interact with each other in an auxin-dependent manner, we propose that TMK1 and ABLs/ABP1 act as co-receptors for the apoplastic auxin. Many co-receptors bind their ligands synergistically, e.g., TIR1-IAA7, PYL-PP2C, BRI1-BAK1, FLS2-BAK1 9,49–52. Indeed the mixture of TMK1-ex with either ABP1 or ABL1, exhibited greatly higher IAA binding affinity with a KD of 94 nM or 525 nM, respectively, compared with either ABP1/ABL1 or TMK1-ex itself (Figures 6A–6C), demonstrating a synergistic auxin binding between the TMK1 and ABP1/ABL1 co-receptors, similar to the TIR1-Aux/IAA auxin co-receptor system 9. Altogether, our combined genetic, biochemical and cell biological data clearly indicate that ABL1/ABP1 and TMKs act as co-receptors for apoplastic auxin in plants.

ABL1 and ABL2 belong to a family of ancient apoplastic auxin receptors

The existence of apoplastic auxin receptors has been a Holy Grail in plant biology. For nearly half a century, the role of ABP1 as an apoplastic auxin receptor has been debated for various reasons 7,36,53–55, although recent studies have resurrected the function of ABP1 by demonstrating its role in auxin-mediated rapid phosphorylation and vasculature canalization in Arabidopsis
3,20,23. Because ABP1 is primarily localized to ER and its knockout mutants lack obvious morphological phenotypes, the extracellular auxin receptor remains puzzling 23.

Our findings here have resolved this century-long puzzle by demonstrating that ABL1 and ABL2 are two apoplastic auxin receptors. They belong to the family of ancient auxin-binding proteins that are exclusively localized to the apoplast, and also regulate a wide range of cytoplasmic auxin responses and functionally overlap with but are distinct from ABP1 in Arabidopsis. These apoplastic proteins bind auxin with an affinity similar to that for ABP1, and their auxin-binding activity is required for their biological functions in the regulation of auxin responses and plant development. Importantly they regulate a series of cytoplasmic auxin responses such as PIN1 polarization and rapid changes in ROP GTPase activation and protein phosphorylation. Hence, they are bona fide receptors for extracellular auxin (Figure 7).

ABL1 and ABL2 belong to a superfamily of GLPs. Although ABP1 is also a member of this superfamily, it shares little sequence similarity to ABL1 and ABL2 except for a conserved metal-binding motif involved in auxin binding. However, many GLP members are much more closely related to ABL1 and ABL2 than ABP1 and are deeply conserved in plants, suggesting that at least some GLPs may be functionally redundant with ABL1 and ABL2. This is consistent with our observations that both abl1/2 and abp1;abl1/2 mutants are conditional (Figures 3 and 4). This is in contrast to condition-independent severe developmental defects in tmk1;tmk2;tmk3;tmk4 and abp1;abl1;tmk1−/+;tmk4 (Figure 5) 14. Hence further studies are needed to identify other ABLs that interact with TMKs and function as co-receptors for extracellular auxin.

GLPs that are closely related to ABLs are conserved in bryophytes and green algae, so are TMKs 56–58. It is interesting that ultrafast auxin responses including auxin-induced phosphorylation that is independent of the TIR1/AFBs are also deeply conserved 20,21. Thus, apoplastic ABL/TMK-based perception of extracellular auxin most likely controls this ancient auxin signaling mechanism in plants.
