## Full Text

Abstract

In the developing central nervous systems (CNS), neural progenitor cells generate neurons and glia in sequential order. However, the influence of neurons on glia generation remains elusive. Here, we report that photoreceptor cell‐derived Jag2b is required for Notch‐dependent Müller glia (MG) generation in the developing zebrafish retina. In jab2b −/− mutants, differentiating MGs are re‐specified into lineage‐related bipolar neuron fate at the expense of mature MG. Single‐cell transcriptome analysis and knock‐in animals reveal that jab2b is specifically expressed in crx + ‐photoreceptor cells during MG generation. Crx promoter‐driven jag2b , but not other Notch ligands, is sufficient to rescue the loss of MGs observed in jag2b −/− mutants. Furthermore, we observe a severe and moderate decrease in the number of MGs in notch3 −/− and notch1b −/− mutants, respectively, and the activation of Notch3 or Notch1b rescues the MG loss in jag2b −/− mutants. Together, our findings reveal that the interaction of Jag2b and Notch3/Notch1b mediates the crosstalk between neurons and glial cells to ensure the irreversible differentiation of MG, providing novel mechanistic insights into the temporal specification of glial cell fate in a developing vertebrate CNS structure.

Keywords: Jag2b , Müller glia, neuron‐to‐glia crosstalk, Notch receptor

The Notch ligand Jag2b mediates the neuron‐to‐glia crosstalk to ensure the irreversible differentiation of Müller glial cells, providing novel mechanistic insights into the temporal specification of glial cell fate in the developing vertebrate retina.

Introduction

In the central nervous system (CNS), neurons and glia are two primary cell types derived from a common pool of neural progenitor cells (Vecino et al , 2016 ). During development, these neural progenitor cells produce distinct neuron subtypes and glial cell types (such as astrocytes and oligodendrocytes) in a stereotyped temporal order: early‐born neurons, followed by late‐born neurons, and finally glial cells (Kohwi & Doe, 2013 ). Remarkably, the sequential generation of neurons and glia can even be preserved in cultured primary neural progenitors (Shen et al , 2006 ), pointing to an intrinsic program. For instance, an increased number of signaling molecules have also been characterized for glial fate choices in different CNS tissues, such as Lhx2 and Hesr2 in the developing retina (Tetsu Satow et al , 2001 ; De Melo et al , 2018 ), NFIA and Sox9 in the developing spinal cord (Stolt et al , 2003 ; Deneen et al , 2006 ), MEK in the developing cortex (Li et al , 2012 ), and glide / gcm in the lateral glia in Drosophila (Rossana De Iaco et al , 2006 ). More recently, NFI transcription factors have been reported as cell‐cycle exit regulators in late‐stage retinal progenitor cells (RPCs) that could control bipolar and MG fate specification in the developing mouse retina (Clark et al , 2019 ).

Besides, Notch signaling is intrinsically crucial for glial fate specification, including MG in the zebrafish retina (Gaiano & Fishell, 2002 ). In zebrafish, four Notch receptors (Notch1a, Notch1b, Notch2, and Notch3), and two groups of ligands, including five Delta ligands (DeltaA, DeltaB, DeltaC, DeltaD, and Delta‐like4 (Dll4)), and four Jagged ligands (Jag1a, Jag1b, Jag2a, and Jag2b), have been characterized (Hyde et al , 2022 ). The activation of Notch receptors by ligands can lead to a sequential cleavage of the Notch receptor intracellular domain (NICD), which in turn forms a CSL/RBPJk‐activator complex to activate the transcription of downstream target genes, such as HEY/HES‐family members (Radtke & Raj, 2003 ). The Notch signaling acts in a context‐dependent manner via different ligand‐receptor pairs. DeltaB interacts with Notch3 to maintain MG quiescence, thereby acting as a negative regulator of neuron regeneration in the light‐damaged zebrafish retina (Campbell et al , 2021 ). DeltaC interacts with Notch1a/3 receptors on adjacent epidermal cells to prevent deltaC ( dlc ) expression from those cells, resulting in generating keratinocytes (Lu et al , 2021 ). Interestingly, previous studies also indicated individual ligands could mediate unique functions not entirely due to the receptor they activate (D'Souza et al , 2008 ; Choi et al , 2009 ). In other words, different ligands could interact with the same receptor to mediate distinct cellular effects (Nandagopal et al , 2018 ; Stupnikov et al , 2019 ).

However, the knowledge of the extrinsic mechanism in regulating the temporal generation of glia remains limited (Bassett & Wallace, 2012 ; Rossi et al , 2017 ; Syed et al , 2017 ). Knockdown of the transcription factor sip1 in neocortical progenitors was found to result in prematuration of upper‐layer neurons and an increased postnatal gliogenesis at the expense of deep‐layer neurons (Seuntjens et al , 2009 ), suggesting a possible influence of late‐born neurons on the subsequent gliogenesis. However, late‐born neuron‐derived extrinsic cues that ensure the temporal production of glia remain unknown in vivo .

The zebrafish retina is an outstanding model to study vertebrate CNS development. Like other developing CNS tissues, RPCs generate six major cell types sequentially: retinal ganglion cells (RGC) and GABAergic amacrine cells (ACs), followed by glycinergic ACs, horizontal cells (HCs), bipolar cells (BPs), photoreceptor cells (PRs), and finally MGs (Nawrocki, 1985 ; Livesey & Cepko, 2001 ; He et al , 2012 ). Here, we first characterized a transient Notch activation during MG maturation. We identified Jag2b as the ligand responsible for this transient Notch activation by screening individual Notch ligands. In jag2b −/− mutants, differentiating MGs were predominantly re‐specified into the bipolar cell fate. Interestingly, jag2b was predominantly expressed in crx + PR precursors and PRs during MG generation. Furthermore, crx promoter‐driven jag2b , but not other Notch ligands, could rescue MG loss in jag2b −/− mutants. Finally, we found that notch1b −/− and notch3 −/− mutants largely recapitulated MG loss phenotype found in jag2b −/− mutants, and the misexpression of Notch intracellular domain (NICD) of Notch3 or Notch1b could rescue MG loss phenotype in jag2b −/− mutants. Thus, our results revealed that the interaction of Jag2b and Notch3/Notch1b mediated the crosstalk between late‐born photoreceptor cells and differentiating MGs in controlling the terminal MG differentiation.

Results

Transient Notch activation at early stages of MG maturation

Our previous clonal studies of the developing zebrafish retina have indicated that one MG was born with one BP at the lineage level (He et al , 2012 ; Wang et al , 2020 ). Meanwhile, pharmaceutical Notch inhibition at 48 hpf could result in a severe loss of MGs (Bernardos et al , 2005 ; MacDonald et al , 2015 ; Richard I. Dorsky et al , 1995 ). Thus, we first wondered Notch dynamics during MG generation at the lineage level (depicted by Tp1 reporter, the direct target of the intracellular domain of Notch receptors (NICD) generated upon Notch activation) (Parsons et al , 2009 ; Ninov et al , 2012 ; Quillien et al , 2014 ). We co‐injected Tp1 ‐ mNeonGreenCAAX plasmid and nls‐Kaede mRNA into embryos at the 16‐cell stage, marked single mNeonGreeenCAAX + RPCs by nls‐Kaede photoconversion at 48 hpf, and analyzed 2‐cell clones (Fig 1A–C ). In total, we collected 43 clones. 26 of them are 2‐cell clones, among which only four were BP‐MG pairs. Notably, each clone comprised one Tp1 high MG and one Tp1 absent BP (Fig 1C ), supporting that a mother RPC with the high Notch activity asymmetrically gave rise to one differentiating MG with high Notch activity (Notch high ) and one BP without Notch activity (Notch absent , Fig 1D ).

Figure 1. Transient Notch activation during MG maturation.

Furthermore, we examined Notch activity of differentiating MGs (Notch high ) during the entire course of MG maturation (at 60 hpf, and 3, 5, 7 days postfertilization, dpf) using combined Tg ( Tp1bglob : mVenusPEST ) ( Tp1:mVenus used below, a reporter fishline of Notch activity) with glutamine synthase (GS, an indicator of MG maturation) immunostaining. At 60 hpf, differentiating MGs ( Tp1 + ) had little GS expression but exhibited the characteristic MG‐like morphology, that is, cell bodies within the inner nuclear layer (INL); an apical process reaching the middle line of the outer nuclear layer (ONL); a basal process ending at the basal membrane of the retinal neuroepithelium (Fig EV1A ). At 3 dpf, most differentiating MGs ( Tp1 + ) became GS positive, indicating MG maturation (Fig 1E and F ). This maturation occurred from the central to peripheral of the retina (Fig EV1B ), reminiscent of retinal neuron differentiation waves (He et al , 2012 ). Notably, Tp1 + GS + MGs peaked at 5 dpf and became almost abolished from the central retina and mainly restricted to the retina periphery at 7 dpf (Fig 1E and F ). We also performed RT–qPCR of mVenus and glula (the gene encoding GS) mRNA in sorted MGs isolated from the retinae of Tg ( blbp:dsred ) fish at different developmental stages (Fig EV1C ) and found that the expression dynamic of both markers was largely recapitulated at the mRNA level (Fig 1G ). This reduction of Notch activity in MG at 7 dpf was consistent with the previous study showing a decreased mCherry signal during the MG maturation in Tg ( Tp1:mCherry ; gfap:GFP ) fish (Sahu et al , 2021 ). Taken together, differentiating MGs derived from Notch high RPCs, retained a transient Notch activation during the maturation.

Figure EV1. Transient Notch activation during MG generation.

Jag2b is necessary for MG generation

Next, we wondered which Notch ligands were responsible for this transient Notch activation during MG maturation. We first examined the expression of different Notch ligands in the scRNA‐seq data of the developing retina at 48 hpf, when MG began to emerge (Xu et al , 2020 ), and found the expression of four Delta ligands ( dla , dlb , dlc , and dld ) and two Jagged ligands ( jag1b and jag2b ) in 48‐hpf retinae (Fig EV1D ). Using CRISPR/Cas9‐based gene disruption, we further screened genes encoding these six Notch ligands (Fig EV2A ). The consequence and efficiency of gene disruption were verified by DNA sequencing (Fig EV2B ). Notably, jag2b disruption resulted in a significant loss of Notch activity (shown by Tp1 signals) in the inner nuclear layer and matured MGs (indicated by GS immunostaining) (Fig EV2C–E ). Besides, the disruption of dla and dld moderately decreased transient Notch activation, and the number of GS + matured MGs, while dlb , dlc , jag1b , and scrambled sgRNAs showed little effect (Fig EV2C–E ). To examine whether jag2b disruption also affected other retinal cell fates, we examined cell‐type composition in jag2b ‐disrupted retinae using SoFa fishline (Almeida et al , 2014 ) and found that besides the MG loss (Fig EV2C–E ), the number of BPs was significantly increased, whereas that of PRs was decreased (Fig EV2F and G ).

Figure EV2. Genetic disruption reveals the effects of different Notch ligands on MG generation.

Furthermore, we generated dla −/− , dlb −/− , dlc −/− , dld −/− , jag1b −/− , jag2b −/− mutant fish (Fig 2A–C ). DNA sequencing verified their mutated sequences (Figs 2D and EV3A ). Consistent with the result above (Fig EV2C–E ), there was a nearly complete loss of Notch activity (indicated by Tp1 signals) and matured MGs (indicated by GS signals) in 4‐dpf retinae of jag2b −/− mutants (Fig 2A–C ). We also observed the loss of GFAP signals (another MG marker) in jag2b −/− mutants (Fig EV3B ). Remarkably, jag2b −/+ and jag2b −/− mutants showed a dose‐dependent loss in gfap + MGs (Fig EV3C and D ). Besides, there was a moderate decrease in Notch activity and matured MGs in either dla −/− or dld −/− mutants, while there was a little alteration of MGs in the mutants of dlb −/− , dlc −/− , and jag1b −/− (Fig 2A–C ). Taken together, jag2b was necessary for transient Notch activation in differentiating MGs, thereby ensuring MG complete maturation.

Figure 2. Genetic mutants confirm the effects of different Notch ligands on MG generation.

Figure EV3. The efficiency of individual Notch ligand mutants.

PR‐specific jag2b expression during MG generation

To explore which cell types Jag2b derived from, we performed single‐cell RNA sequencing of retinal cells using Tg ( Tp1 : mVenus ) at 60 hpf, at which stage differentiating MGs exhibited a high level of Notch activity (Fig 1F and G ). We obtained 9,013 qualified 60‐hpf retinal cells, which were aggregated into 22 clusters (Fig 3A and B ). We then annotated each cluster according to the expression of major retinal cell‐type‐specific marker genes, including gap43 , pou4f1 , rbpms2b (RGCs); tfap2a , meis2a , gad2 (ACs); samsn1a , vsx1 , neurod4 (BPs); rem1 , rprmb , prox1a (HCs); elovl4b , crx , nr2e3 (PRs); rlbp1a , cavin2a , rhbg (MGs), pcna , cdk1 , notch1a (RPCs), and fabp11a , col15a1b (CMZ cells) (Xu et al , 2020 ) (Fig 3C ). Notch ligands, dla and dld , were mainly expressed in RPCs (clusters 3, 8, 9, 10) and MCs (cluster 16), whereas dlb and dlc were only expressed in RPCs (clusters 3, 8, 9, 10; Fig 3D ). Single‐cell transcriptome data of 3‐dpf retinae that we obtained previously (Xu et al , 2020 ) showed that the expression of all four delta ligands was highly restricted in the CMZ (Fig EV3E ). In situ results further showed that dla/dlb/dlc/dld were mainly expressed at RPCs in the peripheral retina, including the CMZ, in the retinae at 60 hpf and 3 dpf (Fig EV3F ). Besides, jag1b was weakly expressed in the CMZ and MCs at 60 hpf and 3 dpf (Figs 3D and EV3E ). Thus, at 60 hpf and 3 dpf, the expression of delta ligands and jag1b was mainly excluded from cells in the central retina except for the moderate expression of dla and dld in MGs.

Figure 3. Endogenous expression of Notch ligands.

Interestingly, jag2b was specifically expressed in crx + PR (precursors; clusters 4, 5), weakly expressed in RGCs (cluster 1) and BPs (clusters 2, 7, 11, 12) at 60 hpf (Fig 3D ). Its expression became mainly restricted in crx + PR at 72 hpf (Fig EV3E ). Unfortunately, in situ hybridization could not efficiently detect the jag2b expression, which was likely due to its low transcript level. We then created a jag2b knock‐in line ( jag2b ‐ Flag‐P2A‐EGFP , jag2b ‐ EGFP used below), in which the cassette of 3xFlag‐P2A‐EGFP was inserted into the TGA stop codon of jag2b (Fig 3E ). The insertion was verified by DNA sequencing and the qPCR analysis of EGFP + cells (Fig EV3G and H ). An injection of jag2b sgRNA and Cas9 protein into the knock‐in embryos led to a loss of the EGFP signal further verified the successful generation of the knock‐in line jag2b ‐ Flag‐P2A‐EGFP efficiency (Fig EV3I ). We also generated the transgenic line Tg ( crx:tdTomato‐nls ) to mark PR precursors and PRs (Fig EV3J ). By crossing the knock‐in line jag2b ‐ Flag‐P2A‐EGFP with Tg ( crx:tdTomato‐nls ), we found the co‐expression of jag2b and crx in the ONL at 60 hpf and 3 dpf (Figs 3F and EV3J ). Together, our results indicated that jab2b was derived from crx + PR precursors and PRs.

Photoreceptor cell‐specific jag2b overexpression was sufficient to rescue MG loss in jab2b −/− mutants

Next, we wondered if crx promoter‐driven jag2b overexpression in PR precursors/PRs could rescue MG loss in jag2b −/− mutants. Due to the fact that jag2b −/− mutants cannot survive longer than 15 days, we generated the transgenic lines of Tg ( uas : mjag2b ‐ P2A‐H2B‐ECFP ) and Tg ( crx : gal4FF ) on the jag2b +/− background, respectively. We crossed two fish lines to achieve an overexpression of jag2b in crx + PR precursors/PRs, and subsequently screened out ones with jag2b −/− genetic background for further rescue experiments (Fig EV4A ). Notably, GS + MGs were frequently present underneath PRs with the jag2b expression (indicated by H2B‐ECFP expression, Fig 4A ), indicating that crx promoter‐driven jag2b in PRs (precursors) could rescue MG loss in jag2b −/− mutants. To evaluate the specificity of this rescue experiment, we found that the number of GS + MGs with CFP + PRs above (GS + MGs‐CFP + ) per section was significantly more than that of GS + MGs without CFP + PRs above (GS + MGs‐CFP − ) in the rescue group. This statistical difference was abolished in the control group, in which only CFP was overexpressed in PR precursors/PRs (Fig 4B ). Also, we calculated the ratio of GS + MGs‐CFP + to all GS + MGs and found that the ratio of the rescue group was significantly higher than the control group (Fig 4C ). These results indicated the increased number of GS + MGs in the rescue group is jag2b overexpression‐dependent rather than a random event.

Figure EV4. Crx promoter‐driven jag2b expression rescues MG loss in jab2b −/− mutants.

Figure 4. Crx promoter‐driven jag2b rescues MG loss in jab2b −/− mutants.

To further verify the extrinsic property of Jag2b in MG generation, we wondered whether transplanted donor RPCs from jag2b ‐disrupted embryos could produce MGs in wild‐type retinae. Specifically, we injected scramble / jag2b sgRNA and H2B‐RFP mRNA into Tg ( Tp1 : mVenus ) donor embryo at the one‐cell stage, then transplanted these jag2b ‐disrupted cells into wild‐type (WT) embryo at 4 hpf (Fig EV4B ). We collected 21 and 24 clones derived from jag2b ‐disrupted ( jag2b sgRNA, n = 10 animals) and controlled ( scramble sgRNA, n = 9 animals) host cells, respectively. Notably, all 21 clones derived from jag2b ‐disrupted host cells in WT retinae contained MGs (Fig EV4C ), supporting the idea that Jag2b in the WT retina environment could rescue the MG loss in jag2b ‐disrupted donor clones.

Furthermore, we also attempted to misexpress jag2b in other retinal cell types, like RGC, AC, BP, and only cholinergic AC marker sox2 could drive the expression of jag2b . However, sox2 promoter‐driven jag2b in a subpopulation of ACs could not rescue MG loss in jag2b −/− mutants (Fig EV4D and E ). Thus, photoreceptor‐specific jag2b overexpression was sufficient to rescue MG loss in jab2b −/− mutants.

We also performed the ectopic expression of other ligands dla/dlb/dlc/dld by the crx promoter (Fig EV4F ). The results showed that dlb , dlc had little influence on MG loss phenotype in jag2b −/− mutant, whereas dla , dld could moderately rescue the MG loss in jag2b −/− mutant (Fig EV4G and H ). Considering that the expression of dla , dld was mainly restricted in the peripheral retina, they were unlikely major contributors to Notch activation in differentiating MGs.

Differentiating MGs were re‐specified into bipolar cells in jag2b −/− mutants

Considering a nearly complete MG loss in jag2b −/− mutants (Fig 2B and C ), we further wondered the final fate of differentiating MGs in the absence of jag2b . To do this, we utilized Tg ( Tp1:gal4::uas:Kaede ). At 60 hpf, differentiating MGs with characteristic MG‐like morphology (Fig EV1A ) could be sparsely labeled. We then marked single differentiating MGs by photoconversion and analyzed their final cell fate at 4 dpf (Fig 5A and B ). Among 45 marked differentiating MGs in wild‐type animals, ~ 82% became mature MGs (Fig 5C ). By contrast, within 63 marked differentiating MGs in jag2b −/− mutants, ~ 78% were morphologically identifiable bipolar cells instead (Fig 5C ).

Figure 5. Differentiating MGs were re‐specified into bipolar neurons in jab2b −/− mutants.

To verify these morphologically identifiable bipolar cells do express bipolar cell‐specific marker genes, we crossed Tg ( Tp1:gal4::uas:Kaede ) with Tg ( vsx1 : GFP ), marked single differentiating MG by Kaede photoconversion at 60 hpf, and stained MG‐specific GS in these clones at 4 dpf. In wild‐type groups, Kaede‐red + differentiating MG mainly became MGs (56.6%), which were positive with GS staining but not vsx1 signal (Fig 5D and E ); whereas in jag2b −/− mutants, Kaede‐red + differentiating MGs mainly became bipolar cells, which expressed vsx1 but not GS (61.6%, Fig 5F and G ). These results revealed that the absence of jag2b re‐specified differentiating MGs toward bipolar cells at the expense of mature MG.

In addition, we performed a similar clonal analysis of 60‐hpf differentiating MGs in wild‐type retinae with DAPT, an inhibitor of Notch activity, at 55 hpf (Fig EV5A ). An examination of the clones at 4 dpf found a similar MG‐to‐BP shift after DAPT treatment (Fig EV5B ).

Figure EV5. Notch1b, Notch3 are required for Jag2b‐dependent MG generation.

Notch3/Notch1b required for jag2b‐dependent MGs generation

Finally, we attempted to identify the receptors in MGs, which directly interacted with Jag2b derived from PRs (or precursors). Our scRNA‐seq data of 60‐hpf retinal cells showed that Notch receptors notch1a , notch1b , and notch3 were highly expressed in MGs and RPCs, whereas notch2 was mainly expressed in CMZ cells (Fig EV5C ). In differentiating MGs at 60 hpf, the expression level of notch3 was significantly higher than that of notch1a / 1b (Fig EV5C ). To further test the effects of these three Notch receptors on MG generation, we generated notch1a −/− , notch1b −/− , and notch3 −/− mutants (Fig EV5D–G ). Notably, there was a nearly complete loss of Tp1 + or GS + MGs in notch3 −/− retinae, recapitulating what was found in jag2b −/− retinae (Fig 6A and B ). Meanwhile, there was a moderate decrease in the number of TP1 + or GS+ MGs in notch1b −/− retinae, whereas there was no change in the numbers of TP1 + or GS + MGs in notch1a −/− retinae (Fig 6A and B ).

Figure 6. Notch1a and Notch3 are required for Jag2b ‐dependent MGs generation.

Furthermore, we used gfap promoter‐driven notch1b/3 ‐ NICD expression to examine whether Notch3 or Notch1b activation could rescue the MG loss phenotype in jag2b −/− retinae. Notably, we found that the misexpression of the NICD of either Notch3 or Notch1b could result in a significant increase in the number of GS + CFP + MGs (Fig 6C–F ). Thus, Notch3 and Notch1b were likely major Notch receptors in MGs to interact with PR‐derived Jag2b (Fig 7 ).

Figure 7. Interaction of Jag2b and Notch3/Notch1b mediated the crosstalk between neurons and glial cells to ensure MG irreversible differentiation.

Discussion

Notch activity is essential for MG generation (Bernardos et al , 2005 ; MacDonald et al , 2015 ; Mills & Goldman, 2017 ). Here we reported that this Notch activation was dependent on Jag2b, which was expressed in crx + PRs (precursors) (Figs 3D and F , and EV5A ). Previous clonal studies from our and other laboratories have shown that PR precursors and PRs were the products of cell lineages derived from atoh7 ‐expressing RPCs, whereas MGs were the result of lineages derived from non ‐atoh7 ‐expressing RPCs (He et al , 2012 ; Almeida et al , 2014 ; Wang et al , 2020 ). Thus, jag2b ‐mediated crosstalk between PR precursors/PRs and differentiating MGs is a type of inter‐lineage interaction (Fig 7 ). Besides, WT embryos could rescue the loss of MGs within clones derived from jag2b ‐disrupted donor cells (Fig EV4B and C ). Also, in the developing zebrafish retina, earlier fate‐mapping studies have also indicated that PRs were late‐born neuron types (Nawrocki, 1985 ; He et al , 2012 ; Wang et al , 2020 ). Considering the timing of PR generation, our finding thus provides a possible mechanism underlying the temporal generation of MGs by the crosstalk between differentiating MGs and neighboring late‐born PR precursors and PRs. In future, it is certainly interesting to examine to what extent a similar interaction is at work in generating other macroglia, including radial glia and astrocytes, in developing mammalian CNS tissues.

Our analysis also showed that the genetic ablation of dla and dld led to a moderate decrease in the number of MGs (Figs 2A–C and EV2A–E ). Previous studies have shown that dla and dld began to express as early as 24 hpf (Xu et al , 2020 ). Different from Notch inhibition at 44 hpf, leading to a nearly complete MG loss, Notch inhibition at the onset of retinal neurogenesis (28–32 hpf) resulted in early maturation phenotype, that is, the over‐production of early‐born retinal neuron types (RGCs and ACs) in the expense of the production of early‐born cell types (BPs, PRs, and MCs) (MacDonald et al , 2015 ). This effect could account for the mild decrease in the number of MG in either dla −/− or dld −/− retinae (Figs 2A–C and EV2A–E ). However, we cannot rule out the possibility that in the case of jag2b −/− retinae, Notch ligands dla and dld may activate the Tp1 Notch activity during the time window of MG generation (Fig 5A and B ). It is critical for future studies to investigate the extent to which dla and dld participate in MG generation.

Materials and Methods

Zebrafish husbandry and fishlines

All zebrafish embryos, larvae, and adults were produced, grown, and maintained at 28°C according to standard protocols. Animal procedures performed in this study were approved by the Animal Use Committee of the Institute of Neuroscience, Chinese Academy of Sciences (NA‐045‐2019).

Published fish lines used in this study include: AB (wild‐type, WT), Tg (gfap:GFP) mi2001 (ZDB‐FISH‐150901‐29307) (Bernardos & Raymond, 2006 ), Tg ( Tp1bglob:VenusPEST , ZDB‐FISH‐150901‐8023) (Ninov et al , 2012 ), Tg (uas:Kaede) (Scott & Baier, 2009 ), Tg(vsx1:GFP) nns5 , ZDB‐TGCONSTRCT‐090115‐3 (Kimura et al , 2008 )， and SoFa lines (ZDB‐FISH‐150901‐13478) (Almeida et al , 2014 ).

Newly generated fishline contained: Tg(uas:mjag2b‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc: mCherry) , Tg(crx:gal4FF) , Tg(crx: tdTomato‐nls) , Tg(Tp1bglob:gal4FF) , Tg (gfap:gal4FF) , Tg(blbp:dsred) , Tg(uas:notch3‐NICD‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry) , knock‐in fishline jag2b ‐ Flag‐P2A‐EGFP , and mutants jag2b −/− , dla −/− , dlb −/− , dlc −/− , dld −/− , jag1b −/− , notch1a −/− , notch1b −/− , notch3 −/− using CRISPR‐Cas9 system.

Generation of Tg (uas:mjag2b‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc: mCherry)

The 10 ng/μl plasmid pTol2‐uas : mjag2b ‐ P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry and 50 ng/μl tol2 mRNA were injected into a wild‐type embryo at the one‐cell stage. Then, embryos with mCherry + heart signal were selected to grow and selected as F0. F0 were then crossed with wild‐type fish to screen F1 which with bright mCherry + heart signal. Tg(uas:mjag2b‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry) stable transgenic line (F1) was used in this paper.

Generation of Tg (crx:gal4FF)

The 10 ng/μl plasmid pTol2‐ crx : gal4FF and 50 ng/μl tol2 mRNA were injected into Tg(uas:Kaede) embryo at the one‐cell stage. Zebrafish embryos with green fluorescent were grown and selected as F0. Generation of knock‐in fishline jag2b‐EGFP

We constructed a knock‐in fishline according to the previous paper (Yao et al , 2018 ). The 10 ng/μl linear donor DNA jag2b‐HAL‐3xFlag‐P2A‐EGFP‐vmhc:ECFP‐jag2b HAR , 200 ng/μl sgRNAs (These sgRNAs targeted jag2b stop codon region: sgRNA‐ki‐1: 5′‐GAGCTCCGATGAGCAGGACG‐3′; sgRNA‐ki‐2: 5′‐GGTCTCCTCGTCCTGCTCAT‐3′), and 400 ng/μl Cas9 protein were injected into wild‐type embryos at the one‐cell stage. Then, the embryos with ECFP + heart signal were grown and selected as F0. The adult F0 fish were crossed with wild‐type fish, and the fish with ECFP + heart signal offspring were selected to perform tail‐cutting genotyping by PCR the fragment crossing jag2b DNA sequence and donor cassette, the fish with correct PCR bands were selected as F1. This knock‐in fishline was named as jag2b‐ EGFP. In this paper, we used F4 stable knock‐in fishline.

Generation of mutant lines

We used the CRISPR/Cas9 system as described previously to perform the specific and rapid gene disruption and mutation fishline construction (Wu et al , 2018 ). The sgRNAs used were listed in Table EV1 . We mixed each sgRNA (100 ng/μl) for a specific gene with 400 ng/μl Cas9 protein and injected them into wild‐type embryos at the one‐cell stage. Adult fish were crossed with wild‐type fish, the offspring were screened by sequencing the sites crossing sgRNAs targeting regions. The fish with ORF shift offspring were selected and grown as F1. Mutants were verified by sequencing, primers used are listed in Table EV2 . To acquire fish in a good condition, we collected heterozygous fish for further studies.

Notch ligands or receptors mutation fish line identification:

We screen out each Notch ligands or receptors fishlines with ORF shift as Fig EV2B , then according to the mutation site to design primer F/R to identify the mutation fish line offspring by PCR, when WT primer pair strips positive, mutation primer pair strips negative, the fishes are WT fishes; when WT primer pair strips positive, mutation primer pair strips positive, the fishes are heterozygous fishes; when WT primer pair strips negative, mutation primer pair strips positive, the fishes are mutant homozygous fishes. All primers used are listed in Table EV2 .

Plasmid construction

Plasmid pT2K‐gfap:gal4FF was a gift from Dr. Toshio Ohshima (Waseda University, Tokyo, Japan) (Shimizu et al , 2015 ). To prepare BAC plasmid pTol2‐blbp:dsred , the dsred cassette was placed at the start‐codon site using a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) recombination technique (Suster et al , 2011 ). For the plasmids pTol2‐uas:mjag2b‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , pTol2‐crx:tdTomato‐nls , pTol2‐crx:gal4FF , pTol2‐Tp1:gal4 , pTol2‐Tp1:mNeonGreenCAAX , pTol2‐uas: H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , pTol2‐uas:dla‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry，pTol2‐uas:dlb‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , pTol2‐uas:dlc‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , pTol2‐uas:dld‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , pTol2‐uas:notch1b NICD‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , and pTol2‐uas:notch3 NICD‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , each DNA fragment cassettes was inserted into the pDestTol2pA2 vector (Kwan et al , 2007 ) using standard recombinant DNA techniques. In preparing plasmid pTol2‐uas:mjag2b‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , we synonymously mutated the target site of jag2b sgRNA‐1 and sgRNA‐2 in jag2b coding sequence in order to avoid self‐cutting. To prepare pTol2‐crx:gal4FF , pTol2‐crx:tdTomato‐nls plasmids, we utilized crx regulatory element (5 kb upstream of the start codon) (Suzuki et al , 2013 ) to drive the gal4FF or nucleus‐localized fluorescent protein tdTomato expression. To prepare pTol2‐Tp1:mNeonGreenCAAX , pTol2‐Tp1:gal4 plasmids, we used the vector from pTol2‐Tp1bglob:EGFP (Addgene plasmid #73586), then recombination with gal4 or mNeonGreenCAAX using ClonExpressMultiS One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, C113‐02). And to prepare pTol2‐uas:dla‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry，pTol2‐uas:dlb‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , pTol2‐uas:dlc‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , pTol2‐uas:dld‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , pTol2‐uas:notch1b NICD‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , pTol2‐uas:notch3 NICD‐P2A‐H2B‐ECFP‐vmhc:mCherry , 10× uas backbone was amplified from plasmid Tol2‐10× uas:loxp‐stop‐loxp‐tdtomato (Zhang et al , 2021 ), and dla/dlb/dlc/dld/notch3‐NICD codon were cloned with zebrafish cDNA.

For the plasmid 3xFlag‐P2A‐EGFP‐vmhc:ECFP , the jag2b homologous arm were amplified from the cDNA library of 48‐hpf zebrafish, and each DNA fragment was then inserted into pCS2 vector. The homolog recombination of each insert and vector was completed by using ClonExpressMultiS One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, C113‐02).

Gene disruption via the CRISPR/Cas9 system

To perform gene disruption, we took advantage of CRISPR‐cas9 system described previously (Wu et al , 2018 ; Xu et al , 2020 ). For dla , dlb , dlc , dld , jag1b , jag2b , notch1a , notch1b , notch3 , we designed four sgRNAs targeting the coding sequence of each gene (Table EV1 ). To generate the template for in vitro transcription of each sgRNA, we amplified DNA fragment with standard sgRNA scaffold (Kistler et al , 2015 ) using a mixture of four forward primers target one gene and one reverse primer (Wang et al , 2020 ). The sgRNAs were in vitro transcribed and purified using the LiCl precipitation approach (MEGAscript T7 Transcription Kit, Invitrogen, AM1334). To disrupt individual Notch ligand genes, we mixed four sgRNAs (in total 200 ng/μl) and Cas9 protein (400 ng/μl, Novoprotein, E365‐01A), and co‐injected them into the yolk of embryos at the one‐cell stage. The efficiency of each sgRNA for genes was shown in Fig EV2C .

Notch inhibition

To perform Notch inhibition, the Notch inhibitor DAPT (Final concentration of 50 μm, Sigma, D5942) or DMSO (Dimethyl sulfoxide, final concentration of 10 mM, Sigma, B8418) was applied freshly to the system water at 28°C incubator.

Cell‐type counting after jag2b disruption

We injected scramble / jag2b sgRNA to SoFa transgenic embryos at the one‐cell stage, then fixed embryo at 4 dpf at 4°C overnight. The embryos were then dehydrated in 30% sucrose and coronally sectioned with 20‐μm‐thick. After staining with DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole), these slices were imaged under the confocal microscope (Olympus FV1200) with 60× (Oil, NA = 1.30) objective. Visualization and measurement were performed according to the previous procedure (Almeida et al , 2014 ; Xu et al , 2020 ). The sections with the optic nerve were selected, and then, we cropped the regions of interest (ROIs) in a sector area, then count each retinal cell type based on the signals of SoFa fishlines.

Transplantation

We injected scramble / jag2b sgRNA and H2B‐RFP mRNA into Tg(Tp1:mVenus) transgenic embryo at the one‐cell stage. Then, these embryos were used as donors, wild‐type embryos were used as hosts, and we performed transplantation (He et al , 2012 ). At the blastula stage (4 hpf), the embryos were dechorionated by 0.3 mg/ml pronase and positioned in the custom‐made transplantation mold. Donor cells were transplanted into the animal pole of host embryos, The host embryos were then recovered at 32°C for 2 h before being returned to 28.5°C and screened on an upright fluorescent microscope at 24 hpf to select those with retinal clones.

Immunostaining

The immunostaining was performed according to the previous protocol (Tang et al , 2017 ). Fish were fixed in 4% PFA (paraformaldehyde, Electron Microscopy Services, USA, 157‐8), and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, then frozen and cryosectioned with 20 μm. The primary antibody mouse anti‐GS (glutamine synthase, 610518, BD Transduction Laboratories) was diluted at 1:500, and chicken anti‐GFP (ab13970, Abcam) was diluted at 1:1,000. All Alexa Fluor 488‐ or 647‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson Immuno Research Laboratories Inc) were diluted as 1:1,000.

In situ hybridization

In this study, we synthesized five probes to detect endogenous dla/dlb/dlc/dld/jag2b . The primers used to synthesize probes for each gene are listed in Table EV3 . Then, digoxigenin (DIG)‐labeled RNA probes were synthesized through T7 RNA polymerase (Promega, P2077) according to the manual.

In situ hybridization was performed according to the previous protocol (Tang et al , 2017 ), Fish were fixed in 4% PFA followed by dehydration in 30% sucrose and then were cryosectioned with 20 μm. On Day 1, 200 ng probes were applied to each slide. On Day 2, slides were incubated in TNB buffer with anti–DIG‐AP (1:500; Roche, 11093274910) at 4°C overnight. On Day 3, the signal was detected by the Fast Red TR/Naphthol AS‐MX Tablets (Sigma, F4648).

Imaging

For Kaede photoconversion, we used an inverted laser‐scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, FV1200) with 60× (Water, NA = 1.20) objectives. We applied a few pulses of 8% 405‐nm laser to single Kaede‐green cells to red fluorescence (Figs 1A–C and 5A and B ).

For live imaging, we collected embryos at the desired stages, anesthetized embryos with 0.04% MS‐222 (Tricaine mesylate, Sigma, A5040), and embedded them in 1% low‐melting agarose (Sigma, A0701). The inverted laser‐scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, FV1200) with 60 × objective (Water, NA = 1.20) was used.

For taking images of in situ hybridization and immunostaining, we also used an inverted laser‐scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, FV1200) with 60× (Water, NA = 1.20) objectives.

To image the knock‐in fishline jag2b ‐ EGFP , fish were embedded in 1% low‐melting agarose, and two‐photon microscopes (Olympus, BX61WI) with 25× (Water, NA = 1.05) objectives were used (Figs 3F and EV5F ).

Single‐cell suspension preparation

Retina single‐cell suspension was prepared according to the previous protocol (Lopez‐Ramirez et al , 2016 ). Retina was dissected and dissociated in 300 μl papain solution at 37°C for 15 min, followed by adding 1,200 μl washing buffer. Then, the cells were centrifuged with 800 g at 4°C for 5 min. Filter cells and washed with washing buffer again, we achieve single‐cell suspension.

RT–qPCR of FACS‐sorted MGs

To collect MGs, we crossed Tg(blbp:dsred) and Tg (Tp1bglob:VenusPEST) , and dissected retina the retinae of Tg ( blbp :dsred:: Tp1bglob :VenusPEST) at 60 hpf, 3 dpf, 5 dpf, and 7 dpf. Subsequently, we dissociated cells using the above procedures and sorted dsred + cells using FACS (Beckman, Moflo XDP). Interesting cells were collected into a 1.5‐ml tube containing lysis buffer (20 mg/ml PK in TE buffer). We then extracted the total RNA of MGs and carried out RT–qPCR of mVenus mRNA.

To collect knock‐in positive retinal cells, we dissected the retina at 3dpf with knock‐in jag2b‐Flag‐P2A‐EGFP . Then, we dissociated cells using the upper procedures and sorted EGFP + cells using FACS (Beckman, Moflo XDP). Interesting cells were collected into a 0.2‐ml tube containing lysis buffer (20 mg/ml PK in TE buffer).

Total RNA was isolated using lysis buffer (20 mg/ml PK in TE buffer). cDNA synthesis was performed by adding RT mix contains: 200 U Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, 18064‐014), 1 × First‐strand buffer (Invitrogen, 18064‐014), 5 mM DTT (Invitrogen, 18064‐014), 20 U Recombinant RNase inhibitor (Clontech, 2313A), 6 mM MgCl2 (Sigma, M8266), 1 μM TSO (Picelli et al , 2013 ); 8% PEG8000 (Sigma, P1458). PCR amplification was performed as previously described (Picelli et al , 2013 ).

Real‐time qPCR reactions were carried out using TB Green Premix Ex Taq mix (Takark, RR420A) on a LightCycler 480 II real‐time PCR detection system (Roche). qPCR primers are listed in Table EV4 . The ΔΔCt method was used to determine the relative expression of mRNAs in retinas at different development stages and normalized to actin mRNA levels. Individual comparisons were done using an unpaired 2‐tailed Student t ‐test. Error bars represented SEM. *** P < 0.001, ** P < 0.01, * P < 0.05, ns, P > 0.05.

Single‐cell RNA sequencing

We dissected the retinae from Tg(Tp1bglob:VenusPEST) and achieved single‐cell suspension using the upper procedures. To perform single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq), 13,000 cells were loaded onto the Chromium Single Cell Chip (10× Genomics, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The library was generated using the Single Cell 3' Library and Gel Bead kit v3.1 Chip kit (10× Genomics, 120237) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Library quantification and quality assessments were performed by Qubit fluorometric assay (Invitrogen) and the fragment analyzer with High Sensitivity Large Fragment 50 kb Analysis Kit (AATI, DNF‐464). The indexed library was sequenced by the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer with the S2 flow cell using paired‐end 150 modes.

Single‐cell sequencing data analysis

Single‐cell FASTQ sequencing reads were processed and converted to digital gene expression matrices after mapping to the zebrafish genome (Zv11) using the Cell Ranger Software (v3.1.0). 55,733 mean reads per cell and 1,462 mean genes per cell were obtained. For further analysis, we R package Seurat ( http://satijalab.org/seurat/ ). After filtering low‐abundance genes, cell doublets, and low‐quality libraries, 9,013 cells were normalized and scaled, and the top 71 principal components were used for the first round of clustering with the Louvain modularity algorithm (FindClusters function, resolution = 0.5) to generate 22 clusters (Fig 4A and B ). Finally, differential gene expression of each clusters were found using FindAllMarkers (parameters: min.pct = 0.1, min.diff.pct = 0.25).

Cell types of each cluster were annotated with well‐known retina cell markers, tSNE plot showing the major cell type in these data (Fig 3B and C ). To detect the gene expression of Notch ligands and receptors at 60 hpf, we superimposed these gene expressions and described their expression in Fig 3D .

Moreover, we also superimposed Notch ligands expression in our previous scRNA‐seq data at 3 dpf (Xu et al , 2020 ), and described their expression at 3 dpf in Fig EV3E .
