Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a severe X-linked recessive disorder marked
by progressive muscle wasting leading to premature mortality1,2. Discovery of
the DMD gene encoding dystrophin both revealed the cause of DMD and helped
identify a family of at least ten dystrophin-associated proteins at the muscle
cell membrane, collectively forming the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex
(DGC)3-9. The DGC links the extracellular matrix to the cytoskeleton, but,
despite its importance, its molecular architecture has remained elusive. Here we
determined the native cryo-electron microscopy structure of rabbit DGC and
conducted biochemical analyses to reveal its intricate molecular configuration.
An unexpected β-helix comprising β-, γ- and δ-sarcoglycan forms an extracellular
platform that interacts with α-dystroglycan, β-dystroglycan and α-sarcoglycan,
allowing α-dystroglycan to contact the extracellular matrix. In the membrane,
sarcospan anchors β-dystroglycan to the β-, γ- and δ-sarcoglycan trimer, while
in the cytoplasm, β-dystroglycan's juxtamembrane fragment binds dystrophin's ZZ
domain. Through these interactions, the DGC links laminin 2 to intracellular
actin. Additionally, dystrophin's WW domain, along with its EF-hand 1 domain,
interacts with α-dystrobrevin. A disease-causing mutation mapping to the WW
domain weakens this interaction, as confirmed by deletion of the WW domain in
biochemical assays. Our findings rationalize more than 110 mutations affecting
single residues associated with various muscular dystrophy subtypes and
contribute to ongoing therapeutic developments, including protein restoration,
upregulation of compensatory genes and gene replacement.

## Full Text

Among the various types of muscular dystrophy, DMD, an X-chromosome-linked muscle-wasting disease, is the most common and impacts approximately 1 in every 3,500–5,000 male newborns worldwide1. This devastating disease is characterized by progressive decline in muscle strength, beginning with mobility difficulties in childhood and eventually leading to fatal cardiac and/or respiratory failure at 20–40 years of age, even with optimal care2. DMD is a recessive disease caused by mutations that disrupt production of the dystrophin protein. Dystrophin is encoded by the largest known human gene, DMD, which comprises 79 exons and spans over 2,200 kb, approximately 0.1% of the human genome1,3. Localized to muscle fibres, dystrophin establishes, either directly or indirectly, interactions with the sarcolemma (that is, the specialized membrane of muscle cells), cytoskeleton componentry, channels and signalling proteins. These proteins together form the dystrophin-associated protein complex (DAPC)8,9, which has at its core the DGC4–7. DGC’s primary function in muscular tissue is to act as a ‘molecular shock absorber’, protecting the sarcolemma from contractile damage10. Beyond this, components of the DGC have been identified in various non-muscular tissues11, including the central nervous system, peripheral nerves and certain epithelial tissues, where they are involved in cell adhesion, signalling and structural support. The DGC is an evolutionarily conserved complex in metazoans and is encoded in the genomes of most animal species12.

The DGC at the sarcolemma contains ten core components4–7: dystrophin, two components in the dystroglycan (DG) subcomplex (α-DG and β-DG, derived from a single gene product through post-translational cleavage), four components in the sarcoglycan (SG) subcomplex (α-SG, β-SG, γ-SG and δ-SG), sarcospan, α-dystrobrevin and syntrophins. The DGC orchestrates cellular functions in three distinct regions: extracellularly (α-DG), in the transmembrane (TM) region (β-DG, SGs, sarcospan) and in the cytoplasm (dystrophin, α-dystrobrevin, syntrophins), as detailed below.

Extracellularly, highly O-glycosylated regions of α-DG7 serve as receptors for the ligands of the extracellular matrix (ECM). Post-translational modifications of DGs, including N- and O-linked glycosylation and proteolytic processing, have been demonstrated to be critical for DG functionality13–15. α-DG is also robustly associated with β-DG, a single-pass TM protein well known for its interaction with dystrophin7. Highlighting the central structural role of DG in muscle stability, mutations affecting DG frequently lead to dystroglycanopathies16,17, characterized by muscle degeneration and morphological brain and eye defects. Nonetheless, mutations affecting DG occur at a much lower frequency than in other DGC glycoproteins, revealing the vital role DG has in early embryonic development18.

Also spanning the membrane are SG’s four components and sarcospan. Like β-DG, SGs are all single-pass membrane proteins with long extracellular domains and relatively short cytoplasmic domains. α-SG, like β-DG, is a type I TM protein with a cleaved N-terminal signal sequence. By contrast, β-, γ- and δ-SG are all type II TM proteins with extracellularly oriented C termini. γ- and δ-SG exhibit mutually similar protein sequences and gene structures, and both have weak sequence similarity to β-SG8. All four SGs are tightly associated with one another, with mutations mapping to γ-, α-, β- and δ-SG causing the four limb-girdle muscular dystrophy (LGMD) subtypes—2C, 2D, 2E and 2F, respectively19–24. The SGs assemble and translocate in the myofibre as a complex, and the absence of any individual subunit adversely impacts the stability and trafficking of the unmutated SG proteins19–26. The other TM protein, sarcospan, is a member of the tetraspanin family27. Sarcospan is predicted to contain four TM α-helices and two extracellular loops and is known to closely engage with the SG complex in the sarcolemma28. Mutations affecting sarcospan initially seem harmless, but their effects compound over time, as sarcospan-deficient mice showed a substantial reduction in DGC levels and muscle deficiencies with increasing age29–31. Moderately increasing sarcospan expression levels in mice disrupts sarcospan–SG interaction, which can lead to a severe phenotype similar to that observed in mouse models of congenital muscular dystrophy32.

On the sarcolemma’s cytoplasmic side, dystrophin preserves its membrane localization through interaction with β-DG33–38. Dystrophin binds to the intracellular actin network to link the cytoskeleton to the DGC, which in turn connects to the basal lamina by association with ECM ligands. α-Dystrobrevin, a dystrophin paralogue, interacts with dystrophin through the C-terminal coiled-coil regions in each protein39, anchoring α-dystrobrevin to the sarcolemma. α-Dystrobrevin-deficient mice exhibit skeletal and cardiac myopathies40, defects in maturation of neuromuscular junctions41 and abnormal myotendinous junctions42, but its absence does not impact the assembly of other DGC components at the sarcolemma40. Syntrophin, which recruits multiple important signalling molecules, was also reported to localize to the sarcolemma by interacting with the C-terminal domain of dystrophin43.

Understanding muscular dystrophy mechanistically depends on understanding the atomic structures of the native DGC with its components as they exist in muscle fibres. However, despite over three decades of investigation, efforts to examine the molecular architecture of the DGC at the structural level have been limited to crystallographic or nuclear magnetic resonance studies of recombinantly produced individual domains44–47. As a result, how the various DGC components interact and how they are post-translationally modified have remained unknown. In this study, we have determined the cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of DGC isolated directly from rabbit skeletal muscle. Integrating this structure with prior data and our structure-guided mutagenesis data reveals the intricate molecular configuration of the DGC, providing insights into the comprehension of molecular pathogenesis and laying a foundation for prospective therapeutic interventions for muscular dystrophy.

The β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer has a distinctive domain architecture integrating structural elements that are reminiscent of bacteriophage tailspike50 and exosialidase51. The configuration exhibits maximum dimensions of 35 Å in width (head diameter) and 170 Å in length along the three-fold symmetry axis of the heterotrimer.The N-terminal TM domain of the trimer, referred to as the grip, forms a twisted three-helix bundle. The central arm of the trimer, spanning over 105 Å, is built by the intertwining triple β-helix interrupted by two small triple β-prism domains (Fig. 2a, left and middle right). The C-terminal head of the trimer features a core with two β-strands (Cβ1 and Cβ2) from each monomer (Fig. 2a, top right). The extended C-terminal loop of each monomer, characterized by two intramolecular cysteine pairs, ensures monomer stability and contributes to formation of the triangular linkage through main chain contacts with the neighbouring C-terminal loop (Fig. 2a, top right). SG complex: the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG β-helix binds α-SG

In the SG complex, the type II TM proteins β-, γ- and δ-SG collectively adopt a boomerang-shaped silhouette, delineated into a ‘grip’, an ‘arm’ and a ‘head’ to reflect the various sections of a boomerang (Fig. 2a, left). The β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer has a distinctive domain architecture integrating structural elements that are reminiscent of bacteriophage tailspike50 and exosialidase51. The configuration exhibits maximum dimensions of 35 Å in width (head diameter) and 170 Å in length along the three-fold symmetry axis of the heterotrimer.

The N-terminal TM domain of the trimer, referred to as the grip, forms a twisted three-helix bundle. This region incorporates a hydrophilic cluster characterized by three pairs of neutral asparagine and threonine residues, probably facilitating the alignment of and interactions among the β-, γ- and δ-SG proteins (Fig. 2a, bottom right). The central arm of the trimer, spanning over 105 Å, is built by the intertwining triple β-helix interrupted by two small triple β-prism domains (Fig. 2a, left and middle right). The C-terminal head of the trimer features a core with two β-strands (Cβ1 and Cβ2) from each monomer (Fig. 2a, top right). The intermolecular interaction involves each Cβ1 strand forming main chain interactions with the Cβ1 strands from the other two monomers, resulting in an interlocked triangular linkage. The extended C-terminal loop of each monomer, characterized by two intramolecular cysteine pairs, ensures monomer stability and contributes to formation of the triangular linkage through main chain contacts with the neighbouring C-terminal loop (Fig. 2a, top right). The importance of these cysteines was illustrated by the observation that single-residue substitution of either C283Y in γ-SG or C307R in β-SG is sufficient to cause LGMD52,53 (Fig. 2d). The interlocked C-terminal head aligns with previous observations that proper assembly of most triple β-helices depends on the initial folding of a C-terminal extension, which precedes a ‘zipping up’ of the respective stalk domains54. Overall, the structural integrity of the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer is summarily maintained by interactions along the length of its grip–arm–head sections, in line with the topology of previously characterized pathogenic single-residue mutants that now, in light of our structure, suggest pathogenesis by triggering triplex unfolding (Fig. 2d).

Intriguingly, the trimer’s β-helix undergoes a pronounced curvature around the midpoint of the arm, precisely between β-prism 2 and β-helix 2. A structural comparison of β-, γ- and δ-SG indicates that their structural differences are primarily located between amino acids 183 and 196 (β-SG numbering) (Fig. 2b). Notably, residues 183–196 of β-SG contain four additional residues (amino acids 184–187) compared with the corresponding region in γ- or δ-SG (Fig. 2c, top). This four-residue insertion and the aromatic nature of its first residue are remarkably conserved across the β-SG proteins of various species (Fig. 2c, bottom), indicating that the insertion may have a crucial role in maintaining curvature of the β-helix. Furthermore, AlphaFold’s structural predictions for the speculative trimeric configurations of γ- and δ-SG consistently yield a β-helix lacking the characteristic curvature (Extended Data Fig. 4). Supporting the importance of the β-helix curvature, point mutations clustered around Y184 in β-SG invariably lead to LGMD (Fig. 2d).

In contrast to the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer, α-SG is a type I TM protein with an N-terminal CDHL domain, followed by a closely associated SEA domain, a 40-residue-long loop and a C-terminal single-pass TM helix (Fig. 2e, left and middle). The CDHL domain comprises seven antiparallel β-strands (designated A to G) arranged in a ‘Greek key’ topology, reminiscent of the immunoglobulin fold (Fig. 2e, top left). The subsequent SEA domain adopts a ferredoxin-like fold with a βαββαβ sequential arrangement at its core, but it lacks the SEA domain’s canonical G|SΦΦΦ motif (where Φ is a hydrophobic residue and | indicates a cleavage site) between β2 and β3 for autoproteolysis (Fig. 2e, bottom left).

α-SG establishes lengthwise interactions with β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer, spanning approximately 150 Å across the grip and arm regions of the SG trimer (Fig. 2e, middle). These interactions involve hydrophobic contacts around the loop in CDHL between βC and βD and hydrophilic interactions around the loop in CDHL between βF and βG (Fig. 2e, top right). In proximity to the TM grip region, a 19-residue loop of α-SG, referred to as the TM-proximal loop, closely associates with the surface of β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer constituted by β-prism 1, β-helix 1 and β-prism 2 (Fig. 2e, middle right), aligning with the results from our co-immunoprecipitation assay (Fig. 2g and Extended Data Fig. 5). Additionally, the TM helix of α-SG engages in direct hydrophobic contacts with δ-SG in the helix bundle of the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer. Previous biochemical data support the robust hydrophobic interaction between α-SG and the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer, demonstrating that α-SG remained associated with β- and γ-SG despite harsh treatment with the detergent n-octyl-β-d-glucoside55.

Overall, within the SG complex, β-, γ- and δ-SG proteins assemble into a tightly packed triple β-helix structure through extensive main chain hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, while α-SG establishes hydrophobic interactions with this β-helix that are less pronounced, yet still considerable (Fig. 2e, right). In line with this observation, previous reports found that only β-, γ- and δ-SG proteins could be cross-linked both in vitro55,56 and in vivo52, despite the fact that all four SG subunits were co-purified as a subcomplex from rabbit skeletal muscle. Furthermore, the β-SG–δ-SG interaction exhibits resilience to elevated SDS concentrations (0.4% SDS), in contrast to α-SG, which undergoes dissociation under milder conditions (0.3% SDS)52. Nevertheless, documented missense mutations affecting any one of the four SGs frequently lead to simultaneous loss of the other SGs in the sarcolemma19–24 and are responsible for LGMD (Fig. 2d,f).

DGs adorn β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG β-helix and contact α-SGIn our DGC structure, DG comprises two CDHL–SEA modules, an adjacent 37-residue loop sequence linking to the TM region, a TM helix and a C-terminal cytoplasmic fragment (Fig. 3a). This configuration closely resembles that of α-SG (Fig. 1d), as both are type I TM proteins. Of note, post-translational autoproteolysis occurs within DG’s second SEA domain (SEA2), leading to cleavage of DG into two subunits, α-DG and β-DG (Fig. 3a).Despite overall structural similarity among DGC’s three SEA domains (Cα root-mean-square deviation (r.m.s.d.) values from 2.1 to 3.2), these domains exhibit low sequence identity, ranging from 8% to 13% (Fig. 3b). Notably, DG’s SEA2 domain lacks an intervening loop in comparison to the other SEA domains (Fig. 3b,e). This aligns with the observation that only SEA2 in DG features the canonical cleavage site G|SΦΦΦ typical of SEA domains (Fig. 3d,e), suggesting a mechanism in which proteolysis in the SEA domain is initiated by imposing conformational stress; by contrast, presence of a long intervening loop facilitates the release of mechanical stress and thereby abrogation of proteolysis in non-cleaving species57. Notably, three N-linked glycans in SEA2 are observed near DG’s cleavage site, with the glycan linked to residue N661 situated specifically between α-DG and β-DG (Fig. 3d). In line with this specificity, substitution of the equivalent glycosylated residue in chicks (N662) completely abolished DG autoproteolysis15, a condition known to cause muscular dystrophy13. As extracellular domains, the three SEA domains of the DGC display distinct patterning of N-glycan sites and disulfide bonds (Fig. 3e), indicating that they may have divergent functional roles.As with the SEA domains, the three CDHL domains in the DGC exhibit considerable structural similarities (Cα r.m.s.d. values from 2.2 to 2.6) but low sequence identity, ranging from 13% to 23% (Fig. 3c, left). A Dali search58 of CDHL2 in α-DG identified a glycoside hydrolase59 (Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID: 6DLH) as the closest structural match with a z score of 7.0 and an r.m.s.d. of 1.9 Å for 89 aligned residues (Fig. 3c, right). In this hydrolase, a Ca2+ ion was found bound to the loop between βB and βC, coordinated octahedrally by one water molecule and five oxygen ligands from the CDHL domain (Extended Data Fig. 6a, left). Superposing the two structures revealed a similar Ca2+-binding site in α-DG’s CDHL2 domain, which is noticeably absent in α-DG’s CDHL1 and α-SG’s CDHL domains (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 6a). In line with this structural observation, of the five coordination residues in the hydrolase, four corresponding residues are conserved exclusively in CDHL2, but not in CDHL1 or CDHL (Fig. 3f). Likewise, no notable sequence conservation was observed for neighbouring hydrophilic residues in either CDHL1 or CDHL (Extended Data Fig. 6b).Despite the structural similarities observed among the individual CDHL and SEA domains, the three CDHL–SEA modules differ substantially in the orientation between the CDHL and SEA domains (Extended Data Fig. 7, left). These specific orientations are upheld by distinct hydrophobic patches near CDHL’s β-sheet and SEA’s helix α1 (Fig. 3g). DGs adorn β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG β-helix and contact α-SG

In our DGC structure, DG comprises two CDHL–SEA modules, an adjacent 37-residue loop sequence linking to the TM region, a TM helix and a C-terminal cytoplasmic fragment (Fig. 3a). This configuration closely resembles that of α-SG (Fig. 1d), as both are type I TM proteins. Of note, post-translational autoproteolysis occurs within DG’s second SEA domain (SEA2), leading to cleavage of DG into two subunits, α-DG and β-DG (Fig. 3a).

Despite overall structural similarity among DGC’s three SEA domains (Cα root-mean-square deviation (r.m.s.d.) values from 2.1 to 3.2), these domains exhibit low sequence identity, ranging from 8% to 13% (Fig. 3b). Notably, DG’s SEA2 domain lacks an intervening loop in comparison to the other SEA domains (Fig. 3b,e). This aligns with the observation that only SEA2 in DG features the canonical cleavage site G|SΦΦΦ typical of SEA domains (Fig. 3d,e), suggesting a mechanism in which proteolysis in the SEA domain is initiated by imposing conformational stress; by contrast, presence of a long intervening loop facilitates the release of mechanical stress and thereby abrogation of proteolysis in non-cleaving species57. Notably, three N-linked glycans in SEA2 are observed near DG’s cleavage site, with the glycan linked to residue N661 situated specifically between α-DG and β-DG (Fig. 3d). In line with this specificity, substitution of the equivalent glycosylated residue in chicks (N662) completely abolished DG autoproteolysis15, a condition known to cause muscular dystrophy13. As extracellular domains, the three SEA domains of the DGC display distinct patterning of N-glycan sites and disulfide bonds (Fig. 3e), indicating that they may have divergent functional roles.

As with the SEA domains, the three CDHL domains in the DGC exhibit considerable structural similarities (Cα r.m.s.d. values from 2.2 to 2.6) but low sequence identity, ranging from 13% to 23% (Fig. 3c, left). A Dali search58 of CDHL2 in α-DG identified a glycoside hydrolase59 (Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID: 6DLH) as the closest structural match with a z score of 7.0 and an r.m.s.d. of 1.9 Å for 89 aligned residues (Fig. 3c, right). In this hydrolase, a Ca2+ ion was found bound to the loop between βB and βC, coordinated octahedrally by one water molecule and five oxygen ligands from the CDHL domain (Extended Data Fig. 6a, left). Superposing the two structures revealed a similar Ca2+-binding site in α-DG’s CDHL2 domain, which is noticeably absent in α-DG’s CDHL1 and α-SG’s CDHL domains (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 6a). In line with this structural observation, of the five coordination residues in the hydrolase, four corresponding residues are conserved exclusively in CDHL2, but not in CDHL1 or CDHL (Fig. 3f). Likewise, no notable sequence conservation was observed for neighbouring hydrophilic residues in either CDHL1 or CDHL (Extended Data Fig. 6b).

Despite the structural similarities observed among the individual CDHL and SEA domains, the three CDHL–SEA modules differ substantially in the orientation between the CDHL and SEA domains (Extended Data Fig. 7, left). These specific orientations are upheld by distinct hydrophobic patches near CDHL’s β-sheet and SEA’s helix α1 (Fig. 3g). Notably, the orientation between the CDHL and SEA domains in each module is non-interchangeable (Extended Data Fig. 7b), indicating the essential nature of each module’s unique orientation for maintaining the module’s interaction with the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG scaffold.

These unique orientations are involved in the differing interactions of DGs with SGs: β-DG lacks direct association with SG except for through its TM helix, whereas α-DG establishes major interactions with SG primarily mediated by loops in its CDHL2 and CDHL1 domains (Fig. 3a). Specifically, α-DG’s CDHL2 domain forms hydrophilic contacts with β-helix 2 of the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer. This interaction involves the CDHL2 loop between βC and βD, the loop preceding βA, and βF and βG, which in concert interact with an edge of the β-helix formed by the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer (Fig. 3h, left). Contacting the surface of the β-helix scaffold, α-DG’s CDHL2 domain also interfaces with α-SG’s CDHL domain through hydrophobic interaction between Y524 of CDHL2 and the L187-P188-I189 hydrophobic cluster of α-SG, as well as through an immediately adjacent hydrophilic region (Fig. 3i, left). Furthermore, the CDHL1 domain of α-DG establishes contacts with both the β-helix and C-terminal head domain of the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer while concurrently localizing in close proximity to α-SG (Fig. 3a).

In accordance with these structural observations, our co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed that DG’s CDHL2–SEA2 module interacted directly with both the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer and α-SG (Fig. 3h,i, right). Additionally, despite alanine substitution of three interface residues, DG maintained its interaction with the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer (Fig. 3h, right). Meanwhile, the association between DG and α-SG remained in the absence of β-, γ- and δ-SG and withstood a single-residue substitution in DG (Y524R) targeting the aforementioned hydrophobic interaction (Fig. 3i, right), indicating that the interactions noted above may be robust.

We conclude that the predominant interactions between DGs and SGs occur on the surface of the C-terminal half of the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer (Fig. 3a). The essential role of this region in facilitating DG interactions is evidenced by a premature truncation of γ-SG that results in the loss of more than half of γ-SG’s extracellular domain: this truncation leads to both LGMD and cardiomyopathy yet without affecting assembly of the SG complex60. This interaction probably contributes to the clustering of the three CDHL–SEA modules around the heavily O-glycosylated laminin-binding protein α-DG (Fig. 3a), corroborating the documented carbohydrate-binding capacity of both CDHL61,62 and SEA63 domains.

This observation aligns with previous biochemical findings that highlighted two phosphorylation sites (Y3352 and T3354) in the aforementioned loop of the dystrophin ZZ domain that are necessary for recognition of β-DG38. Here we unveiled the intricate molecular configuration of the DGC (Fig. 6) and spatially mapped more than 110 pathogenic single-residue alterations linked to various muscular dystrophy subtypes (Figs. 2d,f and 5i and Supplementary Table 1), enabling identification of therapeutic targets with which to restore folding or interactions in the DGC. Extracellularly, our structure reveals an unexpected assembly of β-SG, γ-SG and δ-SG that intertwine in a triple β-helix (Fig. 2a), conferring extraordinary stability to the trimeric scaffold. Of the pathogenic mutations, 31 map to the β-helix, probably impairing proper triplex folding (Fig. 2d). The robust elongated shape exhibits a pronounced curvature near the midpoint of the β-helix (Fig. 2a,b) and enables β-, γ- and δ-SG to protrude from the sarcolemma. The curvature of the β-helix may enhance its mechanical properties by increasing flexibility and resilience, thus reducing localized stress concentrations and improving the protein’s capacity to withstand shear forces. This supports DGC’s role as a molecular shock absorber protecting the sarcolemma during muscle contractions10. Consistent with this, single-residue alterations in β-SG (Y184C, T182P, T182A, S181R, F180L) around the β-helix curvature invariably result in LGMD (Fig. 2d). The protrusion indicates that β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer may serve as a platform to bridge the DGC and the ECM, analogous to how triple β-helix-containing virulence factors (frequently found projecting from the surface of pathogens) orchestrate interaction with host lipopolysaccharides50,75–77. Indeed, the membrane-distal region of the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer exclusively engages three CDHL–SEA modules from DGs and α-SG (Fig. 3a) implicated in carbohydrate-binding events61–63. Both the specific orientation of the CDHL and SEA domains in each module (Fig. 2g and Extended Data Fig. 7) and the contact of CDHL domains with the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer (Figs. 2e and 3h) promote assembly of CDHL–SEA modules around the heavily O-glycosylated laminin-binding α-DG, probably positioning the unresolved mucin region of α-DG to bind to the ECM. Across the sarcolemma, the cytoplasmic fragment K780–K794 of β-DG directly interacts with β8 and its following loop in dystrophin’s ZZ domain (Fig. 5h), establishing a crucial link to intracellular actin. Furthermore, the cytoplasmic segments of α- and δ-SG make direct contact with dystrophin’s ZZ domain (Fig. 5a,i). Evidencing its critical function, dystrophin’s ZZ domain is susceptible to missense mutations that result in DMD or BMD (Fig. 5i).

Upregulation of compensatory genes is being pursued as a strategy to develop treatment options for all DMD cases, regardless of the specific mutation affecting dystrophin. Sarcospan overexpression in DMD mouse models improves membrane integrity and mitigates disease severity, highlighting its potential as a target for pharmacological upregulation78,79. Our results contribute to these efforts by correcting four prior assumptions concerning sarcospan’s interactions with SGs and DGs. First, sarcospan’s TM3 and TM4 motifs do not make direct contact with SG (γ-SG) in the sarcolemma but instead connect through the mediator β-DG TM helix (Fig. 4a, bottom right). Second, no contact is observed between sarcospan and α-SG, indicating that SG–sarcospan loss in α-SG-null mice may be due to destabilization of the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer, not direct interaction68,80. Third, although previous literature has proposed that sarcospan might act as a chaperone for α-DG during endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi trafficking31, our structural analysis now shows that sarcospan’s association with α-DG is indirect, mediated instead through β-DG. Fourth, although a model has been proposed in which sarcospan forms homo-oligomers to cluster DGC components in the membrane67, our structure reveals that a single sarcospan in the DGC binds β-DG and β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer (Fig. 4a). This aligns with reports that mild sarcospan upregulation enhances DGC levels but tenfold upregulation disrupts SG–sarcospan interactions32,69.

A more direct DMD treatment strategy is gene therapy using micro/mini-dystrophin transgenes, which fundamentally requires accurate identification of essential regions of dystrophin to engineer into viral vectors81. Although large portions of dystrophin’s central rod domain (the spectrin repeats in Fig. 6) can be omitted, debate exists around removing the coiled-coil-containing C-terminal domain, which has been reported to be essential for interaction with α-dystrobrevin39. Of note, the C termini of dystrophin and α-dystrobrevin are not visible in our map, probably owing to flexibility. Instead, we observe a substantial rotation of dystrophin’s WW domain towards its EF1 domain (Fig. 5g) as compared to a reported structure of dystrophin with β-DG’s C-terminal peptide44. This exposes a new interface between dystrophin and α-dystrobrevin (Fig. 5d), which was verified through our co-immunoprecipitation and confocal assays, showing that deletion of dystrophin’s WW domain or even a single G3052D substitution in the WW domain (G3059D in human protein, which causes BMD) was sufficient to decrease dystrophin’s interaction with α-dystrobrevin (Fig. 5e,f). In line with our observation, dystrophin’s cysteine-rich region exhibits remarkable conservation across species12 and has been shown to eliminate fatality in dystrophin–utrophin double-null mice, even without the C-terminal domain82. Thus, this region probably represents the minimal segment required for dystrophin in DGC assembly74. Indeed, mutations affecting the cysteine-rich region often result in severe DMD (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Table 1), underscoring this region as an indispensable element for designing micro- and mini-dystrophin constructs to treat DMD.

In summary, the native DGC structure reported here maps numerous pathogenic mutations and highlights critical therapeutic targets for various muscular dystrophies. Given the DGC’s high degree of sequence conservation across a broad spectrum of metazoans12, this structure provides a robust framework that explains a large body of previous functional data and paves the way for therapeutic developments against the devastating disease of muscular dystrophy.

MethodsPreparation of rabbit skeletal muscle membranesThe heavy microsomes from rabbit skeletal muscle were prepared as previously described83, with certain modifications. Model building and refinement

To facilitate modelling process for DGC, a total of three CDHL–SEA modules from DG (two) and α-SG (one) were initially predicted using neural network-based modelling Colab AlphaFold2 (refs. 96,97) with high confidence score (pLDDT). Among them, the CDHL2–SEA2 module of DG and CDHL–SEA α-SG were rigidly docked into the low-pass-filtered map of the 4.3 Å map using Chimera98, delineating the extracellular, cytoplasmic and TM regions of the DGC map. The above model enabled us to identify additional densities in the extracellular segment of DGC, with the majority conforming to a curved β-helix. Traversing the secondary structures of all extracellular domains in the DGC (four SGs and two DGs), we observed that substantial β-strands were present only in β-, γ- and δ-SG. Indeed, an AlphaFold3 prediction99 of the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer encompassing its TM and extracellular domain revealed an intertwining β-helix that fits into the extracellular segment of the DGC map.

In the membrane, adjacent to the TM helices of the β-SG–γ-SG–δ-SG trimer, we observed additional densities corresponding to two TM helices connecting to DG’s CDHL2–SEA2 module and α-SG’s CDHL–SEA module, respectively. Furthermore, in close proximity to the TM helix of DG, additional densities corresponding to four TM helices were identified. Through AlphaFold prediction and rigid docking, these TM helices were subsequently identified as the tetra-membrane-spanning sarcospan. In the cytoplasm, the cysteine-rich regions of dystrophin and α-dystrobrevin were predicted using AlphaFold and rigidly docked into the map and manually adjusted using Coot100. Despite high structural similarity, dystrophin’s cysteine-rich region exhibited additional densities for the WW domain, enabling differentiation between dystrophin and α-dystrobrevin.

The central regions of the cryo-EM map, ranging in resolution from 3.5 to 4.5 Å (Extended Data Fig. 2e), exhibited sufficient detail for manual tracing and de novo model building using Coot. Sequence assignment primarily relied on discernible densities corresponding to amino acid residues with bulky side chains, such as tryptophan, tyrosine, phenylalanine and arginine (Extended Data Fig. 3). In the extracellular domain, the identification of asparagine residues carrying N-linked glycans contributed to the assignment process (Fig. 1e). Distinctive patterns in sequence segments containing these residues served as benchmarks for validating residue assignments. Furthermore, secondary structure predictions using PSIPRED 4.0 (ref. 101) also guided the model building process.

Resolutions for the peripheral regions of the cryo-EM map were insufficient for de novo atomic modelling. In the final model of the DGC, the following regions were built with AlphaFold modelling and rigid docking: CDHL1–SEA1 of α-DG (tentative model), four TM helices of sarcospan, the TM helix of α-SG, the ZZ, EF-hand and WW domains of dystrophin and α-dystrobrevin. In addition, we tentatively traced the main chain of the stretched loop (residues 720–740) between the CDHL2–SEA2 module and the TM helix of β-DG. No densities for the residues of the following domains were observed in our cryo-EM map: the mucin-like domain of α-DG; the N-terminal ABD, spectrin repeats and coiled-coil-containing C-terminal tail of dystrophin; and the coiled-coil-containing C-terminal tail of α-dystrobrevin.

The DGC model was refined using Phenix in real space102 with secondary structure and geometry restraints. Refinement statistics are summarized in Extended Data Table 1. The model was also evaluated on the basis of MolProbity scores103 and Ramachandran plots (Extended Data Table 1). Representative densities are shown in Extended Data Fig. 3. All structure-related images in this paper were generated using UCSF ChimeraX104 and Chimera.
