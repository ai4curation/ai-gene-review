Ninjurin-1 (NINJ1) is an active executioner of plasma membrane rupture (PMR), a
process previously thought to be a passive osmotic lysis event in lytic cell
death. Ninjurin-2 (NINJ2) is a close paralog of NINJ1 but cannot mediate PMR.
Using cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM), we show that NINJ1 and NINJ2 both
assemble into linear filaments that are hydrophobic on one side but hydrophilic
on the other. This structural feature and other evidence point to a PMR
mechanism by which NINJ1 filaments wrap around and solubilize membrane fragments
and, less frequently, form pores in the plasma membrane. In contrast to the
straight NINJ1 filament, the NINJ2 filament is curved toward the intracellular
space, preventing its circularization or even assembly on a relatively flat
membrane to mediate PMR. Mutagenesis studies further demonstrate that the NINJ2
filament curvature is induced by strong association with lipids, particularly a
cholesterol molecule, at the cytoplasmic leaflet of the lipid bilayer.

ResultsIn vitro purified NINJ2 forms linear oligomersHuman NINJ1 and NINJ2 share 52% sequence identity (FigureS1), yet NINJ2 is not capable of mediating PMR, nor showing cytotoxicity when overexpressed in HEK293T cells, as NINJ1 is3. Since it has been demonstrated that NINJ1-mediated PMR is correlated with NINJ1 oligomerization3, we wanted to test if NINJ2 is capable of oligomerization or not. We transiently expressed N-terminally 3xFLAG-tagged human NINJ2 in suspension Expi293F cells, and purified it from the membrane fraction with detergent solubilization followed by peptidisc reconstitution22. A very broad peak was observed in size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) (FigureS2A), indicating large variation in size of the product. Indeed, cryo-EM of three representative fractions showed a spectrum of oval to belt-like particles in 2D class averaging, indicating linear assembly of a helix-rich subunit in various lengths (FigureS2B). In summary, in vitro purified NINJ2 forms linear oligomers, likely in a similar fashion as NINJ1.Cryo-EM structure of the NINJ2 oligomerTo understand the inter-molecular interactions responsible for NINJ1 or NINJ2 oligomerization, we sought to determine atomic structures of those oligomers using cryo-EM. Considering the severe cytotoxicity of NINJ1 overexpression3 and its low yield in purification, we used NINJ2 to explore different strategies of membrane protein purification to get a more homogeneous sample suitable for high-resolution cryo-EM structure determination. With many trials, we found that after detergent solubilization and affinity purification of NINJ2, exchanging the detergent with amphipol using a stepwise dilution method (see detail in Materials and Methods) helped to generate relatively long and uniform NINJ2 oligomers, as indicated by a sharp peak close to the void volume in the SEC profile (FigureS2C). Negative-staining EM and cryo-EM showed both straight and slightly curved thin filaments in the length of 50–70 nm (FigureS2D and S2E). Cryo-EM 2D class averages suggested that the straight ones were the top or bottom views of the filament if we define the curved ones as the side views (Figure1A). Initial cryo-EM data processing indicated that the particles had preferred orientations. By collecting more data with the sample tilted to 30°, and carefully balancing the number of particles from different views to be included for 3D reconstruction, we managed to obtain a structure of the NINJ2 oligomer at 3.1 Å resolution (Figure1B and 1C, FigureS3A–D), which enabled us to build a reliable atomic model (Figure1D, TableS1).The NINJ2 oligomer looked like a slightly arched bridge (Figure 1B), with two sidewalls built up by crosslinked NINJ2 subunits running in anti-parallel directions (Figure 1D), sandwiching two layers of lipid as the deck of the bridge. Geometry of the two lipid layers was obviously similar to that of a lipid bilayer in a biological membrane (Figure1C and 1D), suggesting that they were endogenous lipids tightly associated with the protein and survived detergent solubilization. As topology of NINJ2 was predicted to be extracellular for both the N-terminus and the C-terminus, after modeling the protein densities, we were able to differentiate the two lipid layers and we refer to them as “exoplasmic leaflet” and “cytoplasmic leaflet” in our following discussions (Figure1D).Cryo-EM structure of the NINJ1 oligomerWe applied the same strategy of detergent solubilization followed by amphipol exchange to purify NINJ1 oligomers for cryo-EM structure determination. Our initial trials always ended up with very low yield when we used the cellular fraction for purification as we did for NINJ2 (FigureS5A). Inspired by the observation that NINJ1 overexpression frequently led to spontaneous PMR3 and cell lysis, we turned to the cell culture media fraction for NINJ1 purification. After pelleting the cells, we ultracentrifuged the media fraction and collected small debris in there for detergent solubilization. Indeed, the yield of NINJ1 was boosted to a similar level as that of NINJ2 purified from the cell bodies (FigureS2C and S2F). This observation suggested that most of the NINJ1 protein was released into the media because of spontaneous PMR induced by NINJ1 overexpression.The purified NINJ1 oligomers looked similar to those of NINJ2 under negative-staining EM and cryo-EM with filamentous shape, but they appeared to be slightly skinnier and less curved compared to the NINJ2 oligomers (FigureS2D vs S2G, and FigureS2E vs S2H). The differences became more prominent when cryo-EM 2D class averages were compared (Figure1A vs 1E). Of particular interest, the side view of NINJ1 filament was straight (Figure1E), in stark contrast to the curved side view of NINJ2 (Figure1A). Structure determination of the NINJ1 filament was done in a similar way as NINJ2, with tilted images combined with non-tilted ones to cope with the preferred-orientation problem. Resolution of the final density map was 2.8 Å (FigureS3E–H). As expected, the NINJ1 filament shared the same architecture as NINJ2, with two anti-parallel layers of protein sandwiching a thin lipid bilayer (Figure1F–H). In addition to the different curvatures, another notable difference between the two filaments was in their associated lipids, as described in detail below.Molecular interactions responsible for the oligomerization of NINJ1 or NINJ2Despite the different curvatures of NINJ1 and NINJ2 oligomers, their individual subunits are highly similar in structure (Figure3G), so are their intermolecular interactions responsible for the oligomerization. Both NINJ1 and NINJ2 were predicted to have an extracellular N-terminal coil, an amphipathic helix (AH), followed by two transmembrane helices (named TM1 and TM2 here), and an extracellular C-terminus. Human NINJ2 has 142 residues, while NINJ1 has 152, with a major insertion in the N-terminal coil (FigureS1). However, for the regions that were well resolved in our structures, from Asn39 to Phe139 for NINJ1 and Asn25 to Phe125 for NINJ2 (Figure2A and Figure2G), every residue could be matched from one to the other (FigureS1) and each pair were located at roughly the same position, even for those in the loop regions (Figure3G). The root mean square deviation (RMSD) for the 101 pairs of C-alpha atoms was measured to be 0.792 Å. For sake of clarity, in the following descriptions, NINJ1 sequence would be used, with the corresponding NINJ2 sequence given in parenthesis. The Alphafold2-predicted NINJ1 (or NINJ2) structure23 fitted amazingly well to our cryo-EM density map in the TM1 and TM2 regions, but the predicted single amphipathic helix was divided into two short ones in the real structure, named AH1 and AH2 here, with a nearly 90-degree kink around residue Met51 (Met37) (Figure2A and Figure2G). AH2 bundled with the upper half of the transmembrane helices at the interface of two neighboring subunits, while AH1 extended transversely to the lower-middle part of the transmembrane helices in a neighboring subunit (Figure2A and Figure2G). Intuitively, this crosslinking by AH1 would allow slight hinged-movement at the kink while maintaining the integrity of the filamentous assembly, explaining the lateral flexibility of the filament as described later.As expected, hydrophobic interactions are abundant between transmembrane helices within the subunit, across neighboring subunits, and with the hydrophobic side of AH2 or AH1 (Figure2B, 2D and Figure2H, 2J). However, we believe that interactions between a few polar/charged residues on the TM helices and AH1/AH2 are of particular importance, as these interactions, once formed, are resistant to the solvation effect of the lipid environment as suffered by the hydrophobic interactions; therefore, they likely make the filament assembly an irreversible process. In this regard, Asn60 (Asn46) on AH2 seemed to be particularly important as it formed a network of hydrogen bonds, likely mediated by a H2O molecule, with a highly conserved triad of polar residues, Ser87 (Ser73), Gln91 (Gln77) on TM1 and Asn133 (Asn119) on TM2 (Figure2C and Figure2I). These interactions were within the same subunit. On AH1, Lys44 (Lys30) formed hydrogen bond with Asn119 (Asn105) on TM2, and Lys45 (Lys31) formed salt bridge with Asp53 (Asp39) in the kink region, of the neighboring subunit (Figure2E and Figure2K). At the very end of AH1, the sidechain of Asn39 (Asn25) formed hydrogen bond with the backbone carboxyl of Leu52 (Leu38) in the kink region of the neighboring subunit. As an additional consequence, this interaction seemed to have resulted in the rotation of the protein backbone, which in turn directed the remaining N-terminal coil region towards the side of the filament and avoided steric hindrance with the AH1 of the next subunit (Figure2E and Figure2K). It is worth noting that all these residues discussed above are completely conserved among NINJ1 and NINJ2 sequences in human, mouse and rat (FigureS1).Folding of AH1 and AH2 to one side of the transmembrane helices concealed most of the hydrophobic residues on that side. The remaining surface-exposed residues on TM1, TM2, AH1, and AH2 were less conserved compared to those discussed above, but they were more-or-less limited to charged, polar, or only moderately hydrophobic ones. Indeed, surface rendering of the filamentous assembly of NINJ1 or NINJ2 showed a highly hydrophilic surface facing the bulk solution, in stark contrast to the other side that remains highly hydrophobic and binds tightly to the lipid-bilayer (Figure2F and Figure2L). As discussed later, this distinctive surface property of NINJ1 filament has great implication in its function of mediating PMR.Associated lipids in NINJ1 and NINJ2 filamentsNINJ1 and NINJ2 filaments had apparent differences in their associated lipids. Overall, NINJ1 filament trapped less lipids in between its two protein layers compared to NINJ2. This explained why NINJ1 filaments appeared slightly skinner than NINJ2 filaments in negative staining EM (FigureS2G vs S2D) and cryo-EM 2D class averages (Figure1E vs 1A) as described above.At the exoplasmic leaflet of the trapped bilayer, lipid densities were relatively weak and continuous in NINJ2 (Figure3D and FigureS4B), but strikingly strong and in a punctate distribution in NINJ1, with six aliphatic chains discernible in each punctum (Figure3A and FigureS4A). Each of these aliphatic chain densities in NINJ1 could find a match in NINJ2 at the corresponding position relative to the protein sidewalls. It could be regarded as if one extra layer of lipids were trapped in the middle of the NINJ2 filament (FigureS4). The higher intensity of lipid densities in the exoplasmic leaflet of NINJ1 suggested their tighter association with the protein compared to those in NINJ2. We propose that four highly conserved (in mouse, human, and rat) sequence variations between NINJ1 and NINJ2 may have contributed to this difference in lipid association at the exoplasmic leaflet. These include Phe76, Val78, Pro79 and Val82 in NINJ1, versus Tyr62, Thr64, Thr65 and Thr68 in NINJ2 (Figure3B and 3E). In NINJ1, the aromatic ring of Phe76 was more or less in the same plane as those of Phe139 and Phe135, and the three were in turn stacked with one aliphatic chain of the associated lipids (Figure3B). At corresponding positions in NINJ2, the aromatic rings of Tyr62, Phe125 and Phe121 were not so well aligned, likely due to the polarity of Tyr62 (Figure3E). A similar stacking effect was observed between the aliphatic chain and Pro79 in NINJ1 (Figure3B), which was replaced by polar residue Thr65 in NINJ2 (Figure3E). Obviously, replacing the hydrophobic Val78 and Val82 in NINJ1 (Figure3B) by polar residues Thr64 and Thr68 in NINJ2 (Figure3E) would also reduce the strength of lipid association at the exoplasmic leaflet.At the cytoplasmic leaflet, each subunit of NINJ2 was associated with three lipid densities, including one very prominent cholesterol-like density and two slightly weaker, aliphatic chain-like densities (Figure3D and FigureS4B). While in NINJ1, only one lipid density was associated with each subunit (Figure3A and FigureS4A). We modeled this density as a cholesterol for its slightly planar shape and short stature (Figure3C). The most significant sequence variation responsible for this discrepancy in lipid association at the cytoplasmic leaflet was probably Gln103 of NINJ2 versus Phe117 of NINJ1. Gln103 could form hydrogen bond with the hydroxyl group of an incoming cholesterol (Figure3F), while the bulky Phe117 sterically hindered the binding of a cholesterol at this place (Figure3C). Interestingly, this sequence variation between human NINJ1 and NINJ2 seems to be conserved in mouse and rat, even though in mouse NINJ2 the glutamine has been replaced by a lysine (FigureS1), which could also form hydrogen bond with the hydroxyl group of a cholesterol. On the other transmembrane helix (TM1) of NINJ1, the bulky sidechain of Phe100 (corresponding to Val86 in NINJ2) may have also blocked the association of a lipid at that position (Figure3C and 3F). As a result, three associated lipids observed in NINJ2 were reduced to only one in NINJ1 (Figure3F vs 3C). Other conserved sequence variations between NINJ1 and NINJ2, including Gly93 versus Val79, Leu121 versus Ala107, and Gly124 versus Ile110 might have also played a minor role in the different lipid-association patterns at the cytoplasmic leaflet (Figure3C and 3F).Lipid association affects filament curvature and PMR capability of NINJ1 and NINJ2The fact that NINJ1 and NINJ2 filaments were highly similar in protein-protein interactions prompted us to hypothesize that it was their difference in lipid association that resulted in their different curvatures and eventually their different capabilities of mediating PMR. To validate this hypothesis, we swapped the above-described lipid-interacting residues between NINJ1 and NINJ2 (Figure4A) and tested their effects on filament assembly and PMR capability (Figure4B and 4C). As demonstrated before, PMR capability is correlated with cytotoxicity, which can be measured and quantified with a “killing score”3. For simplicity, those mutants with four mutations in lipid-interacting residues at the exoplasmic leaflet are named M4exo, those with five mutations in lipid-interacting residues at the cytoplasmic leaflet are named M5cyto, and those with all nine mutations combined are named M9. As expected, the NINJ1-M9 mutant behaved more or less like NINJ2-WT (wild type), with slightly curved filaments and a killing score near zero. However, to our surprise, the NINJ2-M9 mutant did not behave like NINJ1-WT, as its killing score remained low. A purification trial indicated that it completely failed to assemble into filaments. With a more careful inspection of the structure, we speculated that the V86F mutation in NINJ2-M9 might have caused steric hindrance with the bulky sidechain of Trp99 (corresponding to Ala113 in NINJ1) from a neighboring subunit, preventing the filament assembly. Indeed, NINJ2-M9+W99A mutant partially gained PMR capability, with a killing score close to 0.5 (the killing score of NINJ1-WT was defined as 1). Purification of this mutant yielded a mixture of highly skewed filaments and many small disc-like structures. As explained later, those discs were likely PMR products and they were only seen in NINJ1-WT purifications but not in NINJ2-WT purifications. Therefore, NINJ2-M9+W99A at least partially copied the phenotype of NINJ1-WT.Next, we tested partial mutants M4exo and M5cyto to assess the effects of lipid-association at the two leaflets separately. NINJ1-M4exo and NINJ2-M5cyto mutants are nearly equivalent in lipid-binding residues, and according to our hypothesis, they would have weakened lipid-association at both the exoplasmic leaflet and the cytoplasmic leaflet. To our surprise, NINJ1-M4exo maintained a relatively high killing score (~0.5), and produced NINJ1-WT like filaments and discs in purification. It seems that the very strong lipid-association at the exoplasmic leaflet in NINJ1 may have contributed to, but is not essential for, its PMR capability. Unlike NINJ1-M4exo, NINJ2-M5cyto did not gain in killing score and it produced highly skewed filaments in purification. However, as NINJ2-M5cyto might have also suffered from steric hindrance between bulky sidechains like NINJ2-M9, no solid conclusion could be drawn from it. In place of that, we constructed NINJ2-Q103F single-point mutation that would specifically disrupt the strong cholesterol binding at the cytoplasmic leaflet. As expected, NINJ2-Q103F gained a significant rise in killing score (to ~0.4), and consistent with that, its purification product was rich in short, straight filaments and triangular, disc-like particles, all indicating its PMR capability. These observations suggest that the strong lipid-association at the cytoplasmic leaflet, particularly binding with the cholesterol molecule, has a dominant negative effect on the PMR capabilities of NINJ1 or NINJ2. This dominant negative effect was further validated in NINJ1-M5cyto, NINJ2-M4exo, and NINJ1-F117Q mutants, all of which would have strong lipid-association at both the exoplasmic leaflet and the cytoplasmic leaflet. Indeed, all three constructs behaved similarly with low killing scores and their purification products were a mixture of irregular aggregates and short filaments. A plausible explanation is that strong lipid-association at the two leaflets are incompatible with each other and their competition would disrupt the assembly of relatively long, straight filaments needed for mediating PMR.Combining the above mutagenesis results with structural information, we could explain mechanistically how lipid association affects NINJ1 or NINJ2 filament curvature. The bundling of AH2 with the upper half of the TM helices made the NINJ1 or NINJ2 subunit more or less like an inverted trapezoid, with the upper half slightly wider than the lower half (Figure3G–I). When such building blocks are put together in a row, they would line up straight if their upper halves were closely packed and lower halves slightly gapped; alternatively, if their lower halves were more compactly packed than their upper halves, they would form an arch or even a circle (Figure3I). Obviously, these two cases represent the NINJ1 and NINJ2 filaments, respectively. In NINJ1, the spacing of neighboring subunits was measured to be 21.3 Å on the top [average distance between alpha-carbons (Cα) of Ser73 in neighboring subunits], 21.1 Å at the waist (between Cα of Phe127), and 21.0 Å at the bottom (between Cα of Ala110) (Figure3H). In contrast, the spacing in NINJ2 filament was measured to be 23.2 Å on the top (between Cα of Ser59), 21.4 Å at the waist (between Cα of Phe113), and 19.7 Å at the bottom (between Cα of Glu96) (Figure3H). It could be regarded as if the distance between neighboring subunits in the curved NINJ2 filament narrowed by 1.6 Å at the bottom and widened by 1.6 Å on the top compared to a hypothetic, straight NINJ2 filament. This rigid-body transformation was likely induced by the tightly associated lipids at the cytoplasmic leaflet, particularly the cholesterol molecule that sits at the boundary of two neighboring subunits (Figure3D and 3I). In this regard, these lipids act like “molecular glues” to stick together the lower halves of NINJ2 and maintain the curvature of the NINJ2 filament. The tightly associated lipids at the exoplasmic leaflet in NINJ1 may also function as molecular glues to strengthen the interactions between the upper halves of the subunits, but our mutagenesis studies suggest that they are not essential for the assembly of a straight NINJ1 filament. As a default, NINJ1 or NINJ2 would assemble into straight filaments, unless there is strong lipid association at the cytoplasmic leaflet to redirect the assembly towards a curved filament.How NINJ1 mediates PMR and why NINJ2 cannotAlthough our NINJ1 and NINJ2 structures were both double-layered filaments, barely any protein-protein interactions across the two layers were observed. The two layers were held together merely by hydrophobic interactions with the lipids trapped in between, suggesting that a single-layered filament would be expected in a native membrane environment. Interestingly, some single-layered NINJ1 filaments we occasionally observed in the cryo-EM micrographs appeared to be much more flexible than the double-layered ones, and at least a few times longer than the average length of most double-layered filaments (FigureS5D). We postulate that the single-layered filaments would become stiffened and straightened when they come together to form the double-layered filaments, similar to the case that double-stranded DNA has a persistence length over 60 times greater than that of a single-stranded DNA24,25. The stiffness of the double-layered filament would make it more prone to be broken into short ones by the shearing force of the solution during sample handling compared to the flexible, single-layered filament.The lateral flexibility of the single-layered NINJ1 filament and its distinctive surface property with one hydrophobic side and one hydrophilic side are reminiscent of membrane scaffold proteins (MSP) widely used in making lipid nanodiscs for cryo-EM structural studies of membrane proteins26. This hints for a PMR mechanism by which a NINJ1 filament wraps around a membrane fragment to “solubilize” it from the plasma membrane, making the membrane leaky to large molecules such as the DAMPs. Indeed, lipid nanodisc-like assemblies were abundantly observed in negative-staining EM micrographs of purified NINJ1 samples (Figure4C, S2G and S5C), but not in NINJ2 samples (Figure4C and S2D). Those discs were capped by single-layered NINJ1 filaments at the edge, as evidenced in some discs with excess filament coming together to form a double-layered “tail” (FigureS5C). Although rarely, some discs had a polygonal, instead of roundish, shape, suggesting that they were capped by several short NINJ1 filaments instead of one continuous filament (FigureS5C). This observation implies that the size of a NINJ1-solublized membrane fragment can be bigger than what an average-length NINJ1 filament can encircle. In summary, our observations strongly support that membrane solubilization is a possible mechanism for NINJ1-mediated PMR.During our manuscript preparation, a 3.8 Å resolution cryo-EM structure of human NINJ1 expressed in Escherichia coli was reported by Degen et al.27. The bacterial-derived NINJ1 filament had a very similar structure to ours from the mammalian cell, although the two layers were more closely packed as barely any lipids were trapped in between. However, the atomic model reported in the Degen paper (PDB 8CQR) had a three- or four-residue shift in registering in the two amphipathic helices. This discrepancy in modeling was likely due to the limited resolution of their cryo-EM map, instead of a true structural difference. Interestingly, because the shift in registering was just about one helical turn, it did not affect much the sidechain distribution on the surface of the NINJ1 filament. Indeed, the hydrophilic nature of that side of the NINJ1 filament was correctly recognized in the Degen paper. However, in the Degen paper, a mechanism similar to that of pore-forming proteins, such as gasdermins, perforins or bacterial toxins, was proposed for NINJ1-mediated PMR, by which a ring of NINJ1 filament perforates the membrane with their hydrophobic side capping the membrane edge and their hydrophilic side facing the pore27. Obviously, the topology of the NINJ1 filament in this pore formation model was just opposite to that in our membrane solubilization model. In retrospect, we did observe many ring-like structures in the negative staining EM when we purified NINJ1 from the cellular fraction (FigureS5B), consistent with this pore formation model. However, considering that many times more NINJ1 was recovered from the media fraction than from the cellular fraction (FigureS5A), we believe that membrane solubilization is the major effect of NINJ1 in mediating PMR and pore formation plays a minor role. Other recent work also observed that NINJ1 cuts and releases membrane disks, which the authors compared to a cookie cutter28.Curving of the NINJ2 filament towards the intracellular space is incompatible with membrane solubilization or pore formation, as the two ends of a curved NINJ2 filament would not be able to join each other in the same plane via lateral flexibility. Furthermore, due to the limited lateral flexibility, one would expect that only NINJ1/NINJ2 filaments longer than a certain length would be able to circularize and induce pore formation or membrane solubilization. However, curvature of NINJ2 may prevent the assembly of a long filament in a regular cell with relatively flat plasma membrane, as this would result in the two ends of the filament poking into the cytoplasm. To quantify the curvature of the NINJ2 filament, we fitted many copies of the NINJ2 density map head-to-tail and obtained a spring-shaped virtual assembly (Figure3J). The diameter of this spring was measured to be about 45 nm. In contrast, a typical animal cell measures 10–20 microns in diameter. Therefore, the plasma membrane of a regular cell is too flat to accommodate a curved NINJ2 filament. However, it is intriguing to think that NINJ2 filament might be able to mediate membrane segmentation in a highly curved or tubular membrane, e.g., the processes of glia cells or the axons of neurons. Another possibility is that NINJ2 may serve as a negative regulator of NINJ1 function. As their structures and intermolecular interactions are highly similar, NINJ2 might be able to incorporate into a growing NINJ1 filament and stop/slow down its further extension. The sequence variations between human NINJ1 and NINJ2 are mostly well conserved in mouse and rat (FigureS1), suggesting that NINJ2 might have evolved to have a specialized function instead of being a defective copy of NINJ1.

Cryo-EM structure of the NINJ2 oligomerTo understand the inter-molecular interactions responsible for NINJ1 or NINJ2 oligomerization, we sought to determine atomic structures of those oligomers using cryo-EM. Molecular interactions responsible for the oligomerization of NINJ1 or NINJ2Despite the different curvatures of NINJ1 and NINJ2 oligomers, their individual subunits are highly similar in structure (Figure3G), so are their intermolecular interactions responsible for the oligomerization. Both NINJ1 and NINJ2 were predicted to have an extracellular N-terminal coil, an amphipathic helix (AH), followed by two transmembrane helices (named TM1 and TM2 here), and an extracellular C-terminus. Human NINJ2 has 142 residues, while NINJ1 has 152, with a major insertion in the N-terminal coil (FigureS1). However, for the regions that were well resolved in our structures, from Asn39 to Phe139 for NINJ1 and Asn25 to Phe125 for NINJ2 (Figure2A and Figure2G), every residue could be matched from one to the other (FigureS1) and each pair were located at roughly the same position, even for those in the loop regions (Figure3G). The root mean square deviation (RMSD) for the 101 pairs of C-alpha atoms was measured to be 0.792 Å. For sake of clarity, in the following descriptions, NINJ1 sequence would be used, with the corresponding NINJ2 sequence given in parenthesis. The Alphafold2-predicted NINJ1 (or NINJ2) structure23 fitted amazingly well to our cryo-EM density map in the TM1 and TM2 regions, but the predicted single amphipathic helix was divided into two short ones in the real structure, named AH1 and AH2 here, with a nearly 90-degree kink around residue Met51 (Met37) (Figure2A and Figure2G). AH2 bundled with the upper half of the transmembrane helices at the interface of two neighboring subunits, while AH1 extended transversely to the lower-middle part of the transmembrane helices in a neighboring subunit (Figure2A and Figure2G). Intuitively, this crosslinking by AH1 would allow slight hinged-movement at the kink while maintaining the integrity of the filamentous assembly, explaining the lateral flexibility of the filament as described later.As expected, hydrophobic interactions are abundant between transmembrane helices within the subunit, across neighboring subunits, and with the hydrophobic side of AH2 or AH1 (Figure2B, 2D and Figure2H, 2J). However, we believe that interactions between a few polar/charged residues on the TM helices and AH1/AH2 are of particular importance, as these interactions, once formed, are resistant to the solvation effect of the lipid environment as suffered by the hydrophobic interactions; therefore, they likely make the filament assembly an irreversible process. In this regard, Asn60 (Asn46) on AH2 seemed to be particularly important as it formed a network of hydrogen bonds, likely mediated by a H2O molecule, with a highly conserved triad of polar residues, Ser87 (Ser73), Gln91 (Gln77) on TM1 and Asn133 (Asn119) on TM2 (Figure2C and Figure2I). These interactions were within the same subunit. On AH1, Lys44 (Lys30) formed hydrogen bond with Asn119 (Asn105) on TM2, and Lys45 (Lys31) formed salt bridge with Asp53 (Asp39) in the kink region, of the neighboring subunit (Figure2E and Figure2K). At the very end of AH1, the sidechain of Asn39 (Asn25) formed hydrogen bond with the backbone carboxyl of Leu52 (Leu38) in the kink region of the neighboring subunit. As an additional consequence, this interaction seemed to have resulted in the rotation of the protein backbone, which in turn directed the remaining N-terminal coil region towards the side of the filament and avoided steric hindrance with the AH1 of the next subunit (Figure2E and Figure2K). It is worth noting that all these residues discussed above are completely conserved among NINJ1 and NINJ2 sequences in human, mouse and rat (FigureS1).Folding of AH1 and AH2 to one side of the transmembrane helices concealed most of the hydrophobic residues on that side. The remaining surface-exposed residues on TM1, TM2, AH1, and AH2 were less conserved compared to those discussed above, but they were more-or-less limited to charged, polar, or only moderately hydrophobic ones. Indeed, surface rendering of the filamentous assembly of NINJ1 or NINJ2 showed a highly hydrophilic surface facing the bulk solution, in stark contrast to the other side that remains highly hydrophobic and binds tightly to the lipid-bilayer (Figure2F and Figure2L). As discussed later, this distinctive surface property of NINJ1 filament has great implication in its function of mediating PMR.

The higher intensity of lipid densities in the exoplasmic leaflet of NINJ1 suggested their tighter association with the protein compared to those in NINJ2. We propose that four highly conserved (in mouse, human, and rat) sequence variations between NINJ1 and NINJ2 may have contributed to this difference in lipid association at the exoplasmic leaflet. A similar stacking effect was observed between the aliphatic chain and Pro79 in NINJ1 (Figure3B), which was replaced by polar residue Thr65 in NINJ2 (Figure3E). The most significant sequence variation responsible for this discrepancy in lipid association at the cytoplasmic leaflet was probably Gln103 of NINJ2 versus Phe117 of NINJ1. Gln103 could form hydrogen bond with the hydroxyl group of an incoming cholesterol (Figure3F), while the bulky Phe117 sterically hindered the binding of a cholesterol at this place (Figure3C). On the other transmembrane helix (TM1) of NINJ1, the bulky sidechain of Phe100 (corresponding to Val86 in NINJ2) may have also blocked the association of a lipid at that position (Figure3C and 3F). As a result, three associated lipids observed in NINJ2 were reduced to only one in NINJ1 (Figure3F vs 3C). Other conserved sequence variations between NINJ1 and NINJ2, including Gly93 versus Val79, Leu121 versus Ala107, and Gly124 versus Ile110 might have also played a minor role in the different lipid-association patterns at the cytoplasmic leaflet (Figure3C and 3F).

Lipid association affects filament curvature and PMR capability of NINJ1 and NINJ2The fact that NINJ1 and NINJ2 filaments were highly similar in protein-protein interactions prompted us to hypothesize that it was their difference in lipid association that resulted in their different curvatures and eventually their different capabilities of mediating PMR. To validate this hypothesis, we swapped the above-described lipid-interacting residues between NINJ1 and NINJ2 (Figure4A) and tested their effects on filament assembly and PMR capability (Figure4B and 4C). As demonstrated before, PMR capability is correlated with cytotoxicity, which can be measured and quantified with a “killing score”3. For simplicity, those mutants with four mutations in lipid-interacting residues at the exoplasmic leaflet are named M4exo, those with five mutations in lipid-interacting residues at the cytoplasmic leaflet are named M5cyto, and those with all nine mutations combined are named M9. As expected, the NINJ1-M9 mutant behaved more or less like NINJ2-WT (wild type), with slightly curved filaments and a killing score near zero. However, to our surprise, the NINJ2-M9 mutant did not behave like NINJ1-WT, as its killing score remained low. A purification trial indicated that it completely failed to assemble into filaments. With a more careful inspection of the structure, we speculated that the V86F mutation in NINJ2-M9 might have caused steric hindrance with the bulky sidechain of Trp99 (corresponding to Ala113 in NINJ1) from a neighboring subunit, preventing the filament assembly. Indeed, NINJ2-M9+W99A mutant partially gained PMR capability, with a killing score close to 0.5 (the killing score of NINJ1-WT was defined as 1). Purification of this mutant yielded a mixture of highly skewed filaments and many small disc-like structures. As explained later, those discs were likely PMR products and they were only seen in NINJ1-WT purifications but not in NINJ2-WT purifications. Therefore, NINJ2-M9+W99A at least partially copied the phenotype of NINJ1-WT.Next, we tested partial mutants M4exo and M5cyto to assess the effects of lipid-association at the two leaflets separately. NINJ1-M4exo and NINJ2-M5cyto mutants are nearly equivalent in lipid-binding residues, and according to our hypothesis, they would have weakened lipid-association at both the exoplasmic leaflet and the cytoplasmic leaflet. To our surprise, NINJ1-M4exo maintained a relatively high killing score (~0.5), and produced NINJ1-WT like filaments and discs in purification. It seems that the very strong lipid-association at the exoplasmic leaflet in NINJ1 may have contributed to, but is not essential for, its PMR capability. Unlike NINJ1-M4exo, NINJ2-M5cyto did not gain in killing score and it produced highly skewed filaments in purification. However, as NINJ2-M5cyto might have also suffered from steric hindrance between bulky sidechains like NINJ2-M9, no solid conclusion could be drawn from it. In place of that, we constructed NINJ2-Q103F single-point mutation that would specifically disrupt the strong cholesterol binding at the cytoplasmic leaflet. As expected, NINJ2-Q103F gained a significant rise in killing score (to ~0.4), and consistent with that, its purification product was rich in short, straight filaments and triangular, disc-like particles, all indicating its PMR capability. These observations suggest that the strong lipid-association at the cytoplasmic leaflet, particularly binding with the cholesterol molecule, has a dominant negative effect on the PMR capabilities of NINJ1 or NINJ2. This dominant negative effect was further validated in NINJ1-M5cyto, NINJ2-M4exo, and NINJ1-F117Q mutants, all of which would have strong lipid-association at both the exoplasmic leaflet and the cytoplasmic leaflet. Indeed, all three constructs behaved similarly with low killing scores and their purification products were a mixture of irregular aggregates and short filaments. A plausible explanation is that strong lipid-association at the two leaflets are incompatible with each other and their competition would disrupt the assembly of relatively long, straight filaments needed for mediating PMR.Combining the above mutagenesis results with structural information, we could explain mechanistically how lipid association affects NINJ1 or NINJ2 filament curvature. The bundling of AH2 with the upper half of the TM helices made the NINJ1 or NINJ2 subunit more or less like an inverted trapezoid, with the upper half slightly wider than the lower half (Figure3G–I). When such building blocks are put together in a row, they would line up straight if their upper halves were closely packed and lower halves slightly gapped; alternatively, if their lower halves were more compactly packed than their upper halves, they would form an arch or even a circle (Figure3I). Obviously, these two cases represent the NINJ1 and NINJ2 filaments, respectively. In NINJ1, the spacing of neighboring subunits was measured to be 21.3 Å on the top [average distance between alpha-carbons (Cα) of Ser73 in neighboring subunits], 21.1 Å at the waist (between Cα of Phe127), and 21.0 Å at the bottom (between Cα of Ala110) (Figure3H). In contrast, the spacing in NINJ2 filament was measured to be 23.2 Å on the top (between Cα of Ser59), 21.4 Å at the waist (between Cα of Phe113), and 19.7 Å at the bottom (between Cα of Glu96) (Figure3H). It could be regarded as if the distance between neighboring subunits in the curved NINJ2 filament narrowed by 1.6 Å at the bottom and widened by 1.6 Å on the top compared to a hypothetic, straight NINJ2 filament. This rigid-body transformation was likely induced by the tightly associated lipids at the cytoplasmic leaflet, particularly the cholesterol molecule that sits at the boundary of two neighboring subunits (Figure3D and 3I). In this regard, these lipids act like “molecular glues” to stick together the lower halves of NINJ2 and maintain the curvature of the NINJ2 filament. The tightly associated lipids at the exoplasmic leaflet in NINJ1 may also function as molecular glues to strengthen the interactions between the upper halves of the subunits, but our mutagenesis studies suggest that they are not essential for the assembly of a straight NINJ1 filament. As a default, NINJ1 or NINJ2 would assemble into straight filaments, unless there is strong lipid association at the cytoplasmic leaflet to redirect the assembly towards a curved filament.

Lipid association affects filament curvature and PMR capability of NINJ1 and NINJ2

The fact that NINJ1 and NINJ2 filaments were highly similar in protein-protein interactions prompted us to hypothesize that it was their difference in lipid association that resulted in their different curvatures and eventually their different capabilities of mediating PMR. To validate this hypothesis, we swapped the above-described lipid-interacting residues between NINJ1 and NINJ2 (Figure4A) and tested their effects on filament assembly and PMR capability (Figure4B and 4C). As demonstrated before, PMR capability is correlated with cytotoxicity, which can be measured and quantified with a “killing score”3. For simplicity, those mutants with four mutations in lipid-interacting residues at the exoplasmic leaflet are named M4exo, those with five mutations in lipid-interacting residues at the cytoplasmic leaflet are named M5cyto, and those with all nine mutations combined are named M9. As expected, the NINJ1-M9 mutant behaved more or less like NINJ2-WT (wild type), with slightly curved filaments and a killing score near zero. However, to our surprise, the NINJ2-M9 mutant did not behave like NINJ1-WT, as its killing score remained low. A purification trial indicated that it completely failed to assemble into filaments. With a more careful inspection of the structure, we speculated that the V86F mutation in NINJ2-M9 might have caused steric hindrance with the bulky sidechain of Trp99 (corresponding to Ala113 in NINJ1) from a neighboring subunit, preventing the filament assembly. Indeed, NINJ2-M9+W99A mutant partially gained PMR capability, with a killing score close to 0.5 (the killing score of NINJ1-WT was defined as 1). Purification of this mutant yielded a mixture of highly skewed filaments and many small disc-like structures. As explained later, those discs were likely PMR products and they were only seen in NINJ1-WT purifications but not in NINJ2-WT purifications. Therefore, NINJ2-M9+W99A at least partially copied the phenotype of NINJ1-WT.

Next, we tested partial mutants M4exo and M5cyto to assess the effects of lipid-association at the two leaflets separately. NINJ1-M4exo and NINJ2-M5cyto mutants are nearly equivalent in lipid-binding residues, and according to our hypothesis, they would have weakened lipid-association at both the exoplasmic leaflet and the cytoplasmic leaflet. To our surprise, NINJ1-M4exo maintained a relatively high killing score (~0.5), and produced NINJ1-WT like filaments and discs in purification. It seems that the very strong lipid-association at the exoplasmic leaflet in NINJ1 may have contributed to, but is not essential for, its PMR capability. Unlike NINJ1-M4exo, NINJ2-M5cyto did not gain in killing score and it produced highly skewed filaments in purification. However, as NINJ2-M5cyto might have also suffered from steric hindrance between bulky sidechains like NINJ2-M9, no solid conclusion could be drawn from it. In place of that, we constructed NINJ2-Q103F single-point mutation that would specifically disrupt the strong cholesterol binding at the cytoplasmic leaflet. As expected, NINJ2-Q103F gained a significant rise in killing score (to ~0.4), and consistent with that, its purification product was rich in short, straight filaments and triangular, disc-like particles, all indicating its PMR capability. These observations suggest that the strong lipid-association at the cytoplasmic leaflet, particularly binding with the cholesterol molecule, has a dominant negative effect on the PMR capabilities of NINJ1 or NINJ2. This dominant negative effect was further validated in NINJ1-M5cyto, NINJ2-M4exo, and NINJ1-F117Q mutants, all of which would have strong lipid-association at both the exoplasmic leaflet and the cytoplasmic leaflet. Indeed, all three constructs behaved similarly with low killing scores and their purification products were a mixture of irregular aggregates and short filaments. A plausible explanation is that strong lipid-association at the two leaflets are incompatible with each other and their competition would disrupt the assembly of relatively long, straight filaments needed for mediating PMR.

Combining the above mutagenesis results with structural information, we could explain mechanistically how lipid association affects NINJ1 or NINJ2 filament curvature. The bundling of AH2 with the upper half of the TM helices made the NINJ1 or NINJ2 subunit more or less like an inverted trapezoid, with the upper half slightly wider than the lower half (Figure3G–I). When such building blocks are put together in a row, they would line up straight if their upper halves were closely packed and lower halves slightly gapped; alternatively, if their lower halves were more compactly packed than their upper halves, they would form an arch or even a circle (Figure3I). Obviously, these two cases represent the NINJ1 and NINJ2 filaments, respectively. In NINJ1, the spacing of neighboring subunits was measured to be 21.3 Å on the top [average distance between alpha-carbons (Cα) of Ser73 in neighboring subunits], 21.1 Å at the waist (between Cα of Phe127), and 21.0 Å at the bottom (between Cα of Ala110) (Figure3H). In contrast, the spacing in NINJ2 filament was measured to be 23.2 Å on the top (between Cα of Ser59), 21.4 Å at the waist (between Cα of Phe113), and 19.7 Å at the bottom (between Cα of Glu96) (Figure3H). It could be regarded as if the distance between neighboring subunits in the curved NINJ2 filament narrowed by 1.6 Å at the bottom and widened by 1.6 Å on the top compared to a hypothetic, straight NINJ2 filament. DiscussionThe importance of maintaining plasma membrane integrity cannot be overstated for any cell, so the action of NINJ1 must be tightly regulated. NINJ1 is constitutively expressed on the plasma membrane of many, if not all, types of cells. In a healthy cell, they must adopt a relatively stable conformation (referred to as the “resting state” herein) that prevents them from spontaneously transitioning into the filament structure as seen here. From a structural point of view, the key is to hide the AH-binding interface of the TM helices so that the AH cannot easily get access to it. Moreover, on this side of the TM helices, there are a bunch of polar residues, such as Ser87, Gln91 and Asn133 (the “triad”), that are interacting with polar residues on the AH (e.g., Asn60 on AH2) in the filament structure (Figure2C) but need to be masked from the hydrophobic membrane environment by other means in the resting state. We propose that a simplest solution to these two conundrums is to form homo-dimers of NINJ1, with their AH-binding interface of the TM helices facing each other and the polar residues interacting with each other across the two subunits at the cleft. Indeed, an AlphaFold-Multimer29 predicted NINJ1 dimer (with their N-terminal and AH regions removed, as otherwise the program tended to predict helix bundle between the TM region and the AH if the full-length sequence was used) seemed to be consistent with this hypothesis (FigureS6A). Furthermore, when we predicted the structure of a NINJ1 tetramer, three of the five top-ranking candidates were a dimer of dimer (FigureS6B). Interestingly, the dimer-dimer interface in this predicted structure was essentially the bundling of TM helices in a way very similar to that observed in the NINJ1 filament structure. Intuitively, this points to a further hypothesis that the NINJ1 homo-dimers might be able to oligomerize linearly (FigureS6C). When the cell is triggered for lytic cell death, the plasma membrane is ballooned and stretched. This may somehow promote the clustering of NINJ1 dimers and small oligomers of dimer into a longer chain of dimer, which is like a zipper on a bag. The lateral tension of the ballooned plasma membrane would be pulling from the two sides of the zipper, weakening the interactions across the central line. As the tension grows beyond a certain point, the zipper may split open, allowing the amphipathic helices to fold down and crosslink the neighboring subunits. A pore would form and expand as this splitting-and-crosslinking reaction propagates through the entire length of the zipper. If all NINJ1 subunits in the zipper happen to be crosslinked, a ring of NINJ1 with a pore in the middle would be left on the plasma membrane. Consistent with this hypothesis, previous blue-native PAGE analysis of endogenous NINJ1 in unstimulated bone marrow-derived macrophages showed a ladder of bands (see Figure 4c in reference 3), with the smallest one roughly corresponding to the expected molecular weight of a NINJ1 dimer.

Even though more experimental evidence is needed to confirm the existence of NINJ1 dimer and oligomer of dimer at the resting state, this hypothesis is very tempting as it provides a possible explanation how NINJ1 rings and discs are formed at the same time with opposite topology (Figure5). When the cell is triggered for lytic cell death, the plasma membrane is ballooned and stretched. This may somehow promote the clustering of NINJ1 dimers and small oligomers of dimer into a longer chain of dimer, which is like a zipper on a bag. The lateral tension of the ballooned plasma membrane would be pulling from the two sides of the zipper, weakening the interactions across the central line. As the tension grows beyond a certain point, the zipper may split open, allowing the amphipathic helices to fold down and crosslink the neighboring subunits. A pore would form and expand as this splitting-and-crosslinking reaction propagates through the entire length of the zipper. If all NINJ1 subunits in the zipper happen to be crosslinked, a ring of NINJ1 with a pore in the middle would be left on the plasma membrane. Otherwise, the broken ring of NINJ1 would continue to extend into a filament and eventually wrap around a membrane fragment with its hydrophobic side, simply driven by thermodynamics. Conceivably, it is a relatively rare event for every subunit in the zipper to be crosslinked, particularly at the two ends, explaining the less abundance of NINJ1 rings compared to NINJ1 discs. Essentially, this mechanism of action relies on the physical strength of plasma membrane ballooning, explaining the inertia of NINJ1 in a healthy cell and its bursting action at the last moment of a dying cell.

It was recently shown that glycine treatment protected cells against plasma membrane rupture by preventing the action of NINJ130. It seems possible in the context of our hypothesis that glycine may stabilize the NINJ1 dimers and make the zipper more difficult to split open. Specifically, glycine might replace the water molecule associated with Ser87, Gln91 and Asn133 (Figure2C) and form more hydrogen bonds to mediate their interactions across the two subunits in the dimer. Future structural studies of NINJ1 at the resting state with or without glycine treatment may shed more light on this hypothesis.

Limitations of the StudyHere we try to deduce the possible mechanism of action for NINJ1-mediated plasma membrane rupture based on observations on the final products of this complicated process. Although our model seems to be self-consistent and supported by some indirect evidence such as structure predictions and clues from previous studies, it is imperative to have solid experimental evidence to confirm it. In this case, a real structure of NINJ1 homodimer at the resting state is the key. Unfortunately, the small size of NINJ1 and its lability to detergent solubilization at the resting state present a great challenge to structure determination with cryo-EM. A recently reported monoclonal antibody blocking mouse NINJ1 oligomerization and plasma membrane rupture31 might be a useful tool to stabilize NINJ1 in its resting state, and more importantly, to make a larger complex for cryo-EM structural studies.

Frames were averaged with dose weighting33 and 2x binning, producing micrographs with a pixel size of 0.66Å/pix. To cope with strong preferred-orientation observed in both NINJ1 and NINJ2 samples, we also collected data with the sample tilted to 30° and combined it with untilted data.

Cryo-EM data processing was performed with cryoSPARC34 following the procedure for helical reconstruction, but no helical symmetry was applied throughout the data processing. Separation distance between segments in filament tracing was set to be 27.5Å (half of diameter, which was set to be 55Å), slightly larger than the 22Å separation distance of neighboring subunits as measured from initial reconstructions. To balance the number of particles from different views and alleviate the preferred-orientation artifact in reconstruction, only a portion of the highest-quality “top-view” particles were selected for reconstruction. Statistics of the datasets and structures are detailed in Table S1.

Firstly, lentivirus transfer constructs were prepared by restriction free cloning (using GeneArt™ Gibson Assembly HiFi Master Mix) of the NINJ1 and NINJ2 WT/mutant ORFs from the pcDNA3.1(-) vectors to pLL3.7 vector under the CMV promoter. Lentiviruses were harvested 72hr post transfection, filtered, and concentrated by centrifugation. A control batch of GFP-expressing lentiviruses were always included as control to visually inspect the quality and titer of lentivirus preparations using a fluorescence microscope.
