SUMMARY

Synthetic polymers have transformed modern life, giving rise to a wide spectrum of versatile materials commonly known as plastics. They are essential to industries including packaging, medical devices, automotive, textiles, and many consumer goods. However, significant environmental challenges have emerged because of the same properties that make plastics so useful. Of the estimated 400–450 million tons (Mt) of plastics produced each year, nearly 80 percent end up in the environment. Many of these plastics will persist in nature for hundreds or even thousands of years because they are mostly not biodegradable or poorly biodegradable. The identification of polymer-active microorganisms and enzymes that target most fossil fuel-based plastics is one of the greatest challenges microbiologists are facing today. Currently, more than 255 functionally verified plastic-active enzymes from more than 11 microbial phyla are known. Here, we summarize current knowledge on the microbial pathways and enzymes involved in the degradation of polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polyamide (PA) oligomers, ester-based polyurethane (PUR), and polycarbonates (PC), as well as some of the most widely used bioplastics. We also highlight the challenges microbiologists face in identifying microorganisms acting on highly persistent commodity polymers such as polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), ether-based PUR, PA, polystyrene (PS), epoxy resins, and synthetic rubber (SR), for which no truly efficient degraders are currently known. We highlight methods used to discover novel microorganisms and enzymes involved in biodegradation and measure and quantify their activities. Finally, we will review the biotechnological applications of microbial-driven plastics recycling.

INTRODUCTION

Worldwide, an estimated 400–450 million metric tons (Mt) ( 1 ) of so-called commodity plastics are produced annually ( Fig. 1 ). From these, an estimated fraction of less than 10% are recycled globally, and it is further assumed that large fractions are deposited in landfills, and 2%–5% of the annual production end up in oceans ( 2 – 4 ). In recent years, there has been growing interest in the potential of microorganisms to interact with, colonize, degrade, and upcycle these synthetic polymers. Research has focused on identifying specific microbes and enzymes capable of breaking down various types of plastics, shedding light on the fundamental mechanisms underlying microbial degradation ( 5 – 7 ).

Fig 1.

Despite advances in understanding microbial degradation of plastics, significant challenges remain. For some plastics, such as polyethylene terephthalate (PET) and some bioplastics, effective microbial and enzymatic solutions have been found, enabling their degradation under controlled conditions ( 5 ). However, for other synthetic plastics such as polyamides/nylon (e.g., PA6 and PA66), we have only just begun to understand the mechanisms of degradation of oligomers, and in many cases, no effective microbial or enzymatic solutions have been identified yet (e.g., for polyolefins and vinyl polymers) ( 9 ). Addressing these challenges and establishing industrial or environmental processes that allow microbial- and enzyme-driven re- and up-cycling or environmental clean-up is a major challenge for future generations of microbiologists. It requires a multifaceted approach that combines fundamental research with applied biotechnological innovations.

This review will cover aspects involved in microbial plastic degradation including classification and properties, abiotic degradation processes (i.e., weathering), the formation and dynamics of the “plastisphere,” and known microbial degradation pathways for some of these plastics and/or their monomers. In addition, the molecular mechanisms of enzymes and selected pathways involved in the metabolism of plastics on the laboratory scale and environment will be discussed. We will review methods for identifying these microorganisms and their enzymes, including computational and analytical techniques. Furthermore, new approaches involving strain engineering will be explored, particularly those designed to produce valuable products and upcycle plastic waste. By offering a comprehensive overview of these topics, this review seeks to enhance our understanding of microbial plastic degradation, both in natural environments and in industrial settings. This review will further discuss concepts of how microbes can be harnessed to address the global plastic pollution crisis.

CHEMICAL AND PHYSICAL PROPERTIES DETERMINE MICROBIAL DEGRADATION AND ENZYME ACCESSIBILITY

Plastics can be classified based on many aspects, including their source, chemical structure, polymerization process, hydrophobicity, surface charge, polymerization degree, glass transition temperature, crystallinity, or the presence of additives ( 10 , 11 ). For a more detailed classification, see Table 1 and references therein. This multidimensional classification provides insight into their environmental impact, particularly concerning their biodegradability and interaction with microbial ecosystems.

Polymers derived from oil-based resources dominate the global markets with respect to their use in not only packaging, automotive, health, and other industries but also households ( 1 ; Fig. 1 ; Table 1 and references therein). These fossil-based polymers account for approximately 90%–95% of global plastic production. In 2023, the total production of oil-based plastics was estimated to be around 400–450 Mt ( 1 , 30 ; Fig. 1 ). In contrast, renewable and biodegradable polymers such as polylactic acid (PLA) and polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs), represented about 2.5 Mt of the total plastic production, constituting roughly 0.5%–1% of the market ( 1 ).

In general, polymers with pure carbon backbones, such as polyethylene (PE) and other polyolefins, are more resistant to microbial degradation compared to polymers with heteroatomic backbones incorporating, in principle, enzymatically hydrolyzable ester, amide, urethane, carbonate, or ether bonds. Linear polymers, such as high-density PE (HDPE) and polyvinyl chloride (PVC), consist of long, unbranched chains that confer high strength and crystallinity and hamper microbial degradation. In contrast, branched polymers like low-density PE (LDPE) and polypropylene (PP) feature side chains that influence their density and melting points. Cross-linked polymers, including flexible polyurethane (PUR) foams, vulcanized rubber, and epoxy resins, contain inter-chain bonds that enhance mechanical properties and thermal stability. Network polymers, a more specific type of cross-linked polymers, such as rigid PUR foams, phenol-formaldehyde resin (Bakelite), or cured epoxies, form three-dimensional networks resulting in highly durable and heat-resistant materials.

The polymerization process offers yet another basis for classification and possible clues to biotransformation. Polyolefins, for which hardly any microbial enzymes or degradation pathways are known, are formed through the repeated addition of monomer units without the loss of any small molecules. Examples include PE, PP, and vinyl polymers such as polystyrene (PS) and PVC ( 31 ). In contrast, condensation polymers involve the elimination of small molecules during polymerization. Notable examples are polyesters like polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polyamides like nylon (e.g., PA6 or PA66), polycarbonates (PCs, e.g., Makrolon/Lexan), and PURs, which range from flexible to rigid forms ( 32 ). For some condensation polymers, enzymes and microorganisms are known to act on either the polymers and/or oligomers.

The potential for microbial degradation depends on the polymer’s interaction with and accessibility to water. Most plastics are primarily hydrophobic, but their degree of hydrophobicity varies based on their side chains or bonds within the chain, which may enhance their capacity to interact with water. Polymers such as PP, PE, PS, bisphenol A polycarbonate (BPA-PC), and PVC are among the most hydrophobic, having higher LogP(SA) −1 values—a measure that indicates hydrophobicity adjusted for the molecular surface area—making them suitable for water-resistant applications ( 33 ). On the other hand, bio-based polymers such as polybutylene succinate (PBS), polylactic acid (PLA), and polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB), as well as nylons with short acyl chains like PA66 and PA6, are among the least hydrophobic. Plastics with “intermediate” hydrophobicity include PET, polybutylene adipate terephthalate (PBAT), and polycaprolactone (PCL). These surface properties significantly influence how plastics interact with environmental conditions and microbial colonization.

The degree of crystallinity of a plastic significantly affects its susceptibility to enzymatic attack, and it can vary across a polymer film. Amorphous regions, with their loosely packed and disordered chains, are more accessible to enzymes, allowing easier penetration and breakdown by microbial activity. This is because the random arrangement leaves more space for enzymes to bind and act on the polymer chains. In contrast, crystalline regions offer a tightly packed structure, making it difficult for enzymes to access and degrade the polymer, thereby providing resistance to enzymatic attack. As a result, plastics with a higher degree of crystallinity tend to be more resistant to biodegradation compared to those with significant amorphous content. This interplay between amorphous and crystalline regions is crucial in determining the overall degradability of a plastic material. Additionally, the degree of crystallinity also influences the glass transition temperature (T g ), with more crystalline plastics typically having a higher T g , affecting their mechanical properties and stability under different temperature conditions.

In the case of PET, the T g is approximately 70°C. This marks a critical transition state when the hard and brittle polymer changes to a softer, rubber-like state ( 34 ). The high surface area and flexibility make the polymer accessible for enzymatic degradation. As for the degree of crystallinity, T g also varies within a polymer. When in contact with water, however, the surface T g of PET has been observed to decrease down to 40°C, which makes enzymatic PET degradation feasible at mesophilic temperatures ( 35 , 36 ). Within this respect, polymers are often not homogeneous and possess dual amorphous phases (mobile amorphous phase and rigid amorphous phase) with different chain mobilities ( 37 , 38 ). T g is also influenced by the presence of additives, for example, plasticizers.

Additives can further act as antioxidants, acid scavengers, antistatic agents, biocidal agents, anti-counterfeiting agents, (UV) light and heat stabilizers, coloring pigments, reinforcement fibers, slip compounds, biocides, anti-wetting compounds, and flame retardants. They are often included in the formulation within the polymer chain or dispersed in the polymer matrix to enhance the performance characteristics of polymers. Additives can make up to 70% of the overall weight of the polymer ( 39 – 42 ). The presence of certain additives might inhibit microbial activity or serve as a preferred carbon source for microbes, leading to the incomplete degradation of the polymer matrix itself. The chemical compounds used for the additives differ widely. Additives are often not only organic compounds, but also different metal ions, and up to 13,000 different chemical compounds have been listed as suitable additives ( 41 , 43 ). Thereby, plasticizers are often esters of phthalic, trimellitic, benzoic, and adipic acids. Bis(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), and dibutyl phthalate (DBP) are most widely used. Most of them are well-known substrates of microorganisms and are biodegradable ( 44 ). Plasticizers that are phthalate esters can be degraded under aerobic and anaerobic conditions by a wide range of microorganisms ( 45 ). The two routes employed under oxic and anoxic conditions differ largely. Under oxic conditions, microorganisms employ mono- and di-oxygenases to introduce hydroxyl groups into the phthalate esters. This allows decarboxylation by employing either aromatizing dehydrogenases or cofactor-free decarboxylases. This pathway is elucidated in more detail in one of the following sections (see “Terephthalate polyesters”). In the absence of oxygen, phthalate esters are transformed into thioesters. This is accomplished by using coenzyme A (CoA)-ligases or CoA-transferases, followed by a decarboxylation ( 45 ).

Finally, physical properties, including particle size, surface area, and surface charge, also affect microbial attachment and degradation efficiency. In this respect, smaller particles with larger surface areas can be more readily colonized by microbes ( 46 , 47 ).

WEATHERING AND COLONIZATION ARE THE FIRST STEPS LEADING TO MICROBIAL PLASTICS DEGRADATION

Microbial degradation is a promising approach to reducing plastic pollution, but it remains a slow and often complicated process. The preliminary stages of this process (i.e., weathering) are essential to facilitate biodegradation in nature. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the initial stages is fundamental to optimizing biodegradation strategies, advancing sustainable waste management solutions, and understanding biotic degradation processes in nature ( 48 ). Weathering involves physical, chemical, and biological changes and degradation of plastics during environmental exposure. It is divided into two stages—abiotic and biotic weathering.

Following the disposal of macro-plastic items such as bags, bottles, and fishing nets into natural ecosystems, they undergo both biotic and abiotic weathering at the same time—a process extending over decades to estimated time frames of thousands or millions of years and generating micro-, nano-, and even pico-plastic particles ( 49 , 50 ). As particle size decreases, the surface area expands significantly, allowing more space for microbial attachment and subsequent enzymatic degradation. Consequently, the surface area of a standard 500  mL PET bottle (approximately 950  cm²) would increase to that of a classical soccer field (7,140 m²), assuming it is broken down into spherical nanoparticles with a diameter of 12  nm ( 9 ). Both biotic and abiotic weathering will result in the release of additives present in the polymers. They will either serve as readily available nutrients or have biocidal effects (see previous section).

Abiotic weathering depends on factors like ultraviolet (UV) radiation, mechanical forces, and fluctuations in temperature and pH ( Fig. 2 ). For instance, ester bonds in contrast to C-C bonds are sensitive to pH changes and may hydrolyze at extreme pH values. In marine and coastal environments, the mechanical action of waves, coupled with abrasive sand particles, facilitates the fragmentation of plastics. UV radiation is particularly detrimental to polymers containing aromatic structures, such as PET, leading to discoloration, structural degradation, and alterations in surface hydrophobicity ( 51 – 54 ).

Fig 2.

Furthermore, random oxidation reactions—partly induced by UV exposure and the occurrence of oxygen radicals—may result in molecular breakdown, thereby increasing polymer brittleness and fragmentation ( 49 , 55 ). Over time, abiotic weathering alters polymer crystallinity, rendering plastics increasingly fragile and promoting further mechanical degradation. In the case of semicrystalline polymers like PE, abiotic weathering can lead from a less ordered (amorphous) state to a well‐organized state (high crystallinity) until a thermodynamic equilibrium is reached.

Biotic weathering is in part caused by insects, snails, and other animals feeding on larger plastic particles and using their ingestive systems and teeth (e.g., mandibula, radula) to mechanically produce microplastics. This is a feature well described for larvae of the wax moth ( 56 ), mealworm ( 57 ), and the snail Lissachatina fulica ( 58 ).

During weathering, plastic surfaces will be subject to microbial colonization. This phase is marked by the adhesion of microorganisms (bacteria, archaea, fungi, and microalgae) to polymer surfaces and the subsequent formation of biofilms, which may play a crucial role in biodegradation ( 59 , 60 ). Microbial adhesion is initiated by the adsorption of organic molecules such as proteins and polysaccharides onto the plastic surface. This conditioning layer mediates microbial attachment by fostering interactions between the polymer and bacterial cells ( 61 ). Hydrophobic interactions, van der Waals forces, and electrostatic attractions govern microbial adhesion ( 62 ). Following initial adhesion, microorganisms secrete extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), leading to the establishment of biofilms. These biofilms confer structural integrity and protect microbial communities from environmental stressors while simultaneously enhancing the enzymatic degradation of plastics ( 63 ). Notably, the microbiota affiliated with plastics was designated as “plastisphere.” For a detailed review of the diversity of the microbiota affiliated with the plastisphere and the main players involved in the initial attachment, we refer to references ( 64 , 65 ). Within the plastisphere, synergistic interactions between microbial species optimize the metabolic potential for polymer degradation. Microbial biofilms formed on plastic particles are most likely mixed species biofilms harboring bacteria, fungi, and algae rather than single species.

During life in the plastisphere microorganisms will secrete a wide range of hydrolytic and degradative enzymes at various concentrations, and this will in part affect the polymer. These catalysts will either act unspecific and randomly on the polymer backbone or with a high specificity targeting specific polymer bonds. Randomly acting enzymes are mainly oxidases, peroxidases, and peroxygenases (EC 1) that may initiate the breakdown of polymer bonds by random oxidation processes ( 66 ). This is, however, a very slow process requiring long time periods in nature. On the contrary, specific enzymes (e.g., esterases, lipases, cutinases, amido and carbamate hydrolases, and protease) secreted by microbial consortia facilitate the depolymerization of plastics if hydrolyzable bonds are accessible. Although hydrolases are often promiscuous enzymes converting a wider range of substrates ( 67 ), they still target specific bonds in the polymer chain, and we will review their mode of action in the following section in detail. Even if many of the enzymes are often not secreted at significant levels, it is likely that they will be released at very low levels once the microbial cells die. The biofilm matrix will retain these enzymes near the polymer surface, thereby increasing degradation efficiency.

Furthermore, during growth on the plastic surface, microbial metabolites include short-chain organic acids, produced through fermentation under microaerobic and anoxic conditions. These acids will, together with available oxygen radicals, form various highly reactive per-acids. The latter will randomly interact with the polymer and introduce hydroxyl groups at various positions. These can be further oxidized to keto-groups by unspecific dehydrogenases, and this will ultimately result in the breakage of the polymer albeit at very low frequencies over extended time periods. This process will be very slow and will not allow rapid degradation as observed for enzyme-driven polymer hydrolysis ( Fig. 2 ).

MICROORGANISMS DEGRADE AND UTILIZE ONLY A FEW PLASTIC TYPES

As of June 2025, the PAZy database lists 255 verified plastic-active wild-type enzymes across diverse bacteria and mainly derived from Actinomycetota and Pseudomonadota, and a few derived from Archaea and Eukaryota ( 8 ). A Sequence-Similarity Network (SSN) analysis using 175 microbial sequences revealed 13 main clusters with at least five enzymes (I–XIII; Fig. 3 ; Fig. S1 for all sequences). Clusters I–IX feature polyester hydrolases (EC 3.1.1) targeting PET, PLA, PBAT, PBS, and PHA. Notably, Cluster I comprises bacterial enzymes that can degrade PET, whereas Cluster II includes enzymes from the domain of the Archaea and the phylum of the Bacillota, focusing largely on PET. Cluster III consists of carboxylesterases active on PET, PLA, PBAT, and PUR, and Clusters IV–VI include both eukaryotic (fungal) esterases and bacillotal lipases acting on PET, PBAT, PLA, and PUR. Cluster VII contains pseudomonadotal lipases targeting PUR, and Clusters VIII and IX house depolymerases for PHA. Clusters X and XI involve carbamate hydrolases and amidases (EC 3.5.1) targeting PA and PUR. Cluster XII features proteases (EC 3.4.21) specific to PLA, and Cluster XIII includes actinomycetal latex (rubber) clearing proteins (EC 1.13.11.87).

Within this framework, the next section explores the diversity of microbes and their enzymes for degrading synthetic and/or bio-based plastics. We focus on three main polymer categories based on their chemical structure and susceptibility: enzymatically hydrolyzable synthetic polymers, not directly hydrolyzable synthetic polymers, and biodegradable polymers.

Enzymatically hydrolyzable synthetic polymers

This group includes terephthalate/aromatic polyesters (e.g., PET), polyamides (PA), polyurethanes (PUR), and polycarbonates (PC). A common feature of these polymers is that they contain bonds that can be hydrolyzed and are the targets of specific microbial enzymes ( 8 ).

Terephthalate polyesters (PET, PBT, PTT, PCT, PEN, and LCPs)

Aromatic polyesters based on terephthalic acid (TPA) are typical commodity polymers recognized for their excellent mechanical properties, thermal stability, and resistance to abiotic stress. The resins are synthesized via polycondensation of aromatic rings based on benzene and glycols of different chain lengths. Of these, polyethylene terephthalate (PET) is the most widely used, with an expected global annual production rate of 35.3 Mt in 2024 ( 1 ). The semi-crystalline thermoplast, which contains consecutive ester bonds (R-C(=O)-O-R’), is composed of the monomers terephthalic acid (TPA) and ethylene glycol (EG). It is used in various applications due to its high versatility, flexibility, durability, and strength and is one of the main materials used for packaging, bottles, or textile fibers ( Table 1 and references herein).

Other aromatic polyesters include polybutylene terephthalate (PBT), polytrimethylene terephthalate (PTT), polycyclohexylene dimethylene terephthalate (PCT), and polyethylene naphthalate (PEN). The uses of these polyesters are described in Table 1 . PEN differs from all other polyesters due to the introduction of naphthalate instead of a single benzene ring using naphthalene-2,6-dicarboxylic acid as a precursor. PEN displays enhanced stability, higher T g , and rigidity compared with other polyesters, making it suitable for use in high-performance plastic bottles or films ( Table 1 ). Finally, liquid crystalline polymers (LCPs) are a heterogeneous group of high-performance synthetic polymers able to self-assemble and are derived from monomers such as 4-hydroxybenzoic acid ( 68 ).

Microbial degradation of aromatic polyesters

While the microbial depolymerization and degradation of aromatic polyesters is still an emerging field of study, considerable research has focused on PET due to its global market size and relevance, resulting in the identification of over 100 microorganisms harboring enzymes capable of hydrolyzing it. Although the full mechanisms of enzyme action—such as polymer binding and oligomer uptake—are not yet completely understood for all plastics in this class, significant progress has been made, especially for PET. Over a hundred wild-type hydrolases have been discovered ( Fig. 3 and 4 ), with many enhanced through engineering, and the development of industrial-scale enzyme-based upcycling processes reflects this progress ( 69 , 70 ). Given the depth of research and effective degradation strategies developed for PET, we will explore this polymer in more detail. For additional insights, see “Biotechnological and synthetic approaches are key to a sustainable plastics-based bioeconomy.”

Although it is occasionally suggested otherwise in publications, as early as 2005, researchers had already identified wild-type enzymes and microorganisms capable of degrading PET in Gram-positive bacteria related to the genus Thermobifida ( 74 , 75 ) and the fungus Fusarium solani ( 76 ). Sulaiman et al. reported in 2012 the identification of a cutinase derived from metagenomic DNA of leaf compost. This enzyme was designated LCC (leaf-compost cutinase) ( 77 ), and the wild-type enzyme is generally regarded as the most active wild-type enzyme reported, with a general 5-fold to 8-fold higher catalytic activity compared with most of the other reported wild-type enzymes ( 35 ). The original study did not identify the source organism for this enzyme. However, subsequent analysis suggests that it likely originates from a Thermobifida (or Thermomonospora ) fusca isolate, probably closely related to the bacterium HRB29. BhrPETase, a PET-active hydrolase identified in this bacterium, is 95% similar on the protein sequence level to the LCC ( Fig. 4 ) ( 78 ). In 2016, a Japanese research team reported the identification of a Gram-negative bacterium Ideonella sakaiensis (recently renamed to Piscinibacter sakaiensis ) that grows on PET as the sole carbon and energy source. The main responsible enzyme was designated IsPETase ( Fig. 4 ) and is a secreted esterase ( 79 , 80 ).

As of June 2025, 121 verified microbial PET-active enzymes are listed in the PAZy database ( 8 ). A phylogenetic tree containing 110 bacterial and archaeal enzymes is depicted in Fig. 4 . Although a few enzymes have been identified from fungi and plants, as well as two recently reported mammalian enzymes, the majority of currently known and biochemically characterized enzymes originate from seven bacterial and two archaeal phyla ( Fig. 4 ). Most bacterial enzymes derive from the Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota phyla. Additionally, enzymes have been sourced from Bacillota, Bacteroidota, Chloroflexota, Deinococcota, and Thermodesulfobacteriota. Furthermore, a significant number of enzymes have been reported originating from non-cultivated microorganisms and their metagenomes ( 69 , 81 , 82 ). Moreover, a few archaeal and highly thermostable enzymes have been identified ( 81 , 83 ). These archaeal-derived enzymes are believed to be ancient feruloyl esterases with broad substrate spectrum, linked to the Bathyarchaeota and Thermoproteota phyla. Although they show only weak activity on PET, they are among the most active enzymes on the degradation intermediates bis(2-hydroxyethyl) terephthalate (BHET) and mono(2-hydroxyethyl) terephthalate (MHET) at high temperatures. These enzymes are highly thermostable, with a T m of up to 86°C ( 81 , 83 ).

Although over 100 prokaryotic wild-type PET-active enzymes are known, the biodiversity appears to be limited, as most of them are homologs of LCC or IsPETase ( Fig. 4 ). However, the already-known enzymes indicate a global occurrence of the affiliated microorganisms originating from very diverse marine and terrestrial habitats and show activity across diverse environmental conditions, including a broad range of temperatures and salt concentrations ( 82 ).

Notably, some enzymes like LCC have a very wide substrate spectrum and degrade other aromatic polyesters like PBT and PTT, releasing 1,4-butanediol or 1,3-propanediol and TPA as the final products ( 84 ).

Although the first known enzymes acting on PET were cutinases (EC 3.1.1.74 today, it is well-known that PET-active enzymes are also affiliated with lipases (EC 3.1.1.3) and carboxylesterases (EC 3.1.1.1, EC 3.1.1.2, EC 3.1.1.101). They hydrolyze the ester bond, resulting in either BHET, MHET, or TPA and EG. The enzymes are mainly exo-enzymes. Once taken up by the cells, MHET can be hydrolyzed using a specific periplasmatic hydrolase designated MHETase (EC 3.1.1.102), and TPA is metabolized by cleaving the aromatic ring structures using known aryl degradation pathways ( 7 , 85 , 86 ). PET-active enzymes are frequently referred to as “PETases.” However, this designation is not entirely accurate since these enzymes are hydrolases with broad substrate specificity, and PET is just one of the substrates they can act upon. In most cases, the native substrate of these enzymes remains unidentified.

Common biochemical and structural traits of PET-active hydrolases

Analysis of 3D crystal structures revealed that PETases carry the typical α/β-hydrolase fold, a Gly-X1-Ser-X2-Gly consensus motif, and a conserved catalytic triad. Joo et al. proposed a classification of PETases into type I and type II based on structural similarities ( 87 – 89 ). Type I enzymes, such as LCC and Cut190 ( Fig. 4 ), possess a single disulfide bond and have conserved key residues in their subsites, distinguishing them from type II enzymes. In contrast, type II enzymes are characterized by additional disulfide bonds and extended loops that contribute to the stability and flexibility of the enzyme structure, affecting the loops surrounding the catalytic triad. Two subgroups within the type II enzymes can be distinguished based on their amino acid composition. Phe/Tyr sites in type IIa enzymes are substituted with Ser/Thr sites in type IIb enzymes. Subsite I of the type IIb enzyme IsPETase comprises residues like Tyr87, Gln119, Met161, Trp185 and subsite II Thr88, Ala89, Trp159, Ser238, and Asn241, where subsite I is more conserved among all PETases. This subsite is crucial and contains the binding triad (Tyr58, Met132, and Trp156), which is responsible for efficient PET binding. The wobbling Trp156 appears to have a strong impact on enzyme performance due to three different conformations ( 90 , 91 ).

PET hydrolysis occurs via a two-step Ser-hydrolase mechanism mediated by the Ser-His-Asp catalytic triad ( Fig. 5 ; 91 ). Enzymatic depolymerization of PET is facilitated via two main steps and involves the creation of a first tetrahedral intermediate state, where the catalytic Ser hydroxy group is deprotonated and activated by His and Asp. During the nucleophilic attack, the hydroxyl oxygen binds to the carbonyl C of the substrate, releases the alcohol, and stabilizes the transition complex. An oxyanion hole further stabilizes the negative charge on the tetrahedral intermediate. In the second step, the deacylation, a water molecule performs a nucleophilic attack on the ester bond of the acyl-enzyme intermediate, generating a second tetrahedral intermediate. The His deprotonates the water and transfers it to the Ser, which releases the carboxylic acid as a product in the final step. The rate-limiting step in catalysis appears to be the formation of the first tetrahedral complex ( 91 ).

Microbial growth using PET as a single carbon and energy source

Already in 2016, it was shown that Ideonella ( Piscinibacter ) sakaiensis grows on PET as the sole carbon and energy source. PET is initially degraded by IsPETase to the intermediates BHET and MHET. The latter is processed to the monomers TPA and EG ( 79 , 80 , 92 ). Figure 6 outlines the main enzyme-driven PET degradation steps as proposed for I. sakaiensis and other gram-negative bacteria. In I. sakaiensis , the assimilation and uptake of TPA is thought to be facilitated by a specific substrate-binding protein designated IsTBP, allowing subsequent hydroxylation and decarboxylation in the cytoplasm ( 93 ). Although much of the data postulated in I. sakaiensis are derived from modeling and in-depth genome analyses, a wealth of experimental and mutational data has been collected for TPA transport and metabolism in gram-negative bacteria affiliated with the genus Comamonas . Previously identified as part of the plastisphere ( 94 ), Comamonas sp. does not degrade polymeric PET but can internalize and degrade TPA very efficiently. In the first step, TPA is transported into the cell using a highly specific TTT-family transporter ( 95 ). TTT-family transporters are a distinct class of three component transporters involved in small molecule and substrate transport in many bacteria and are located in the cytoplasm membrane ( 96 ; Fig. 6 ). The transporter consists of a periplasmic binding protein (TphC) and two membrane-anchored proteins (TpiA and TpiB). Although tphC is encoded in the conserved tph operon, tpiA and tpiB are encoded elsewhere in the genome. Using RNA-seq and mutational approaches, it has been shown that TPA uptake and degradation are tightly regulated, and the periplasmic transporter gene tphC is induced up to 80-fold by low levels of TPA ( 97 ). TPA is metabolized to protocatechuate (PCA). Therefore, TPA is initially converted to 1,2-dihydroxy-3,5-cyclohexadiene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (1,4-DCD) by a 1,2-dioxygenase, which is encoded by the tphA1-A3 genes. The compound 1,4-DCD is converted to PCA by the dehydrogenase TphB ( 98 ). In C. thiooxidans S23 (CTS23), these enzymes are encoded in a conserved single operon downstream of the transporter gene tphC ( Fig. 6B ). The same operon is present in strains E6 and in C. testosteroni strain YZW-D and has been functionally analyzed in both these organisms ( 98 – 100 ). Interestingly, strain E6 carries two almost identical copies of this operon ( 101 ). Transcription of the metabolic genes is presumably activated by TphR, an IclR-type transcriptional regulator ( 102 ) that binds upstream of tphC and most likely acts as an activator ( 101 ).

Deletion of the metabolic genes tphA1-B in CTS23 resulted in the loss of growth of TPA as the sole carbon and energy source ( 97 ). PCA is then further converted to pyruvate and oxaloacetate using the PCA 4,5-cleavage pathway ( 104 , 105 ). This pathway is encoded in a distinct operon in CTS23 and encompasses at least nine genes. Some Comamonas strains harbor a second partial PCA 4,5-cleavage pathway encoded elsewhere in the genome. Overall, the proteins involved in TPA transport and aromatic ring cleavage in I. sakaiensis strain 201-F6 appear to be homologs of the Comamonas proteins ( 79 , 93 ; Fig. 6 ). Since this operon is highly conserved in Ideonella and Comamonas, it is likely that it was acquired via horizontal gene transfer. ISF6_0226 is a homolog of the Comamonas periplasmic binding protein TphC, and ISF6_0076 and ISF6_0077 are the functional homologs of the TTT transporter proteins TpiA and TpiB in Comamonas . ISF6_0227 codes for the large subunit of the oxygenase component of the TPA 1,2 dioxygenase (TPADO), and ISF6_0228 is the small subunit of the TPADO. ISF6_0229 is a functional homolog of TphB, the 1,2-dihydroxy-3,5-cyclohexadiene-1,4-dicarboxylate (1,4-DCD) dehydrogenase, and ISF6_ 0230 codes for the TPADO reductase ( Fig. 6 ). Notably, in I. sakaiensis, downstream of the metabolic genes, a LysR-type receptor (ISF6_0231) is encoded. The function of this regulator is not studied, but it is likely that it is involved in the regulation of the expression of the metabolic genes. Furthermore, the regulator gene coding for the tphR homolog, pcaR (recently renamed to mrp [ 103 ]), appears to be fragmented through the insertion of a gene coding for the mono(2-hydroxyethyl) terephthalate hydrolase (MHETase) (ISF_0224) and involved in initial PET hydrolysis. Although it is not clear if the fragmented small predicted PcaR (ISF_0225) is still functional, Mrp (ISF_0223) retains its promoter binding activities and appears to be partially functional ( 103 ). Although recently much effort has been invested in the molecular analysis of TPA metabolism in I. skaiensis , it is widespread in nature and has been identified in many gram-negative bacteria under aerobic and anaerobic conditions. For a detailed review of this topic, we refer to reference ( 45 ).

Finally, in I. sakaienis, EG is metabolized using the alcohol dehydrogenase IsPedE and IsPedH and the rare earth element type I alcohol dehydrogenase. EG is oxidized to glyoxylic acid and further metabolized via the citric acid cycle ( 106 , 107 ).

Concepts of regulation of PET gene expression

For natural polymers, for example, cellulose, starch, or chitin, it is well known that their dimers or monomers, which serve as primary degradation products, are involved in the activation of the pathways and transporters essential to metabolize the respective polymers and degradation products ( 108 – 110 ). The activation of the genes involved in the catabolism of the respective monomers is subject to regulation by cAMP-CRP and in part by c-di-GMP signaling in most bacteria ( 111 – 113 ). In contrast, little is known about PET or any other synthetic polymer. Recent work implied that the transcriptional regulator MRP (MHETase gene-regulating protein) synonym to ISF_0223 or PcaR (see section above and Fig. 6 ) positively controls the expression of the gram-negative I. sakaiensis MHETase and the genes involved in TPA metabolism. This first study suggested that MRP possibly mediates the PET-dependent induction of the MHETase and the mono- and di-oxygenase genes involved in TPA degradation ( 103 ). These findings are supported by the earlier observation made for C. thiooxidans strains E6 and S23 ( 98 ). In C. thiooxidans , the tphC-A operon is involved in binding to TPA in the periplasm, and its transcription is regulated in the presence of TPA at nM levels ( 97 , 99 , 101 ). Notably, both strains do not carry active PETase genes. However, they are both able to import TPA and grow on it as a single carbon and energy source ( 95 , 97 ). Thus, it is possible that they potentially benefit from PET degradation products if other organisms provide the necessary enzymes. Within this setting, recent transcriptomic analyses from Vibrio gazogenes imply that the native PET-active hydrolase gene ( pet6 ) is expressed at very low but significant levels under various environmental conditions. Increasing BHET concentrations, however, result in increased transcription of the pet6 gene. Thereby, BHET and TPA appear to be nutritional signals interfering with the three main regulatory and interconnected circuits involved in QS, cAMP-CRP, and c-di-GMP signaling ( 114 ). Further research is needed to fully unravel the regulatory networks involved in PET metabolism.

Polyamides (PAs, e.g., PA6, PA66, PPA, and aramides)

PAs are petroleum-derived thermoplastics with repeating units linked via amide bonds (R-C(=O)-NR’R”) as in proteins and peptides. Synthetic PAs are characterized by high mechanical tenacity, thermal stability, and chemical resistance ( 13 , 14 ). PAs account for 8 Mt of global plastic production, representing one of the smallest shares among major plastic types. However, the demand for PAs continues to rise due to their diverse applications and favorable properties ( 1 ). The first PA polymers synthesized in the 1930s are known as Nylon (PA66; 115 , 116 ). PA66 is produced by polycondensation of the two monomers 1,6-hexamethylenediamine (HMDA) and adipic acid (AA). Another widely used PA, known as Perlon or PA6, is composed of repeating units derived from 6-aminohexanoic acid (6-Ahx). It is produced through the ring-opening polymerization of ε-caprolactam. PA66 and PA6 alone constitute 90% of the total global production. Depending on their linker, PAs are divided into different categories: aliphatic, semi-aromatic, and aromatic ( Table 1 ; 115 – 117 ). Aliphatic PAs, such as PA6 and PA66, along with those formed from other chain-length combinations (e.g., PA46, PA11, or PA12), are characterized by their linear carbon chains. Semi-aromatic PAs, also referred to as polyphthalamides (PPAs), consist of aliphatic chains and aromatic compounds ( 118 ). PA6T is a prominent example consisting of the aliphatic HMDA and the aromatic TPA ( 119 ). In the case of aromatic PAs, also named aramids, the polymers consist fully of aromatic compounds. For example, the aramid Kevlar is synthesized in a polycondensation from the monomers p -phenylenediamine and terephthaloyl chloride ( 120 , 121 ). In addition to homopolymers, PAs also come in copolymers. Depending on the linkers used, PAs have different physical and chemical properties, which makes them versatile and usable in industry ( 122 ).

Microbial metabolism of PAs

Today it is not clear if native and crystalline PA polymers can be fully degraded using enzymes and/or if smaller fragments of the polymer chain or oligomers need to be available to allow enzyme-driven biodegradation. The microbial conversion of PAs primarily depends on the hydrolysis of its amide bonds. Bacterial degradation of PAs is accomplished by employing different types of hydrolases that cleave the amide bond in PA-oligomers. PA enzymatic degradation involves, in the case of PA6 and PA66, the conversion of the monomers 6-Ahx, AA, and HMDA. Although the degradation of polyphthalamides and aramides has not been reported, a possible metabolic route for PA6 degradation in Paenarthrobacter ureafaciens (previously Arthrobacter guttatus / Flavobacterium sp.) strain K172 was revealed by Negoro and colleagues ( 123 , 124 ). The bacterium possesses three amidohydrolases named NylA, NylB, and NylC for the depletion of PA6 oligomers into the monomer 6-Ahx. PA6 is degraded via the caprolactam degradation pathway ( 125 ). A 6-Ahx-oligomer endo-hydrolase (EC 3.5.1.117) degrades the 6-Ahx-oligomers into Ahx-dimers. Cyclic 6-Ahx-dimers are linearized by a 6-Ahx-cyclic-dimer hydrolase (EC 3.5.2.12). At last, the 6-Ahx-dimer hydrolase (EC 3.5.1.46) cleaves the 6-Ahx-dimer in its monomer 6-Ahx. Further studies showed that 6-Ahx is metabolized into adipate semialdehyde by a 6-Ahx-aminotransferase (EC 2.6.1.116) and an adipate semialdehyde (6-oxohexanoate) dehydrogenase (EC 1.2.1.63) converts adipate semialdehyde into AA ( 126 , 127 ). Furthermore, AA undergoes several conversions from adipyl-CoA into 3-oxoadipyl-CoA. Finally, the 3-oxoadipyl-CoA can be transformed into succinyl-CoA or acetyl-CoA, which enters the TCA cycle, where it is further oxidized for energy production ( Fig. 7 ). The microbial degradation of HMDA may be initiated by oxidative enzymes such as laccases (EC 1.10.3.2), oxidases (EC 1.4.3.22), or aminotransferases (EC 2.6.1.29), which are known to degrade structurally similar biogenic amines like cadaverine and putrescine—essentially the C5 and C4 versions, respectively ( 128 ). However, the specific mechanisms and microbial species responsible for HMDA degradation have yet to be fully elucidated. Recent research has advanced our understanding by employing adaptive laboratory evolution to enable P. putida KT2440, initially incapable of growing on aliphatic diamines longer than C5, to metabolize HMDA. In this evolved strain, HMDA is converted to 6-Ahx via either γ-glutamylation or transamination, subsequently funneling into central metabolism ( 129 ). Nonetheless, it remains uncertain whether any unmodified microorganisms naturally possess this capability, and further research is essential to verify these potential pathways in nature.

The capability of the amide bonds to form strong hydrogen interactions is not only a main reason for the stability of the polymers but also a main reason why enzymatic access is hampered and explains why today, only very few microbes and enzymes are known to degrade them ( 14 ). PA-degrading bacteria were first discovered in the 1970s, and the Paenarthrobacter ureafaciens strain K172 was first reported to convert PA6 oligomers ( 123 , 130 ). Further studies have identified Pseudomonas sp . strain NK87, and a variety of others to grow on PA6 and PA66 ( 124 ). In addition, more recent studies have identified Bacillus cereus , Vibrio furnissii , Bacillus sphericus, Brevundimonas vesicularis, Lysinibacillus sp . , Alcaligenes faecalis and Enterococcus faecalis, and Brevibacillus brevis being able to grow on PA6 and PA66 as the sole carbon and energy source ( 131 – 133 ).

Enzymes involved in PA hydrolysis

Most enzymes degrading PA are hydrolases ( Fig. 5 ). Currently, less than 30 enzymes with activity on short-chain PA or oligomers are known, and none are known to effectively depolymerize the full PA polymer. The known enzymes were isolated from various gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria affiliated with the genera Pseudomonas , Paenarthrobacter , Agromyces , Kocuria , Nocardia sp . Microbacterium sp., Pseudoxanthomonas sp. ( 134 – 142 ), and others ( Fig. 3 ). In addition, a significant number of enzymes have been identified that originate from non-cultivated microorganisms ( 143 – 145 ). The best-studied PA oligomer-active enzymes are designated NylA, NylB, and NylC of Paenarthrobacter ureafaciens ( Fig. 5 ). NylA (E.C 3.5.2.12) is a 6-Ahx cyclic dimer hydrolase (amidase), NylB (E.C 3.5.1.46) is a linear 6-Ahx dimer hydrolase with a Ser-Tyr-Lys catalytic triad, and NylC (EC 3.5.1.117) acts on linear trimers, tetramers, and hexamers of 6-Ahx performing an endo-type hydrolysis ( 134 ). Furthermore, several proteases employing either Ser or Cys residues as catalytic nucleophiles (EC 3.4.21 and 3.4.22) are known to act on nylon oligomers ( 146 , 147 ). Finally, a few cutinases (E.C. 3.1.1.74) have been reported to be active on PAs ( 148 – 150 ).

Bell and colleagues recently reported in a landmark publication on the screening of over 40 novel potential nylonases acting on PA6 and using mass spectrometry to quantify eight compounds resulting from enzymatic hydrolysis. Their best-performing enzyme was designated NylCK-TS hydrolyzed 0.67 wt% pretreated PA6-film and released between 250 and 300 μM 6-Ahx oligomers and 6-Ahx dimers after 10 days of incubation on PA6 film. Interestingly, the amount of enzyme employed did not significantly affect the release of dimers or oligomers when NylC was used at either 0.01 or 1.0 µM. Furthermore, it became evident that the depolymerization did not proceed after an initial increase of oligomer and dimer release. This implies that the enzymes have no or very poor access to the crystalline polymer itself. Also, the addition of fresh enzymes did not overcome this limitation ( 143 ). Furthermore, Hoffman et al. provided - via targeted genome mining - a thermostable enzyme TvgC from the Gram-positive bacterium Thermovenabulum gondwanense , which grows optimally at 65°C ( 151 , 152 ). With a T m of 93°C, TvgC achieved 1.2 wt% degradation of PA6 film and demonstrated high thermostability with no measurable loss of activity on PA6 and PA66 film after 1 week at 60°C ( 152 ). TvgC also revealed a preference for PA66 films. However, further investigations were hindered by the presence of plasticizers and unidentified byproducts, which interfered with the UPLC-MS column. Recently, de Witt et al. enriched the diversity of nylonases by library screenings and in silico analyses. Three novel nylonases were found referred to as NylC 1 , NylC 2 , and NylC 3 , which are active on cyclic/linear PA6 oligomers and synthesized polyester amides ( 153 ).

Further known enzymes are the aryl-acylamidases from Nocardia globerula and N. farcinicia . These are Ser-( cis )Ser-Lys containing amidases (EC 3.5.1.4; 134 , 135 , 143 ). Additionally, it was recently reported that Pseudoxanthomonas sp. TN-N1 codes for a partially characterized and novel hydrolase involved in the hydrolysis of PA4, which is a biopolymer. The authors of this study provided convincing evidence that TN-N1 was able to convert PA4 to γ -aminobutyric acid oligomers ( 141 ). However, another type of enzyme involved in PA66 degradation was identified as a Mn-dependent peroxygenase (E.C. 1.11.1.13) from white rot fungi ( 154 , 155 ).

The most efficient enzymes manage to break down 0.67%–1.2% of pretreated PA6 polymer ( 143 , 152 ). Notably, all these enzymes are homologs of NylC previously derived from Paenarthrobacter ureafaciens and do not have the capability of full PA polymer degradation ( 123 , 156 , 157 ). Beyond these NylC homologs, it might be worthwhile to explore enzyme classes, such as proteases and peptidases with respect to PA degradation.

Polyurethanes

Polyurethanes belong to a versatile polymer class primarily characterized by its urethane bonds (R-NH-C(=O)-O-R'), also known as carbamate bonds in small molecules ( 15 , 16 ). Unlike some other polymers, PUR is not defined by a single repeating unit but by distinctive linkages, which allow various polymer structures with additional bonds. PUR is synthesized through the reaction of a diisocyanate and a polyol, producing polymers that can be either linear or crosslinked, depending on the formulation. Due to their inherent chemical properties, PURs are utilized in a broad array of applications, including coatings, elastomers, and both flexible and rigid foams ( Table 1 ). They are particularly valued for their durability and versatility, contributing to PURs being the sixth most produced plastic type, 58.7 Mt per year ( Fig. 1 ; 1 , 16 ). The production process typically involves isocyanates such as methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (MDI), toluene diisocyanate (TDI), and hexamethylene diisocyanate (HMDI), with the choice of polyol varying based on the intended application ( 16 ). This flexibility allows for the creation of polyurethanes with tailored properties. Interestingly, urethane/carbamate bonds are extremely rare in nature but can be found in certain natural compounds, such as the alkaloid physostigmine, which occurs in the Calabar bean ( Physostigma venenosum ) and acts as a reversible inhibitor of acetylcholinesterase ( 158 ). Ethyl carbamate can be found in fermented foods, like bread, cheese, kimchi, soy sauce, wine, yogurt, beer, and spirits. Even if also called urethane, it is not a monomer of polyurethane. It naturally occurs by a reaction of urea, citrulline, or cyanate with ethanol ( 159 ).

Many studies investigating microbial or enzymatic PUR degradation utilize ester-based linear PURs, such as Impranil DLN, interpreting halo formation as an indication of degradation ( 160 ). These studies predominantly reported microbial and enzymatic activities targeting the ester bonds in aliphatic polyester-based PURs and using halo formation in agar plates. Once these ester bonds are degraded, the resulting PUR oligomers can be further broken down by targeting the carbamate bonds. Despite this focus on ester bond degradation, enzymes involved in these processes are often broadly categorized as PUR-degrading enzymes. Although halo formation can also be the result of pH sensitivity or aggregation without any enzymatic activity involved, it is necessary to use quantitative molecular spectroscopy or other sophisticated tools to verify the observed PUR transformations ( 160 ).

Microbial degradation of PURs

As indicated above, many of the studies linked to PUR degradation have enriched esterase activities. Thereby, mainly bacteria affiliated with the genus Pseudomonas such as P. chlororaphis ( 161 , 162 ), P. fluorescens ( 163 ), Acinetobacter generi P7 ( 164 ), P. otitidis , Burkholderia gladioli , and P. putida ( 165 ) were identified. Furthermore, one study identified PUR-active esterases in Bacillus sp ( 166 ).

Furthermore, a significant number of fungal species have been reported to be able to degrade PUR (ester bonds). Among those are the Ascomycota Pestalotiopsis microspore ( 167 ), Curvularia senegalensis , Aureobasidium pullulans , Cladosporium sp. ( 168 ), Geomyces pannorum , Penicillium inflatum , Alternaria sp., Neonectria ramulariae , Penicillium viridicatum , and others ( 167 – 172 ).

To address the biodegradation of ether-based PUR, most studies have used weight loss as an indicator for microbial degradation by both fungi and bacteria. Unfortunately, many of these studies were inconclusive as no enzymes were reported. These studies were recently summarized in reference ( 173 ). To overcome this bottleneck, ethyl carbamate (EC) as a small molecule was used for further screenings. EC can be cleaved into NH 3 , CO 2 , and ethanol by an ethanol-resistant ethyl carbamate-degrading amidase (AmdA). Based on this new approach, few bacterial isolates were recently identified to be able to perform this reaction. Among those were Rhodococcus erythropolis, Sporosarcina ureae , Agrobacterium tumefaciens, and others ( 174 ). Within this framework, using other model substrates resulted in the identification of Aspergillus sp., P. aeruginosa , B. subtilis , and Rhodococcus sp. as potential PUR degraders. The model substrates employed in these studies were molecules like [4, 40-methylene bis(phenylisocyanate)-alt-1,4-butanediol/poly(butylene adipate)] or related compounds ( 175 – 177 ).

The degradation of the most produced PURs includes the metabolization of the hydrolysis degradation products, which are 2,4-toluendiamine (TDA), 2,6-toluendiamine, hexamethylenediamine (HMDA), and 4,4'-methylenedianiline (MDA). Although 2,4-TDA can be metabolized via the citric acid cycle by Pseudomonas capeferrum TDA1 ( 178 ), no microbial degradation pathways are known for 2,6-TDA and MDA ( Fig. 8 ). Since HMDA is also a monomer of PA66, its microbial degradation is discussed in the section on PA degradation.

Enzymes involved in PUR hydrolysis

Some previously described promiscuous enzymes known from their activities on PET were also found to hydrolyze ester-based PUR. Among those LCC, TfCut_2, Tcur1278, Tcur0390 ( 179 ), PueA, PueB ( 180 , 181 ), PulA ( 163 , 182 ), Hfor-PE-H ( 153 ), CE_Ubrb ( 183 ), CR lipase ( 184 ), CpCut1 ( 185 ), PET27, PET28, PET29, and PET30 ( 186 ) were found to be active.

Beyond esterases, only five enzymes have been reported to cleave intramolecular carbamate bonds, highlighting the need for further exploration into specific carbamatases capable of degrading these structures. UMG-SP1, UMG-SP2, and UMG-SP3 were screened for MDA-methanol, MDA-benzyl alcohol, MDA-ethanol, TDA-ethoxyethanol, and TDA-diethylene glycol. These compounds can be gained from post-consumer PU foams via glycolysis. The degrading products MDA, 2,4-TDA, and 2,6 TDA were detected using UHPLC ( 144 , 187 ). UMG-SP1 was also shown to be slightly active on a model TPU substrate via supercritical fluid chromatography ( 145 ). The catalytic mechanism of degrading a carbamate bond was investigated using the UMG-SP2 enzyme ( 188 ). UMG-SP3 was shown to be active on di-urethane ethylene methylenediamine (DUE-MDA) via LC-MS ( 189 ).

The NylB-type (see PA degradation) enzyme GatA is capable of degrading ethyl carbamate via the release of ammonia ( 190 ). In a further study, GatA was combined with the PET-degrading LCC cutinase, and it was suggested that LCC degrades poly(1,4-butylene adipate)-PUR (PBA-PUR) into oligomers, and in the second step, GatA degrades these carbamates under the release of MDA. However, the detected concentration of 26.7 µM MDA via HPLC is still a relatively small amount, and further improvements in the catalytic activities are needed ( 191 ).

Furthermore, the enzyme NfpolyA was found during a search for PA-degrading enzymes. Recently, NfpolyA was fused to a substrate-binding module of a polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) depolymerase. Surprisingly, this fusion protein was able to cleave the urethane bond 1-methoxypropan-2-yl(4-nitrophenyl) carbamate. Also, small amounts of degradation products of two PU polymers were detected ( 192 ).

The currently known PUR-degrading enzymes can be grouped based on their topology of the active site into three different types ( Fig. 5 ). Although the first type of enzyme shows a binding site located on the surface, the second has a more buried substrate binding site. In this case, the enzyme may have adapted to allow larger polymer chains binding. The last type of enzyme carries a well-like type binding site, which is only accessible for end groups and makes it presumably less accessible for larger polymer chains.

A further categorization of the known PUR-active enzymes can be made with respect to their catalytic triad. Although the esterases have the Ser-His-Asp triad, urethanases have Ser, Lys, and a special ( cis )Ser triad ( Fig. 5 ). In contrast to the conserved amino acids in the catalytic triads, the positions forming the oxyanion holes are not conserved. Altogether, these structural and sequenced-based analyses indicate a high variability. Because of the high chemical variability of PUR substrates, each compound may require its own specific enzyme ( 173 ).

Polycarbonates (PCs)

Synthetic polycarbonates (PCs) are very robust thermoplastic polymers known for their high impact resistance and transparency. Chemically, they feature carbonate groups (R-O-(C = O)-O-R') in their structure. Carbonate linkages are like carboxyl esters in that both contain the ester functional group prone to hydrolysis, but they resemble amide bonds in their higher stability relative to simple esters due to the involvement of multiple oxygen atoms providing enhanced electron delocalization ( 17 , 18 ). Unlike common linkages found in natural biomolecules (e.g., ester, amide), carbonate linkages are rare in organic compounds but are prevalent in inorganic forms such as carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) and calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) found in seashells and coral skeletons.

The most common PC type, the aromatic bisphenol A PC (BPA-PC), is synthesized by polymerizing bisphenol A (BPA) with phosgene (COCl₂) through interfacial or melt polymerization methods ( Table 1 ). Copolymer PCs are also produced by incorporating other diols, such as bisphenol S (BPS) or tetramethyl bisphenol F (TMBPF), along with BPA to achieve tailored properties like enhanced heat resistance or impact strength. Aliphatic PCs are generally synthesized by phosgenation of hydroxy compounds, transesterification of diols with lower dialkyl carbonates, or by ring-opening polymerization of six-membered cyclic carbonates ( 193 ). They offer enhanced flexibility and UV resistance and are more easily biodegradable but generally have lower thermal and mechanical strength, limiting their use to niche applications ( Table 1 ).

PCs find wide-ranging applications, including optical lenses, CDs, protective gear, automotive components, and construction materials, owing to their durability and clarity ( 17 ). Globally, approximately 7–8 Mt of PCs are produced annually, reflecting their significant industrial importance ( 194 ). Notably, a significant process has been made in developing bio-based PCs ( 195 ). However, their biodegradability needs to be assessed in future works.

Microbial degradation of PCs

The breakdown of PCs is initiated by the hydrolysis of carbonate bonds, which results in the release of two alcohols and CO 2 . In the case of BPA-PC, BPA is released, which is both an environmental pollutant and an endocrine disruptor ( 196 , 197 ). Microbial degradation of BPA by various bacteria and fungi has been reviewed in depth recently ( 198 ). It involves various routes starting from either C-C bond cleavage to form monophenolic compounds, oxidation, demethylation, or hydroxylation and results in the formation of ring-cleavage products and ultimately pyruvate and acetaldehyde ( Fig. 9 ). Some of the degradation intermediates found in B. megaterium include 4-(2-hydroxypropan-2-yl)phenol, 4-isopropenylphenol, 4-isopropylphenol, and benzoic, propanoic, and butanoic acids ( 198 ).

In contrast, aliphatic PCs primarily degrade into simple aliphatic diols, such as EG, 1,3-propanediol, 1,4-butanediol, or 1,6-hexanediol, which can be easily funneled into the central metabolism ( 193 ). Enzymes involved include alcohol dehydrogenases (EC 1.1.1.1, EC 1.1.1.202), aldehyde dehydrogenases (EC 1.2.1.21, EC 1.2.1.3), and oxidases (EC 1.1.3.15) to yield glyoxylate, propionyl-CoA, or succinate ( Fig. 9 ).

Compared with the other plastics discussed so far, there are relatively few publications detailing microbial isolates, their enzymes, and the metabolic pathways involved in degradation. However, some microorganisms have been identified that can degrade mainly aliphatic PCs, their byproducts, or BPA. These microorganisms are found across various phyla, including Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Bacillota, and fungi from the Ascomycota and Basidiomycota. Within Pseudomonadota, the first isolates were classified as Pseudomonas sp. 35L and Variovorax sp. WFF52 degrading poly(hexamethylene carbonate) (PHC) and poly(butylene carbonate) (PBC; 199 ). Other Pseudomonas strains capable of BPA degradation have been isolated from marine samples ( 200 ). Furthermore, Pseudoxhantomonas sp. NyZ600, isolated from activated sludge, was shown to release BPA and 4-cumylphenol from BPA-PC, altering the surface and reducing the MW distribution, but no enzyme or metabolic pathway has yet been identified ( 201 ). At least two Sphingomonas sp. isolates have been shown to degrade BPA, for which the nonylphenol monooxygenase NmoA could be one of the key enzymes ( 202 , 203 ). In the Actinomycetota phylum, the polyester-degrading Amycolatopsis sp. HT-6 and Arthrobacter sp. have been documented to degrade aliphatic PC ( 204 ). Bacillota seems to have delivered several isolates able to degrade BPA-PC to some extent ( 196 ). Finally, Artham and colleagues investigated the degradation of pretreated BPA-PC, including UV treatment, using fungi such as Engyodontium album MTP091 and Penicillium spp. MTP093 (Ascomycota), as well as Phanerochaete chrysosporium NCIM 1170, a white rot fungus from Basidiomycota. Notably, only a 5.4% weight loss was observed over the course of 1 year, highlighting the relatively low degradation rate achieved ( 205 ).

Enzymes involved in PC degradation

The breakage of the carbonate bond in PC materials can be facilitated by carboxyl esterases, albeit not very effectively, due to the structural similarity between it and the carboxyl ester bond discussed earlier ( Fig. 5 ). Early microbial enzymes that were attributed to the degradation of PC are the cholesterol esterase from Candida cylindracea, lipoprotein lipase from Pseudomonas sp ., and lipase preparation from C. cylindracea , Chromobacterium viscosum , Pseudomonas sp., and Rhizopus arrhizus ( 206 ). Since then, some more hydrolases have been described, including the lipases of C. rugosa and C. antarctica CalB (Novozymes 435; 207 ). A recent study showed enzymatic degradation with cutinases, where full conversion was only achieved with the addition of 30% DMSO ( 208 ). In the enzymatic degradation of BPA, both laccases and peroxidases from bacterial and fungal sources, which also play a role in lignin degradation, have been found to interact with BPA. These enzymes break carbon-carbon (C-C) bonds, enabling them to separate the aromatic rings and initiate ring-opening processes ( 209 ).

In conclusion, although aliphatic PCs are relatively easy to degrade and certain strains have shown the ability to degrade BPA, there is a lack of studies demonstrating the degradation of BPA-PC without prior treatment. Therefore, more research should be directed toward this area to better understand and enhance the biodegradation processes of untreated BPA-PC. Also, better enzymes should be characterized and engineered to facilitate the breakdown of BPA-PC more effectively and efficiently.

Not directly hydrolyzable synthetic polymers

This category encompasses a variety of polymers characterized by their C-C backbone, and in some cases, C-N or other linkages, which lack functional groups amenable to fast and direct enzymatic hydrolysis. This makes them inherently more resistant to microbial degradation. This group includes polyolefins such as polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP), vinyl polymers like polyvinyl chloride (PVC) and polystyrene (PS), as well as most synthetic rubbers and epoxy resins. Although it is possible to oxidize and degrade small and medium-sized C-C chains, monomers, or shorter versions of these polymers, their complex 3D structure in the polymeric state makes this very challenging. Some recent reports demonstrate that oxidative enzymes can modify these polymers randomly and at a very low rate. However, convincing data demonstrating specific enzymatic activities resulting in the microbial degradation of polyolefins on a significant scale have not been published.

Polyolefins and vinyl polymers (PE, PP, PS, and PVC)

Polymers such as polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), and polystyrene (PS) consist entirely of carbon (C) and hydrogen (H) atoms, giving them a very stable C-C backbone. Additionally, polyvinyl chloride (PVC) includes chlorine atoms along the backbone ( Fig. 1 ). This structure contributes to their durability and resistance to chemical reactions, such as hydrolysis or oxidation, making these plastics particularly persistent in the environment ( 20 ). Additionally, the lack of polar elements such as oxygen or nitrogen makes them highly hydrophobic, which affects their interaction with water and other substances. PE is produced from the polymerization of ethylene gas. Depending on the temperature and pressure during synthesis, the resulting polymer has different properties. At lower temperatures and pressures, low-density polyethylene (LDPE) is formed, characterized by branched polymer chains and a more flexible structure with less crystalline regions. Higher temperatures and pressures during synthesis produce high-density polyethylene (HDPE), which has a much denser, more crystalline, and linear structure. To produce PP, propene is used as the monomer. PS is created by the polymerization of styrene, and depending on the intended use, the polymer can be foamed to create expanded polystyrene (PS-E). For the manufacturing of PVC, vinyl chloride is used as the monomer. All these plastics are characterized as thermoplasts and find use in very diverse industries, including packaging, automotive, construction, and healthcare ( Table 1 ). Combined, they account for more than 60% of global plastic production ( Fig. 1 ). Their C-C backbone poses significant challenges for enzymatic and microbial breakdown. Their usually hydrophobic nature and poor water solubility together with the very densely structured polymer chain clearly limit the accessibility for enzymes.

Potential microbial degradation of polyolefins and vinyl polymers

Although recently several studies suggested the possibility of PE, PP, PS, and PVC biodegradation, the exact description of the molecular mechanisms behind these observations remains sparse, and as of today (June 2025), no enzymes have been reported, releasing significant amounts (>10 −6 M) of monomers or oligomers after enzymatic treatment of any of the above-mentioned polymers, or any derivatives thereof. Since these polymers are in principle n-alkanes, it is theoretically feasible that they can be degraded after initial breakage to shorter chain length ( 210 ). Although the chain length in the native polymer is certainly above 1,000, its length is far too long to be degraded by bacteria without prior strand breakage. A chain length of >40 will strongly hinder water solubility and enzyme access. Altogether, a relatively small number of long-chain n -alkane-degrading bacterial species are known. They originate from a limited number of bacterial genera including Alcanivorax, Arthrobacter, Acinetobacter, Bacillus, Marinobacter, Pseudomonas, Rhodococcus, Xylella, Thermoleophilum, Thalassolituus, and a few others ( 210 ). Most of the above-mentioned microorganisms can degrade n-alkanes with a chain length ranging between 10 and 30 carbon atoms, and only a few are known to act on n-alkanes with a chain length of up to 44. Among those are the Acinetobacter sp. M-1 isolate ( 211 ) and Arthrobacter nicotianae KCC B35 ( 212 ). The well-studied model organism Alcanivorax borkumenis can degrade n-alkanes having a chain length ranging from C 8 – C 32 ( 213 ). The initial degradation of long-chain n-alkanes under aerobic conditions is facilitated by oxidation of the terminal group to form a primary alcohol. In addition, sub-terminal oxidation is possible. Thereby, two types of enzymes are involved: The first type includes cytochrome-P450-related enzymes, and the second type of enzymes are alkane hydroxylases (pAHs). Following the initial oxidation of the n-alkane, the corresponding alcohol is further oxidized through an alcohol dehydrogenase and an aldehyde dehydrogenase. By this, a corresponding aldehyde and then a carboxylic acid are formed. The resulting carboxylic acid is activated by adding a CoA ester and employing the acyl-CoA synthase and finally funneled into the β-oxidation pathway. For further reading on bacterial n-alkane oxidation, we refer to references ( 210 , 214 ).

Within this framework, multiple recent studies have reported on the identification of putative polyolefin-active and vinyl-polymer degrading microorganisms. For example, Zampolli and colleagues reported on laccases involved in possible PE degradation ( 215 , 216 ). The identified enzymes were affiliated with Rhodococcus opacus R7, R. erythropolis D4, and P. citronellolis E5 and are unspecific multicopper laccases. Similar reports have already pointed to a potential and unspecific oxidative role of alkane monooxygenases and/or laccase-like enzymes ( 217 , 218 ). Furthermore, very promising studies reported on the degradation of polymers like PVC ( 219 ), PS ( 220 ), or PE ( 221 ). Intriguingly, in none of these studies, significant amounts of degradation products were observed, although sophisticated analytical tools were employed. Remarkably, in the study by Sanluis-Verdes, two recombinantly produced hexamerins derived from the saliva of wax moth larvae ( Galleria mellonella ) were identified and assumed to be responsible for PE degradation. In this context, a recent reinvestigation of the proposed PE- and PVC-degrading activities, previously observed in wax worm larva saliva ( 221 ) and in a Klebsiella sp. isolate ( 219 ), failed to detect any enzymatic activities on PE or PVC, respectively ( 222 ). Interestingly, the assumed catalase-peroxidase from Klebsiella sp. presumably active on PVC was found to be active on Amplex Red as a model substrate but had no effect on PVC. The wax worm larva enzyme was inactive when retested on model substrates. However, the wax worm saliva itself revealed phenol-oxidase activity but had no impact on PE ( 222 ). Thus, further work will have to identify truly active enzymes and bacteria acting on polyolefins and vinyl-polymers using smart enrichment technologies and other sophisticated approaches.

Epoxy resins

Epoxy-based polymers are formed through the reaction of three-membered rings containing an oxygen atom with curing agents, resulting in the formation of various bonds, such as secondary and tertiary amine (R-NH-CH 2 -, R-N(CH 2 -CH 3 )-CH 2 -), carboxyl ester (R-(C = O)-O-R'), ether (R-O-R'), and thiol (R-S-R') linkages. This so-called curing process results in the cross-linking of polymer chains, creating a durable three-dimensional network that imparts high strength and durability to the final material ( 21 , 22 ). The term "epoxy resin" refers to both cured and uncured forms. Epoxy-based polymers are ideal for a wide array of advanced applications, including paints, adhesives, coatings, electronic components, and biomedical devices, and they are extensively used in aerospace, automotive, and construction industries ( Table 1 ), especially in combination with carbon fiber reinforcements (CFRP; 21 ). The global epoxy resin market was approximately 3.6 Mt in 2022 ( 223 ). Although the exceptional properties of epoxy resins contribute to their widespread application, their inherent chemical stability and extensive cross-linking (curing) of these epoxy polymers, combined with the lack or inaccessibility of easily hydrolyzable linkages, make them highly resistant to microbial biodegradation ( 224 – 226 ).

Potential microbial degradation of epoxy resins

Recent studies primarily employed BPA- or DGEBA (bisphenol A diglycidyl ether)-based epoxy resins to identify microorganisms with the potential to degrade this polymer. For example, the co-cultivation of Rhodococcus rhodochrous and Ochrobactrum anthropi , isolated from soil near an epoxy-producing facility, indicated the first evidence of microbial growth on low-viscosity Araldite LY 5052 resin as a sole carbon source, although this resin is uncured ( 227 ). This implies that bacteria can target uncured forms rather than fully cross-linked epoxies. A comparable scenario is observed in studies using BPA as a substrate, which demonstrates that microorganisms can effectively degrade this pre-epoxy derivative monomer. The metabolism of BPA has already been reviewed in the PC section ( 228 – 230 ).

The microbial activity on fully cured epoxy coatings has also been explored. Marine bacteria, including P. putida , P. aeruginosa, and Bacillus flexus, demonstrated decreased corrosion resistance of epoxy-coated steel by forming biofilms after a month of immersion in seawater ( 231 – 233 ). Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) results verified specific interactions that P. putida oxidized hydroxyl (C-OH) groups, whereas P. aeruginosa decreased C-O-C and C-O groups significantly, increasing the coating’s hydrophobicity. B. flexus was noted for breaking down aromatic rings and epoxy groups, likely due to secreted organic acids. The harsh conditions in seawater, such as high salt concentrations that can corrode epoxy coatings, may initially facilitate microbial biofilm formation, exacerbating the degradation process. Additionally, the presence of sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) has been shown to lead to coating deterioration via the reduction of sulfate to sulfide, reacting with iron in the steel to create ferrous sulfide, a marker of degradation ( 234 ).

Epoxy coatings may not only harbor various additives, pigments, and fillers that enhance performance but can also provide nutrients for microbial growth. A study by Cappitelli and colleagues addressed this issue. They identified a diverse group of microorganisms, including the bacterial genera Bacillus , Brevibacillus , Micrococcus , Staphylococcus , Kocuria , Pseudomonas , Agrobacterium , and Ochrobacterium , as well as fungi from Alternaria sp. and Cladosporium spp., that were attached to epoxy coatings on the marble of the Milan Cathedral ( 235 ). Marble surfaces were consolidated with epoxy resin and treated with poly(isobutyl methacrylate) (PiBMA). Although X-ray diffraction confirmed epoxy presence, deterioration was likely due to other protective agents, such as acrylics, which are more prone to microbial degradation. Biodegradation assays showed fungi could degrade PiBMA but not epoxy resin, suggesting that the deterioration was mainly caused by these ester-based agents, not the epoxy itself.

Although thicker epoxy polymers and composites intended for structural application may exhibit greater resistance to microbial degradation, one of the first investigations into the biodegradation of epoxy-based composites was conducted in 1997 by Gu and colleagues ( 236 ). The study found that Hercules 3501-6 epoxy resin/graphite fiber composites exhibited susceptibility to colonization by a fungal consortium comprising Aspergillus versicolor , Cladosporium cladosporioides , and Chaetomium sp. SEM analysis after a month showed fungal hyphae and spores on the resin surface. Mechanical testing revealed changes in pore size and number, and a weakened bond between the reinforcing fibers and the resin matrix due to fungal activity. Another study by Negi and colleagues revealed that bacterial consortia, comprising Bacterium Te68R PN12 ( DQ423487 ), Microbacterium sp. MK3 ( DQ318884 ), and P. putida MK4 ( DQ318885 ), survived and multiplied in minimal media where epoxy served as the primary carbon source after a 15-day incubation ( 237 ). Further analyses indicated the loss of C–N, C–OH, and C–O, along with a 34% weight loss of the polymer. Similarly, a recent study convincingly demonstrated the potential role of the fungus Ganoderma adspersum (Empa 645) and a microbial consortium consisting of the fungi Trichoderma harzianum, Aspergillus calidoustus , and the bacteria Variovorax sp. and Methyloversatilis discipulorum in altering the surface of and properties of cured epoxy polymers ( 238 ).

Although these studies are only examples of many other studies, they indicate that microorganisms can colonize cured epoxy polymers and that they can modify their surface. These studies are all very solid publications that enriched a fascinating biodiversity with great metabolic potential. However, none of the studies provided clear and convincing evidence for degradation of the cured epoxy polymer at significant (micro- or milli-molar) levels. It can be assumed that the observed growth was mainly due to the metabolism of additives.

Potential enzymes involved in epoxy resin initial transformation

Today, no enzymes are known to degrade cured epoxy polymers (see PAZy database). The first tests were using an epoxy model substrate known as N,N-bis(2-hydroxypropyl)-p-toluidine (NNBT), which is derived from the Hexflow RTM6 epoxy polymer. NNBT contains key motifs such as a tertiary amine and exhibits solubility in organic solvents, which reduces its hydrophobicity and facilitates enzymatic reactions. In their studies, Dolz and colleagues identified unspecific peroxygenases (UPOs) that play a potential role in the degradation of this model substrate. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analyses confirmed the N-dealkylation of NNBT by UPOs (EC 1.11.2.1; 239 ).

Rubbers (isoprene polymers, PI, SR, and styrene butadiene)

In 2023, global rubber production amounted to 28.8 Mt ( 240 ). Rubber can be divided into natural (NR) and synthetic rubber (SR), and they are elastomeric polymers mainly used for manufacturing automotive tires, disposable gloves, medical devices, and construction materials. NR is a natural polymer whose chemical formula is poly-cis-1,4-isoprene, and in the SR, polymers are used in a wide variety of backbones. Among SR, the most common ones are poly-isoprene rubber (IR), styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR), and butadiene rubber (BR). Synthetic rubbers display properties such as high abrasion resistance, thermal stability, and improved resistance to aging ( 23 , 24 ).

For industrial use, rubber is vulcanized or cured using sulfur and other additives at temperatures between 120°C and 160°C. The vulcanizing agents introduce a three-dimensional polymer network resulting in increased stability and performance ( 241 ). However, the three-dimensional crosslinking makes rubber highly resistant to microbial and enzymatic degradation as it hinders enzyme access to the polymer.

Microbial degradation of rubbers

Rubber-degrading microorganisms can use NR as their sole carbon source and have been isolated from rubber-polluted environments, such as rubber dump sites or landfills ( 242 ) and others. Rubber-degrading microorganisms can be divided into two groups: (i) microorganisms capable of forming clearing zones (i.e., halos) on solid media containing NR due to the secretion of extracellular enzymes, and (ii) microorganisms that do not form clear zones but perform an adhesive growth on NR’s surface through a biofilm-mediated adhesion ( 243 , 244 ). Bacterial genera belonging to the first group include Actinoplanes, Streptomyces, and Micromonospora , whereas members of the second group mainly belong to the Actinomycetes phylum. Rubber biodegradation has also been reported in fungi. Penicillium , Aspergillus, and white rot fungi were among the first reported to be capable of growing in NR-containing liquid medium ( 245 , 246 ).

NR degradation is initiated through fungal laccases, Mn-peroxidases, and other oxidative enzymes ( 247 , 248 ). Since commercial rubber contains many high amounts of additives like sulfur, research has also focused on microbial biodesulfurization as a sustainable pretreatment for degrading vulcanized materials ( 249 ). Furthermore, some studies have addressed the degradation of other additives in NR using B. subtilis , P. aeruginosa , and Streptomyces sp ( 249 – 251 ).

Recent studies implied that microbial consortia derived from polluted sites could perform biodegradation in a very efficient way. This was attributed to the complexity of rubber and the relatively high levels of additives ( 252 , 253 ). Nawong et al. compared pure, mixed, and consortia cultures from rubber-contaminated soil using rubber latex gloves, with the mixed culture achieving the highest weight loss (18%), outperforming the pure culture (9.36%; 254 ). Communities that were either enriched or designed appeared to be the most efficient strategy to advance NR microbial degradation ( 254 , 255 ).

Rubber oxygenases are involved in rubber degradation

Since polyisoprene consists of a pure hydrocarbon backbone, oxidative reactions are necessary for the primary attack. The best-studied model enzymes capable of NR degradation are the latex-clearing protein (Lcp) and rubber oxygenases (RoxA and RoxB). They have been identified in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. They are extracellular enzymes, cleaving NR through an oxidative reaction, resulting in isoprene-containing degradation products with aldehyde and ketone terminal groups ( Fig. 10 ). Lcp cleaves the polymer via an endo-type mechanism into a mixture of short oligo-isoprenoids. RoxA, on the other hand, cleaves NR through an exo-type mechanism and delivers one main degradation product, a C15 oligo-isoprenoid called 12-oxo-4,8-dimethyltrideca-4,8-diene-1-al (ODTD; 256 ). RoxB is structurally like RoxA but performs an endo-type cleavage as described for Lcp ( 257 ). The resulting intermediates are then taken up by the cells and further metabolized. In Streptomyce s sp. K30, where Lcp was first detected, oxidoreductases OxiA and OxiB are responsible for the further degradation of the compounds before they enter the β-oxidation pathway ( 243 ).

Biodegradable polymers (bioplastics)

In response to the growing environmental pollution from oil-based polymers, attention has shifted in recent years to the development of biodegradable and compostable plastics ( 258 ). These so-called “bioplastics” are made from renewable resources such as sugars, corn, potatoes, or non-food sources like cellulosic feedstock or residual biomass. Among these, polylactic acid (PLA) and polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) stand out as inherently biodegradable polymers. Their polymeric structure is partly designed by nature, which makes biodegradability largely possible. The commonality among these bioplastics lies in their structure, which includes functional groups—predominantly esters—that facilitate microbial degradation under appropriate environmental conditions. Generally, there are three types of degradable plastics. Photodegradable bioplastics include a light-sensitive group in their polymer backbone, allowing for degradation upon exposure to light. Compostable bioplastics are defined as plastics that fully decompose biologically at a rate like other compostable materials. The most promising group, biodegradable bioplastics, is formed by polymers completely degraded by microorganisms leaving no toxic residues. Currently, bioplastics make up about 1% (2.1 Mt per year) of the market but are expected to grow by up to 30% annually in the coming years ( Fig. 1 ). They are mainly used in the packaging and medical industries, particularly for biocompatible and bioabsorbable medical devices ( 25 – 29 ). Other notable examples of bioplastics include polycaprolactone (PCL), polybutylene adipate terephthalate (PBAT), polybutylene succinate (PBS), and polyethylene furanoate (PEF).

Polylactic acid (PLA)

PLA is a synthetic polymer that can be produced from renewable monomers. It is obtained from lactic acid (LA), which can either be synthesized chemically or by microbial fermentation of glucose or other sugars obtained from natural sources like corn. Chemically, PLA is synthesized by polycondensation through ring-opening polymerization of lactide, which is a cyclic dimer of lactic acid. PLA made up 31% (675 thousand tons per year) of the worldwide produced bioplastics in 2023, and its production is predicted to grow approximately by 3.2 Mt per year by 2028. It is a linear thermoplastic, whose stereochemical structure can be modified by using a different mixture of its L- or D-isomers. Therefore, either high-molecular-weight amorphous or crystalline polymers can be synthesized, depending on their application. Polymers derived from L-lactide are predominantly utilized for biodegradable packaging materials, whereas polymers made from D-lactide are primarily employed in bioengineering applications and medical applications ( 259 – 261 ).

Microbial degradation of PLA

PLA degradation has been observed in both fungi and bacteria and was recently reviewed ( 262 – 264 ). The ability to degrade PLA appears to be widespread in nature ( 265 ).

Enzymatic degradation of PLA is relatively slow and depends on the degree of crystallinity, the type of the copolymer, and the additives. The enzymes involved are primarily proteases (EC 3.4), with a smaller percentage of lipases (EC 3.1.1.3) and cutinases (EC 3.1.1.74), esterases (EC 3.1.1), and serine proteases (EC 3.4.21). Relatively few enzymes have been characterized in more detail, and to date, 41 enzymes are listed in the PAZy database. The bacterial enzymes characterized are mainly affiliated with the genus of Amycolatopsis, Paenibacillus, Lederbergia, and Alcanivorax ( 266 , 267 ). The few fungal enzymes known originate from Parengyodontium, Aspergillus, and Cryptococcus ( 268 – 270 ). In addition, few metagenome-derived enzymes have been identified ( 271 , 272 ). For a complete list of all known verified PLA-active enzymes, we refer to the PAZy database ( 8 ).

One of the first identified enzymes to degrade PLA was proteinase K from the fungus Tritirachium album ( 273 ). Proteinase K primarily hydrolyzes amorphous regions. In addition, the cutinase-like enzyme (CLE), isolated from the yeast Cryptococcus sp. S-2 was identified as PLA degrading enzyme ( 270 ).

Poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL)

Poly( ε -caprolactone) is a relatively frequently used biopolymer, mainly because of its miscibility with a wide range of other polymers, its ideal mechanical properties, and its biodegradability. PCL is synthesized via two different pathways. The first one is the polycondensation of a hydroxycarboxylic acid (6-hydroxyhexanoic acid), and the second one is the ring-opening polymerization of a cyclic monomer lactone (ε-caprolactone; 274 ). It can also be combined with other polymers, such as cellulose propionate or PLA, to improve stress crack resistance or adhesion ( 275 ). Despite its relatively wide use as a biopolymer, only relatively few studies have addressed the identification and biochemical characterization of enzymes acting on the polymer.

Microbial degradation of PCL

The thermophilic fungus Thermomyces lanuginosus was associated with the complete degradation of PCL at 50°C during a 91-day time period ( 263 ). Additionally, the mold Pullularia pullulans was reported to degrade low molecular weight PCL ( 276 ). Furthermore, a variety of marine fungi appear to be able to degrade the polymer ( 277 ).

The few enzymes that have been identified include fungal lipases that are well known for their promiscuous activities on other polymeric substrates like the Candida antarctica lipase CalB ( 278 ). One further example is the fungal lipase from Mucor miehei , which was able to catalyze the degradation of PCL with a maximum conversion degree of 70% after 1 h within the temperature range of 40–60°C ( 279 ). Prominent bacterial lipases acting on PCL were derived from Lactobacillus plantarum and were found to efficiently degrade PCL, mainly degrading the amorphous regions of the polymer ( 280 ). Another PCL degrading enzyme is the thermophilic lipase isolated from Brevibacillus thermoruber . The optimal conditions for this lipase were at 55°C and at pH 7.5. Additionally, the presence of Ca 2+ enhanced the PCL degradation, whereas all other metal ions tested had a decreasing effect on the activity of the lipase ( 281 ).

Polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs)

Polyhydroxyalkanoates are polyesters composed of different hydroxyalkanoates that are synthesized by bacteria and accumulate intracellularly. PHAs are highly crystalline thermoplastics, which are mainly used in the packaging industry and for disposable items such as razors and other hygiene products and biomedical applications. The best-characterized and most common type of PHA is poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) (P3HB) ( 282 ). PHAs are synthesized by a wide range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria belonging to various genera. Biosynthesis is best studied in Cupriavidus necator H16 (formerly Ralstonia eutropha; 283 – 285 ).

Microbial degradation of PHAs

Both PHA-producing and non-producing organisms—including bacteria, fungi, and yeast—are involved in extracellular PHA degradation. Alcaligenes faecalis was one of the first identified PHA-degrading bacteria in activated slugs, whereas thermophilic strains like Schlegelella thermodepolymerans can degrade PHAs at high temperatures. Fungi such as Aspergillus fumigatus are also known to decompose PHAs, and certain yeasts like Candida guilliermondii can degrade PHB. Overall, diverse microorganisms contribute to PHA degradation across a wide range of different habitats ( 286 – 288 ).

Microbial and enzyme-driven PHA degradation is well studied in a few model organisms like Cupriavidus necator , P. putida, and a few others ( 287 ). The enzymes involved in the first steps are either secreted extracellular PHA (e-PHA) or internal PHA (i-PHA) depolymerases ( 285 , 287 , 289 ). Extracellular PHA depolymerases are further classified into two groups based on their substrate specificity. One group consists of SCL-PHA depolymerases, which specifically degrade PHB and its copolyesters. MCL-PHA depolymerases make up the second group, which mainly breaks down aliphatic and aromatic PHAs ( 290 ).

All known depolymerases are carboxylesterases (EC 3.1.1.75 and EC 3.1.1.76) and the enzymes biochemically characterized are summarized in a recent review ( 291 ). An up-to-date list can be found in the PAZy database. Depolymerases have been found in a wide range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Some bacteria like Cupriavidus necator ( Ralstonia eutropha ) and Paucimonas lemoignei produce multiple and different types of intra- and extra-cellular PHA-depolymerases. Enzymes from these two model organisms and from P. putida KT2442 are well-studied ( 292 ). The primary degradation product of PHA-depolymerases is 3-ß-hydroxy-butyrate. It is converted by specific 3-ß-hydroxy-butyrate dehydrogenases (EC 1.1.1.30) to form acetoacetate. This product is activated to form acetoacetyl-CoA employing either a succinyl-CoA transferase (EC 2.8.3.5) or an acetoacetyl-CoA synthetase (EC 6.2.1.16; 293 ). The produced acetoacetyl-CoA is cleaved by β-ketothiolase (EC 2.3.1.9) into two molecules of acetyl-CoA and metabolized via the TCA cycle and/or the glyoxylate pathway ( 294 ).

Polybutylene adipate terephthalate (PBAT)

PBAT is an aliphatic–aromatic co-polyester, which is produced by a random polymerization of TPA and AA with 1,4-butanediol. Since it has similar properties to low-density PE, the polymer is mainly used for biodegradable mulching foils in agriculture and as a packaging material ( 295 ).

Microbial degradation of PBAT

Its aliphatic components and the ester bonds make PBAT readily accessible to enzymatic degradation. Its high biodegradability makes it an ideal material to be used in agriculture and for composting. A wide range of bacteria and fungi are predicted to be able to degrade the polymer ( 291 ). Enzymes known to degrade PBAT are carboxylesterases, lipases, and cutinases. Examples are the esterase PpEst from Pseudomonas pseudoalcaligenes ( 296 ), the lipase PfL1 from Pelosinus fermentans ( 297 ), and thermophilic hydrolase TfH from Thermobifida fusca ( 298 ). Furthermore, the pathogen Clostridium botulinum harbors an esterase acting on PBAT ( 299 ). The fungus Humicola insolens -derived esterase HiC is also known to hydrolyze PBAT ( 299 ). Since all known PET-active enzymes are highly promiscuous enzymes, it is likely that many of these will be able to hydrolyze PBAT as well. A comprehensive list of well-characterized PBAT-acting enzymes is available in the PAZy database.

Since the monomers of PBAT are TPA, AA, and 1,4-butanediol, it can be expected that they are degraded using different metabolic routes. TPA will be metabolized as outlined above ( Fig. 6 and section on PET degradation). AA and other medium-chain length α,ω-dicarboxylate degradation pathways have been investigated in Acinetobacter sp. and P. putida KT2440 ( 300 , 301 ). In the first step, adipate is activated by adding a CoA cofactor to form adipyl-CoA. In the next step, 2,3-didehydro-adipyl-CoA is formed using an oxidoreductase. Two additional steps involving a transferase and oxidoreductase are then employed to form 3-hydroxyadipyl-CoA, 3-oxoadipyl-CoA, and finally, succinyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA formed, involving yet a final transferase ( Fig. 7 and section on PA degradation).

1,4-butanediol degradation is well studied in P. putida KT2440 ( 302 ). 1,4-butanediol is metabolized in several steps, of which the first one includes oxidation to 4-hydroxybutyrate. Three possible bacterial pathways are available for further metabolism of 4-hydroxybutyrate in this model organism. The first option uses direct oxidation to succinate. In the second pathway, CoA is added for activation and to allow oxidation to succinyl-CoA. The third route involves β-oxidation to form glycolyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA ( 302 ).

Polybutylene succinate (PBS)

PBS is a biodegradable aliphatic polyester synthesized by condensation of 1,4 butanediol and succinic acid. It is a thermoplast, and its mechanical properties are like those of the high-density PE and isotactic PP ( 303 ). The PBS monomers can either be derived from chemical synthesis using fossil resources or from biomass. PBS is mainly used for mulching foils and other wrapping materials.

Microbial degradation of PBS

Because of the central ester bond, it is fully decomposable, and because of the known promiscuity of lipases and esterases, it can be expected that many enzymes from different microbial phyla will be able to degrade the polymer. Thereby, it was shown that species from the genera Methylobacterium, Deinococcus, Hymenobacter, Bacillus, Sphingomonas, and some unclassified Comamonadaceae can degrade the polymer ( 304 ). Furthermore, it has been reported that fungi (i.e., Ascomycota) are capable of PBS degradation ( 305 ). Metabolic routes leading to the uptake of 1,4-butanediol have been explained above (see “Microbial degradation of PBAT”).

Polyethylene furanoate (PEF)

Polyethylene furan-2,5-dicarboxylate/furanoate is a bio-based polyester emerging as a sustainable alternative to PET. It is synthesized through the polymerization of 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA) and EG. Remarkably, FDCA can be produced from industrial carbon dioxide and biowaste, enhancing the sustainability of PEF production ( 306 ). PEF is noted for its superior gas barrier properties compared with PET, providing enhanced protection against oxygen, carbon dioxide, and water vapor. This makes it ideal for applications such as food and beverage packaging, where it helps maintain product freshness and extends shelf-life ( 307 ).

Microbial degradation of PEF

Relatively few studies have addressed this research topic. Based on these studies, it appears that due to enzyme promiscuity, few of the well-known PET-active cutinases and esterases can convert PEF. Among those that have been verified to degrade PEF were the LCC ( 308 ), the IsPETase and its variant FAST-PETase from Ideonella ( Piscinibacter ) sakaiensis ( 309 ), the Thc_cut1 from Thermobifida cellulosilytica ( 310 ), or the HiC from Humicola insolens ( 311 ).

Next target polymers for microbial and enzymatic degradation

In recent years, considerable advancements have been made in the enzymatic degradation of polymers, particularly PET. The knowledge from PET studies is now being applied to other polymers like PAs and PURs, where literature is advancing fast. However, further research is necessary to fully exploit this potential across a wider range of polymers. So far, most efforts have concentrated on a few polymers, such as PET, PA6, Impranil DLN, aliphatic PCs, and various bioplastics. However, as shown in Table 1 , there are many other polymers within these classes, that is, PEN, PA66, aramids, or BPA-PC, which remain under-researched. To bridge this gap, it is crucial to systematically evaluate both known and novel microorganisms and enzymes on these lesser-explored polymers. This approach could uncover new catalysts and degradation mechanisms, enhancing our biodegradation capabilities. Enzymes that degrade specific chemical bonds should be tested on other polymers containing the same bond, potentially creating a "fingerprint" of bond-specific enzymatic activity. This strategy could guide future research efforts, supported by the integration of AI and big data to predict and optimize enzyme-substrate interactions across multiple substrates.

Despite significant efforts to degrade non-hydrolyzable polymers (i.e. polyolefins, vinyl polymers, epoxy resins, or vulcanized rubbers), substantial challenges persist, and the identification of effective microorganisms and enzymes is still in its early stages.

Further, smaller polymers like per- and poly-fluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) and derivatives such as fluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) represent a significant global challenge. These compounds are used widely for their resistance to heat, water, and oil ( 312 ). However, they persist in the environment, are toxic, and pose high risks to ecosystems and human health ( 313 , 314 ). Therefore, research efforts need to intensify PFAS dehalogenation and degradation, using both established microbial systems and developing new catalytic approaches to find sustainable solutions.

In summary, a comprehensive approach is needed to advance knowledge on microbial and enzymatic degradation research, embracing a wider scope and cultivating collaborative efforts. Expanding our focus will help discover effective degradation strategies and contribute significantly to reducing polymer waste and its environmental impact.

MINING BACTERIAL GENOMES AND METAGENOMES FOR NOVEL PLASTIC-ACTIVE ENZYMES

Finding novel plastic-active microbes and enzymes is a complex yet essential task in unraveling the molecular key involved in microbial plastic transformation. Two key strategies, sequence-based and functional approaches, are widely utilized to uncover enzymes ( Fig. 11 ). While sequence-based methods use already characterized plastic-active enzymes to mine genomic and metagenomic data from diverse environmental samples and databases ( 9 , 82 ), functional approaches rely on the cultivation and enriching of microbial communities or direct screening of environmental DNA (eDNA) for enzymatic activity against plastic polymers without prior knowledge of their gene sequences ( 82 , 87 ).

Sequence-based mining approaches

Different databases listing plastic-degrading enzymes have been published and are available for sequence-based mining. Table 2 and references therein summarize useful databases to identify plastics-active bacteria and enzymes. The Plastics Microbial Biodegradation Database (PMDB; 315 ) and PlasticDB ( 316 ) list both characterized as well as many predicted plastics-active enzymes and microorganisms. Both databases contain multiple entries that are not verified and predict in part incorrectly enzyme activities on polymers like PE, for which no truly active enzymes have been characterized (see above). The Plastics-Active Enzymes Database (PAZy; 8 ), which was created and is manually curated by co-authors of this review, lists only biochemically verified enzymes. Because of the strict requirement of biochemical characterization of every deposited enzyme, it has much fewer entries. Furthermore, the Plastics Meta-omic Database (PMDB) has recently been published, which deals with the analysis of public, plastics-associated metagenomes ( 317 ). Although this database includes a list of known plastic-active enzymes, the data were extracted from multiple sources including the PlasticDB and PAZy databases, which has resulted in duplicate or triplicate entries. Several studies have shown the advantages of bioinformatic approaches, identifying new plastic-degrading enzymes by utilizing databases ( 83 , 186 , 324 , 325 ). Within this framework, searches using profile Hidden Markov-Models (HMM) have been very successful. One of the first studies mined over 130 metagenomes and thereby demonstrated the global occurrence of PET-active enzymes and implied that crude oil sites are rich sources for identifying plastic-degrading microorganisms ( 82 ). Erickson and colleagues recently used profile HMM searches to identify 74 putative PETases from databases, of which 51 were successfully expressed and tested for activity on PET ( 81 ). Although some of these enzymes were already reported in previous studies, Erickson and colleagues obtained 21 novel PETases. In both these studies, the HMM motifs were carefully designed and were the key to the successful identification of active enzymes. Notably, in the publication by Danso et al., a relatively high cutoff value was used ( 82 ), whereas the Erickson study used a lower cutoff, which resulted in the detection of higher biodiversity ( 81 ). The use of poorly designed HMMs, however, will result in the prediction of many false positive hits ( 326 ). Despite such successful case studies, they are limited by the number and diversity of previously identified plastic-degrading enzymes. To find truly novel enzyme candidates, functional metagenomics and culture-based approaches are still considered a crucial addition to bioinformatic searches ( 9 , 327 ).

Culturomics and cultivation of plastic degraders

For this, microorganisms are cultivated by using plastic (powder or foil) as the main or even sole carbon and energy source, allowing for selective enrichment of consortia able to grow on the plastic polymer ( Fig. 11 ). Excellent reviews of approaches taken to enrich potential plastics-degrading bacteria have been published ( 328 , 329 ). Afterwards, strains and enzymes are identified by either molecular or bioinformatic approaches ( 327 ). Samples taken for inoculation are usually plastic-contaminated sites such as soil, compost, or marine samples and landfills ( 79 , 330 – 332 ). Additionally, HMM-searches of different metagenomes listed in the IMG database in 2018 resulted in a crude-oil metagenome showing the highest relative abundance of putative PETases, making crude-oil sites a promising sample collection site for finding plastic-degrading microorganisms ( 82 ). Culture-based approaches are generally seen as limited, as it is estimated that so far only around 1% of microorganisms have been cultivated. However, despite these limitations, most of the published plastics-degrading enzymes have been identified this way ( 264 , 333 ).

Function-based mining of metagenomes and genomes

Next to cultivation and computational approaches, functional metagenomics has been shown to be an invaluable tool for discovering highly active plastic-degrading enzymes ( Fig. 11 ). The construction of DNA libraries from environmental samples enables quick access to enzymes of even non-culturable microorganisms ( 327 ). Functional screening could thus uncover enzymes that degrade plastics through mechanisms or structural motifs that are not present in previously identified sequences, thereby expanding the functional diversity of plastic-active enzymes ( 82 ). Although this approach generally suffers from low hit rates when screening clones, multiple plastics-degrading enzymes have already been found using different methods such as λ ZAP phage, plasmid, or fosmid libraries ( 77 , 271 , 272 , 334 – 336 ). The most prominent example, the LCC, an enzyme cloned from a fosmid library of a leaf-branch compost metagenome, is still considered the best active wild-type PETase to date, underlining the importance of functional metagenomics at finding novel highly active enzymes ( 77 ).

Promising approaches yet to be explored

Besides searching for novel enzymes in databases or nature, designing new, synthetic plastics-active enzymes is yet another promising approach to advance the biodegradation of plastics ( Fig. 11 ). One such approach has previously been demonstrated by introducing the serine hydrolase catalytic triad commonly found in PETases into FraC, a membrane pore-forming protein ( 337 ). This protein, normally devoid of any catalytic activity, showed degradation activity on PET after its mutation.

The use of other already naturally occurring enzyme scaffolds is thus another considerable option to explore the biodegradation of plastics. However, it is likely that future approaches will include the development of protein language models (PLMs) and structure-based searches, which will be AI-assisted ( 338 ).

METHODS FOR DETECTION AND QUANTIFICATION OF MICROBIAL PLASTIC DEGRADATION

There is a wide array of analytical methodologies available to probe microbial degradation activity. The choice of method depends on the plastic type under investigation, the nature of the bioactive component (e.g., whole cells or enzymes), and the environment under which the degradation is to be assessed (laboratory or environmental conditions). Visual and growth-related methods

Most microbial degradation studies rely on the determination of weight loss in combination with microbial growth and or biofilm formation on the polymer surface ( 7 ). However, this technology is not very precise and can be misleading. Microorganisms frequently prefer to grow on easier-to-degrade additives that are often contained in the polymers and use these as sole carbon and energy source. In addition, other organic traces and impurities often present in culture media may allow poor but reproducible growth in long-term enrichment cultures. Finally, microbial consortia often harbor chemolithoautotrophic bacteria facilitating growth by CO 2 fixation. Altogether, these challenges frequently lead to inaccuracies and overinterpretation of data ( 9 , 44 , 340 , 341 ).

Another method relying on bacterial growth is linked to halo formation on agar plates containing polymeric substrates. Here, clearing zones (halos) may indicate substrate degradation or conversion. Halo formation in agar plates is mainly employed for high-throughput screening of plastic-degrading organisms and has been frequently used for polymers like PCL or PET. Besides being used in solid agar media, turbidimetric assays can also be conducted in liquid media/buffer systems. These include screening for hydrolytic lysis on the polyester polyurethane dispersion Impranil DLN or monitoring turbidity reduction in PET nanoparticle dispersions in liquid media. However, these approaches are challenging when assessing microorganisms, as their growth itself produces turbidity, potentially interfering with accurate measurements. These approaches are further hindered by reproducibility problems with respect to agar plate thickness, proper quantification of halos, and the production of the dispersion and its stability over time - especially for Impranil DLN, which can tend to aggregate without actual degradation ( 160 , 376 ). In addition, the secretion of bacterial chelators and secreted promiscuous enzymes may interfere with the observed results and likewise result in false-positive hits.

Classical light microscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), and lens-less digital holographic microscopy (LDHM) have been employed in a multitude of studies. Although often providing straightforward indications of polymer modification microbial colonization and in part degradation, the visual assessment does not allow for a quantitative assessment of the degradation.

Chromatography and other analytical tools

Reversed-phase HPLC methods (RP-HPLC) in conjunction with UV absorption detectors are often used to detect and follow polymer degradation, especially for PET ( 79 , 186 , 324 , 339 ) and others. Monomers of other types of plastic may have to be detected using different chromatographic principles and detection methods. For example, gas chromatography (GC) was employed to characterize the assumed breakdown products of PE ( 350 ). The chromatographic detection of higher molecular weight oligomeric degradation products can be facilitated via gel permeation chromatography (GPC; 362 , 377 ). Furthermore, chromatographic separation coupled with mass spectrometry (MS) allows for a more thorough identification of degradation products as for example shown for screening a larger number of novel potential PA-hydrolyzing enzymes from metagenomes ( 143 ).

Spectroscopic approaches are frequently used to detect degradation products through absorption or fluorescence. Some compounds may require conjugation with absorbing or fluorescing molecules for detection. Examples include hydroxylation of TPA to yield a detectable fluorophore ( 360 ), quantification of total aromatic PET degradation products via their UV absorption ( 361 ), or the development of a continuous assay to monitor PET depolymerization kinetics ( 378 ).

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) and attenuated total reflection infrared spectroscopy (ATR-IR) assess chemical group changes and provide sensitive detection of chemical modifications induced by biocatalysts ( 379 ). These methods allow for sensitive detection of biocatalyst-induced changes to the polymer surface and can be employed to monitor not only the complete cleavage of polymer chains but also the introduction of functional groups to the polymer. This makes them especially valuable tools for the assessment of the transformation of more recalcitrant polyolefins like PE. However, while being a very useful and sensitive tool, one caveat when using these methods can be the possible misinterpretation of spectra through improper sample preparation and contamination by the employed biocatalysts ( 379 ).

In addition, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy provides molecular-level insights into structural modifications like chain scission or functional group transformations. However, its high cost and complexity may limit its practical applicability in many studies. Furthermore, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) has been used to assess polymer film degradation. Here, a PET membrane subjected to enzymatic degradation was monitored using an impedimetric measurement setup ( 364 ).

Furthermore, measurements of reaction-induced pH changes ( 34 ) are frequently employed as it is a cost-effective and easy-to-set-up method, especially for the assessment of hydrolytic polymer cleavage. Another indirect method to assess degradation reactions is isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), which has been used to monitor the heat changes generated by enzymatic plastic degradation ( 367 ).

Further noteworthy methods include thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), which distinguishes polymers from their degradation products based on decomposition or evaporation temperatures and has been applied to monitor bacterial degradation of PCL ( 280 ), PET ( 368 ), PUR ( 191 ), and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). DSC detects changes in polymer thermal properties, such as melting, glass transition, and crystallization temperatures, and has also been used in multiple studies ( 370 , 371 ).

Biosensors as versatile tools

Besides these often highly sophisticated chemical or physical measurements, several whole-cell biosensors have been developed aimed at the detection of biological plastic degradation. Especially for the PET degradation product TPA, several biosensors have been developed. Pardo et al. constructed a fluorescent reporter strain in Acinetobacter baylyi with a 10  µM TPA detection limit ( 372 ). In a further study, the promiscuous receptor and regulator XylS from Pseudomonas putida, which is responsible for the transcription activation of the meta -toluate degradation pathway, was adapted for TPA detection in E. coli with a sensitivity of 10 µM TPA ( 373 ). Furthermore, a luminescent biosensor based on the luciferase LuxAB from Photorhabdus luminescens was used for the detection of TPA. In this study, the authors coupled a carboxylic acid reductase (CAR) and the luciferase LuxAB proteins to generate a luminescent signal in E. coli . The readout semi-quantitatively correlated with the TPA concentration and was suitable for high-throughput screenings. TPA concentrations as low as 1  mM were detected with this reporter strain ( 374 ). The most sensitive strain reported is based on a Comamonas thiooxidans deletion mutant incapable of TPA assimilation, able to detect nM amounts of TPA ( 97 ).

BIOTECHNOLOGICAL AND SYNTHETIC APPROACHES ARE KEY TO A SUSTAINABLE PLASTICS-BASED BIOECONOMY

Microbial degradation of plastics offers a huge potential for industrial biobased upcycling and environmental remediation. The importance of this field has recently been described in detail ( 5 , 380 , 381 ). Currently, three main strategies are employed to advance this fast-emerging field, which was only invented shortly after the discovery of the first highly active PETases LCC and IsPETase ( 77 , 79 , 380 ). The first strategy includes the isolation of novel robust enzymes and the subsequent evolution of these for industrial use. The second strategy implements these enzymes in recombinant microorganisms to upcycle the primary degradation products to valuable biomolecules. The third strategy combines the first two to address the degradation and upcycling of mixed plastic waste. In the following section, we highlight the most important findings with respect to the three strategies outlined above.

Enzyme engineering approaches

As mentioned above, multiple wild-type enzymes are known to act on PET and several of these offer promising scaffolds for engineering approaches to further improve the catalytic activities. Among those are the well-known LCC and IsPETase ( 77 , 79 ). In addition, enzymes derived from extremophiles harbor huge potential for their industrial implementation ( 382 ). Some of the most successful and improved examples are the DuraPETase ( 383 ) with a significantly increased T m and an over 300-fold increased catalytic performance, the HotPETase ( 384 ) engineered to be active on semicrystalline PET, and the FAST-PETase ( 385 ). Finally, one hallmark study reported on the engineering of the LCC ICCG variant with a 9.3°C increased melting temperature and strongly improved catalytic activity. LCC ICCG hydrolyzes 16.7 g TPA L −1 h −1 pretreated post-consumer PET with a conversion rate of over 90% in 10 h ( 70 ). Although these are only the most prominent examples, others have been reported and are in part listed in the PAZy database ( 8 ).

Inspired by these promising results, very recently a highly efficient and bio-based industrial recycling process at a multiton scale for PET degradation was established by the French company Carbios. This process depends on pretreated plastic using a thermal process and employing a recombinant LCC variant. The process allows 98% of PET conversion into the monomeric products TPA and EG in 24 h at 68°C with 1 mg enzyme g PET −1 at high substrate loadings of 200 g L -1 ( 70 , 386 ).

Multienzyme and fusion proteins for improved PET-hydrolysis

Within this framework, recent studies also described combination and fusion of various enzymes acting on PET to achieve faster degradation processes, thereby overcoming the problem that intermediates of the PET degradation (e.g., MHET, BHET, and short oligomers) rapidly accumulate during the oligomer hydrolysis and are only slowly converted ( 387 ). This problem was in part solved by combining a PETase with an effective MHETase in a multienzyme complex ( 388 , 389 ) and combining it in part with surface display ( 390 ). In one of these studies, a native PETase expressed in Microbacterium oleivorans ( 391 ) was combined with a BHET-active cutinase from Thermobifida fusca . This resulted in a significantly increased degradation of PET films ( 392 ). Furthermore, the fusion of cellulose-binding domains with PETases and employing the cellulosome scaffolding complexes appears to be a promising strategy to enhance PETase binding to the polymer and hydrolysis ( 393 ). In light of these observations, the recent identification of bacteroidetal SusD-like specific PET binding proteins might be a powerful tool to further advance these engineering approaches and improve the st for an active PETase binding of the hydrolytic enzymes to the polymer ( 394 ).

Although the above-described approaches all rely on recombinant enzymes applied to PET powder or particles, other approaches have tried to engineer various bacterial, yeast, or algal strains for PET conversion. Among those were reports on Pichia pastoris ( Komagataella phaffii ) ( 395 ), E. coli ( 396 ) , Clostridium thermocellum ( 397 ) , B. subtilis ( 398 ), and B. thermoamylovorans ( 399 ) equipped with different PETases for expression and secretion. Furthermore, the use of the microalga Phaeodactylum tricornutum as a host for an active PETase secretion was also successfully demonstrated ( 400 ). In another case study, the IsPETase was expressed and secreted by the alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii ( 401 ). Altogether, these are very promising studies to further advance microbial and enzyme-driven PET degradation and upcycling.

Microbial upcycling and strain engineering approaches

Finally, using synthetic biology approaches appears to be a promising line of work that allows the design of engineered strains for fast polymer hydrolysis and PET upcycling. One promising approach generated adipic acid from cis , cis -muconic acid derived from PET degradation ( 402 ). Another promising approach in E. coli used the monomer TPA and converted it into the small molecule vanillin, which is of high value in the food and cosmetics industries ( 403 ). A more recent study employed P. putida and engineered it to convert EG to the biopolymer PHA ( 404 ). Within this framework, recently, the engineering of Rhodococcus jostii resulted in an efficient upcycling of both degradation products - TPA and EG - to lycopene, a powerful antioxidant ( 405 ). For a more detailed insight into this rapidly emerging field of strain engineering, we refer to Fig. 12 and other references ( 406 , 407 ). Advanced engineering and smart upcycling concepts will certainly be a very valuable research topic providing solutions to some of the bottlenecks affiliated to environmental plastic recycling.

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Within this review, we have highlighted many of the recent key findings related to microbial plastic degradation on various polymers and using a wide variety of different bacteria, archaea, and fungi. Although tremendous progress has been made in identifying enzymes mainly acting on PET, ester-based PUR, and nylon (PA) oligomers, large knowledge gaps exist with respect to most of the other synthetic polymers. For the majority of these (e.g., PE, PP, PS, PVC, and others), no truly active enzymes or effective organisms are currently known. Thus, future work will have to mine biodiversity to identify genes and pathways involved in the degradation of these recalcitrant and very stable polymers. Identifying enzymes and microorganisms that alter these synthetic polymers would be very valuable. Thus, identifying dehalogenase that acts on PVC or other per- or poly-halogenated polymers (i.e., PFAS) would be a first and major step toward identifying potential concepts for microbial and enzymatic degradation. Similarly, identifying highly active laccases, peroxygenases, and peroxidases involved in the initial oxygen-activation of polyolefins and other highly stable polymers (see above) would be a further major advancement. Within this framework, it is also noteworthy that the currently known biodiversity of enzymes and bacteria linked to PET, PUR, and PA-degrading microorganisms is still rather limited and needs to be expanded further to allow the degradation of highly crystalline polymers at the industrial scale.

Although enzyme-driven plastic degradation at the laboratory or industrial scale is now relatively well studied for the polymer PET, the role of the microorganisms involved in enzyme-driven PET degradation in the environment is not yet well understood. Within this framework, it is not clear which molecular keys are involved in the initial attachment of the bacteria to the polymer and if different polymers can be sensed by different bacteria. Only a few studies have identified possible proteins involved in synthetic polymer binding ( 394 ). Thus, further studies that advance our knowledge of early recognition and binding proteins involved will be very valuable. Further knowledge gaps center around the question of which signals are involved in activating the transcription of bacterial genes coding for plastic-active enzymes and which regulatory pathways are involved in individual bacteria and plastic-degrading microbial consortia. Finally, it is not well understood to which extent and at which rate microorganisms degrade plastics in their respective ecological niches (i.e., soil, water, or gut). Although this is not possible for most synthetic polymers, it might be for PET and other hydrolyzable polymers. To answer some of these questions, studies on a larger global scale would be a major achievement and would potentially deliver new clues about the long-term persistence of ester-based polymers in nature.

Future work will also have to answer the question if primary degradation products are interfering with the human microbiota or different environments, and if and to which extent these and/or the released additives have a toxic impact at various levels. Thereby, research will have to address the question, of whether the additional carbon as part of the polymer backbone will affect the microbiota and interfere with nutrient cycles in ecological niches.

Within this setting, future research will also have to identify a possible impact on the composition of human and animal gut microbiota as presumably being influenced by the occurrence of synthetic polymers and/or degradation products. With this respect, it is not clear if any synthetic polymers are degraded in the human gut or bloodstream and if any of the presumed degradation products are released at significant and potentially harmful concentrations. Since it has previously been shown that some bacteria in the gut harbor the potential for PET degradation, PET might be the model polymer for such initial studies.

Since viruses and phages share the same niches, are of a similar size, and bind to each other, it will be of high interest to elucidate the coevolution and interaction of the viral load and microplastic and/or the occurrence of genes enzymes involved in the microbial degradation.

Besides answering these fundamental research questions, developing further applied bioremediation concepts based on some of the above-mentioned plastic-degrading bacteria will be very promising research fields that will make valuable contributions to micro- and nanoplastic clean-up processes. Thereby, the concept of identifying better and more specific binding proteins toward a specific plastic can help develop more sophisticated biosensors for rapid and reliable plastic detection in environmental samples.

Furthermore, engineering of bacterial strains for polymer degradation and industrial upcycling of pure and mixed polymers are not too far-fetched and realistic goals for some polymers like PET, ester-based PUR, and some short-chained PAs. Finally, employing mixtures and hybrid polymers of native and synthetic commodity polymers will be one avenue to increase biodegradability.

CONCLUSION

Nearly 80% of the world’s plastics are disposed in the environment and will remain there for unknown time periods. This ever-increasing plastic waste represents a significant environmental and societal challenge. It will affect human and animal health and global nutrient cycles. The continuous accumulation of plastics in all environments has triggered the search for microorganisms involved in the biodegradation of mainly fossil-based resources. This has already led to the identification of microorganisms acting on several polymers such as PET, oligomeric PA, and ester-based PUR. For PET, microbial and enzymatic degradation is meanwhile well-studied. However, microbial pathways and enzymes involved in the biodegradation of ether-based PUR, PA, PC, PE, PP, PVC, PS, epoxy resins, and SR are still missing. Meeting this challenge and delivering solutions is an urgent task for future generations of microbiologists.
