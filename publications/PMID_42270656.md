Pathogens compete for glucose with macrophages, which disrupts host glycolysis,
modulates antimicrobial responses and causes macrophage death. We show that
glucose starvation induced by major fungal pathogens Candida albicans and
Candida auris causes macrophage lysis by activating NINJ1, the executioner of
membrane rupture during cell death. In glucose-starved macrophages, NINJ1
ruptures membranes independently of known cell death programs. Consistently,
NINJ1 is the dominant effector of fungal-induced macrophage damage amongst host
cell death factors. Supplementation of the amino acid alanine rescues
glucose-starved macrophages better than glucose, and it does so by inhibiting
NINJ1 oligomerization. Moreover, C. albicans infection disrupts amino acid
metabolism in mice and reduces serum alanine. Finally, NINJ1-mediated membrane
rupture enables C. albicans egress from macrophages together with the toxin
candidalysin. We establish the mechanism of glucose starvation-induced
macrophage damage by NINJ1, and demonstrate the roles of NINJ1 and alanine in
immune responses to Candida and fungal escape.

Supplementing glucose rescues C. albicans-infected macrophages by promoting their viability and cellular integrity, but it also potently promotes fungal growth and thus provides an advantage to C. albicans1. We therefore considered whether metabolite rescue of macrophages without advantaging C. albicans is feasible. Amino acid metabolism may be a possible avenue to achieve this goal, since amino acids can support macrophage immunometabolism35–37. Furthermore, while C. albicans grows on amino acids, they are not its preferred carbon source.The effects of fungal infection on global host amino acid homeostasis are poorly understood. We found that mice systemically infected with C. albicans display perturbed amino acid homeostasis. In contrast, glutamine, glutamate and others remained unchanged, while the levels of valine increased upon infection (Figs. 1A, and  S1). It is likely that alterations to systemic, immune cell and pathogen metabolism are collectively contributing to broadly changed amino acid homeostasis in infected animals. Proline and isoleucine also had no effect (Fig. S2). Alanine is an important metabolic substrate in mammals, both as a gluconeogenic substrate and feeding into the Krebs cycle for ATP production. To do so, we utilized our live-cell imaging platform with bone marrow-derived mouse macrophages (BMDMs)1. Here, macrophage plasma membrane permeabilization is quantified over time using the membrane-impermeable DNA-binding dye DRAQ7. In this assay, C. albicans depletes glucose, resulting in glucose starvation of macrophages and cell death several hours after infection1 and (Fig. 1B). Supplementation of alanine rescued macrophages from glucose starvation-induced plasma membrane permeabilization, and the rescue was more potent than supplementation of glucose at the same concentrations (Fig. 1B). C. albicans growth was the same in the presence or absence of alanine (Fig. S3A), showing that alanine rescued macrophages without providing an advantage to C. albicans. Moreover, hyphal growth, which contributes to macrophage damage, was also unaffected by alanine, either during macrophage interactions (Fig. S3B) or in vitro in macrophage infection medium (Fig. S3C).

In addition to reducing macrophage membrane permeabilization (measured by DRAQ7-staining, Fig. 1B), alanine also reduced C. albicans-induced PMR and macrophage lysis, as shown by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 1C) and reduced lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release (Fig. 1D). In contrast, the reduction of ATP levels due to cell death was not inhibited by alanine (Fig. 1E). Thus, alanine preserves membrane integrity but does not reduce macrophage cell death caused by C. albicans.

Deletion of Ninj1 rescued C. albicans-infected macrophages from damage caused by glucose starvation, inhibiting both plasma membrane permeabilization (DRAQ7-staining, Fig. 4C and Movie S1) and PMR (LDH release, Fig. 4D). Ninj1 loss conferred the strongest rescue of C. albicans-infected macrophages when compared with other host cell death factors that we have tested (compare Fig. 4C with data in Fig. 3 and our previous work1,28,40,45). The C. albicans tye7Δ/Δ gal4Δ/Δ mutant is slower in consuming glucose and therefore slower in causing glucose starvation-dependent macrophage lysis1. In wild-type macrophages, the tye7Δ/Δ gal4Δ/Δ was ~4 h slower in triggering macrophage membrane damage, while in Ninj1–/– macrophages the delay was reduced to ~1 h (Fig. S5B). This is consistent with NINJ1 being the main driver of glucose starvation-dependent macrophage lysis. The intraperitoneal mouse model of C. albicans infection was used to test the role of NINJ1 in vivo. Relative to wild-type animals, deletion of Ninj1 caused a trend towards higher spleen weight and fungal load 24 h following infection, albeit with a comparable weight loss (Fig. S6A–D). However, these differences did not achieve statistical significance. There was no difference between wild-type and Ninj1–/– animals in kidney weight or fungal load (Fig. S6C–E). To explore potential organ-specific effects, we compared fungal burdens between the spleen and kidney within each genotype. This showed that Ninj1–/–, but not wild type animals, showed statistically significantly higher fungal load in spleen compared to kidneys (Fig. S6F). In another C. albicans infection model (intravenous injection), fungal growth is better controlled in the spleen than in the kidneys due to larger numbers of innate immune phagocytes and inhibition of invasive fungal filamentation46. Our data suggest that NINJ1-mediated PMR may contribute to controlling C. albicans proliferation in the spleen in the intraperitoneal infection model. Confirming its broad role in response to fungi, deletion of Ninj1 also reduced membrane damage in macrophages infected with C. auris (Fig. 4E).

Alanine did not further rescue Ninj1–/– macrophages (neither membrane permeabilization nor PMR) (Fig. 4C, D), showing that alanine needs NINJ1 to exert its protective effects. NINJ1 oligomerizes in the membrane as a prerequisite for PMR33,47. Consistent with a previous report48, alanine reduced NINJ1 oligomerisation as seen in cross-linking and BN-PAGE experiments (Figs. 4F and S7A). Collectively, these results show that alanine rescues glucose-starved macrophages by inhibiting NINJ1. Glycine, an amino acid that is structurally similar to alanine, is reported to inhibit PMR by preventing NINJ1 oligomerization48. We could recapitulate this effect in our experiments (Figs. 4F and S7A). Therefore, to further test the involvement of NINJ1 in glucose starvation and fungal infections, we asked if glycine acts similarly to alanine in rescuing glucose-starved macrophages via NINJ1. Indeed, glycine rescued C. albicans-infected macrophages in a NINJ1-dependent manner by reducing both membrane permeabilization (DRAQ7 staining, Fig. 4G) and PMR (LDH release, Fig. 4D). Since NINJ1 ruptures macrophages during pyroptosis33, this result explains how glycine inhibits C. albicans-induced pyroptosis41. However, NINJ1 has an additional role in response to glucose starvation, since glycine rescued Gsdmd–/– macrophages, both mouse (Fig. S8A) and human (Fig. S8B). This is consistent with no role for GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis in response to NINJ1-dependent membrane damage caused by glucose stress.

IL-1ß secretion was reduced in C. albicans-infected Ninj1–/– immortalised macrophages (iBMDMs), with no further reduction upon supplementation with alanine or glycine (Fig. 4H). However, no reduction in IL-1ß secretion was seen in C. albicans-infected primary Ninj1–/– BMDMs (Fig. 4I), although alanine caused lower IL-1ß secretion in primary BMDMs (Fig. 1F). Proteolysis of IL-1ß was normal in Ninj1–/– macrophages, suggesting that inflammasome signaling is normal (Fig. 4J). Similarly, caspase-1 and GSDMD were proteolytically processed in C. albicans-infected Ninj1–/– macrophages, although less cleaved GSDMD was found in supernatants consistent with reduced PMR (Fig. 4J).

To further test the contributions of NINJ1 and candidalysin in Candida infections, we used C. albicans clinical isolates that display substantial diversity in candidalysin expression levels. The reference strain SC5314 (used in most of our experiments) readily forms hyphae under in vitro conditions and secretes high levels of candidalysin49. We selected four additional bloodstream infection isolates with reduced candidalysin expression52–54. These isolates also display lower hyphal growth within macrophages28. Deletion of Ninj1 strongly rescued macrophages in infections with all low-candidalysin isolates, and the rescue was stronger than with the candidalysin-high strain SC5314 (Fig. 5D). For instance, at 20 h post-infection, plasma membrane permeabilization was seen for 30% of Ninj1–/– macrophages for SC5314, 15% for P57055, 17% for P57072, 8% for P76067 and 6% for P78042. Collectively, these results show that NINJ1 has a conserved role in macrophage plasma membrane damage across diverse C. albicans strains and an increasingly important role with strains that express lower candidalysin levels.

DiscussionIn this study, we establish the mechanism by which glucose-starved macrophages lyse in response to fungal pathogens. We show that the mechanism is mediated by NINJ1, which causes macrophage plasma membrane permeabilization and rupture. This mechanism is conserved in both mouse and human macrophages, and establishes glucose starvation as a trigger for NINJ1 activation in infected macrophages. Our findings further show that supplementation of alanine, an important mammalian metabolite, inhibits NINJ1-dependent damage without providing a growth advantage to C. albicans. Moreover, NINJ1 is the dominant host effector of macrophage damage in response to C. albicans, since inactivation of NINJ1 has the biggest effect in reducing macrophage damage compared to other cell death programs studied to date. For C. auris, NINJ1 is the only host factor discovered so far driving macrophage damage. We also demonstrate that C. albicans uses NINJ1-dependent plasma membrane damage to egress from macrophages. This shows that, in addition to the reported role of NINJ1-dependent egress of norovirus particles from immune phagocytes56, much larger fungal hyphae (measuring tens of μm in length) escape from immune containment due to NINJ1-dependent membrane damage. This suggests substantial rupture and disintegration of the membrane structure by NINJ1 to allow for the release of a very large pathogen. This may contribute to the dissemination of infection, and it may also orchestrate inflammatory responses that control pathogens or even drive pathological inflammation during infection. Current infection studies in Ninj1 mutant mice support several of these possibilities. For example, Ninj1 mutant mice showed increased mortality upon bacterial infection with Citrobacter rodentium or Yersinia pseudotuberculosis33,57, and we show higher fungal burden in the spleen relative to kidneys in Ninj1 mutant mice (but not in wild type controls) following intraperitoneal infection with C. albicans (Fig. S6). However, Ninj1 mutant mice show reduced mortality in sepsis caused by bacterial flagellin, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) plus heat shock, and influenza A virus lung infection58–60. Collectively, these studies suggest that the outcome of NINJ1-inhibition in infected hosts may depend on the pathogen and stage of disease, and may be useful when uncontrolled inflammation becomes a driver of poor outcomes.As we show here and previously, glucose starvation causes changes in mitochondrial membrane polarization, whereby hyperpolarization is followed by depolarization coinciding with plasma membrane permeabilization and damage (Fig. 3) and1. Like our results with C. albicans, the ATP synthase inhibitor oligomycin causes mitochondrial hyperpolarization and triggers NINJ1-dependent macrophage membrane damage33. Therefore, changes to mitochondrial function are a possible trigger for NINJ1 activation in response to glucose starvation and metabolic dysfunction caused by C. albicans. Furthermore, we show that the fungal pore-forming toxin candidalysin plays a role in activating NINJ1. Our results are supported by data showing that bacterial pore-forming toxins streptolysin and listeriolysin activate NINJ133. The precise activating mechanisms for NINJ1 oligomerization and membrane rupture are still to be resolved and may differ depending on the stimulus. However, the current consensus is that changes to membrane structure, such as movement of phospholipids (scrambling), swelling or mechanical stress, are involved47,61–63. Candidalysin damages mitochondrial membranes and the plasma membrane of host cells49,50,64, providing the likely mechanism by which it activates NINJ1. Interestingly, a recent study showed that, in addition to membrane damage caused by pore-forming toxins, mechanical damage is needed for full rupture mediated by NINJ163. In line with our results with C. albicans, NINJ1-dependent membrane rupture triggered by mechanical challenge does not require the characterised cell death programs63. In the case of C. albicans, the mechanical damage may be provided by the growing fungal hyphae exerting force on the membrane55. Based on these considerations, we propose that during infection, a combination of metabolic dysfunction, membrane-damaging toxins secreted by pathogens and mechanical stress on immune cell membranes from microbial growth are key stimuli for triggering NINJ1-mediated damage and PMR.Our findings further show that supplementation of alanine rescues glucose-starved macrophages from C. albicans-induced membrane damage in a NINJ1-dependent manner. Alanine is an important metabolite under metabolic stress38, and our data in infected mice is consistent with disruption of amino acid homeostasis in C. albicans-infected animals. Although host amino acid homeostasis was broadly perturbed by C. albicans, of all of the amino acids that we tested in this study and previously1 only alanine and glycine rescue the integrity of glucose-starved macrophages, and we show that alanine metabolism is not required. This indicates that amino acid metabolism cannot compensate for disrupted glucose metabolism in Candida-infected macrophages. Consistent with this, we show that alanine rescues macrophages without correcting their metabolic dysfunction or inhibiting cell death, in line with studies showing that NINJ1-induced membrane damage occurs independently of metabolic dysfunction during cell death33,47,65. Alanine has been found to inhibit macrophage lysis and NINJ1 oligomerisation downstream of pyroptosis48,66. However, we show here that supplementation of alanine causes a reduction in both membrane permeabilization and PMR in C. albicans-infected macrophages in a manner that is independent of pyroptosis, apoptosis or necroptosis. Accordingly, the proteolytic processing of caspase-1, GSDMD and IL-1ß in response to C. albicans is not prevented by alanine, although the amount of IL-1ß released by infected macrophages is reduced. In addition to alanine, glycine, and the GABA receptor agonist muscimol also display NINJ1-inhibitory activity48,65. The biochemical mechanism by which these metabolites inhibit NINJ1 oligomer formation remains to be understood48,65. Alanine and glycine are structurally similar, and muscimol also shares features of its chemical structure with glycine65. Therefore, the mechanism of NINJ1 inhibition by these molecules may be conserved.The requirement for NINJ1 for both membrane permeabilization and PMR of C. albicans-infected macrophages is similar to ferroptosis, infection with Yersinia or Salmonella, treatment with the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the presence of heat shock and mechanical membrane challenge as a trigger for NINJ157,59,63,67. Downstream of pyroptosis, NINJ1 is required for terminal PMR but has no role in the earlier GSDMD-mediated membrane permeabilization event or secretion of IL-1ß33. The explanation is that, during pyroptosis, membrane permeabilization and IL-1ß secretion depend on GSDMD pores, which form independently of NINJ133. Given that, as we and others have shown, C. albicans triggers NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis40,41, the question is why NINJ1 mediates membrane permeabilization in our experiments. The answer is that C. albicans triggers pyroptosis early after macrophage infection40,41, while glucose starvation occurs later and triggers NINJ1-dependent damage independently of GSDMD or pyroptosis, as we show here. Consistently, we show that alanine only needs NINJ1 but not any of the pyroptotic factors to rescue macrophages, and the same is true for glycine. Therefore, downstream of glucose starvation, GSDMD has no role in macrophage membrane damage, explaining why NINJ1 is involved in both membrane permeabilization and PMR under these conditions.Inactivation of NINJ1 does not impair the release of IL-1ß in response to the mentioned bacterial pathogens and LPS plus heat shock57,59, although Ninj1 mutant mice had lower levels of plasma IL-1ß in response to infection with Escherichia coli58. We show that, in response to C. albicans, inhibition of NINJ1 by alanine resulted in reduced macrophage IL-1ß secretion, and genetic inactivation of Ninj1 reduced IL-1ß secretion from immortalised mouse macrophages (iBMDMs) but not by primary mouse macrophages (BMDMs). In this study, we establish the mechanism by which glucose-starved macrophages lyse in response to fungal pathogens. We show that the mechanism is mediated by NINJ1, which causes macrophage plasma membrane permeabilization and rupture. This mechanism is conserved in both mouse and human macrophages, and establishes glucose starvation as a trigger for NINJ1 activation in infected macrophages. Our findings further show that supplementation of alanine, an important mammalian metabolite, inhibits NINJ1-dependent damage without providing a growth advantage to C. albicans. Moreover, NINJ1 is the dominant host effector of macrophage damage in response to C. albicans, since inactivation of NINJ1 has the biggest effect in reducing macrophage damage compared to other cell death programs studied to date. For C. auris, NINJ1 is the only host factor discovered so far driving macrophage damage. We also demonstrate that C. albicans uses NINJ1-dependent plasma membrane damage to egress from macrophages. This shows that, in addition to the reported role of NINJ1-dependent egress of norovirus particles from immune phagocytes56, much larger fungal hyphae (measuring tens of μm in length) escape from immune containment due to NINJ1-dependent membrane damage. This suggests substantial rupture and disintegration of the membrane structure by NINJ1 to allow for the release of a very large pathogen. This may contribute to the dissemination of infection, and it may also orchestrate inflammatory responses that control pathogens or even drive pathological inflammation during infection. Current infection studies in Ninj1 mutant mice support several of these possibilities. For example, Ninj1 mutant mice showed increased mortality upon bacterial infection with Citrobacter rodentium or Yersinia pseudotuberculosis33,57, and we show higher fungal burden in the spleen relative to kidneys in Ninj1 mutant mice (but not in wild type controls) following intraperitoneal infection with C. albicans (Fig. S6). However, Ninj1 mutant mice show reduced mortality in sepsis caused by bacterial flagellin, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) plus heat shock, and influenza A virus lung infection58–60. Collectively, these studies suggest that the outcome of NINJ1-inhibition in infected hosts may depend on the pathogen and stage of disease, and may be useful when uncontrolled inflammation becomes a driver of poor outcomes.

As we show here and previously, glucose starvation causes changes in mitochondrial membrane polarization, whereby hyperpolarization is followed by depolarization coinciding with plasma membrane permeabilization and damage (Fig. 3) and1. Like our results with C. albicans, the ATP synthase inhibitor oligomycin causes mitochondrial hyperpolarization and triggers NINJ1-dependent macrophage membrane damage33. Therefore, changes to mitochondrial function are a possible trigger for NINJ1 activation in response to glucose starvation and metabolic dysfunction caused by C. albicans. Furthermore, we show that the fungal pore-forming toxin candidalysin plays a role in activating NINJ1. Our results are supported by data showing that bacterial pore-forming toxins streptolysin and listeriolysin activate NINJ133. The precise activating mechanisms for NINJ1 oligomerization and membrane rupture are still to be resolved and may differ depending on the stimulus. However, the current consensus is that changes to membrane structure, such as movement of phospholipids (scrambling), swelling or mechanical stress, are involved47,61–63. Candidalysin damages mitochondrial membranes and the plasma membrane of host cells49,50,64, providing the likely mechanism by which it activates NINJ1. Interestingly, a recent study showed that, in addition to membrane damage caused by pore-forming toxins, mechanical damage is needed for full rupture mediated by NINJ163. In line with our results with C. albicans, NINJ1-dependent membrane rupture triggered by mechanical challenge does not require the characterised cell death programs63. In the case of C. albicans, the mechanical damage may be provided by the growing fungal hyphae exerting force on the membrane55. Based on these considerations, we propose that during infection, a combination of metabolic dysfunction, membrane-damaging toxins secreted by pathogens and mechanical stress on immune cell membranes from microbial growth are key stimuli for triggering NINJ1-mediated damage and PMR.

Our findings further show that supplementation of alanine rescues glucose-starved macrophages from C. albicans-induced membrane damage in a NINJ1-dependent manner. Alanine is an important metabolite under metabolic stress38, and our data in infected mice is consistent with disruption of amino acid homeostasis in C. albicans-infected animals. Although host amino acid homeostasis was broadly perturbed by C. albicans, of all of the amino acids that we tested in this study and previously1 only alanine and glycine rescue the integrity of glucose-starved macrophages, and we show that alanine metabolism is not required. This indicates that amino acid metabolism cannot compensate for disrupted glucose metabolism in Candida-infected macrophages. Consistent with this, we show that alanine rescues macrophages without correcting their metabolic dysfunction or inhibiting cell death, in line with studies showing that NINJ1-induced membrane damage occurs independently of metabolic dysfunction during cell death33,47,65. Alanine has been found to inhibit macrophage lysis and NINJ1 oligomerisation downstream of pyroptosis48,66. However, we show here that supplementation of alanine causes a reduction in both membrane permeabilization and PMR in C. albicans-infected macrophages in a manner that is independent of pyroptosis, apoptosis or necroptosis. Accordingly, the proteolytic processing of caspase-1, GSDMD and IL-1ß in response to C. albicans is not prevented by alanine, although the amount of IL-1ß released by infected macrophages is reduced. In addition to alanine, glycine, and the GABA receptor agonist muscimol also display NINJ1-inhibitory activity48,65. The biochemical mechanism by which these metabolites inhibit NINJ1 oligomer formation remains to be understood48,65. Alanine and glycine are structurally similar, and muscimol also shares features of its chemical structure with glycine65. Therefore, the mechanism of NINJ1 inhibition by these molecules may be conserved.

The requirement for NINJ1 for both membrane permeabilization and PMR of C. albicans-infected macrophages is similar to ferroptosis, infection with Yersinia or Salmonella, treatment with the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the presence of heat shock and mechanical membrane challenge as a trigger for NINJ157,59,63,67. Downstream of pyroptosis, NINJ1 is required for terminal PMR but has no role in the earlier GSDMD-mediated membrane permeabilization event or secretion of IL-1ß33. The explanation is that, during pyroptosis, membrane permeabilization and IL-1ß secretion depend on GSDMD pores, which form independently of NINJ133. Given that, as we and others have shown, C. albicans triggers NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis40,41, the question is why NINJ1 mediates membrane permeabilization in our experiments. The answer is that C. albicans triggers pyroptosis early after macrophage infection40,41, while glucose starvation occurs later and triggers NINJ1-dependent damage independently of GSDMD or pyroptosis, as we show here. Consistently, we show that alanine only needs NINJ1 but not any of the pyroptotic factors to rescue macrophages, and the same is true for glycine. Therefore, downstream of glucose starvation, GSDMD has no role in macrophage membrane damage, explaining why NINJ1 is involved in both membrane permeabilization and PMR under these conditions.

Inactivation of NINJ1 does not impair the release of IL-1ß in response to the mentioned bacterial pathogens and LPS plus heat shock57,59, although Ninj1 mutant mice had lower levels of plasma IL-1ß in response to infection with Escherichia coli58. We show that, in response to C. albicans, inhibition of NINJ1 by alanine resulted in reduced macrophage IL-1ß secretion, and genetic inactivation of Ninj1 reduced IL-1ß secretion from immortalised mouse macrophages (iBMDMs) but not by primary mouse macrophages (BMDMs). In all cases, however, the release of large pro-inflammatory danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) by C. albicans-infected macrophages, specifically LDH, was reduced by both genetic and alanine-based inhibition of NINJ1. This is consistent with NINJ1-dependent PMR releasing large pro-inflammatory DAMPs in response to many upstream signals33. Our data with high – and low-candidalysin isolates and candidalysin mutant strains show that NINJ1 works together with candidalysin to damage macrophage membranes and promote C. albicans escape.

Live-cell imaging

Macrophages were harvested, seeded and stained following protocols detailed in our previous studies1,24. Following staining, macrophages were infected with Candida at a yeast-to-macrophage ratio of either 1:1, 3:1 or 6:1. Where specified, BMDM culture medium was supplemented with metabolites including glucose, alanine and glycine at concentrations detailed in the figure legends. These metabolites were added after the 1 h phagocytosis and wash steps. For experiments assessing the role of caspase inhibition, macrophages were pre-incubated with 10 µM Quinoline-Val-Asp-Difluorophenoxymethylketone (QVD) for 1 h. Following this, cells were washed and maintained in fresh BMDM medium prior to infection with C. albicans. Control (uninfected) macrophages were subjected to the same treatment conditions. After infection, culture media in both experimental and control wells were replaced with fresh medium either with or without 10 µM QVD, which was maintained for the duration of the assay.
