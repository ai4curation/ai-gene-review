Some prokaryotes compartmentalize select metabolic capabilities. Salmonella
enterica subspecies enterica serovar Typhimurium LT2 (hereafter S. Typhimurium)
catabolizes ethanolamine (EA) within a proteinaceous compartment that we refer
to as the ethanolamine utilization (Eut) metabolosome. EA catabolism is
initiated by the adenosylcobalamin (AdoCbl)-dependent ethanolamine ammonia-lyase
(EAL), which deaminates EA via an adenosyl radical mechanism to yield
acetaldehyde plus ammonia. This adenosyl radical can be quenched, requiring the
replacement of AdoCbl by the ATP-dependent EutA reactivase. During growth on
ethanolamine, S. Typhimurium synthesizes AdoCbl from cobalamin (Cbl) using the
ATP:Co(I)rrinoid adenosyltransferase (ACAT) EutT. It is known that EAL localizes
to the metabolosome, however, prior to this work, it was unclear where EutA and
EutT localized, and whether they interacted with EAL. Here, we provide evidence
that EAL, EutA, and EutT localize to the Eut metabolosome, and that EutA
interacts directly with EAL. We did not observe interactions between EutT and
EAL nor between EutT and the EutA/EAL complex. However, growth phenotypes of a
ΔeutT mutant strain show that EutT is critical for efficient ethanolamine
catabolism. This work provides a preliminary understanding of the dynamics of
AdoCbl synthesis and its uses within the Eut metabolosome.

Introduction

Ethanolamine (EA) is a component of the phospholipid phosphatidylethanolamine, and is present in the intestinal environment as a result of phospholipid degradation and epithelial cell turnover (Kaval & Garsin, 2018). Ethanolamine can be utilized as a source of carbon, nitrogen, and energy by several enteric microorganisms, including commensals and pathogens such as Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica sv. Typhimurium str. LT2 (hereafter S. Typhimurium), Escherichia coli, Listeria monocytogenes, Enterococcus faecalis, and Clostridium difficile (Brinsmade et al., 2005, Buchrieser et al., 2003, Kaval et al., 2019, Nawrocki et al., 2018).

Under anoxic conditions, S. Typhimurium respires EA to tetrathionate, a metabolic capability that confers a fitness advantage to this bacterium in the murine gut (Price-Carter et al., 2001, Baumler et al., 2011). Additionally, it has been shown that S. Typhimurium utilizes ethanolamine as a signal to activate transcription of genes required for macrophage survival (Anderson et al., 2015).

The functions required for ethanolamine catabolism in S. Typhimurium are encoded in a single ethanolamine utilization (eut) operon (Fig. 1A) (Roof & Roth, 1989). The first step of ethanolamine catabolism begins with the conversion of ethanolamine to the reactive intermediate acetaldehyde plus ammonia by the adenosylcobalamin (AdoCbl)-dependent ethanolamine ammonia-lyase (EAL). In S. Typhimurium, EAL is comprised of two subunits, the large subunit (EutB) and the small subunit (EutC) (EC:4.3.1.7, EAL) (Fig. 1A) (Roof & Roth, 1989, Chang & Chang, 1975, Faust et al., 1990). EAL activity is dependent on the formation of an adenosyl radical produced by the homolytic cleavage of the Co – C bond connecting the adenosyl group to the corrin ring (Krouwer et al., 1980, Bandarian et al., 1999). EAL can be inactivated by irreversibly binding Co(II)cobalamin (Co(II)Cbl), which can occur in the event the radical is quenched (Fig. 1B) (Kaplan & Stadtman, 1968). The ATP-dependent EAL reactivase, EutA, replaces inactive Co(II)Cbl by facilitating the removal of Co(II)Cbl from the active site of the enzyme and its replacement with AdoCbl (Mori et al., 2004). The ATP:Co(I)rrinoid adenosyltransferase (ACAT), EutT (EC:2.5.1.17), is responsible for generating AdoCbl from Co(II)balamin (Cbl) (Fig. 1B) (Buan & Escalante-Semerena, 2006, Buan et al., 2004).

The reactive acetaldehyde intermediate produced by EAL can be converted either to acetyl-CoA by the CoA-dependent acetaldehyde dehydrogenase (EutE) or to ethanol by the alcohol dehydrogenase (EutG) (Fig. 1) (Kaval & Garsin, 2018). Cells have developed several mitigation strategies for circumventing metabolic stress caused by reactive metabolic intermediates like acetaldehyde (Choudhary et al., 2012). In many prokaryotes, including S. Typhimurium, the reactive acetaldehyde intermediate produced in EA catabolism is sequestered within a proteinaceous compartment called the ethanolamine utilization (Eut) metabolosome (Fig. 1B) (Brinsmade et al., 2005).

The metabolosome is made up of five different shell proteins that assemble into pentamers or hexamers containing a central pore, and then self-assemble into the metabolosome structure (Yeates et al., 2013, Sutter et al., 2017). The pores function as gates, permitting the ingress of ethanolamine and egress of acetate and ethanol, while preventing the egress of acetaldehyde (Takenoya et al., 2010).

In addition to sequestration of acetaldehyde, the Eut metabolosome maintains its own private cofactor pools (Huseby & Roth, 2013). EutE reduces NAD+ and consumes CoA to produce acetyl-CoA from acetaldehyde. The NADH generated is oxidized back to NAD+ by EutG, and CoA is regenerated by EutD in the production of acetyl-P (Fig. 1B). Deletion of either eutG or eutD produces a growth defect that is suppressed by mutations in the metabolosome shell proteins, supporting the idea that the native metabolosome is impermeable to NAD+ and CoA and must recycle these cofactors in situ (Cheng et al., 2012, Liu et al., 2015, Huseby & Roth, 2013). There is some evidence of enzyme localization to the Eut metabolosome for these purposes. Studies have demonstrated that the N-terminal end of EutC serves as a localization tag to the Eut metabolosome (Choudhary et al., 2012). A localization tag has also been identified bioinformatically on the N-terminal end of EutE, the enzyme responsible for catabolizing acetaldehyde to acetyl-CoA (Jakobson et al., 2015).

In this work, we performed localization studies of EAL, EutA, and EutT as a first step to investigate whether AdoCbl could also be recycled within the metabolosome. We confirmed that EAL localizes to the metabolosome and established that EutA and EutT also localize to the eut metabolosome. Additionally, we report in vivo and in vitro evidence that EutA and EAL interact and stabilize each other in solution. Direct interactions were not found between EutT and EutA, or EutT and EutA in complex with EAL. However, EutT expression was critical for efficient ethanolamine catabolism, despite the presence of two additional cytosolic ACATs, confirming previous observations (Buan et al., 2004).

in trans (Fig. 3). At 200 nM CNCbl supplementation, all of the strains displayed wild-type growth behavior (Fig. 3A). At 10 nM CNCbl supplementation, the ΔeutT strain showed a substantial growth defect, as compared to eutT+ strain (Fig. 3B, green vs yellow circles). The growth defect of the ΔeutT strain was corrected when S. Typhimurium eutT+ was expressed in trans (Fig. 3B, purple squares vs yellow circles), confirming that the growth defect was due to the absence of EutT and not to polar effects in the eut operon. The growth rates for each strain were calculated in the exponential phase (Fig. 3C). Typhimurium ΔeutA, ΔeutBC, and ΔeutABC strains, and asked whether the resulting strains could use ethanolamine as a carbon and energy source. The results of these experiments are shown in figure 7. Typhimurium eutA+ allele expressed in trans restored growth of a ΔeutA strain (Fig. 7A, orange squares). In contrast, expression of the L. monocytogenes eutA+ allele did not restore growth (Fig. 7A, yellow squares), unless L. monocytogenes eutBC+ genes were also expressed in trans (Fig. 7A, teal triangles). Typhimurium ΔeutA strain (Fig. 7A, taupe diamonds), ruling out the possibility that LmEAL could remain active in the absence of LmEutA. Similarly, expression of L. monocytogenes eutBC+ in an S. Typhimurium ΔeutBC mutant strain conferred modest growth (Fig. 7B, taupe diamonds), but growth was significantly improved by co-expression of L. monocytogenes eutBC+ and eutA+ (Fig. 7B, teal triangles). Notably, expression of L. monocytogenes eutBC+ did not complement an S. Typhimurium ΔeutABC mutant strain, indicating that the SeEutA enzyme was still required for the observed modest complementation afforded by the expression of L. monocytogenes eutBC+ genes (Fig. 7B, 7C, compare taupe diamonds).It was also notable that the SeEAL enzyme could support growth on ethanolamine of S. Typhimurium ΔeutA or ΔeutABC strains (Fig. 7A, 7C, purple diamonds). We interpreted these results to mean that SeEAL underwent several turnovers before it became inactive, but whatever the number of turnovers was, it allowed the cell to grow, albeit modestly. Overall, these data suggested that SeEutA could reactivate LmEAL to a degree that allowed modest growth, while LmEutA could not sufficiently reactivate SeEAL to allow any growth with ethanolamine. Importantly, since the inclusion of LmEutA and LmEAL together improved growth of the strains tested compared to strains with these enzymes alone, these data lent support to the idea that direct, compatible interactions between EutA-EAL pairs was critical for reactivation of EAL during ethanolamine catabolism.

When SeEAL was analyzed on the size exclusion column a high molecular mass oligomer (~1,300 kDa) was also observed, which was consistent with the aggregation behavior mentioned above. However, the largest population of protein eluted at a calculated mass of 487 kDa, which agreed with the reported size of the SeEAL oligomer consisting of a trimer of dimers (Faust & Babior, 1992). When SeEutA and SeEAL (0.5 mg each) were combined and loaded on the size exclusion column, both of the SeEAL and SeEutA higher molecular mass oligomers observed in isolation were significantly reduced (Fig. 9B). The most prominent signal was observed at a molecular mass of 475 kDa, a shift from the 487 kDa oligomer observed with SeEAL alone. This shift was significant as determined by the 95% confidence intervals for each peak (474.6 – 476.1 kDa and 486.1 – 488.5 kDa, respectively). There were two additional oligomeric states at 241 and 63 kDa which formed in the combined SeEutA and SeEAL preparation. Without more information about the oligomeric ratio of SeEutA to SeEAL, it was difficult to make predictions regarding the composition of the oligomers with masses of 475, 241, and 63 kDa in the SeEutA and SeEAL mixed samples. The point, however, was clear, SeEAL and SeEutA interacted and stabilized each other by forming a complex. To identify which regions of EutA and EAL were interacting with each other, co-purified SeEutA and SeEAL were dialyzed in PBS, diluted to 10 μM, and incubated with 0.5 mM of the crosslinker disuccinidyl glutamate (DSG) or vehicle. These samples were dialyzed again, and the crosslinked sample and control were applied to the same column used in figure 9 (Fig. 10). Compared to the no crosslink control, the crosslinked EutA and EAL sample observed an increase in height of the higher molecular mass peak also observed in the size exclusion chromatography shown in figure 9.

There was also a shift in the size of the major peak that corresponded to a calculated ~40 kDa shift in size. The peak observed at ~241 kDa in figure 9 and ~293 kDa in figure 10 was absent in the crosslinked sample, suggesting that crosslinking stabilized a transient or weak protein-protein interaction. Another notable shift was an increase of ~26 kDa of the smaller peak observed in the crosslinked sample compared to the no crosslink control. The elution was collected into fractions, and these fractions were separated on two SDS-PAGE gels. One gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R, and the other was transferred to a PVDF membrane and presence of SeEutA and SeEAL were visualized by western blot. SeEutA and SeEAL were detected most strongly in fractions 8, 9 and 10. SeEutA signal was strongest in fraction 8, while SeEAL signal was strongest in fraction 9. The smallest peak, located in fraction 12, contained SeEutA and SeEutB subunit of SeEAL, but a band corresponding to the size of SeEutC was not detected.

Concentrated protein samples collected from fractions 8, 9 and 10 were submitted to mass spectrometry analysis and identification of crosslinked peptides. The full list of the statistically significant crosslinked peptide fragments (FDR < 0.01) can be found in tables S1-S4 and select crosslinked peptides of interest are depicted in figure 11. These analyses mostly identified interactions between K3 of EutC and the same lysyl residue on another subunit of EutC, or with other surface lysines on EutA, EutB, and EutC. This region of EutC corresponds to an N-terminal localization tag, for which the AlphaFold software package had low sequence coverage and poor folding precision (Jumper et al., 2021). It is possible that this region is highly flexible, a feature that may have resulted in the majority of cross-linked peptides. When looking at other cross-linked peptides, some interactions appeared across all three fractions, while others were found only in one or two fractions.

Looking at crosslinks between EutA and EAL, two crosslinked peptide fragment pairs were identified. In fractions 8 and 9, the EutA 39YEFIKR44 peptide was identified crosslinked to EutB peptide 16DVKEVLAK23. In fraction 10, EutA peptide 60QGGLKEVELK69 was identified crosslinked to EutC peptide 128SEISK133. Other crosslinked peptides were identified for EutA, EutB, and EutC that possibly corresponded to interprotein crosslinks between EutA-EutA and EAL-EAL pairs. In summary, these data provide some evidence that EutA and EAL do interact in vitro.

Experimental procedures

Strains and plasmids.Strains used in this study were all derivatives of Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica sv. Typhimurium str. LT2 or E. coli BL21 (Table 1). Mutant strains were constructed using the λRed recombinase system as described previously (Datsenko & Wanner, 2000). Typhimurium eutA and eutBC genes were cloned into pSAPKO-WT vector as described elsewhere (Table 2) (VanDrisse & Escalante-Semerena, 2016, Galloway et al., 2013). For in vivo complementation, eutA, eutBC, and eutABC genes were cloned into pCV3 or pTAC-85 complementation vectors (Table 2) as described (VanDrisse & Escalante-Semerena, 2016, Marsh, 1986).Chemicals and growth media. Difco™ nutrient broth (NB) (8 g/L) with NaCl (5 g/L), or lysogeny broth (LB) (20 g/L) were used as undefined, rich culture media. Media were solidified by the addition of Difco™ Agar (15 g/L). All chemicals were purchased from Millipore-Sigma unless otherwise stated. All chemicals were reagent grade and were used without further purification. The ribose minimal medium used in this work was no-carbon essential medium (NCE) (Berkowitz et al., 1968), supplemented with MgSO4 (1 mM), Wolfe’s trace minerals,(Balch & Wolfe, 1976) ribose (20 mM), and methionine (0.5 mM). Ethanolamine (30 mM) and CNCbl (200 nM) were added to the culture medium to trigger the EutR-dependent transcriptional activation of the eut operon. For plasmid maintenance in cultures growing in rich media the following antibiotic concentrations were used: i) chloramphenicol (25 μg/mL) for pCV3 and derived vectors, ii) ampicillin (100 μg/mL) for pTAC-85 and derived vectors, and iii) kanamycin (50 μg/mL) for pSAPKO-WT-derived vectors. For cultures growing in minimal media, all antibiotics were used at one-fifth of the concentration that was used in rich media. To induce transcription of genes of interest cloned into complementation plasmids, L-arabinose (100 μM) was added to the medium to induce the expression of genes cloned into plasmid pCV3, or isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, 100 μM) was added to the medium to express genes cloned into plasmid pTAC-85. Strains were grown in 1 mL NB for 16 h at 37 °C shaking at 150 RPM. These cultures were then sub-cultured at 1% v/v in the ribose minimal media, or 2.5% in the ethanolamine minimal medium.

Protein purification protocols.EutA and EAL proteins were purified individually or in combination using the following method. E. coli BL21-CodonPlus cells were transformed with the plasmid pEUT194 or pEUT195 and plated on LB with kanamycin (50 μg/mL) and chloramphenicol (25 μg/mL) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Five mL of fresh LB was added to the plate, and cells were scraped with a sterilized plate spreader. The cell suspension was transferred to a 2 L flask containing a metal spring and 1L of terrific broth (TB) medium supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/mL) and chloramphenicol (25 μg/mL). Cultures were grown to an OD600 = 0.6 – 0.8 at 25 °C, at which point IPTG was added at a final concentration of 1 mM. Cultures were incubated at 25 °C for an additional 16 h, at which point cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6,000 × g for 15 min in a refrigerated Avanti J-20 XPI equipped with a JLA 8.1 rotor. The cell pellet was resuspended in 50 mL of Buffer A (50 mM Tris•HCl, pH 7.5 at 4 °C) supplemented with protease inhibitor phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride (PMSF,1 mM), lysozyme (1 mg/mL), and DNase (0.1 mg/mL). When EutA and EAL were co-purified, at this point cell suspensions from cells expressing plasmids pEUT194 or pEUT195 were combined. The resulting cell suspension was sonicated on ice with a Fisher Scientific/Brandon Sonifier Model 150 Cell Disruptor using the following settings: 2 s on, 2 s off for 30 s at 60% amplitude. This was repeated one more time after a 5 min rest. Cell suspension was then stirred at 4 °C for 30 min before centrifugation at 40,000 × g for 30 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45-μm polyether sulfone filter (VWR) and loaded onto a HiPrep DEAE FF 16/10 (Cytiva) anion exchange column equilibrated with Buffer A. The column was washed with 20 column volumes (CV) of buffer A, then a gradient was applied of 0–40% of Buffer B [50 mM Tris•HCl, pH 7.5 at 4 °C + NaCl (2 M)]. Fractions containing EutA and/or EAL were combined and concentrated with NH4SO4 (25% w/v) at 4 °C and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min. The protein pellet was incubated in a smaller volume of Buffer A overnight at 4 °C, and pelleted protein was gently brought back into solution the next morning. The re-solubilized protein was dialyzed against 1 L of Buffer A three times, and the solution was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 15 min before storing soluble protein at −80 °C until used.

We collected fractions of peaks estimated at sizes 800 and 555 kDa, and an additional fraction capturing the tail end of the peak at size 555 kDa. Fractions were concentrated by adding freshly suspended 100% (w/v) trichloracetic acid (TCA) to a final concentration of 10% (w/v) TCA and incubated for 60 min on ice. Precipitated protein was collected by centrifugation at 21,000 × g for 10 min and washed with ice cold 100% acetone. Protein pellet was dried, and then resuspended in 100 μL of Tris•HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5 at 25 °C).

Mass spectrometry analysis of crosslinked complexes.Fractions 8, 9 and 10 of DSG-crosslinked protein sample, and corresponding fractions without crosslinker, were submitted to the Mass Spectrometry/Proteomics Facility at the University of Wisconsin-Madison for identification of cross-linked residues. Samples were digested with trypsin and analyzed using Orbitrap LC/MS/MS. Sequence coverage was >90% for all samples. Data was analyzed with MeroX/StavroX crosslinking identification tool (version 2.0.1.4). Crosslinked peptide identifications with scores below a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.01 were excluded from the final analysis.
