Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase α, a heterodimer of catalytic p110α and one of
five regulatory subunits, mediates insulin- and insulin like growth
factor-signaling and, frequently, oncogenesis. Cellular levels of the regulatory
p85α subunit are tightly controlled by regulated proteasomal degradation. In
adipose tissue and growth plates, failure of K48-linked p85α ubiquitination
causes diabetes, lipodystrophy and dwarfism in mice, as in humans with SHORT
syndrome. Here we elucidated the structures of the key ubiquitin ligase
complexes regulating p85α availability. Specificity is provided by the substrate
receptor KBTBD2, which recruits p85α to the cullin3-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase
(CRL3). CRL3KBTBD2 forms multimers, which disassemble into dimers upon substrate
binding (CRL3KBTBD2-p85α) and/or neddylation by the activator NEDD8
(CRL3KBTBD2~N8), leading to p85α ubiquitination and degradation. Deactivation
involves dissociation of NEDD8 mediated by the COP9 signalosome and displacement
of KBTBD2 by the inhibitor CAND1. The hereby identified structural basis of p85α
regulation opens the way to better understanding disturbances of glucose
regulation, growth and cancer.

INTRODUCTION

Phosphatidylinositol-3 kinases (PI3Ks) are critical regulators of a wide range of cellular activities, including metabolism, proliferation, differentiation, and migration 1. They are also implicated in various human diseases 2. Class IA PI3Ks are heterodimers consisting of a catalytic p110 subunit (p110α, -β, or -δ) and a regulatory subunit (p85α, p55α, p50α, p85β, or p55γ) 3. Among these, the p110α-p85α dimer (PI3Kα) has special importance in cancer and diabetes 4,5, with excessive or diminished catalytic activity associated with cancer or diabetes, respectively. The abundance of p85α is tightly controlled to maintain appropriate levels of P13Kα signaling 6.

KBTBD2 (Kelch repeat and BTB domain-containing protein 2) has been identified as an essential regulator of p85α abundance affecting insulin sensitivity 7,8. KBTBD2 assembles with Cullin3 (Cul3) and Rbx1 into the Cullin3-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase (CRL3KBTBD2). CRL3KBTBD2 recruits and ubiquitinates p85α 7. In mice with a homozygous Kbtbd2 mutation called teeny, KBTBD2 deficiency caused up to 30-fold increase in the adipose tissue levels of p85α, which interrupted the insulin signaling pathway and resulted in a severe metabolic disorder, characterized by insulin-resistant diabetes, lipodystrophy, hepatic steatosis, and growth retardation 7,8. Notably, human PIK3R1 mutations affecting the encoded p85α protein also cause a similar spectrum of phenotypes, named SHORT syndrome 9.

Cullin-RING E3 ubiquitin ligases (CRLs) play crucial roles in protein ubiquitination and proteasome-mediated protein degradation, regulating a broad spectrum of protein substrates in diverse cellular pathways 10. CRLs are composed of a Cullin (Cul) protein that serves as a scaffold to bring together an adaptor, a variable substrate receptor, and a RING domain-containing protein (Rbx1 or Rbx2) necessary to recruit an E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme 11–14. In humans there are eight Cul proteins [Cul1, 2, 3, 4A, 4B, 5, 7 and 9 15,16] for which the corresponding CRL subfamily is named. CRLs are activated by neddylation with NEDD8 17,18, and deactivated by COP9 signalosome (CSN)-mediated deneddylation 19,20. The CRL catalytic cycle is also regulated by CAND1 (Cullin-associated and neddylation-dissociated protein 1) 21,22, which mediates substrate receptor exchange by displacing the adaptors employed by most CRLs to connect Cul proteins with substrate receptors.

Distinctly, in CRL3, Cul3 binds directly to its substrate receptors, the BTB (Broad-complex, Tramtrack, Bric à brac) proteins, without an intervening adaptor 23,24. By virtue of more than 180 substrate receptors, the CRL3 subfamily regulates numerous cellular processes. For example, Cul3-Keap1 controls the degradation of the Nrf2 transcription factor involved in oxidative stress response pathway 25 and Cul3-SPOP plays critical roles in promoting or inhibiting tumorigenesis by regulating different substrates 28. Most BTB proteins are composed of a BTB domain for Cul3 binding and a Kelch domain for substrate recruitment. As BTB domains tend to form dimers, CRL3 has been proposed to form stable dimers 29,30, in contrast with other CRLs that mostly exist as monomers 11,12,14,31,32, with some occasions forming dimers and tetramers 33–35. The dimeric property of BTB proteins makes CRL3 more complicated for structural study. Previous studies have reported the individual BTB-BACK and Kelch structures 36,37. To date, the overall structure of the full-length BTB proteins or CRL3 assembly remains unknown.

In this study, we determined the 3D structures of human CRL3KBTBD2 and its substrate-bound state, CRL3KBTBD2-p85α, to the resolution of 3.96 Å and 4.60 Å respectively. We showed that CRL3KBTBD2 forms autoinhibited multimers ranging from tetramer to octamer, more complicated than other known structures of CRL members. Together with cross-linking mass spectrometry (XL-MS) and biochemical characterization, the structure of CRL3KBTBD2-p85α shed light on the substrate recruitment mechanism of the CRL3 family. Furthermore, the cryo-EM structures of neddylated and deneddylated CRL3KBTBD2, and Cul3-Rbx1-CAND1 revealed the mechanism of CRL3KBTBD2 regulation by NEDD8, CSN, and CAND1. Based on these CRL3KBTBD2 complex structures in seven states, we proposed a model for the activation cycle of the CRL3 family.

RESULTSCRL3KBTBD2 forms unique multimersWe expressed and co-purified full-length KBTBD2, Cul3, and Rbx1 to assemble the CRL3KBTBD2 complex. Size exclusion chromatography and negative staining EM showed that CRL3KBTBD2 forms multimers larger than dimers (Extended Data Fig. 1). The purified complex was used for cryo-EM study and three different maps were generated, including an octamer at 7.41 Å, a hexamer at 7.99 Å and a tetramer at 7.86 Å resolution (Fig. 1b–1d; Supplementary Fig. 1 and 4). To improve the resolution, one dimer (two CRL3KBTBD2) was locally refined to 3.96 Å, allowing the model building of the CRL3KBTBD2 dimer. The multimer models were obtained by fitting the dimer model into the corresponding maps (Table 1).CRL3KBTBD2 dimer formation is mediated by KBTBD2 through its N-terminal BTB subdomain (residues 1–129), following which is a BACK subdomain (residues 130–252) and a C-terminal Kelch domain (253–623) (Fig. 1a, 2c–2d; Extended Data Fig. 3a). The BTB subdomain contains 6 short helices (α1-α6) and 3 β-sheets (β1-β3) (Fig. 2d), structurally similar to other adaptor proteins, such as SPOP (adaptor of Cul3), Skp1 (adaptor of Cul1), and ElonginC (adaptor of Cul2) despite the sequence divergence 38 (Extended Data Fig. 3f–3g). The BACK subdomain is characterized by four pairs of helices (α7-α14) (Fig. 2d). The Kelch domain contains six kelch-repeats, packing into a cluster structure (Extended Data Fig. 3d–3e). In addition to the expected BTB-BACK interaction, we define novel contacts between Kelch and BTB-BACK domains in which the Kelch domain interacts with both BTB and BACK subdomains mainly through polar interactions between the positively charged surface of BACK and the negatively charged surface of Kelch. K126, K127, N129, E131, R154, H158, N185 and N187 from BTB-BACK forms intensive hydrogen bonds with P362, D407, E405, E405, Q357, D317, Q356, Y587 and K265 from Kelch domain (Fig. 2f–2g).Cul3 serves as a scaffold that binds to the RING protein (Rbx1) and the substrate receptor (KBTBD2) by its C-terminal globular-like domain and N-terminal three repeats (Fig. 1a; Fig. 2b), separately. Cul3 presents a conserved structure feature with other cullin proteins (RMSD ranging from 2.9 Å to 6.4 Å, Supplementary Fig. 3b–3c) 11–14,39. Cul3CTD contains four subdomains: four-helix bundle (4HB), α/β, winged-helix motif A (WHA), and winged-helix motif B (WHB) (Fig. 1a; Extended Data Fig. 3b). The C-terminal RING domain of Rbx1 (Rbx1RING), an essential domain for CRLs to recruit ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes (E2) 40, binds with WHB by a relatively weak interaction, allowing high flexibility of Rbx1RING. The interface between KBTBD2BTB and Cul3NTD is mediated by both hydrophilic and hydrophobic interactions (Extended Data Fig. 3c).CRL3KBTBD2 dimers associate through their Cul3CTDs to form three larger multimers: tetramers, hexamers and octamers (Fig. 1b–1d). Composed by four CRL3KBTBD2 dimers (named Dimer-1 to Dimer-4) (Fig. 1d–1e), the octamer has pseudo-D4 symmetry, with the 2-fold axis of the obligate BTB-driven CRL3KBTBD2 dimer roughly perpendicular to the weaker and exchangeable 4-fold axis. The hexamer and tetramer are the D4 octamer complex missing either one dimer or two adjacent dimers, respectively (Supplementary Movie 1). Within multimers, Cul3 proteins stretch outside to embrace KBTBD2Kelch domains inside (Fig. 1e). The H24/H25 helices of Cul3CTD are mainly involved in the multimer formation (Fig. 1b–d, Fig. 2a). Kelch domains are close to each other, forming possible interactions through their flexible loops (Fig. 1e).To investigate the function of Cul3CTD and KBTBD2Kelch on multimer assembly, we performed the following experiments: Firstly, we mutate residues around the Cul3CTD-Cul3CTD interface. Q489A, H490A, Q492A, T494A, V496A mutations on Cul3 H24 and 537A, K542A, S544A mutations Cul3 H25 were introduced. Size exclusion chromatogram and negative staining EM showed that these mutations on H24 or H25 dramatically reduce the ratio of large CRL3KBTBD2 multimers, indicating Cul3CTD facilitates multimer assembly (Extended Data Fig. 4a–d, 4f). Secondly, we delete the Kelch domain of KBTBD2. Without Kelch domain, CRL3KBTBD2 sample mostly aggregates, indicating that Kelch domain is important for the ordered multimer assembly (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Thirdly, we mutated the residues on a small loop of Cul3WHB (713-SRKK-716), which stays close with Kelch domain (Fig 2a), into alanines. However, KBTBD2 and p85α form an unstable dynamic complex (Fig. 3a). Since phosphorylation of a Tyr-Xaa-Xaa-Met motif in KBTBD2 (250-YKSM-253) promotes its association with p85α 41,42, we attempted to increase the binding affinity between KBTBD2 and p85α by mutating Y250 and S252 to the non-phosphorylatable mimic Ala or the phosphor-mimetic Asp. Only S252D strongly increased the interaction between KBTBD2 and p85α (Fig. 3a–3b). Based on this local map, the models of KBTBD2 and p85αiSH2 were built then fitted to the CRL3KBTBD2-p85α dimer map together with Cul3-Rbx1 to complete model building of the CRL3KBTBD2-p85α dimer (Fig. 3e; Supplementary Fig. 5).P85αiSH2 spans the BACK and Kelch domains of KBTBD2 (Fig. 3e–3f). In addition, the C-terminus of Kelch domain inserts into the iSH2 domain, which might introduce additional contacts and stabilize the interaction between KBTBD2 and p85α (Fig. 3h). However, due to the flexibility of nSH2 and cSH2, the model of these two domains may only provide a rough position.SHORT syndrome mutations located at the p85α-KBTBD2 interfaceBoth KBTBD2 and p85α are highly conserved from mice to humans. Partial lethality is observed in teeny mutants and Kbtbd2 knockout mice 7, but phenotypically significant KBTBD2 mutations have not been observed in human patients to date. In contrast, a collection of dominant mutations in human PIK3R1, which encodes the alternatively spliced p85α, p55α, and p50α regulatory subunits of PI3K, cause SHORT syndrome (short stature, hyperextensibility of joints, ocular depression, Rieger anomaly, and teething delay) 20,48,49. One of the most common SHORT syndrome mutations is c.1945C>T; p.Arg649Trp9,50,51, which is localized in the cSH2 domain of p85α. Other reported SHORT syndrome mutations affect A486 in the iSH2 domain of p85α, including c.1457C>T; p.Ala486Val52 and c.1456G>A; p.Ala486Thr 53. Both R649 and A486 residues are identical in the human and mouse p85α proteins. The structure shows that R649 is located at the KBTBD2-p85αcSH2 interface and A486 is located at the KBTBD2-p85αiSH2 interface (Fig. 3h). The R649W mutation changes a long hydrophilic side chain to a long hydrophobic one. The A486 mutation replaces the Ala with short side chain with Val or Thr residues with longer side chains. Consistently, we observed that single mutations of p85α (R649W, A486T or A486V) reduced p85α interaction with KBTDB2 (Fig. 3i). Conversely, CSN deneddylates CRLs by removing the NEDD8 18,31,54–58. To trap CRL3 in the activated state, we performed a neddylation assay on CRL3KBTBD2, showing that CRL3KBTBD2 was successfully neddylated by NEDD8 (Extended Data Fig. 7b). We then purified the neddylated CRL3KBTBD2 (CRL3KBTBD2~N8) complex and determined its cryo-EM structure to 6.36 Å resolution (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Fig. 6 and 7), showing that most neddylated CRL3KBTBD2 (89%) are dimers. During neddylation, Gly76 of NEDD8 forms an isopeptide bond with K712 of Cul3 59. The NEDD8 density is missing in the cryo-EM map due to its flexibility.Structural comparison between CRL3KBTBD2~N8 and CRL3KBTBD2 monomer showed that the overall structures of Cul3 are similar with the Cα RMSD 0.5 Å. The major difference is located at Rbx1RING. While the N-terminal β-strand of Rbx1 remains anchored in Cul3CTD, Rbx1RING rotates ~27º away from the Cul3CTD-Rbx1 interface (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Movie 3). This conformational change reduces the contact area between Rbx1RING and Cul3 from 911 Å2 to 472 Å2, giving the Rbx1RING domain more flexibility, as required for subsequent substrate ubiquitination. Superimposing the CRL3KBTBD2~N8 dimer onto the CRL3KBTBD2 multimer maps showed that the Rbx1RING domain clashes with the neighboring Cul3CTD (Extended Data Fig. 7f–7h), indicating that, upon neddylation, it is necessary for CRL3KBTBD2 multimers to disassemble into dimers to allow Rbx1 movement.Structure comparison between CRL3KBTBD2~N8 and other neddylated CRL complexes 14,31,60,61. showed that the Rbx1RING adopts a similar conformation (Extended Data Fig. 7c–7e). Thus, the mechanism of CRL3 activation by neddylation is conserved with that of other CRLs: Cullin neddylation causes a conformational change of its Cul3CTD domain, releases the Rbx1RING domain, and converts the Rbx1 from a “closed” state to an activated “open” state ready for substrate ubiquitination.CSN deactivates CRL3KBTBD2~N8CSN consists of eight subunits (CSN1-CSN8) named in order of decreasing molecular weight ranging 57–22 kDa 62,63. Six CSN proteins (CSN1-CSN4, CSN7-CSN8) assemble into a horseshoe-shaped ring (Fig. 5c) and two (CSN5-CSN6) assemble into a globular heterodimer. All eight subunits are interconnected through a helix bundle (Fig. 5d) formed by their similar extended C-terminal α-helices 63. The structures of CSN in complex with CRL1, CRL2, and CRL4A revealed that CSN5 is responsible for the deneddylase activity of CSN 63.To verify the deneddylation activity of CSN toward CRL3KBTBD2, we expressed eight CSN proteins (CSN1–8, Fig. 4c), assembled the CSN complex, and incubated it with neddylated CRL3KBTBD2. Deneddylation assays revealed that CSN rapidly removed NEDD8 from CRL3KBTBD2~N8 within 3 minutes, generating a CRL3KBTBD2~N8Δ-CSN complex, which is named as “deactivated” state as the complex was produced following NEDD8 activation and CSN deactivation (Fig. 4d). The deneddylation activity of CSN has been localized to the CSN5 metalloprotease active site where H138, H140, D151 and E104 coordinate a Zn2+ ion 63. DISCUSSIONOur study visualizes snapshots of key steps in the catalytic cycle of CRL3KBTBD2 including assembly, substrate recruitment, neddylation, CSN-mediated deneddylation, and CAND1-mediated substrate receptor exchange (Fig. 6).In CRL3KBTBD2 assembly, KBTBD2 mediates CRL3KBTBD2 dimer formation via tight KBTBD2BTB-KBTBD2BTB interactions, while Cul3CTD-Cul3CTD interactions induce the formation of larger multimers. Disturbing the Cul3CTD-Cul3CTD interactions would prevent the formation of large multimers. For example, Cul3 neddylation breaks down large CRL3KBTBD2 multimers (4-, 6-, or 8-mers) into dimers by modifying the Cul3CTD and introducing steric hindrance at the Cul3CTD-Cul3CTD interface. In addition, the binding of p85α or CSN causes CRL3KBTBD2 multimers to dissociate into dimers by introducing steric hindrance between CRL3KBTBD2 dimers (Extended Data Fig. 1 and 2). Thus, the CRL3KBTBD2 multimers might represent autoinhibited states in which the neddylation and substrate binding sites are inaccessible. The multimer state may serve to stabilize the inactive CRL3KBTBD2 ligase. It has been reported that dimeric and multimeric Cul3/Rbx1 proteins are present in both the CRL3Keap1 and Cul3SPOP complex in vivo
76, indicating that CRL3 multimer states might exist in cells. The CRL4DCAF1 complex was similarly observed to form a tetrameric autoinhibited state in which the Rbx1RING is inaccessible for E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme 34. Most CRLs exist in monomer form.Previous studies on the Kelch domains of other BTB proteins and on small substrate-derived peptides provided limited understanding of the mechanism of substrate recruitment by CRL3, which emphasized the importance of the Kelch domain. This finding broadens our perspective when investigating substrate recruitment by other BTB proteins. Moreover, the molecular interactions at the KBTBD2-p85α interface provide a plausible rationale for the effects of certain p85α mutations that cause SHORT syndrome. After centrifugation, cleared lysates were incubated with Flag antibody-conjugated beads (M2, Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 h at 4 °C (Supplementary Table 1). Beads were washed three times with 1 mL of Nonidet P-40 lysis buffer and then eluted with 3xFlag peptides (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 4 °C. For immunoblot analysis, samples were resolved by NuPAGE 4–12% (wt/vol) Bis-Tris gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific), transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad), blotted with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight and the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature, and then visualized by chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The following antibodies were used in this study with the specified dilutions: mouse anti-HA (HA-7, Sigma-Aldrich, 1:5000), mouse anti-Flag (M2, Sigma-Aldrich, 1:5000), and goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) HRP (115–035-146, Jackson ImmunoResearch, 1:5000) (Supplementary Table 1).
