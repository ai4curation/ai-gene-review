Mitochondrial matrix Ca2+ concentration ([Ca2+]m) is theorized to be an
essential regulator of mitochondrial metabolism by positively regulating key
mitochondrial dehydrogenases. However, ablation or functional inhibition of the
mitochondrial calcium uniporter channel (mtCU) fails to significantly perturb
basal metabolism and is largely phenotypically silent in the absence of stress.
Here we demonstrate that MICU proteins, the reported gatekeepers of mtCU,
function in coordination to impart calcium-dependent regulation to
FADH2-dependent mitochondrial dehydrogenases through metabolon formation
independently of the mtCU and [Ca2+]m. Our results demonstrate that MICU
proteins differentially localize to mitochondrial microdomains and form
heterodimers and interactomes in response to intermembrane space Ca2+ binding
their respective EF-hand domains. Using an equimolar expression platform coupled
with unbiased proteomics, we reveal unique interactomes for MICU1/MICU2 versus
MICU1/MICU3 heterodimers and demonstrate that MICU proteins control coupling of
mitochondrial glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase and succinate
dehydrogenase/complex II and impart calcium-dependent changes in activity. We
propose that MICU-mediated mitochondrial metabolons are a fundamental system
facilitating matching of mitochondrial energy production with cellular demand
and is the primary physiological calcium signaling mechanism regulating
homeostatic energetics, not mtCU-dependent changes in [Ca2+]m.

## Full Text

[Ca2+]m is theorized to be a principal regulator of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle flux by positively regulating the activity of key dehydrogenases located in the mitochondrial matrix. This model is supported by literature dating back over a half-century containing reductionistic and often indirect evidence that [Ca2+]m enhances the activity of key metabolic enzymes that exist within the mitochondrial matrix.

Some of the earliest evidence for calcium-driven respiration comes from a 1942 study by Elvehjem’s group describing a link between calcium and succinate-dependent respiration1. Even so, the most well-characterized calcium control point of metabolism is pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH)2, a multiprotein complex found in the mitochondrial matrix. PDH phosphorylation was first described in 1969 as a key PDH regulatory mechanism governing carbon entry into the TCA cycle3. Two years later, the ability of calcium to enhance the activity of PDH phosphatase, thereby activating PDH, was described in isolated mitochondria4 and supported by isolated enzyme studies5. Shortly thereafter, in 1979, McCormack & Denton6 published a study probing other TCA cycle enzymes for calcium sensitivity, essentially looking for additional calcium control points besides PDH. Although the behavior of certain TCA cycle enzymes was altered in high versus low [Ca2+] conditions, none of the tested enzymes demonstrated an increase in maximum velocity (Vmax) as seen with PDH in this study6. Despite this, more recent studies have suggested that additional NADH-linked enzymes, such as α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and isocitrate dehydrogenase, are activated by [Ca2+]m7–10.

Until recently, the ability to causally interrogate the relationship between [Ca2+]m and mitochondrial energetics was limited by the lack of available tools due to the unknown genetic identity of the transporters responsible for Ca2+ uptake and efflux into and out of the mitochondria. The genetic identity of proteins comprising the mtCU, the primary mitochondrial Ca2+ (mCa2+) uptake pathway, was characterized in the 2010s, with the focus on MCU11,12, the essential pore-forming subunit of the mtCU. Additional mtCU components characterized include MICU1 (ref. 13), MICU2 (ref. 14), MICU3 (ref. 14), EMRE15, MCUB16 and MCUR1 (ref. 17). The genetic identity of the mitochondrial sodium/calcium exchanger (NCLX), the primary mCa2+ efflux pathway, was reported in 2010 (ref. 18), and in vivo confirmation was provided in 2017 (ref. 19). Since these discoveries, several studies have manipulated the mitochondrial calcium handling machinery in numerous model systems with varying results.

Interestingly, despite the purported longstanding essentiality of mCa2+ uptake to basal physiology, germline deletion of mtCU function (through deletion of MCU or EMRE) is largely phenotypically silent in the absence of stress20–22. Similarly, tissue-specific loss of MCU or EMRE from cardiac and skeletal myocytes23–27, brown adipocytes28, neurons29,30 and other tissues31 is phenotypically silent in homeostatic conditions. Furthermore, many of these studies demonstrate no detectable metabolic changes with functional ablation of mtCU in the absence of stress23,25,27. Upon introduction of pathological stress, ablation of mtCU function through deletion of Mcu23,25 or Smdt1(ref. 27) or transgenic overexpression of the uniporter’s dominant-negative subunit, MCUB32, is protective in contexts such as ischemia-reperfusion injury, β-adrenergic stress23,25,33, diabetic neuropathy34 and Alzheimer’s disease30. Even so, the absence of a gross baseline phenotype or substantial metabolic perturbation with ablation of mtCU function is perplexing given the dogmatic importance of matrix calcium signaling in the regulation of metabolism and cellular energetics. Indeed, the concept that calcium is the second messenger communicating cellular workload to mitochondria to meet energetic demands is cemented in biology35,36.

MICU1 is the primary regulator of mtCU-mediated mCa2+ uptake. Current models posit that along with homologs MICU2 and MICU3, MICU1 serves as a rheostat for mtCU-mediated mCa2+ uptake, tuning the rate of mCa2+ uptake relative to intracellular calcium concentrations ([iCa2+]) in the microenvironment. According to existing models, MICU2 and MICU3 bind the mtCU in a MICU1-dependent manner. The MICU dimer species (MICU1/1, MICU1/2 or MICU1/3) interacting with MCU/ EMRE determines the uniporter’s propensity for mCa2+ uptake relative to local inner membrane space (IMS) [Ca2+]37. Although certain aspects of MICU-mediated regulation are debated, such as if MICU1 occludes the mtCU basally or potentiates uptake38–43, multiple groups have shown energetic consequence to MICU deletion13,40,44–46, which was rationalized by altered mtCU regulation causing a change in [Ca2+]m and subsequently altering mitochondrial dehydrogenase activity47. Unlike deletion of MCU, MICU1 deletion does not ablate mtCU functionality and is embryonic lethal in both Drosophila48 and mice49. Existing models would explain this embryonic lethality by mCa2+ overload caused by ungated mtCU-mediated mCa2+ uptake, resulting in metabolic derangement and fulminant cell death. However, the lethality caused by Micu1 deletion is not rescued by ablating mtCU function through deletion of MCU (Mcu−/− × Micu1−/−)48 or EMRE (Smdt1−/− × Micu1−/−)49, and neither EMRE (Smdt1−/−) nor MCU (Mcu−/−) deletion is lethal, suggesting that MICU1 functions independently of its role in regulating the uniporter. In support, we recently discovered that MICU1 regulates mitochondrial architecture independent of the uniporter50. This work is corroborated by a previous study demonstrating that MICU1 modulates cristae junction integrity and spatially anchors the uniporter to the inner boundary membrane (IBM) during stimulation51.

The discrepancy in phenotypic consequence between MCU deletion and MICU1 deletion suggests that the existing mCa2+-centric model of mitochondrial calcium signaling is incomplete. Oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production are calcium sensitive, although the mechanisms of this are debated52–54. Thus, we have a substantial gap in knowledge; if not strictly through the mtCU, how does calcium regulate metabolism in the absence of stress?

Here we demonstrate that MICU1, MICU2 and MICU3 function in coordination to impart Ca2+-dependent regulation to key metabolic complexes fundamental to mitochondrial metabolism independent of the mtCU and [Ca2+]m. Our results suggest that MICU proteins are differentially localized to mitochondrial microdomains and that MICU heterodimer formation occurs upon Ca2+ binding their respective EF-hand domains in the IMS. In support, physiological signaling, such as IP3R-Ca2+ release, elicits a near-instant increase in MICU1/2 heterodimerization at the IBM. Using an equimolar expression system coupled with unbiased proteomics, we reveal unique interactomes for MICU1/2 versus MICU1/3 heterodimers. Highlighting the physiological importance of this system, we identified that MICU1/2 heterodimers interact with mitochondrial glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GPD2) and succinate dehydrogenase (SDH)/complex II (C-II) to couple TCA cycle metabolite flux and oxidative phosphorylation. We propose that MICU dimer formation and submitochondrial localization is a fundamental system to facilitate [IMSCa2+]-sensitive metabolon formation and localization. Our results suggest that this may be the primary physiological calcium signaling mechanism regulating cellular energetics.

MICU heterodimer formation is sensitive to [IMSCa2+]We next designed experiments to define the mechanisms governing MICU dimerization. MICU1, MICU2 and MICU3 each contain two conserved EF-hand domains essential for their ability to sense environmental [Ca2+]. Previous isolated protein studies suggested that MICU dimer formation is calcium sensitive55,57; to our knowledge, this has not been shown in an in vitro or in vivo system. Therefore, we hypothesized that local [IMSCa2+] controls MICU dimer formation. To test this, cells transfected with MICU1-HA-T2a-MICU2-Myc-T2a-MICU3-Flag were treated with the Ca2+ ionophore ionomycin (1.5 μM, 3 μM or 6 μM or vehicle (Veh)) to increase IMS[Ca2+] (Extended Data Fig. 3) or the cell-permeable free Ca2+ chelator BAPTA-AM (Veh, 2.5 μM, 5 μM or 10 μM) 12 hours prior to lysate collection. IP of MICU1-HA revealed an increase in MICU1/2 heterodimer formation in response to increasing [ionomycin] and a decrease in MICU1/2 heterodimer formation in response to Ca2+chelation, increasing [BAPTA-AM] (Fig. 3a). This result was confirmed using a similar experimental paradigm and the intracellular Ca2+ chelator EGTA-AM (Fig. 3b). To examine this further, we inserted EF-hand point mutations in our MICU1, MICU2 and MICU3 equimolar expression system such that all MICUs independently, and in combination, were rendered insensitive to calcium (Fig. 3c,d). The mutations introduced disrupt the Ca2+-binding aspartic acid residues and Ca2+-coordinating glutamic acid residues found in the EF-hand domains58. The triple mutant construct and WT constructs were expressed, and IP of MICU1-HA or MICU1EFmut-HA was performed. Loss of Ca2+binding significantly reduced MICU heterodimer formation (Fig. 3e). To investigate this further, MICU1, MICU2 and MICU3 triple expression plasmids were generated harboring EF-hand mutations of MICU1, MICU2 or MICU3 and expressed in HEK 293 T cells. IP of the C-terminal HA tag on MICU1-HA/MICU1EFmut-HA revealed decreased formation of the MICU1/MICU2 heterodimer upon mutation ofEF-hands on either MICU1 or MICU2 (Fig. 3f) indicated by loss of the dimer band, further supporting that the MICU1/MICU2 heterodimer forms most readily when bound to Ca2+. Notably, the reduction of MICU1/2 heterodimerization with EF-hand mutation is independent of Ca2+ binding MICU3 (Extended Data Fig. 4). These data demonstrate that Ca2+ binding the EF-hand domains ofindividual MICU proteins dictates dimerization and stability.

Metabolomic analysis of TCA cycle intermediates in WT, Micu2−/−, Mcu−/− and Micu2−/− × Mcu−/− cells revealed a MICU2-dependent and MCU-independent increase in the succinate/fumarate ratio (Fig. 6e), suggestive of decreased oxidation of succinate by C-II with loss of MICU2 that is unaffected by the loss of MCU and acute mCa2+ uptake. This result was corroborated by a C-II activity assay in which intact cells were permeabilized and provided succinate, ADP and rotenone to inhibit complex I (C-I) such that respiration (that is, rate of oxygen consumption) is directly a result of C-II driven respiration (Fig. 6f). Micu2−/− and Micu2−/− × Mcu−/− cells demonstrated significantly decreased state 3 respiration, with no change in state 4 respiration, and a trending decrease in the respiratory control ratio (Fig. 6g and Extended Data Fig. 8). Taken together, these data demonstrate that MICU2 regulates C-II activity independent of uniporter and mCa2+ uptake.

Next, we examined if GPD2 activity was impacted by loss of MICU2. To this end, we performed a GPD2 + C-III activity assay in which we isolated mitochondria, permeabilized them using freeze-thaw cycles, provided G3P as a substrate, treated with rotenone to inhibit C-I and measured cytochrome c reduction by C-III, as previously reported69,70. Micu2−/− and Micu2−/− × Mcu−/− mitochondria demonstrated a significant reduction in GPD2 + C-III activity (Fig. 6h). Interestingly, when isolated mitochondria are fed G3P as a substrate, C-III is inhibited using antimycin A, C-I is inhibited with rotenone and GPD2 activity is measured directly, and there is no difference between genotypes (Fig. 6i), suggesting that the observed metabolic deficit caused by MICU2 loss is due to altered coupling rather than deficiency of GPD2 itself.

Finally, we examined if MICU2 confers calcium sensitivity to the SDH/GPD2 metabolon through differential association of Ca2+-free versus Ca2+-bound MICU2. To this end, we performed IPs with either MICU2-Myc or MICU2EFmut-Myc (D185A, E196K, D375A, E386K) expressing cells. Interestingly, the physical interaction between GPD2 and MICU2 was destabilized by mutation of the EF-hands, suggesting that Ca2+-bound MICU2 more strongly associates with the SDH/GPD2 metabolon (Fig. 6j). To investigate whether Ca2+-bound MICU2 differentially affects function of the SDH/GPD2 metabolon, MICU2 and MICU2EFmut were expressed in Micu2−/− cells. Rescue of MICU2, but not MICU2EFmut, was sufficient to restore C-II activity (Fig. 6k,l). Taken together, these results demonstrate that MICU2 is necessary for coupling of the SDH/GPD2 metabolon to oxidative phosphorylation and that Ca2+ binding MICU2 increases the activity/function of the SDH/GPD2 metabolon independent of MCU and acute mCa2+ uptake.

As life transitioned from unicellular to multicellular anywhere from approximately 700 million72 to approximately 2 billion73 years ago, ‘competition was replaced by cooperation’, and evolutionary pressure shifted to calcium sensing and signaling71. The ability to transduce environmental calcium as a proxy for the presence/absence of other organisms allowed for more efficient and effective resource utilization and sharing and survival of multicellular life71. When sustained evolutionary pressure on environmental calcium sensing is considered in the context of endosymbiotic theory, one can easily postulate that organelles harbor sophisticated calcium signal transduction machinery on the outer leaflet of the organellar membrane or envelope. Over the last 15 years, the genetic identification and functional characterizations of various mitochondrial calcium handling components has reinvented the field and improved understanding of this system in both physiology and disease. Thus, calcium sensing, signaling and transport at and across organellar membranes are intrinsically tied to ATP production as these cellular processes have experienced equivalent evolutionary pressure.

Original biochemical studies performed and published in the 1970s established two fundamental notions that sit at the foundation of the existing model of how calcium regulates metabolism. First was the finding that mitochondria contained a channel capable of supporting inward rectifying Ca2+ current74–76. Even so, the mitochondrial and metabolism fields remain puzzled by discrepancies in the literature, namely that ablation of mitochondrial calcium uptake is phenotypically silent at baseline while deletion of MICU1 is lethal. This discrepancy can be explained in one of two ways.

The first possible explanation is that there is a secondary mode of uptake that is capable of compensating for loss of mtCU. Although other mitochondrial Ca2+ transporters do exist—namely, the mitochondrial Ca2+/H+ antiporter leucine zipper/EF-hand-containing transmembrane protein 1 (LETM1)78—these transports contribute minimally, if at all, to acute mCa2+ uptake79. Furthermore, although it has been suggested that mtCU-independent modes of mCa2+ uptake are relevant to certain muscular pathophysiology80, this mechanism was proposed to be through altered permeability transition and cell death rather than metabolic perturbation and was observed only in the context of genetic deletion of Mcu. Given the acute timescale in which metabolic adaptation must occur and the near-complete ablation of acute mCa2+ uptake that occurs with loss or inhibition of mtCU function, it is highly unlikely that physiological calcium-mediated regulation of mitochondrial metabolism occurs within the matrix through a calcium uptake pathway other than the mtCU.

The second possible explanation is that [Ca2+]m is not the master regulator ofbasal mitochondrial metabolism. Although initially perplexing, this notion is supported by the many studies that report that mtCU function is dispensable to homeostasis23,25,27 ,30,32–34. Prior to the discovery of the uniporter’s genetic identity, Kirichok et al.81 demonstrated that the uniporter is largely inactive at resting cytosolic [Ca2+] (<100 nM) or during the cytosolic [Ca2+] rise that occurs with stimulation (100–500 nM). This implies that the uniporter is primarily relevant during stress, injury or insult. The notion that uniporter localization may affect channel conductance was partially addressed by our co-authors, showing that the uniporter is basally housed within the cristae membrane and, during stimulation, shuttles to the IBM in a MICU1-dependent manner51. Interestingly, the majority of MICU1 and MCU do not basally co-localize in the absence of stimulation, suggestive that MICU1 does not basally occlude the uniporter, as was previously suggested42,43. Furthermore, this baseline positioning ofthe uniporter within the cristae membrane, not directly at a Ca2+ microdomain capable of reaching [Ca2+] substantial enough to trigger its activation, has prompted further work suggesting that MICU1 regulates uniporter activity through remodeling ofmitochondrial cristae architecture and mitochondrial substructure membrane potential gradients84. However, our previous finding that MICU1 modulates mitochondrial architecture independent of the uniporter50 brings into question the primary function of MICU proteins. Even so, the view that [Ca2+]m is not the principal regulator of metabolism during physiology but becomes relevant during pathology resolves the discrepancies in the literature; however, one essential question remains: How then does calcium regulate metabolism in the absence of stress?

We propose that the MICU interactome serves as a fundamental calcium registry at the IMM and is the primary physiological calcium signaling mechanism regulating cellular metabolism and energetics. In regulating localization, formation and function of the GPD2/C-II metabolon, we demonstrate proof of concept for the existence of this system. We propose that, upon introduction of pathological stress, the uniporter becomes relevant as a high-capacity Ca2+ transporter to increase [Ca2+]m, activate known Ca2+-controlled NADH-dependent dehydrogenases7 (isocitrate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and pyruvate dehydrogenase) and increase ATP bioavailability in response to stress. With prolonged stress (Ca2+overload), uniporter activation causes metabolic derangement, permeability transition and cellular demise. These findings challenge the mCa2+-centric dogma that bioenergetic calcium signaling occurs exclusively in the mitochondrial matrix and support a new concept: that bioenergetic Ca2+-driven metabolon formation is the primary means by which mitochondria meet the energetic demand of cellular workload.

Although it has long been hypothesized that individual mitochondria within a single cell are functionally distinct, only recently was it causally shown that distinct subpopulations of mitochondria specialize in oxidative versus reductive biosynthesis85. One could easily hypothesize that sophisticated microenvironmental signal transduction pathways, such as the one we outline here, exist to activate unique metabolic programs within individual mitochondria, allowing them to better serve their microenvironment.

In the present study, we discovered and outlined how MICU proteins are essential to bioenergetic mitochondrial calcium signaling, independent of [Ca2+]m. Using a novel equimolar MICU expression platform, we demonstrate that MICU heterodimerization and stoichiometry are independent of mCa2+ uptake and acutely sensitive to physiological calcium signaling at discrete microdomains within the IMS (Extended Data Fig. 9). We used an IP/LC/MS approach to demonstrate unique MICU1/2 and MICU1/3 heterodimer interactomes, and we show that the MICU1/2 heterodimer binds to and determines coupling of GPD2 and SDH/C-II. SDH/C-II is unique as it functions as both an ETC complex and a TCA cycle enzyme. Given the duality of SDH/C-II biology, one would expect distinct signaling mechanisms governing SDH/C-II flux coupling to the TCA cycle versus ETC. However, very little is known related to C-II regulation beyond the identification of SDH/C-II assembly factors (SDHAF1 (refs. 86,87), SDHAF2 (ref. 88), SDHAF3 (ref. 89) and SDHAF4 (ref. 90)). Given the energetic cost of complex assembly/disassembly and acute timescale that metabolic adaptation must occur, especially when considering the energetic responsiveness of oxidative phosphorylation, there is a substantial gap in knowledge with regard to SDH/C-II regulation. The identification of MICU2 as a regulator of SDH/C-II function not only underscores the validity of our model but is also a major step forward in the understanding of SDH/C-II biology.

Similar to SDH/C-II, GPD2 functions in multiple metabolic pathways and represents a metabolic nexus. GPD2 converts G3P to DHAP, reduces FAD+ to FADH2 and, like SDH/C-II, feeds electrons to C-III in the form of reduced CoQ. GPD2 is essential to glycolysis, glycerophospholipid biosynthesis and the glycerophosphate shuttle. Despite superficial differences in substrates, products and cofactors, SDH/C-II and GPD2 are both critical contributors to the CoQ pool, both passing electrons to C-III independent of NADH and C-I. GPD2 itself serves as a critical source of electron leak53,91, and it has been suggested that GPD2-dependent reactive oxygen species formation occurs at SDH via reverse mode electron transport92. One can envision that functional coupling of SDH/C-II and GPD2 would serve as an elegant system for controlling redox balance and oxidative phosphorylation.

The MICU2/GPD2/C-II metabolon that we have identified is a metabolic nexus, existing at the intersection of glycolysis, the TCA cycle and the ETC. Thus, we have characterized a calcium control point at the intersection of the three major metabolic pathways independent of acute mCa2+ uptake.

[Ca2+] was determined by the following formula: [Ca2+] = Kd × (F − Fmin) / (Fmax – F), where Kd (561 nm) is the Ca2+ dissociation constant, and Fmin and Fmax are the fluorescence intensities in Ca2+-free and Ca2+-saturated conditions, respectively.

Cells were then imaged using a Zeiss Axio Observer.Z1 microscope at the appropriate Ex/Em wavelength for the probes being used: fluo-4 (490/20-nm Ex + 535/50-nm Em), mtRGECO1 (572/35-nm Ex + 632/60-nm Em), cal590 (572/35-nmEx + 632/60-nm Em) and IMS-GEM-GECO1 (360/40-nm Ex + 455/50-nm Em and 535/50-nm Em). Calcium signals were induced by addition of 10 μM ionomycin or by 200 μM ATP. In case of SOCE, cells were pretreated with 10 μM thapsigargin followed by rinsing with extracellular medium containing 100 μM EGTA and addition of 4 mM CaCl2 in Ca2+-free HBSS. Cal590 and IMS-GEM-GECO were calibrated in vitro by adding in the end of the run 10 μM ionomycin followed by 20 mM EGTA/Tris (pH 8.5). [Ca2+]IMS was determined by the following formula: [Ca2+] = Kd × [(R − Rmin) / (Rmax − R)]1/n, where Kd (340 nm) is the Ca2+ dissociation constant; R is the ratio of the fluorescence intensities at 455/535-nm emission; Rmin and Rmax are the fluorescence ratios in Ca2+-free and Ca2+-saturated conditions, respectively; and n (2) is the Hill coefficient. [Ca2+] was determined by the following formula: [Ca2+] = Kd × (F − Fmin) / (Fmax – F), where Kd (561 nm) is the Ca2+ dissociation constant, and Fmin and Fmax are the fluorescence intensities in Ca2+-free and Ca2+-saturated conditions, respectively.

IBM association indexThe IBM association index was calculated as described previously51. In short, images were subjected to background subtraction (Mosaic Suite, background subtractor; National Institutes of Health (NIH)) with a sliding rectangle diameter of 50 pixels. The reference channel (MTG, TMRE, MICU2–mCherry and MICU1–EGFP) was Otsu auto-thresholded and further dilated and eroded in two independent subsets. Pixelwise subtraction of the erosion reference of the dilated reference image yields a hollow structure, used as a mask to measure the mean intensity in the mitochondrial periphery or IBM-related area in the object channel. The erosion reference served as a mask to measure the bulk or cristae mean fluorescence intensity. The ratio of IBM/cristae membrane mean intensity is a value to estimate changes in the object label distribution inside a mitochondrion, which is referred to as the IBM association index. The higher the ratio value, the higher the distribution of protein labels in the IBM. For image analysis, the freeware program ImageJ was used.
