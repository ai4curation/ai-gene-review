Pseudouridine (Ψ) is an abundant modification in small RNA catalyzed by multiple
pseudouridine synthases (PUSs). However, the substrate specificity of human PUSs
remains elusive. Here, we adopted PRAISE, a quantitative Ψ detection method, to
profile pseudouridylation in small RNA, including cytosolic and mitochondrial
tRNAs, snRNA, and snoRNA. We found that snoRNA pseudouridylation is mediated not
only by RNA-guided DKC1, but also by the stand-alone enzyme PUS7 at a specific
site. Interestingly, several PUS enzymes, including PUS1, RPUSD1, and PUS7,
which install nearby Ψ sites within tRNA anticodon stem-loop, can influence
pseudouridylation catalyzed by other PUSs, revealing an unrecognized interplay
during Ψ formation. For the three RluA family enzymes, RPUSD1 catalyzes the
canonical Ψ30 in tRNA-Ile and Ψ72 in tRNA-Arg isoacceptors. RPUSD2
pseudouridylates Ψ31 of mt-tRNALeu(CUN), Ψ32 of mt-tRNAPro and mt-tRNACys,
whereas RPUSD3 lacks tRNA activity. Together, our quantitative Ψ profiling
characterized PUS tRNA substrates and revealed unexpected PUS interplay.

Introduction

Pseudouridine (Ψ), known as the “fifth nucleotide” of RNA, is the most abundant post-transcriptional modification and is prevalent in various RNA species1,2. Ψ has unique physiochemical properties due to the additional hydrogen bond donor at the N1 position, which has been shown to increase base-pairing stability3. Emerging evidence suggests that Ψ is dynamically regulated in response to cellular stress and is involved in multiple processes that influence pre-mRNA splicing and translational regulation4,5. Ψ is highly enriched in noncoding RNAs, particularly small RNAs such as tRNA, snRNA, and snoRNA, and is clustered in evolutionarily conserved and functionally important regions6. More than 14 positions have been identified in cytosolic tRNA, which are distributed across critical regions including the D loop, anticodon stem-loop, TΨC loop, and acceptor stem2,7. The widespread occurrence of Ψ in tRNA highlights its regulatory role in tRNA processing and translational control4.

Ψ installation is mediated by pseudouridine synthases (PUSs). In humans, 13 PUS enzymes can be classified into five families: RluA, TruA, TruB, TruD, and PUS108. Mutations and dysregulated expression of several PUS enzymes lead to aberrant pseudouridylation of tRNAs, which affects various cellular pathways and contributes to disease progression5. Genetic mutations in PUS1, which is responsible for the pseudouridylation in specific tRNAs9, lead to mitochondrial myopathy and sideroblastic anemia (MLASA)10,11. A mutation in PUS1 in a patient with an MLASA phenotype impairs mitochondrial function and blocks erythroid differentiation12. PUS7 variants are linked to intellectual disability13, and PUS7-dependent Ψ8 modification in tRNA fragments was found to impact translation and contribute to tumorigenesis14. High expression of PUS7 in glioblastoma and its regulation of select tRNA Ψ50 modification are critical for codon-specific translational control of key regulators of GSCs15. Although emerging evidence demonstrates the regulatory roles of human PUSs in physiological and pathological conditions, the tRNA substrates of most human PUS enzymes remain poorly understood. Further investigation of additional PUS targets is crucial for elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of PUSs.

The distribution of Ψ modifications in RNA substrates relies on Ψ detection technologies. Early transcriptome-wide profiling approaches utilized N-Cyclohexyl-N′-(2-morpholinoethyl)carbodiimide methyl-p-toluenesulfonate (CMC) or azide-modified CMC derivatives to label RNA, leveraging CMC-induced reverse transcription termination to identify Ψ sites16–20. DM-Ψ-seq adopts AlkB demethylases to remove methylations that block reverse transcription, thereby improving the sensitivity for Ψ detection in small RNA21. However, it cannot provide quantitative stoichiometric information due to partial labeling efficiency. A nanopore-based technique, Nano-tRNAseq, reported quantitative estimates of tRNA Ψ modification using base-calling errors, and it has been applied to Saccharomyces cerevisiae tRNAs22. We and others have developed quantitative methods such as PRAISE, BID-seq, and BACS, which employ bisulfite and cyclization chemistries to enable quantification of Ψ stoichiometry in the human transcriptome23–25. These quantitative methods facilitate the investigation of the dense Ψ landscape of small RNA substrates mediated by multiple human PUSs.

In this study, we adopted PRAISE, a quantitative Ψ detection method developed by our group, to generate a global map of Ψ modifications in small RNAs of HEK293T cells. We found that pseudouridylation of snoRNAs is mediated not only by RNA-guided enzyme DKC1 but also by the stand-alone enzyme PUS7. By perturbing ten PUS enzymes using stable knockdown or knockout cell lines, including DKC1, PUS1, PUS7, TRUB1, RPUSD1, RPUSD2, RPUSD3, PUS1L, PUS7L, and PUS10, we identified their individual small RNA substrates. Notably, we report for the first time the tRNA targets of the human RluA family and found that the substrate of RPUSD1 differs from that of its yeast counterparts. In addition, our data reveal that PUS enzymes targeting Ψ sites can mutually regulate one another within the tRNA anticodon stem-loop. This includes the interplay between RPUSD1 and PUS1 in the anticodon stem, as well as the mutual influences between PUS7 and PUS1, and between PUS7 and PUS3, in the anticodon loop. Together, we employed quantitative Ψ profiling to identify the small RNA substrates of PUSs and reveal crosstalk among them during tRNA pseudouridylation.

ResultsAdopting a quantitative detection method for profiling Ψ sites in small RNATo achieve quantitative Ψ detection in small RNA less than 200 nt, we sought to implement the optimized bisulfite chemistry according to our recently published technology, named PRAISE23. However, specific modifications, including m1A and m3C modifications, can block reverse transcription. Inspired by DM-tRNA-seq and DM-Ψ-seq, we aimed to remove these modifications in vitro to improve cDNA synthesis efficiency21,26. We isolated and treated small RNA with AlkB demethylase, and demonstrated that m1A modification nearly disappeared after AlkB treatment using LC-MS detection (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Further integrating ligation-based strand-specific library construction, we established a methodological pipeline for Ψ detection in small RNA (Supplementary Fig. 1b).We first analyzed read coverage across small RNA transcripts and did not find bias for the 5' or 3' end in the small RNA library (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). The fractions of different RNA types showed that cytosolic tRNAs (cyto-tRNAs) accounted for 82.15%, mitochondrial tRNAs (mt-tRNAs) for 0.4%, 5S rRNA for 12.2%, and snRNA and snoRNA together for 5.0%, respectively (Fig. 1a). We then employed PRAISE to identify Ψ sites in small RNAs from HEK293T cells. Since cytosolic tRNA was well-known to contain abundant Ψ sites, we globally identified and quantified Ψ sites in the cyto-tRNAs to test the performance of PRAISE in small RNA. In the cyto-tRNAs, 713 and 714 Ψ sites were detected in two technical replicates, respectively, with 705 overlapping Ψ sites, thus demonstrating high reproducibility of PRAISE (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data 1). Among the 17 non-overlapping sites, the majority of them (12/17) exhibited low modification levels in another replicate (3–5%) (Supplementary Fig. 1e). We further quantified the Ψ level for the individual site based on the deletion signal, and found a high correlation of Ψ modification levels between two technical replicates (R2 = 0.99) (Fig. 1c). In addition, Ψ sites in mt-tRNAs can also be detected by PRAISE. We identified 31 and 35 Ψ sites in two technical replicates, respectively, with 31 overlapping sites (Supplementary Data 2). Notably, these four non-overlapping sites that had been previously validated by mass spectrometry data27 also exhibited detectable modification signals in another PRAISE technical replicate (Supplementary Data 2). Human Leu-AAG-4 tRNA has the highest number of Ψ sites (six sites). When analyzing Ψ sites in different tRNA regions, we found that Ψ occurs more frequently in the anticodon stem loop (332 sites) compared to the TΨC loop (250 sites) and D loop (90 sites), suggesting that Ψ plays a critical role in translational decoding. Among all Ψ sites, Ψ55 in the TΨC loop is the most conserved modification, being present in all cyto-tRNAs. Following this, Ψ38-40 and Ψ27-28 in the anticodon stem are present in 148 of 198 and 132 of 202 cyto-tRNAs that contain uridine at these positions, respectively. Ψ13 in the D loop is relatively less conserved and is present in several types of cyto-tRNAs. Additionally, specific Ψ modification sites are found in individual tRNA; for instance, Ψ72 is present only in the acceptor stem of tRNA-Arg, Ψ8 is present only in tRNA-Gly, and Ψ20 is present only in the D loop of tRNA-Leu, indicating their particular functional and physiological roles in tRNA.

We further quantified the modification levels of individual Ψ sites using PRAISE. We integrated and displayed the median modification level for each position across modified cyto-tRNAs, based on an alignment of tRNA sequences28 (Fig. 1f). From the results, we observed that the universally conserved modification site, Ψ55, exhibited a very high modification level, reaching a median of 83.4%. Ψ35, located at the center of tRNA-Tyr anticodon, showed a high modification level of 86.9%, which may be crucial for the recognition of UAG stop codon29. Other highly-modified sites included Ψ72, Ψ13, and Ψ39, with median modification levels of 62.9, 62.1, and 59.8%, respectively. In contrast, Ψ30-32 showed moderate modification levels of 16.7, 24.3, and 24.6%, respectively. Altogether, we provided a global overview of Ψ profiles and stoichiometries in human cyto-tRNAs.

We next analyzed the Ψ distribution and levels in mt-tRNAs and found Ψ sites in 17 out of the total 22 mt-tRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 1l, m). Compared to cyto-tRNAs, mt-tRNAs display a distinct Ψ landscape. Notably, the highly-modified Ψ13 and Ψ72 sites common in cyto-tRNAs are absent, while Ψ68, though present in the acceptor stem, shows a low modification level (7.3%). Instead, Ψ27 and Ψ28 are the most conserved and highly-modified sites in mt-tRNAs, with median modification levels of 79.9 and 68.3%, respectively (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Data 2). Ψ39 is also conserved, but its modification level varies widely—for example, 12.9% in mt-tRNAPhe versus 84.3% in mt-tRNAHis. Surprisingly, Ψ55, despite being the most conserved site in cyto-tRNAs, is found in only six mt-tRNAs, though its modification levels in mt-tRNALeu(UUR) and mt-tRNASer are as high as 94.5 and 89.2%, respectively. Thus, these results suggested the presence of a differential Ψ installation machinery in mt-tRNAs.

Among the remaining 12 PUSs, the expression levels of most remained unchanged, and only RPUSD1 and RPUSD3, both members of the RluA family, showed a slight upregulation upon PUS1 knockout (Supplementary Fig. 3g). We noted that in PUS1-knockout cells, PUS1 mRNA was not lost despite the loss of protein function, likely because the premature stop mutation did not fully trigger nonsense-mediated RNA decay. Following PUS1 knockout, the most prominent increases in Ψ modification were observed at Ψ30 and Ψ31. tRNAs exhibiting PUS1-dependent increases in Ψ at U30/31 contained a distinct “RGU” (R = G or A) motif, whereas non-interplay tRNAs harbored a “CKU” (K = G or U) motif, indicating that distinct sequence features might account for their differential susceptibility to PUS1-mediated interplay (Supplementary Fig. 3h). Although Ψ31 and Ψ32 have been reported to be catalyzed by RluA family enzymes in yeast40,41, the substrate specificity of the RluA family in humans remains unclear. To further investigate the molecular basis of this interplay, we subsequently analyzed the potential tRNA substrates of human RluA family members.

DiscussionIn humans, 13 distinct PUS enzymes are predicted to catalyze Ψ formation. Ψ is abundant in tRNAs, yet many responsible enzymes are unidentified2, and potential crosstalk among PUSs targeting the same tRNA remains unclear. While Ψ modifications in snoRNAs have been reported, DKC1’s activity in snoRNA remains uncharacterized in humans19,24,25. Here, we adopted the PRAISE method by incorporating AlkB demethylation, bisulfite treatment, and eCLIP library preparation, enabling quantitative and site-specific profiling of Ψ sites in small RNAs. Using nine individual PUS knockout cell lines (i.e., PUS1, PUS7, TRUB1, RPUSD1, RPUSD2, RPUSD3, PUS10, PUS1L, PUS7L) and one stable knockdown line (i.e., DKC1), we identified their respective small RNA substrates. Notably, we found that Ψ sites in snoRNAs can be catalyzed either by the RNA-guided DKC1 or the stand-alone enzyme PUS7. We also report, for the first time, that RPUSD1 modifies Ψ30 and Ψ72 on cytoplasmic tRNAs, while RPUSD2 targets Ψ31 and Ψ32 on mitochondrial tRNAs. In contrast, no tRNA substrates were detected for RPUSD3. PUS1L exclusively modified mt-tRNA Ψ39/40, whereas PUS7L targeted Ψ sites confined to the variable loop of type II cytosolic tRNAs. A BACS-based study published during our revision also identifies major PUS-dependent Ψ sites in human tRNAs49. That study focuses on establishing a comprehensive Ψ map, while ours emphasizes quantitative stoichiometry and PUSs interplay in tRNA pseudouridylation. In humans, 13 distinct PUS enzymes are predicted to catalyze Ψ formation. Ψ is abundant in tRNAs, yet many responsible enzymes are unidentified2, and potential crosstalk among PUSs targeting the same tRNA remains unclear. While Ψ modifications in snoRNAs have been reported, DKC1’s activity in snoRNA remains uncharacterized in humans19,24,25. Here, we adopted the PRAISE method by incorporating AlkB demethylation, bisulfite treatment, and eCLIP library preparation, enabling quantitative and site-specific profiling of Ψ sites in small RNAs. Using nine individual PUS knockout cell lines (i.e., PUS1, PUS7, TRUB1, RPUSD1, RPUSD2, RPUSD3, PUS10, PUS1L, PUS7L) and one stable knockdown line (i.e., DKC1), we identified their respective small RNA substrates. Notably, we found that Ψ sites in snoRNAs can be catalyzed either by the RNA-guided DKC1 or the stand-alone enzyme PUS7. Finally, the 10 bp UMI was extracted by umi_tools (version 1.0.0)66. The key parameters are: umi_tools extract --extract-method=string --bc-pattern=NNNNNNNNNN. In addition, the library construction added 6 bp bases at the 3' end of insertion, which was also removed by umi_tools (version 1.0.0). The key parameters are: umi_tools extract --extract-method = string -−3prime --bc-pattern=NNNNNN.

Cleaned reads alignment of PRAISE

Human cytosolic tRNAs reference (GRCh38) was retrieved from the database GtRNAdb67. Only non-redundant tRNA sequences were retained, and a “CCA” sequence was added to the 3' end of each tRNA. The mitochondrial tRNAs reference was downloaded from NCBI. Other small RNA references (snRNA, snoRNA, microRNA, yRNA, and <200 nt rRNAs) originated from the human transcriptome (GRCh38). The snRNA and snoRNA entries were streamlined to only one representative sequence per type.

A two-step strategy was implemented to ensure the alignment accuracy: (1) BWA-MEM was used to align the cleaned reads to the reference transcriptome of total small RNAs. The key parameter is: bwa mem -Y. (2) A custom Python program was used to realign the mapped reads from the previous step. This program was based on biopython and adopted an optimized scoring matrix to precisely resolve deletion signals. The key parameters were: python realignment.py –fast -ms 4.8.

After that, the custom Python program remove_end_signal.py was used to remove 3'-end deletion signals to reduce false-positive sites. Samtools (v1.14) was used to convert the BAM file into an mpileup format file68. The key parameters are: samtools mpileup -d 1500000 -BQ0 --ff UNMAP, QCFAIL -aa. Finally, the information in the mpileup file was counted and converted into a BMAT format file for subsequent signal identification.

Identification of Ψ signalΨ site identification required three criteria: (1) For each T site, a 2 × 2 contingency table was constructed using deletion counts and non-deletion counts (total counts - deletion counts) from treated and untreated samples. Significant inter-group differences were assessed by χ² or Fisher’s exact test. The p value is required to be <0.0001; (2) The deletion rate was defined as follows: deletion counts/total counts. The deletion rate difference between treated and untreated samples is required to be >5%. (3) Ψ sites must be detected in both technical replicates. The mean detected signal between replicates was reported as the final signal of each Ψ site.

Target prediction on snRNA and snoRNAWe performed an in-silico target prediction analysis based on the snoRNA database36. For each pseudouridine site detected, a 21-nt window containing 10 nt upstream and downstream of the modified position was extracted to generate the target set. The 3' and 5' catalytic hairpin regions of all H/ACA box snoRNAs were obtained using RNAfold (2.7.0) from the ViennaRNA Package70 and used as the snoRNA query library. We then used RNAsnoop (2.7.0) from the ViennaRNA Package to generate specific binding models of the query library and target set. The key parameters are: RNAsnoop -s snoRNA query library -t target set -d 2. We employed filtering criteria in RNAsnoop to minimize false positives: (1) ≥4 base pairs in the 5' duplex, (2) ≥4 base pairs in the 3' duplex, and (3) a total predicted free energy < –10 kcal/mol.
