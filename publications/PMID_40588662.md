Latrophilins are conserved adhesion-type G-protein-coupled receptors associated
with embryonic defects and lethality. However, their mechanistic roles and
ligands in embryogenesis remain unknown. Here, we identified TOL-1, the sole
Toll-like receptor in Caenorhabditis elegans, as a ligand for the C. elegans
latrophilin, LAT-1. The extracellular lectin domain of LAT-1 directly binds to
the second leucine-rich repeat domain of TOL-1. The crystal structure and
cryo-electron microscopy density map of the LAT-1-TOL-1 extracellular region
complex reveal a one-to-one lectin domain interaction with the convex face of a
leucine-rich repeat domain. In C. elegans, endogenous mRNA and protein
localization analyses showed mutually exclusive sites of expression, suggesting
that in vivo LAT-1-TOL-1 interactions mostly occur in trans. Mutagenesis of key
interface residues that disrupt the LAT-1-TOL-1 interaction led to partial
lethality and malformed embryos. Thus, TOL-1 binding to LAT-1 represents a
receptor-ligand axis essential for animal development.

## Full Text

During animal development, cells communicate with each other to follow a sequence of precisely controlled cell division events and to spatially arrange in a coordinated manner, eventually forming the embryo. Cellular communication during these processes is in part mediated by cell surface receptors. Several key cell surface receptors mediate extracellular protein–protein interactions to induce signaling pathways acting at different times and in different regions of the developing embryo1. However, the molecular mechanisms of cellular communication that govern early embryogenesis are incompletely understood.

Latrophilins (LPHNs/ADGRLs) are cell surface receptors with essential functions in animal development, especially in early embryogenesis, in both vertebrates and invertebrates2,3. They are signaling receptors that belong to the adhesion G-protein-coupled receptor (aGPCR) family and are found in all major bilaterian animal taxa3-7. Among the 33 aGPCRs in humans, LPHNs 1–3 are the only aGPCRs, outside of Flamingo/CELSR proteins, that are conserved between vertebrates and invertebrates8. LPHNs have large extracellular regions (ECRs) comprising a lectin, an olfactomedin, a hormone receptor (HormR) and a GPCR autoproteolysis-inducing (GAIN) domain, which precedes the transmembrane region (Fig. 1a)9-11. All LPHN protein domains are conserved from Caenorhabditis elegans to humans, except the olfactomedin domain.

The roles of LPHNs in embryonic development have been mostly studied in the nematode C. elegans, whose genome contains two LPHN orthologs: lat-1 and lat-2 (ref. 3). Although the functions of lat-2 remain unknown, lat-1 is essential for early C. elegans development; it is required for anterior–posterior spindle orientation in the early embryo12,13 and participates in the planar cell polarity pathway14. While a comprehensive analysis of the endogenous expression pattern and localization of LAT-1 is needed, antibody staining and transgenic reporter analysis showed that lat-1 is expressed in early blastomeres, embryonic epithelia, pharyngeal muscles and neurons, as well as in the nerve ring neuropil at the head11-13,15-19. Its activity is needed for embryo elongation, pharyngeal development, neurotransmitter release by pharyngeal neurons and sperm development. The lat-1-null mutant worms display embryonic and larval lethality phenotypes12. Mice carrying homozygous null Lphn1 (refs. 20,21) or Lphn2 (ref. 22) alleles also show embryonic lethality, indicating that LPHN function in embryogenesis is conserved between invertebrates and vertebrates. Lastly, numerous recent studies suggest roles for LPHNs in tissue physiology, such as nervous system development11, heart development23, insulin secretion24, multidrug resistance in acute myeloid leukemia25, endothelial cell adhesion26, cardiac lineage commitment27 and epithelial proliferation28. LPHNs are associated with developmental and neurological defects21, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder29 and cancers29-32. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying LPHN function in development remain mostly unknown.

LPHNs have several known ligands. In vertebrate neurons, the extracellular domains of LPHNs interact with teneurins (TENs) in a trans-synaptic manner and regulate excitatory synapse formation and organization6,1,22,33-41. TENs are large cell surface proteins essential for gastrulation and epithelial to mesenchymal transition (in mice), heart and brain development (in flies) and the correct formation of basement membranes (in worms)42,43. In C. elegans, lat-1 and ten-1 (sole TEN ortholog) are both key molecules for embryonic development. However, genetic interaction data for lat-1 and ten-1 mutant animals suggested that they act in parallel pathways during embryonic development15. LAT-1 and TEN-1 also appear to be expressed in the same cells, conflicting with a model where interactions between neighboring cells dictate tissue polarity and cell migrations in development. Vertebrate LPHNs also interact with fibronectin leucine-rich transmembrane proteins (FLRTs) to mediate neuronal functions44,45 but FLRTs do not exist in invertebrates and, thus, are likely not involved in the fundamental developmental roles of LPHNs. Recent studies reported that Drosophila LPHN (Cirl) cooperates with Toll8 to control planar cell polarity in embryos and the size of the neuroblast pool in the central nervous system46,47. The various developmental roles of LPHNs suggest that other yet-to-be-described ligands of LAT-1 or LPHN mediate intercellular interactions critical for its functions in development3.

Here, we provide mechanistic insights into the poorly understood roles of LPHNs in embryogenesis. To identify proteins that physically interact with LPHNs, we turned to C. elegans, a powerful genetic model to study early development. Using our extracellular protein interaction discovery platform, we identified a cell surface receptor, TOL-1, the sole Toll-like receptor (TLR) in C. elegans, as a binding partner for LAT-1 (LPHN homolog). In addition to their well-established roles in innate immunity48, TLRs are critical for animal development49; in C. elegans, tol-1 and lat-1 loss-of-function (LOF) mutants display similar embryonic phenotypes12,50. Next, we used crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) to determine the structure of the TOL-1 ECR in complex with the lectin domain of LAT-1 and engineered point mutants at the binding interface that break the complex in vitro. We then used CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing to generate C. elegans animals that carry homozygous alleles of tol-1 or lat-1 with point mutations that specifically disrupt the LAT-1–TOL-1 interaction, resulting in developmental defects similar to the ones observed in tol-1 and lat-1 LOF mutant animals. Lastly, we extend previous studies on tol-1 and lat-1 expression11-13,16-19,50,51 by providing their endogenous expression pattern and localization across multiple developmental stages, which favor a model of trans interactions. Altogether, our study provides structural and mechanistic insights by combining in vitro and in vivo mutagenesis of this complex and suggests that the TOL-1–LAT-1 interaction is critical for early development in C. elegans and likely in other animals.

ResultsLAT-1a interacts with TOL-1, the TLR in C. elegansTo reveal unknown interactors for LPHNs, we used the nematode C. elegans, which provides several advantages. Firstly, most conserved cell surface receptor families have fewer members in nematodes, allowing for less extensive screens to cover the putative interaction space. Secondly, there are only two C. elegans LPHN genes, lat-1 and lat-2 but only lat-1 mutants show major developmental phenotypes, circumventing potential redundancy issues as lat-2 appears unable to compensate for genetic loss of lat-1 (ref. 12). Thirdly, there is strong functional conservation between invertebrate and vertebrate LPHNs, as their known phenotypes in nematodes match those in mice, including embryonic lethality20-22. Lastly, embryonic development is visually and genetically tractable in C. elegans.We included the LAT-1 ectodomain in a protein interaction screen designed to discover extracellular interactions (Fig. 1a). The high-throughput assay, extracellular interactome assay (ECIA), uses an ELISA-like setup, where the Fc-tagged bait is captured on solid support and alkaline phosphatase (AP)-tagged prey report on binding to the bait using chromogenic AP substrates (Extended Data Fig. 1a)52-54. We tested LAT-1 against a library of 379 C. elegans ectodomains including protein domains known to be conserved and important in developmental functions across bilaterians55. The LAT-1–Fc bait captured TOL-1–AP prey with the highest significance across the entire library (Fig. 1b). Similarly, when TOL-1–Fc was used as bait, LAT-1–AP gave the strongest binding signal (Fig. 1c). TOL-1, the sole TLR in C. elegans, is a cell surface receptor with two extracellular leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domains, one transmembrane helix and a cytoplasmic Toll–interleukin receptor 1 receptor (TIR) domain (Fig. 1d)56. Overall, these results identified the TLR TOL-1 ECR as a binding partner for LAT-1 ECR.Existing studies on nematode lat-1 and tol-1 demonstrate that these two genes may have closely related developmental functions. We crystallized the complete ECR of TOL-1 containing both N-terminal and C-LRR domains in a complex with the lectin domain of LAT-1 and determined the crystal structure of the complex at 4.0-Å resolution (Table 1). The crystal structure of the TOL-1–LAT-1 lectin complex is a heterodimer with dimensions of ~125 Å × 45 Å × 96 Å (Fig. 2 and Extended Data Fig. 3). The TOL-1 ECR comprises a large semicircular N-LRR domain with 24 LRR repeats followed by a second but smaller C-LRR domain including five LRR repeats, similar to the structure of Drosophila Toll but with different LRR repeat numbers57. Each LRR domain is flanked by N-terminal and C-terminal caps. The C-LRR domain contains the LAT-1-binding site (Fig. 2a). The lectin domain of LAT-1 binds to the N-terminal cap of the C-LRR domain of TOL-1 and also makes several contacts with the LRR repeats within the C-LRR domain. Surprisingly, the interaction of the lectin domain with the convex surface of the TOL-1 C-LRR domain (Fig. 2) is different from other TLR–ligand complexes, where the ligand binding site of TLRs is usually located in the concave surface of the LRR region, including for the Drosophila Toll and Aedes aegypti Toll5A receptors57,58. X-ray crystallography reveals the TOL-1–LAT-1 complex topologyWe next set out to determine the high-resolution structure of TOL-1 in complex with LAT-1. We crystallized the complete ECR of TOL-1 containing both N-terminal and C-LRR domains in a complex with the lectin domain of LAT-1 and determined the crystal structure of the complex at 4.0-Å resolution (Table 1). The TOL-1 ECR comprises a large semicircular N-LRR domain with 24 LRR repeats followed by a second but smaller C-LRR domain including five LRR repeats, similar to the structure of Drosophila Toll but with different LRR repeat numbers57. The LAT-1 lectin domain structure is similar to previously reported lectin domain structures in other aGPCRs36,3,59. While the interaction of vertebrate LPHNs with TENs and FLRTs are well studied and key for their neuronal functions36,38, the ligands for executing essential functions during early embryonic development remained unclear. On the other hand, Toll receptors are a well-studied receptor family with critical roles in innate immunity in both vertebrates and invertebrates. Furthermore, despite their requirement for normal embryonic development, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. TLRs are critical immunological agents because of their important roles in innate immunity against invading pathogens48. TLR signaling involves a family of adaptor proteins that induce downstream protein kinases that then lead to the activation of transcription factors such as nuclear factor κB and the interferon-regulatory factor family. The key signaling domain of the TLR system is the TIR domain, which is located in the cytosolic face of each TLR. However, we do not rule out the possibility that cis binding may mediate some functions of this complex, as suggested by our analysis in the C. elegans nerve ring neuropile (Extended Data Fig. 8e). Supporting this possibility, in the Drosophila nervous system, Cirl (LPHN) and Toll8 (TLR) may interact in cis and in trans47.

Fourthly, TLRs are known to signal by dimerization upon ligand binding65: We did not observe TOL-1 dimers or TOL-1–LAT-1 tetramers in solution or among our cryo-EM particle classes but a plausible arrangement of TOL-1–LAT-1 oligomers is suggested within the lattice of crystals we report here (Extended Data Fig. 10b,c). While our solution data only reveal a 1:1 organization of the ECRs, our biochemical work did not use full-length molecules; therefore, we cannot make strong conclusions about the oligomeric (unliganded or liganded) state of TOL-1. We also did not identify a signaling pathway or mode of action for the TOL-1–LAT-1 complex, as both receptors can act through several pathways or by cell–cell adhesion only46.

Lastly, our study poses the question of whether LPHN–TLR interactions are present in other taxa. In humans, there are three LPHNs and ten TLRs and it is possible that specific sets of human LPHNs and TLRs may interact. It is also possible that the Drosophila LPHN, Cirl, not only interacts with Toll8, as recently proposed by Lavalou et al.46 and Scholz et al.47, but also with other paralogs among the nine-member fly TLR family. Using sequence alignments, we observed that the unique cysteine-rich extension of the C-LRR domain’s N-terminal cap, an important feature of the LAT-1–TOL-1 interaction, is strongly conserved in four of the Drosophila TLRs, including Toll8 (Extended Data Fig. 10d). Wormbase66 also reports that TOL-1 is related to several human genes including Slits, CHAD (chondroadherin), ELFN2 (extracellular LRR and fibronectin type III domain-containing 2) and TRIL (TLR4 interactor with LRRs). Further studies will reveal whether an orthologous interaction exists in vertebrates.

In summary, our data suggest that the direct interaction of the ECRs of LAT-1a and TOL-1 is key for embryo development in C. elegans. LAT-1 and TOL-1 likely exert these functions through transcellular interactions that may activate downstream signaling pathways of LAT-1 and TOL-1. We present the structure of this complex, which reveals an unexpected molecular architecture not previously observed in interactions of related binding modules. Because there are three LPHNs, ten TLRs and numerous LRR-containing homologous proteins in mammals, it seems likely that the transcellular regulatory mechanisms critical for early development observed here may broadly apply to other organisms. This work provides the foundation to reveal unknown axes of cellular signaling and coordination between two receptor families in the developing embryo, pioneering the identification of homologous pathways in mammals.

Image processing and 3D reconstructionStack images were subjected to beam-induced motion correction using MotionCor77. Contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters for each micrograph were determined by CTFFIND4 (ref. 78). In total, 1,492,070 particle projections were selected using automated particle picking and subjected to reference-free 2D classification to discard false-positive particles or particles categorized in poorly defined classes, resulting in 1,346,788 particle projections for further processing. Subsequent 2D classification, multiclass ab initio reconstruction, heterogenous 3D refinement and nonuniform refinement were performed. The map outputs from RELION were deposited to the EM Data Bank (EMDB).

C. elegans brood size and viability assaysThree to five L4 stage-matched hermaphrodite larvae of each genotype were placed on separate 35-mm plates spotted with OP50 or E. coli HT115(DE3). Hermaphrodites were allowed to lay embryos at 20 °C for 24 h. After the 24-h period, adult worms were removed from plates and total progeny were counted at least twice and the average was divided by the number of L4 hermaphrodites originally plated to arrive at brood size number. Progeny at 24 h were also scored into two categories: embryos or larvae. Embryonic survival is expressed as a percentage and was calculated as the number of hatched larvae at 48 h (24 h after adult removal) divided by the total number of embryos counted at 24 h. Larval survival is expressed as a percentage and was calculated as the number of adults counted at 96 h divided by the total number of hatched larvae counted at 48 h. In a minority of cases, the number of larvae exceeded the embryo counts or the number of adults exceeded the larvae counts within the same strain and trial, resulting in survival percentages over 100%. This is likely because of minor counting errors. For transparency, Figs. 5e,f and 6d,e display the raw percentages calculated as described above but survival percentages over 100% are interpreted as 100% here. Brood size and viability assays were repeated at least twice by two independent observers.
