CENP-E (centromere protein E) and CENP-F (centromere protein F), also known as
mitosin, are large, multi-functional proteins associated with the outer
kinetochore. CENP-E features a well-characterized kinesin motor domain at its
N-terminus and a second microtubule-binding domain at its C-terminus of unknown
function. CENP-F is important for the formation of proper
kinetochore-microtubule attachment and, similar to CENP-E, contains two
microtubule-binding domains at its termini. While the importance of these
proteins is known, the details of their interactions with microtubules have not
yet been investigated. We have biochemically and structurally characterized the
microtubule-binding properties of the amino- and carboxyl-terminal domains of
CENP-F as well as the carboxyl-terminal (non-kinesin) domain of CENP-E. CENP-E's
C-terminus and CENP-F's N-terminus bind microtubules with similar affinity to
the well-characterized Ndc80 complex, while CENP-F's C-terminus shows much lower
affinity. Electron microscopy analysis reveals that all of these domains engage
the microtubule surface in a disordered manner, suggesting that these factors
have no favored binding geometry and may allow for initial side-on attachments
early in mitosis.

Introduction

The kinetochore constitutes the region of chromosome-microtubule interaction. It is responsible for the alignment of chromosomes along the metaphase plate and the segregation of sister chromatids into daughter cells during anaphase [1,2]. A major component of the kinetochore essential for microtubule attachments is the highly conserved KMN-network, consisting of the KNL-1 complex, the Mis12 complex, and the NDC80 complex [3]. The Dam1 complex in yeast and the Ska complex in higher eukaryotes, both of which cooperate with the KMN network [4,5,6], are additionally required for the formation of robust kinetochore–microtubule attachments. However, in metazoans two additional outer kinetochore proteins, CENP-E (Centromere Protein E) and CENP-F, also known as mitosin, have also been shown to be critical for this process [7,8,9,10].

CENP-E is the better characterized of these two proteins. It was discovered over two decades ago when it was shown to have an essential role in progression from metaphase to anaphase [11]. This340kDa coiled-coil protein accumulates during G2, associates with kinetochores during M-phase, and is degraded shortly thereafter [12]. It was later found to regulate the spindle assembly checkpoint, which causes cell cycle arrest when kinetochores do not form stable interactions with microtubules [13], by binding and silencing Bub1, an important member of this checkpoint [14]. When CENP-E is depleted in vivo, cells lack robust microtubule-kinetochore interaction [8]. Although in these cases the number of kinetochore-microtubule attachments is normal, the kinetochores are unable to produce sufficient force to generate proper tension between sister kinetochores [7].

CENP-E contains three distinct domains (Fig. 1A): a well-characterized N-terminal kinesin domain [15,16], a central coiled-coil domain, and a C-terminal domain that is required for kinetochore localization [17] and possesses microtubule-binding activity [18], which we refer to here as ‘EC’. The plus-end directed motion of the kinesin domain has been implicated in chromosome congression [19], while the role of the EC domain is not entirely clear. One model of CENP-E’s function invokes an intramolecular interaction between the kinesin and EC domains, where in EC inhibits the motor activity of the kinesin domain until phosphorylated by MPS1 or CDK1-cyclin B early in mitosis [20]. An opposing model does not invoke any intramolecular interaction, focusing on CENP-E’s kinesin domain providing the power necessary to congress chromosomes during prometaphase, while the rest of the protein acts as a tether to kinetochores [16]. Neither of these models has considered the role of EC in microtubule interaction.

CENP-F was also discovered about two decades ago [21]. It is a large 350 kDa coiled-coil protein that remains at low levels during S phase but accumulates during G2 in the nucleus and nuclear envelope, followed by relocalization to kinetochores at the G2/M transition [10]. CENP-F remains associated with kinetochores until anaphase, when it localizes to the spindle midzone and degrades thereafter [22,23]. Several reports [9,24,25,26] have shown that CENP-F is important for kinetochore-microtubule attachment. The depletion of CENP-F in various cell types results in an extended mitotic delay and decreased tension between microtubule-associated kinetochores [26]. CENP-F is also required for proper operation of the spindle assembly checkpoint, as its depletion results in a failure to retain spindle checkpoint proteins BubR1 and Mad1 at kinetochores [24]. Clinically, CENP-F is used as a proliferation marker in several cancers, including lung cancer, breast cancer, node-negative breast cancer, Non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and Mantle Cell lymphoma [27]. Its expression has a positive correlation with tumor size in node-negative breast cancer [28].

CENP-F has been reported to contain two microtubule-binding domains, one ateach terminus of the protein, of unknown function (Fig. 1A)[29]. We refer to the domain at the N-terminus as ‘FN’, and that at the C-terminus as ‘FC’. CENP-F’s primary function has remained elusive, though one study proposed that CENP-F plays a role in microtubule nucleation at the centrosome, inconsistent with its kinetochore localization [30]. CENP-E has been shown to be closely associated with the activity of CENP-F, and has been implicated as a potential interacting partner [17,31].

Despite their discovery two decades ago, the structures and microtubule-binding properties of CENP-F’s two MT-binding domains and CENP-E’s EC domain have not been well-characterized. We have employed both a biochemical approach and electron microscopy to characterize the interaction of these domains with microtubules in vitro.

In order to further characterize the nature of the microtubule interaction with the CENP domains, we cleaved the C-terminus of tubulin from polymerized microtubules using the protease subtilisin, and assayed the effect on CENP binding (Fig. 9A). The CENP-E C-terminus promotes tubulin polymerizationWhen observing EC incubated with paclitaxel microtubules, single microtubules decorated with EC were extremely rare, as the domain demonstrated an extreme propensity to bundle microtubules (Fig. 7A), in line with a proposed role in microtubule cross-linking in vivo [18]. Neither FN nor FC produced extensive MT bundling, suggesting that CENP-F does not cross-link microtubules in vivo. To directly examine the effect of EC on tubulin dynamics, we carried out a microtubule polymerization assay (Fig. 8C). While tubulin alone shows a moderate increase in signal over time, due to self-assembly, the addition of EC dramatically enhances the kinetics of polymerization, eliminating the lag phase. The total turbidity signal increases substantially as well, presumably due to extensive microtubule bundling, as we observed in our EM experiments. We therefore conclude that EC has a positive impact on tubulin polymerization.

Given the extended character of this tubulin tail, factors that bind exclusively via this moiety, without engaging the globular domain of tubulin, are generally flexibly attached to the MT lattice. In order to further characterize the nature of the microtubule interaction with the CENP domains, we cleaved the C-terminus of tubulin from polymerized microtubules using the protease subtilisin, and assayed the effect on CENP binding (Fig. 9A). As previously reported [18], and despite the disordered nature of the interaction we observed, EC binding is relatively insensitive to subtilisin treatment, showing only a 20% reduction in binding to cleaved microtubules (Fig. 9B,C). DiscussionAlthough EC and CENP-F’s terminal domains have been recognized as direct microtubule-binding factors for some time, the importance of this activity relative to the other roles of these large, multipurpose proteins in generating robust kinetochore-microtubule attachments has remained unknown. Our finding that both EC and FN bind microtubules with comparable affinity to the Ndc80 complex, the primary microtubule-binding arm of the KMN network, suggests that these domains could make a substantial contribution to microtubule engagement by metazoan kinetochores. In particular, these results suggest that CENP-F depletion phenotypes may not solely be explained by the resultant inability of dynein to localize to kinetochores through the Nde1 pathway [48].Several lines of evidence highlight the similarities between EC and FN despite the absence of any detectable sequence homology. Structurally, they are very similar in size, show an almost identical elution profile in our analytical gel filtration experiments, possess very similar CD spectra, and display a similar pattern of predicted coiled-coil content at their N-terminal ends. Additionally, their microtubule binding properties are strikingly similar: both have submicromolar affinity for microtubules and form a disordered interaction with the microtubule surface despite the relatively minor contribution of the disordered tubulin E hooks to binding. Cumulatively, these results suggest that these two domains may belong to a class of microtubule-associated proteins (MAPs) that have evolved to make flexible but strong connections to microtubules without competing with traditional MAPs for E hook binding sites. Further studies will be required to investigate if the two domains possess similar three-dimensional structures and to probe their ability to bind microtubules in the presence of other MAPs.CENP-F and CENP-E are two of the earliest factors to localize to kinetochores that are not constitutively associated with centromeres [10,17]. Immuno-EM and high-resolution fluorescence studies have demonstrated that both are localized to the outermost kinetochore layer (i.e. furthest away from the inner centromere) [22,36,49], consistent with them being in the fibrous corona region, along with other microtubule-binding factors such as dynein [50], earlier in mitosis [51]. It is thus tempting to speculate that CENP-E’s C-terminus and CENP-F are involved in making side-on kinetochore-microtubule attachments early in mitosis, facilitating chromosome congression, a process in which the CENP-E kinesin domain has previously been implicated [19]. The absence of a preferred microtubule-binding geometry as well as the ability to bind in the presence of traditional MAPs would be well suited for this role. Investigating the phenotypic consequences of CENP proteins with specific lesions in their MT-binding domains in vivo would serve to test this hypothesis.

CloningThe constructs presented in this study consist of amino acids 2305-2663 of CENP-E (EC) and amino acids 1-385 (FN) and 3023-3210 (FC) of CENP-F (all human sequences).
