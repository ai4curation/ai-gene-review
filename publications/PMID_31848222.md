## Full Text

Abstract

ABC transporters couple ATP hydrolysis to the transport of substrates across cellular membranes. This protein superfamily has diverse activities resulting from differences in their cargo and subcellular localization. Our work investigates the role of the ABCG family member WHT-2 in the biogenesis of gut granules, a Caenorhabditis elegans lysosome-related organelle. In addition to being required for the accumulation of birefringent material within gut granules, WHT-2 is necessary for the localization of gut granule proteins when trafficking pathways to this organelle are partially disrupted. The role of WHT-2 in gut granule protein targeting is likely linked to its function in Rab GTPase localization. We show that WHT-2 promotes the gut granule association of the Rab32 family member GLO-1 and the endolysosomal RAB-7 , identifying a novel function for an ABC transporter. WHT-2 localizes to gut granules where it could play a direct role in controlling Rab localization. Loss of CCZ-1 and GLO-3 , which likely function as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for GLO-1 , lead to similar disruption of GLO-1 localization. We show that CCZ-1 , like GLO-3 , is localized to gut granules. WHT-2 does not direct the gut granule association of the GLO-1 GEF and our results point to WHT-2 functioning differently than GLO-3 and CCZ-1 . Point mutations in WHT-2 that inhibit its transport activity, but not its subcellular localization, lead to the loss of GLO-1 from gut granules, while other WHT-2 activities are not completely disrupted, suggesting that WHT-2 functions in organelle biogenesis through transport-dependent and transport-independent activities.

Keywords: lysosome-related organelle, Rab GTPase, ABC transporter, membrane trafficking, Caenorhabditis elegans

CELL-type-specific elaboration of the conventional endocytic pathway leads to the generation of lysosome-related organelles (LROs). Well-studied LROs include mammalian melanosomes, mammalian platelet alpha and dense granules, Drosophila retinal pigment granules, and Caenorhabditis elegans gut granules ( Bowman et al. 2019 ). LROs share contents, such as the V-ATPase and LAMPs, with conventional lysosomes. However, they often lack degradative hydrolases and instead harbor factors that endow each type of LRO with specialized functions. Despite having diverse contents, morphologies, and activities, most LROs utilize the same evolutionarily conserved machinery for their biogenesis. Some of these biogenesis factors have functions that are dedicated to LRO formation, while others also act within the conventional endolysosomal system ( Bultema and Di Pietro 2013 ; Marks et al. 2013 ; Bowman et al. 2019 ). Defects in the factors promoting LRO formation underlie rare human diseases, including Hermansky–Pudlak and Chediak–Higashi syndromes ( Huizing et al. 2008 ).

C. elegans gut granules are intestinal cell-restricted LROs that are generated early during embryonic development and persist into adulthood ( Laufer et al. 1980 ; Clokey and Jacobson 1986 ; Bossinger and Schierenberg 1992 ; Hermann et al. 2005 ). They contain autofluorescent derivatives of anthranilic acid ( Coburn et al. 2013 ), as well as rhabditin ( Cobb 1914 ), an optically active birefringent material whose molecular identity is currently unknown. Gut granules function in metal and metabolite storage ( Roh et al. 2012 ; Lee et al. 2015 ; Chun et al. 2017 ), anabolic reactions ( Panda et al. 2017 ), and life span regulation ( Folick et al. 2015 ). Gut granule formation requires the function of the Rab32 family member GLO-1 , and the AP-3 ( Hermann et al. 2005 ), BLOC-1 ( Hermann et al. 2012 ), and HOPS complexes ( Delahaye et al. 2014 ). A BLOC-3-related complex comprising CCZ-1 and GLO-3 likely acts as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF), activating GLO-1 and promoting its gut granule association ( Delahaye et al. 2014 ; Morris et al. 2018 ). In addition to these factors, whose homologs function in the formation of mammalian and Drosophila LROs ( Bowman et al. 2019 ), we have identified a role for three different ABC transporters in the biogenesis of gut granules ( Currie et al. 2007 ; Schroeder et al. 2007 ).

ABC transporters represent a superfamily of conserved proteins that often couple ATP hydrolysis to the movement of ions, lipids, sugars, and peptides across cell membranes ( Holland et al. 2003 ). ABC transporters localize to diverse cellular membranes, including conventional lysosomes and LROs ( Ridsdale et al. 2011 ; Chapel et al. 2013 ), and there is evidence that they play a role in the formation of both types of organelles. ABC transporters in yeast function in homotypic lysosome fusion and exhibit direct physical interactions with the membrane trafficking machinery, including subunits of the HOPS complex ( Paumi et al. 2009 ; Sasser et al. 2012 , 2013 ; Snider et al. 2013 ). In Dictyostelium , ABC transporters promote trafficking through the conventional endolysosomal pathway ( Brazill et al. 2001 ). The biogenesis of lamellar bodies and melanosomes, LROs found within human lung alveolar cells and melanocytes require the function of ABC transporters ( Cheong et al. 2007 ; Bergam et al. 2018 ). In C. elegans , the MRP-4 , PGP-2 , and WHT-2 ABC transporters function in the formation of gut granules ( Currie et al. 2007 ; Schroeder et al. 2007 ). MRP-4 also affects the size and function of conventional lysosomes ( Schaheen et al. 2006 ; Huynh et al. 2016 ).

WHT-2 is an ABCG subfamily member that is homologous to the Drosophila White protein ( Sheps et al. 2004 ). Similar to White, which transports pigment precursors into retinal pigment granules ( Sullivan and Sullivan 1975 ; O’hare et al. 1984 ), WHT-2 is required for the accumulation of birefringent material within gut granules ( Currie et al. 2007 ). However, genetic interactions between wht-2 and gut granule biogenesis mutants suggest that WHT-2 has a broader role in the formation of gut granules ( Currie et al. 2007 ; Levitte et al. 2010 ). Here, we show that WHT-2 has significant functions in gut granule biogenesis that extend beyond the accumulation of birefringent material. Notably, our work indicates that WHT-2 functions in the localization of Rab GTPases, a novel activity for an ABC transporter.

Genetic manipulations

Standard genetic tests showed that wht-2 ( ok2775 ) and wht-2 ( gk891224 ) led to recessive zygotic effect defects in birefringent gut granule formation. Extrachromosomal ( Ex ) and integrated ( Is ) transgenes, present in otherwise wild-type strains, were moved into mutant backgrounds by crossing males homozygous or heterozygous for the mutation with hermaphrodites containing the transgenes. The mutation in the resulting strain was confirmed by the presence of the mutant phenotype, PCR, and/or DNA sequencing. To generate wht-2 ( ok2775 ) double mutants, the progeny of transheterozygous individuals homozygous for wht-2 ( ok2775 ) , as evidenced by the number of birefringent gut granules, and heterozygous for the other mutation, were isolated. When generating double mutants between wht-2 ( ok2775 ) and apt-7( tm920 ) , glo-2 ( tm592 ) , and lyst-1 ( gk295717 ) , we used the alteration in autofluorescent gut granule number and morphology exhibited by the apt-7(−) , glo-2 (−) , and lyst-1 (−) mutants to identify individuals homozygous for wht-2 (−) and heterozygous for the other mutation. The homozygous double mutants, which exhibited the other mutant phenotype, were then isolated from these strains. In cases where one Glo phenotype masked another, we confirmed the presence of the masked allele by PCR or DNA sequencing. Single and double mutants were homozygous for each mutation except strains containing cup-5 ( zu223 ) and some strains containing ccz-1 ( ok2182 ) , which were kept heterozygous due to the maternal effect lethality or severe growth defect caused by these mutations ( Hersh et al. 2002 ; Nieto et al. 2010 ). In strains heterozygous for ccz-1 , we identified homozygous mutant adults by the presence of large DIC refractile granules within their embryos ( Nieto et al. 2010 ). unc-36 ( e251 ) linked to cup-5 (−) was used to identify homozygous parents.

wht-2 ( gk891224 ) was identified in an ongoing screen of Million Mutation strains for defects in birefringent gut granule number. gk891224 did not complement the Glo phenotypes of wht-2 ( ok2775 ) and the wht-2 mutation was backcrossed three times to N2 before being characterized. Results

Formation of birefringent gut granules in wht-2(−) null mutants

We previously identified wht-2 in a screen for ABC proteins functioning in the formation of birefringent gut granules. wht-2 (RNAi) causes a reduction in the number of birefringent organelles but does not cause any other obvious defects in gut granule formation ( Currie et al. 2007 ), possibly as a result of incomplete penetrance of wht-2 (RNAi) . Since our initial characterization, two different mutations predicted to disrupt wht-2 (+) activity have been isolated (C. elegans Deletion Mutant Consortium 2012; Thompson et al. 2013 ). ABC transporters have two essential functional domains: an ATPase domain that powers membrane transport, and a transmembrane domain, consisting of six membrane spanning alpha helices, that mediates substrate binding and transport ( Ford and Beis 2019 ). The wht-2 ( ok2775 ) deletion removes a substantial portion of the transmembrane domain of WHT-2 , and the wht-2 ( gk891224 ) point mutation introduces a stop codon prior to both the ATPase and transmembrane domains of WHT-2 ( Figure 1A ). Both mutations are expected to abolish WHT-2 function, and thus, likely represent null alleles. Similar to wht-2 (RNAi) , both wht-2 (−) alleles showed decreased numbers of birefringent granules in pretzel stage embryos ( Figure 1B and Table 1 ). This phenotype was rescued in many wht-2 ( ok2775 ) mutant embryos by the introduction of extrachromosomal arrays containing wht-2 (+) on a fosmid, as well as a PCR product containing just wht-2 (+) and adjacent regulatory sequences ( Table 1 ). The proportion of embryos that were rescued corresponds to the proportion of progeny receiving the array from their parent, indicating that wht-2 function promotes the formation of birefringent granules.

Pretzel stage wht-2 ( gk891224 ) and wht-2 ( ok2775 ) mutant embryos exhibited wide variation in the number of birefringent granules, ranging from none to nearly wild-type levels ( Table 1 ). To investigate what caused this variability, we carried out a time course analysis of birefringent granule number in individual embryos. Our scoring started at the 1.5-fold stage, immediately prior to the pretzel stage, which spans the last 5 hr of embryogenesis. Wild-type embryos always displayed >50 birefringent intestinal granules at each of the time points ( Figure 1C ). All of the wht-2 ( ok2775 ) embryos initially lacked birefringent material and the proportion of embryos lacking birefringent granules gradually decreased, such that by the end of embryogenesis, the majority of the embryos contained between 1 and 50 birefringent intestinal granules ( Figure 1C ). Near the end of embryogenesis, birefringent material often became mislocalized to the intestinal lumen in the wht-2 (−) mutants ( Figure 1, B and C ). These results indicate that variation in birefringent gut granule number in pretzel stage wht-2 (−) embryos is caused by a developmentally linked delay in their formation.

wht-2(−) is the only ABCG transporter mutant that causes defects in the formation of birefringent gut granules

WHT-2 is a member of the ABCG subfamily of ABC transporters ( Sheps et al. 2004 ). Like many other ABCG proteins, it is a half-transporter and likely must homo- or heterodimerize with another ABCG protein to function as a transporter ( Tarr et al. 2009 ). Strains with deletions predicted to disrupt the function of each of the other seven ABCG genes encoded by the C. elegans genome ( wht-1 and wht-3 to wht-8 ) did not show any alteration in the number of birefringent gut granules resembling wht-2 (−) (Supplemental Material, Table S1). Although we cannot rule out the possibility that multiple ABCG transporters function redundantly to promote gut granule formation, we think it is unlikely that WHT-2 heterodimerizes with another ABCG transporter to generate birefringent gut granules.

WHT-2 has a partially redundant role in gut granule biogenesis

wht-2 (−) mutants accumulate birefringent material within the intestinal lumen ( Figure 1B ). To date, all of the mutants that mislocalize birefringent material to the intestinal lumen have been shown to lack or have significantly altered gut granules ( Levitte et al. 2010 ; Hermann et al. 2012 ; Delahaye et al. 2014 ). However, wht-2 (−) mutants often contain birefringent material within their intestinal cells ( Figure 1B and Table 1 ). To investigate whether this material accumulates within gut granules or within a different organelle, we combined wht-2 (−) with mutants that do not generate gut granules. Double mutants containing wht-2 (−) and ccz-1 (−) , glo-1 (−) , glo-3 (−) , or vps-18 (−) had significantly fewer birefringent granules within embryonic intestinal cells than wht-2 (−) single mutants ( Table 1 ), strongly suggesting that birefringent material accumulates within gut granules in wht-2 (−) mutants.

We examined whether gut granules were present within wht-2 (−) mutant embryos by analyzing the localization of PGP-2 , a ABCB subfamily transporter, and CDF-2 , a Zn transporter, both of which exclusively associate with gut granule membranes ( Schroeder et al. 2007 ; Davis et al. 2009 ). The number and morphology of PGP-2 - and CDF-2 ::GFP-marked compartments and the colocalization of these two proteins was similar in wht-2 (−) mutants and wild type ( Figure 2, A and F ). LMP-1 , the C. elegans LAMP-1 ortholog ( Kostich et al. 2000 ), is associated with gut granules and conventional lysosomes ( Hermann et al. 2012 ). Its colocalization with PGP-2 was also unaffected by loss of wht-2 function ( Figure 2, B and G ). When gut granule protein localization pathways are disrupted, CDF-2 ::GFP mistrafficks through the endolysosomal pathway and accumulates at conventional lysosomes ( Hermann et al. 2012 ). We analyzed the colocalization of CDF-2 ::GFP with an mCherry-tagged form of ASM-1 , a lysosomal acid sphingomyelinase ( Lin et al. 1998 ; Guo and Wang 2010 ; Levitte et al. 2010 ), in wht-2 (−) mutants and found that CDF-2 ::GFP was not mislocalized to conventional lysosomes ( Figure 2, C and H ). Furthermore, CDF-2 ::GFP did not accumulate on RAB-5 containing early endosomes or RAB-7 -labeled endolysosomes in wht-2 (−) mutants (Figure S1).

Figure 2.

Gut granules contain lipases that function in lipid metabolism and gut granules are stained by the lipophilic dye Nile Red ( Schroeder et al. 2007 ; O’rourke and Ruvkun 2013 ). Because of the H + transport activity of the V-ATPase, gut granules are also marked by the acidophilic dye Acridine Orange ( Clokey and Jacobson 1986 ; Oka and Futai 2000 ; Hermann et al. 2005 ). Staining with these markers of gut granule activity was not disrupted by wht-2 ( ok2775 ) ( Figure 2, D and E ).

Together these data indicate that gut granules are generated independently of WHT-2 function, albeit with defects in the formation and localization of birefringent material. However, our results do not rule out the possibility that WHT-2 promotes processes that are complementary to, or redundant with, other pathways mediating gut granule biogenesis. To determine whether WHT-2 makes contributions to LRO biogenesis that are only apparent when gut granule formation is partially disrupted, we analyzed the number of birefringent gut granules in double mutants between wht-2 ( ok2775 ) and apt-7( tm920 ) , glo-2 ( tm592 ) , glo-3 ( kx38 ) , and lyst-1 ( gk295717 ) mutants. Malformed gut granules containing birefringent material are present in apt-7( tm920 ) mutants ( Hermann et al. 2012 ), which lack AP-3 complex function; glo-2 ( tm592 ) mutants, which partially disrupt the function of the BLOC-1 complex ( Hermann et al. 2012 ); glo-3 ( kx38 ) mutants, which partially disrupt the function of the proposed GEF for the GLO-1 Rab ( Morris et al. 2018 ); and lyst-1 ( gk295717 ) mutants, which disrupt the function of the C. elegans homolog of LYST and NBEAL2, and are mutated in Chediak–Higashi and gray platelet syndrome, respectively ( Barrett and Hermann 2016 ) ( Table 1 ). Notably, the addition of wht-2 (−) to each of these mutants led to significantly fewer birefringent gut granules than the single mutants ( Table 1 ).

To determine whether wht-2 (−) only altered the formation of birefringent material, we analyzed the distribution of gut granule proteins in the wht-2 (−) containing double mutants. We found that gut granule proteins were mislocalized when wht-2 (−) was combined with lyst-1 (−) and glo-3 (−) . CDF-2 ::GFP nearly always colocalized with PGP-2 in wht-2 (−) and lyst-1 (−) single mutants ( Figure 2, A and F ). However, in the double mutant, CDF-2 ::GFP became mislocalized to the plasma membrane and to organelles lacking PGP-2 , which are likely to be conventional lysosomes based on their apical position, morphology, and number ( Figure 2A ). The localization of LMP-1 to PGP-2 -marked gut granules was normal in wht-2 (−) mutants, partially disrupted in glo-3 ( kx38 ) mutants, and LMP-1 was rarely detected on PGP-2 compartments in wht-2 (−) ; glo-3 ( kx38 ) double mutants ( Figure 2, B and G ). wht-2 (−) also led to a significant effect on glo-3 ( kx38 ) gut granule size. Although glo-3 ( kx38 ) , and to a lesser extent wht-2 (−) , caused enlarged gut granules, the diameter of gut granules in the double mutant was wild type ( Figure 2, B and I ).

The restoration of birefringent material in wht-2 (−) mutants as development proceeds led us to analyze the effects of wht-2 (−) on gut granules in later stage animals. Gut granule numbers increase during larval development, and gut granules in adult intestinal cells are filled with autofluorescent material ( Clokey and Jacobson 1986 ; Hermann et al. 2005 ). The number, distribution, and morphology of autofluorescent gut granules in adults were not obviously affected by either wht-2 (−) mutation ( Figure 2J and Table 2 ). The autofluorescent material present in wht-2 (−) adults is likely contained within gut granules, as it was lacking when wht-2 (−) was combined with mutations in ccz-1 (−) , glo-1 (−) , and glo-3 (−) , which are required for the formation of adult gut granules ( Table 2 ). To determine whether WHT-2 plays a role in adult gut granule formation that is masked by the function of other factors, we combined wht-2 (−) with gut granule biogenesis mutants that retain autofluorescent gut granules. We observed significant reductions in adult autofluorescent gut granule number when wht-2 ( ok2775 ) was combining with mutations in lyst-1 , glo-3 , and the HOPS subunit vps-18 ( Delahaye et al. 2014 ) ( Figure 2J and Table 2 ). For example, while both wht-2 (−) and vps-18 (−) single mutants always displayed wild-type numbers of autofluorescent adult gut granules, many wht-2 (−) ; vps-18 (−) double mutants had few autofluorescent compartments ( Figure 2J and Table 2 ). Based on our analysis of double mutants containing wht-2 (−) , we conclude that WHT-2 makes important contributions to gut granule biogenesis that extend beyond the generation of birefringent material and that WHT-2 functions overlap with the activity of conserved factors that promote LRO biogenesis.

wht-2(−) does not disrupt conventional endosome biogenesis

Many of the proteins that function in the biogenesis of LROs also act in the formation of conventional lysosomes ( Dell’angelica 2009 ; Bultema and Di Pietro 2013 ; Marks et al. 2013 ). We used a number of different assays to investigate whether WHT-2 had a significant role in the biogenesis of lysosomes. Defects in trafficking to conventional lysosomes lead to an increased number of LMP-1 ::GFP-marked endolysosomes ( Xiao et al. 2009 ; Delahaye et al. 2014 ), which we did not observe in wht-2 (−) mutants ( Figure 3, A and B ). Similarly, neither the morphology nor position of RAB-5 -labeled early endosomes and RAB-7 -labeled endolysosomes were obviously changed by disrupting wht-2 (+) function ( Figure 3A and Figure S1). RAB-7 dynamically associates with endolysosomes, and defects in trafficking to lysosomes can alter its steady-state distribution relative to LMP-1 ::GFP ( Chotard et al. 2010 ; Poteryaev et al. 2010 ). Approximately 50% of LMP-1 ::GFP containing endolysosomes were marked by RAB-7 in wild-type embryos, and this was not significantly altered in wht-2 (−) mutants ( Figure 3, A and C ). cup-5 (+) , which encodes a TRPML1 homolog disrupted in mucolipidosis type IV, promotes the reformation of lysosomes following their fusion with late endosomes ( Fares and Greenwald 2001 ; Hersh et al. 2002 ; Treusch et al. 2004 ). As a result, cup-5 ( zu223 ) mutants display enlarged LMP-1 -marked endolysosomes ( Figure 3, D and E ). Attenuating the delivery of material to lysosomes can suppress cup-5 ( zu223 ) , leading to smaller endolysosomes ( Nakae et al. 2010 ), providing an assay for lysosomal trafficking in embryonic intestinal cells ( Morris et al. 2018 ). To further validate the assay, we examined mrp-4 (−) , which reduces the size of Lysotracker stained organelles in cup-5 ( zu223 ) mutants ( Schaheen et al. 2006 ). mrp-4 , like wht-2 , encodes an ABC transporter that is required for the accumulation of birefringent material within gut granules ( Currie et al. 2007 ). We found that combining mrp-4 ( cd8 ) with cup-5 ( zu223 ) led to a significant reduction in endolysosome diameter ( Figure 3, D and E ), consistent with a role for MRP-4 in the formation of conventional lysosomes. In contrast, endolysosomes remained enlarged in cup-5 (−) ; wht-2 (−) double mutants, and while being slightly smaller than cup-5 (−) single mutants ( Figure 3, D and E ), their diameter was similar to what has been seen in other gut granule biogenesis mutants ( Morris et al. 2018 ). Together, these data strongly suggest that WHT-2 does not make significant contributions to conventional lysosome biogenesis.

WHT-2 is expressed in intestinal cells and localizes to gut granules

Our phenotypic analyses point to a role for wht-2 (+) in gut granule, but not endolysosome biogenesis. Gut granules are only found in intestinal cells, while endolysosomes are present in most, if not all, C. elegans somatic cells. To determine which cells express wht-2 , we placed gfp under the control of the wht-2 promoter and found that GFP expression in embryos was limited to intestinal cells, first being detected when the intestinal primordium was composed of four cells (E4 stage) ( Figure 4A ). The activity of the wht-2 promoter persisted in intestinal cells through embryogenesis into adulthood ( Figure 4A ). In adults, the wht-2 promoter was also active in a few unidentified head neurons located near the pharynx ( Figure 4A ).

Figure 4.

To determine how directly WHT-2 might function in gut granule biogenesis, we generated two different anti- WHT-2 peptide antibodies and used them to analyze the subcellular distribution of WHT-2 . Both WHT-2 antisera labeled gut granules marked with CDF-2 ::GFP in embryos ( Figure 4, B and C and Figure S2) and WHT-2 was associated with gut granules in adult intestinal cells (Figure S3). The gut granule signal was lacking in both wht-2 ( ok2775 ) and wht-2 ( gk891224 ) mutants, indicating the specificity of both antisera toward WHT-2 ( Figure 4B and Figure S2).

WHT-2 promotes the gut granule localization of GLO-1

Our double mutant analysis showed that wht-2 and glo-3 have partially redundant functions in gut granule biogenesis ( Figure 2, B and G , Table 1 , and Table 2 ). We have recently shown that GLO-3 promotes the association of GLO-1 with gut granules in embryonic intestinal cells, likely by functioning with CCZ-1 as a GLO-1 GEF ( Morris et al. 2018 ). We therefore analyzed whether wht-2 (−) mutants disrupted the subcellular distribution of GLO-1 . Rab GTPases cycle on and off membranes, which can lead to changes in their association with organelles. Our localization studies show the steady-state distribution of GLO-1 ; however, they do not measure GLO-1 dynamics and will not reveal low levels of GLO-1 organelle association. PGP-2 -marked gut granules were present in wht-2 (−) ( Figure 2, A and B ), allowing us to investigate the gut granule localization of GLO-1 in this mutant. In fixed wild-type embryos, ectopically expressed GFP:: GLO-1 , which can functionally replace GLO-1 ( Hermann et al. 2005 ), was enriched on gut granules ( Figure 5, A and F ). However, in wht-2 ( ok2775 ) mutants, GFP:: GLO-1 was rarely associated with PGP-2 containing gut granules and instead was enriched in the cytoplasm ( Figure 5, A and F ). A similar effect was seen with the wht-2 ( gk891224 ) allele ( Figure 5, A and F ), indicating that loss of wht-2 (+) function disrupts GFP:: GLO-1 localization. Using anti- GLO-1 antibodies, we found that endogenous GLO-1 was absent from gut granules in the wht-2 (−) mutant as well ( Figure 5, B and G ). To rule out the possibility that fixation used during antibody staining alters the association of GLO-1 with gut granules in wht-2 ( ok2775 ) mutants, we examined living embryos and found that GFP:: GLO-1 no longer colocalized with gut granule autofluorescence in wht-2 (−) mutants ( Figure 5, C and H ). Disrupting the function of MRP-4 , another ABC transporter that functions in the accumulation of birefringent material within gut granules ( Currie et al. 2007 ), did not result in the loss of GFP:: GLO-1 from gut granules (Figure S4), indicating that the loss of ABC transporter function or birefringent material per se does not disrupt the steady-state association of GLO-1 with gut granules. Together these data indicate that WHT-2 plays an essential role in the gut granule localization of GLO-1 .

Figure 5.

GFP:: GLO-1 , in addition to being dispersed throughout the cytoplasm, was often localized to small puncta in wht-2 (−) mutants ( Figure 5, A and D ). The morphology and distribution of the puncta are reminiscent of the Golgi, which in C. elegans intestinal cells are distributed throughout the cytoplasm in ministacks ( Chen et al. 2006 ; Grant and Sato 2006 ). We tested the possibility that GLO-1 was associating with the Golgi by examining the distribution of GFP:: GLO-1 relative to the Golgi marker AMAN-2 /Mannosidase (MANS::mCherry) ( Rolls et al. 2002 ). In wild type, GFP:: GLO-1 rarely colocalized with MANS::mCherry ( Figure 5, D and I ). In contrast, nearly all of the GFP:: GLO-1 puncta overlapped with the Golgi in wht-2 (−) mutants ( Figure 5, D and I ). The association of GFP:: GLO-1 with the Golgi was also seen in glo-3 ( kx94 ) mutants ( Figure 5, D and I ), which likely lack GLO-1 GEF activity ( Morris et al. 2018 ). These results suggest that the factors promoting the localization of GLO-1 to gut granules also prevent its enrichment on the Golgi.

wht-2 (−) disrupts the accumulation of birefringent material within embryonic gut granules, whereas adult gut granules in wht-2 (−) mutants do not display any obvious differences from wild type ( Figure 1B and Figure 2J ). To examine whether this might be linked to differences in GLO-1 localization, we analyzed the distribution of GFP:: GLO-1 relative to PGP-2 -marked gut granules in fixed adult intestinal cells. Similar to embryos, GFP:: GLO-1 was enriched on wild-type adult gut granules (Figure S3). However, wht-2 (−) mutants did not cause the loss of GFP:: GLO-1 from gut granules in adults (Figure S3). Therefore, wht-2 (+) is not essential for GLO-1 localization in adults as it is during embryogenesis, possibly due to the expression of factors within adults promoting the association of GLO-1 with gut granules that function redundantly with WHT-2 .

WHT-2 promotes the association of RAB-7 with gut granules in glo-3(kx38) mutants

The similar loss of GLO-1 from gut granules in wht-2 (−) and glo-3 (−) mutants led us to examine whether they exhibited any other phenotypic similarities. RAB-7 does not appreciably associate with gut granules in wild type; however, in glo-3 ( kx38 ) partial loss-of-function mutants, gut granules often misaccumulate RAB-7 ( Morris et al. 2018 ) ( Figure 5, E and J ). We found that neither endocytic Rab, RAB-7 , or RAB-5 associated with gut granules in wht-2 ( ok2775 ) mutants ( Figure 5, E and J and Figure S1). However, RAB-7 was absent from gut granules in glo-3 ( kx38 ) ; wht-2 ( ok2775 ) double mutants ( Figure 5, E and J ), indicating that WHT-2 promotes the association of RAB-7 with gut granules when GLO-3 activity is impaired. Notably, WHT-2 was associated with gut granules in glo-3 ( kx38 ) embryos where it could function in localizing RAB-7 ( Figure 4, B and C ).

GLO-3 and CCZ-1 localize to gut granules in wht-2(−) mutants

GEF localization in large part determines the targeting of a Rab to a specific cellular membrane ( Gerondopoulos et al. 2012 ; Blümer et al. 2013 ). WHT-2 is positioned on gut granules where it could direct the organelle association of GLO-1 and RAB-7 by localizing the GLO-1 and/or RAB-7 GEFs, respectively. We examined the localization of GLO-3 and CCZ-1 tagged with fluorescent proteins, focusing our analysis soon after the reporters are expressed to minimize potential artifacts from overexpression. The embryos analyzed had 4 (E4) or 8 (E8) intestinal precursors and immediately preceded the stage when we have scored Rab localization.

Consistent with our previous work, GLO-3 ::GFP, which functionally substitutes for GLO-3 ( Rabbitts et al. 2008 ), localized to PGP-2 -marked gut granules at both stages ( Figure 6, A, B, and E ). When WHT-2 function was lacking, GLO-3 remained localized to gut granules, albeit at slightly reduced levels compared to wild type ( Figure 6, A, B, and E ).

Figure 6.

CCZ-1 likely functions as part of the GEF that regulates both GLO-1 and RAB-7 ( Kinchen and Ravichandran 2010 ; Nieto et al. 2010 ; Delahaye et al. 2014 ; Morris et al. 2018 ). To determine whether CCZ-1 associates with gut granules, we examined the distribution of CCZ-1 ::YFP, which functionally replaces CCZ-1 in both conventional endolysosome and gut granule biogenesis ( Nieto et al. 2010 ; Delahaye et al. 2014 ). CCZ-1 was enriched on gut granules marked by PGP-2 in both E4 and E8 stage intestinal cells ( Figure 6, C, D, and F ). In wht-2 (−) mutants, we did not detect a difference in the level of CCZ-1 ::YFP association with gut granules at either stage ( Figure 6, C, D, and F ).

To determine whether the localization of the GLO-1 GEF is affected by disrupting the function of one of its subunits, we analyzed the distribution of CCZ-1 in glo-3 (−) mutants. Strong glo-3 (−) alleles lack gut granules, so we analyzed CCZ-1 ::YFP localization in glo-3 ( kx38 ) and glo-3 ( gk582755 ) partial loss-of-function alleles, which contain gut granules in 1.5-fold stage embryos ( Morris et al. 2018 ). PGP-2 -labeled gut granules were lacking from E4 stage intestinal cells in these mutants and CCZ-1 ::YFP was localized to the cytoplasm. At the E8 stage, gut granules were present, but they lacked CCZ-1 ::YFP. These results show that GLO-3 , unlike WHT-2 , is required for the association of CCZ-1 with gut granules, which is similar to the role that SAND-1 plays in localizing CCZ-1 to activate RAB-7 ( Nieto et al. 2010 ).

GLO-1(D132A) localizes to gut granules independently of WHT-2

GLO-3 and CCZ-1 physically associate and act as the GEF to activate and localize GLO-1 to gut granules ( Morris et al. 2018 ). If WHT-2 similarly functions in guanine nucleotide exchange, then GLO-1 would be GDP bound in wht-2 (−) mutants. The GLO-1 (D132A) point mutation, which is predicted to weaken the affinity of GLO-1 for guanine nucleotides and increase its rate of nucleotide exchange, partially bypasses the requirement of the GLO-1 GEF for the localization of GLO-1 to gut granules; in glo-3 (−) mutants, GLO-1 (+) is lacking from gut granules, whereas GLO-1 (D132A) can associate with these organelles ( Morris et al. 2018 ). While GLO-1 (+) was absent from gut granules in wht-2 (−) , GLO-1 (D132A) often colocalized with PGP-2 -marked gut granules in wht-2 (−) mutants ( Figure 7, A and B ). This indicates that GLO-1 (D132A) expression can partially bypass the requirement of WHT-2 in localizing GLO-1 , and suggests that the loss of GLO-1 from gut granules in wht-2 (−) mutants is due to defective GLO-1 activation. However, the level of association with gut granules was variable, ranging from moderate to very little, possibly due to differences in the amount of GLO-1 (D132A), which was expressed from an extrachromosomal array. On average, the localization of GLO-1 (D132A) to gut granules in wht-2 (−) mutants never reached the level seen when GLO-1 (D132A) was expressed in glo-1 (−) mutants ( Figure 7, A and B ), and instead was comparable to the level of association seen in glo-3 (−) and ccz-1 (−) mutants ( Figure 7, A and B ). These results indicate that while WHT-2 and the GLO-1 GEF are not absolutely necessary for the localization of GLO-1 (D132A), they do contribute to its association with gut granules.

Figure 7.

The similarities between wht-2 (−) and the GLO-1 GEF mutants were also seen in the effect of GLO-1 (D132A) expression on the gut granule localization of LMP-1 . Expression of GLO-1 (D132A), similar to GLO-1 (+), supported the association of LMP-1 with gut granules in glo-1 (−) mutants (Figure S5). In contrast, GLO-1 (D132A) did not support the steady state association of LMP-1 with gut granules in glo-3 (−) and ccz-1 (−) mutants ( Morris et al. 2018 ). Similarly, LMP-1 was reduced or lacking from gut granules in wht-2 (−) mutants expressing GLO-1 (D132A) (Figure S5). Although the GLO-1 fast exchange point mutant can functionally replace GLO-1 ( Morris et al. 2018 ), it is likely to act differently than its wild-type counterpart. We therefore think that the defect in LMP-1 trafficking in wht-2 (−) and GLO-1 GEF mutants results from GLO-1 (D132A) being expressed in mutant backgrounds that disrupt the association of GLO-1 with gut granules.

WHT-2 functions independently of GLO-3 and CCZ-1

Given the similar roles of WHT-2 and the GLO-1 GEF in GLO-1 localization, we further explored the functional relationships between these factors. GLO-1 (D132A) expression bypassed the requirement for glo-3 (+) and ccz-1 (+) in the formation of birefringent and PGP-2 -marked gut granules in embryos ( Figure 7A and Table 3 ) ( Morris et al. 2018 ). Consistent with GLO-3 and CCZ-1 functioning together as GLO-1 GEF to facilitate gut granule biogenesis in embryos, we found that GLO-1 (D132A) restored gut granules in glo-3 (−) ; ccz-1 (−) double mutants ( Figure 7A and Table 3 ). In contrast to the GLO-1 GEF mutants, the biogenesis of birefringent gut granules in wht-2 (−) embryos was not rescued by GLO-1 (D132A) expression ( Figure 7C and Table 3 ). Furthermore, in double mutants, wht-2 (−) disrupted the restoration of birefringent organelles by GLO-1 (D132A) in glo-3 (−) and ccz-1 (−) embryos ( Table 3 ). However, the restoration of PGP-2 -marked gut granules in glo-3 (−) and ccz-1 (−) mutants by GLO-1 (D132A) was not disrupted by the addition of wht-2 (−) ( Figure 7A ). These results suggest that WHT-2 functions independently in the formation of birefringent material and GLO-1 localization.

We also investigated the functional relationships between WHT-2 and the GLO-1 GEF subunits in adult gut granule formation. GLO-1 (D132A) expression restored autofluorescent gut granules in adult glo-3 (−) and ccz-1 (−) single mutants ( Figure 7D and Table 4 ) ( Delahaye et al. 2014 ). In contrast, glo-3 (−) ; ccz-1 (−) double mutant expressing GLO-1 (D132A) lacked autofluorescent gut granules ( Table 4 ), suggesting that GLO-3 and CCZ-1 have redundant functions in adults. Adult wht-2 (−) mutants displayed normal numbers of autofluorescent gut granules ( Figure 2J ), which were not significantly altered by the expression of the GLO-1 fast exchange mutant ( Table 4 ). GLO-1 (D132A) was unable to restore autofluorescent gut granules in glo-3 (−) ; wht-2 (−) and ccz-1 (−) ; wht-2 (−) double mutants ( Figure 7D and Table 4 ). wht-2 ( gk891224 ) showed a similar disruption of the rescuing activity of GLO-1 (D132A) in glo-3 (−) animals ( Table 4 ). Therefore, when CCZ-1 or GLO-3 activity is lacking, wht-2 (+) becomes necessary for the GLO-1 fast exchange mutant to promote the formation of autofluorescent gut granules. GLO-1 (D132A) restored autofluorescent gut granules in wht-2 ( ok2775 ) ; glo-1 ( zu437 ) , indicating that wht-2 (+) is dispensable for the activity of the GLO-1 fast exchange mutant to promote gut granule biogenesis in adults ( Figure 7D and Table 4 ). Together these results suggest that WHT-2 , GLO-3 , and CCZ-1 have some independent functions in adult gut granule biogenesis.

WHT-2 functions redundantly with GLO-3 but not CCZ-1 in the localization of GLO-1(D132A)

A low, but significant, amount of the GLO-1 fast exchange mutant was found on gut granules in glo-3 (−) and ccz-1 (−) mutants ( Figure 7, A and B ) ( Morris et al. 2018 ). As expected, the association of GLO-1 (D132A) was not further diminished in the glo-3 (−) ; ccz-1 (−) double mutant ( Figure 7, A and B ), which supports the hypothesis that GLO-3 and CCZ-1 function together as the GLO-1 GEF. To investigate whether WHT-2 promotes the association of the GLO-1 fast exchange mutant with gut granules in the GLO-1 GEF mutants, we examined its localization in wht-2 (−) ; glo-3 (−) and wht-2 (−) ; ccz-1 (−) double mutant embryos. GLO-1 (D132A) was not detected on gut granules in the glo-3 (−) ; wht-2 (−) double mutant ( Figure 7, A and B ), showing that GLO-3 and WHT-2 have redundant functions in localizing GFP:: GLO-1 (D132A). In contrast, GLO-1 (D132A) remained associated with gut granules in wht-2 (−) ; ccz-1 (−) double mutants ( Figure 7, A and B ). These results reveal a difference between the two GLO-1 GEF subunits in localizing the GLO-1 fast exchange mutant when WHT-2 (+) activity is disrupted. Since CCZ-1 , unlike GLO-3 , also acts as a GEF to activate RAB-7 in the conventional endolysosomal pathway ( Poteryaev et al. 2007 ; Morris et al. 2018 ), we examined whether the loss of RAB-7 function in ccz-1 (−) mutants accounted for the difference in GLO-1 (D132A) localization between glo-3 (−) ; wht-2 (−) and ccz-1 (−); wht-2 (−) double mutants. We performed RNAi targeting rab-7 in glo-3 (−) , wht-2 (−) , and glo-3 (−); wht-2 (−) double mutants and did not detect any change in the localization of GLO-1 (D132A) (Figure S5).

Effects of WHT-2 ATPase mutants on gut granule biogenesis

WHT-2 is a member of the ABCG subfamily of ABC membrane transporters ( Sheps et al. 2004 ). The membrane translocation of substrates by ABCG proteins depends upon ATP binding and hydrolysis ( Taylor et al. 2017 ). Mutations changing the conserved lysine in the Walker A motif of ABCG proteins to a methionine or arginine inactivates their ATPase and transport activity ( Ozvegy et al. 2002 ; Henriksen et al. 2005 ; Zhang et al. 2006 ). To examine the requirement of ATP hydrolysis by WHT-2 in gut granule biogenesis, we generated point mutations in the corresponding lysine of WHT-2 and expressed the point mutants using an extrachromosomal array, as well as using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing to precisely alter the endogenous wht-2 locus. The expression of WHT-2 (+) and the WHT-2 point mutants from the extrachromosomal array disrupted the formation of the gut granules (Figure S6), possibly due to saturation and disruption of protein trafficking to the gut granule when WHT-2 is overexpressed. In contrast, the genome-edited point mutations did not disrupt the localization of PGP-2 and CDF-2 ::GFP ( Figure 8, A and B ). We therefore focused our analysis on whether the endogenous wht-2 (K74M) and wht-2 (K74R) point mutants affected gut granule biogenesis.

Both ATPase mutants showed a weaker defect than the wht-2 ( ok2775 ) null mutant in the formation of birefringent gut granules within pretzel stage embryos ( Table 1 ). However, like the null allele, the ATPase mutants lacked birefringent gut granules in twofold stage embryos ( Table 1 ), which immediately precedes the pretzel stage. The loss of birefringent material in twofold stage embryos suggests that the ATPase, and therefore the transport activity, of WHT-2 contributes to the formation of birefringent gut granules. However, the presence of the birefringent material in pretzel stage embryos significantly differs from the null alleles, indicating that the ATPase mutants retain some activity promoting the formation of birefringent gut granules.

To further explore the function of WHT-2 ATPase-dependent transport, we combined the point mutants with glo-3 ( kx38 ) , which revealed roles for wht-2 (+) in RAB-7 localization, gut granule size, and gut granule protein trafficking ( Figure 2, B, G, and I and Figure 5, E and J ). glo-3 ( kx38 ) alone led to reduced numbers of enlarged gut granules that often lacked LMP-1 and misaccumulated RAB-7 ( Figure 8, C–G ). When the WHT-2 ATPase mutants were combined with glo-3 ( kx38 ) , they caused a loss of birefringent organelles similar to wht-2 (−) null allele ( Table 1 ). The ATPase mutants also led to decreased association of RAB-7 with gut granules in glo-3 ( kx38 ) ( Figure 8, D and F ); however, they did not cause the complete loss of RAB-7 from gut granules that was seen with the wht-2 (−) null allele ( Figure 5, E and J ). Unlike the wht-2 ( ok2775 ) null mutant, the wht-2 ATPase mutants did not suppress gut granule enlargement by glo-3 ( kx38 ) ( Figure 2I and Figure 8G ). Moreover, they did not cause the loss of LMP-1 from gut granules that was seen in glo-3 ( kx38 ) ; wht-2 ( ok2775 ) double mutants ( Figure 2, B and G and Figure 8, C and E ). Together, these observations suggest that the WHT-2 (K74M) and WHT-2 (K74R) point mutants retain function and that some WHT-2 activities do not require ATP hydrolysis.

Next we determined whether the membrane transport activity of WHT-2 was necessary for the gut granule association of GLO-1 . Both WHT-2 ATPase mutants led to a significant reduction in the steady-state levels of GFP:: GLO-1 on gut granules ( Figure 9, A and B ). WHT-2 (K74R) lacked gut-granule-localized GFP:: GLO-1 , resembling wht-2 ( ok2775 ) . Some GFP:: GLO-1 associated with gut granules in the WHT-2 (K74M) point mutant, albeit much less than what was seen in wild type ( Figure 9, A and B ). Both WHT-2 (K74M) and WHT-2 (K74R) were associated with gut granules ( Figure 9, C and D ), making it unlikely that the loss or reduction in GLO-1 localization resulted from improper trafficking and loss of WHT-2 from gut granules. These results suggest that the WHT-2 ATPase cycle and its membrane transport activity promote the gut granule association of GLO-1 .

Discussion

WHT-2 promotes gut granule biogenesis

Our work shows that the ABCG subfamily protein WHT-2 does not just act in the accumulation of birefringent material within gut granules, but more generally in the biogenesis of gut granules themselves. In support of this function, wht-2 (−) mutants mislocalize birefringent material into the intestinal lumen, just as in previously described mutants defective in gut granule formation ( Figure 1 ) ( Hermann et al. 2005 , 2012 ; Delahaye et al. 2014 ). As expected for a gene that promotes the trafficking of lipids and proteins to gut granules, the enlargement of gut granules in umps-1 (−) and glo-3 (−) mutants depends upon wht-2 (+) activity ( Figure 2 ) ( Levitte et al. 2010 ). Furthermore, there is a synthetic loss of autofluorescent gut granules and mislocalization of two different gut granule proteins when wht-2 (−) is combined with weak gut granule biogenesis mutants ( Figure 2 and Table 2 ). Finally, wht-2 (−) disrupts the gut granule localization of GLO-1 ( Figure 5 ), a key player in gut granule biogenesis ( Hermann et al. 2005 ; Morris et al. 2018 ). In contrast to its effects on gut granule formation, loss of wht-2 function does not significantly alter the biogenesis of conventional endolysosomes ( Figure 3 and Figure S1).

WHT-2 is the second ABC transporter implicated in gut granule biogenesis. Previously we found that PGP-2 , an ABCB subfamily member, is essential for protein trafficking to gut granules ( Schroeder et al. 2007 ). While PGP-2 plays an obvious role in the delivery of proteins to gut granules, WHT-2 does not. Instead, WHT-2 functions redundantly with important mediators of gut granule formation ( Figure 2 ), suggesting that it has a more specialized or restricted role in gut granule biogenesis.

The role of ABCG proteins in LRO biogenesis is likely not restricted to C. elegans gut granules. White, the founding member of the ABCG subfamily, functions in the uptake of pigment precursors by Drosophila LROs ( Lloyd et al. 1998 ). While white mutants are most well known for their defects in eye pigmentation, they also display altered LRO morphology, including retinal pigment granules ( Nolte 1961 ; Shoup 1966 ; Nickla 1972 ; Stark and Sapp 1988 ; Schraermeyer and Dohms 1993 ) and zinc storage granules in Malpighian tubules ( Tejeda-Guzmán et al. 2018 ). The changes in organelle morphology could be due to defects in their formation. Similar to what we have documented with WHT-2 , White localizes to Drosophila pigment granule membranes ( Mackenzie et al. 2000 ). Moreover, the GLO-1 ortholog Lightoid functions in the biogenesis of this organelle ( Ma et al. 2004 ). It is possible that white , the first Drosophila mutant locus ( Morgan 1910 ), promotes the localization of Lightoid, similar to how WHT-2 supports the localization of GLO-1 .

WHT-2 contributes to Rab localization

Rab GTPases cycle on and off membranes to regulate intracellular cargo transport. The reversible association of Rabs with organelles is coupled with their activity, such that the active GTP-bound form is membrane-associated and the inactive GDP-bound form is typically located in the cytoplasm ( Mizuno-Yamasaki et al. 2012 ; Müller and Goody 2018 ). While GEFs are posited to represent the minimal machinery needed to direct Rab localization, it is likely that each Rab also utilizes a distinct set of interacting factors and mechanisms to ensure its correct spatiotemporal distribution ( Barr 2013 ; Pfeffer 2013 ). Rab cycling is regulated at multiple levels including membrane targeting, activation by its GEF and the resulting stable association with organelle membranes, as well as inactivation by its GTPase activating protein and subsequent extraction into the cytoplasm. Disrupting the function of factors involved in any of these processes can alter the organelle association of Rabs.

In this work, we show that the ABCG protein WHT-2 is necessary for the accumulation of GLO-1 on gut granules when they are first being generated in embryonic intestinal cells. This is demonstrated using GFP:: GLO-1 in both fixed and living embryos as well as with antibodies to endogenous untagged GLO-1 ( Figure 5 ). Notably, gut granules are present within wht-2 (−) embryos, and GLO-1 is the only protein we have found to be absent from these organelles in wht-2 (−) single mutants, suggesting that WHT-2 specifically promotes the gut granule association of GLO-1 . Consistent with a direct role in this process, WHT-2 is expressed in intestinal cells and is localized to the gut granule membrane ( Figure 4 ).

The disruption of GLO-1 localization in wht-2 (−) mutants could result from decreased GLO-1 recruitment to gut granules or altered GLO-1 activation. A heterodimeric complex of GLO-3 and CCZ-1 is the likely GEF that activates and promotes the association of GLO-1 with gut granules ( Delahaye et al. 2014 ; Morris et al. 2018 ), which is further supported by our current studies showing that CCZ-1 ::YFP and GLO-3 ::GFP are localized to gut granules ( Figure 6 ). Phenotypic similarities between wht-2 (−) and GLO-1 GEF mutants point toward WHT-2 functioning with the GLO-1 GEF. Most notably, the loss of GLO-1 localization to gut granules in wht-2 (−) closely resembles glo-3 (−) and ccz-1 (−) mutants and the GLO-1 (D132A) fast exchange mutant associates with gut granules in all three mutants ( Figure 5 and Figure 7 ). Finally, like glo-3 (+) and ccz-1 (+) , wht-2 (+) activity is required for the localization of LMP-1 to gut granules when the GLO-1 fast exchange mutant is expressed (Figure S5) ( Morris et al. 2018 ).

Longin domain-containing GEFs, like CCZ-1 and GLO-3 , appear to function as heterodimers ( Nordmann et al. 2010 ; Gerondopoulos et al. 2012 ; Kiontke et al. 2017 ), and our results suggest that WHT-2 is not a third subunit of the GLO-1 GEF. First, gut granules are present in embryos and adults lacking wht-2 function ( Figure 1 , Table 1 , and Table 2 ), whereas they are completely lacking in strong glo-3 (−) and ccz-1 (−) mutants ( Delahaye et al. 2014 ; Morris et al. 2018 ). Second, while the gut granule association of CCZ-1 requires GLO-3 , both GLO-3 and CCZ-1 remain associated with gut granules in wht-2 (−) mutants ( Figure 6 ), suggesting that WHT-2 is not a gut granule adaptor for the GLO-1 GEF. Finally, while the GLO-1 fast nucleotide exchange mutant bypasses GEF function, restoring gut granules in the glo-3 (−) and ccz-1 (−) mutants ( Figure 7 , Table 3 , and Table 4 ) ( Delahaye et al. 2014 ; Morris et al. 2018 ), this mutant does not cause the restoration of birefringent material within gut granules in wht-2 (−) ( Figure 7 and Table 3 ).

The GLO-1 fast exchange mutant localizes to gut granules in glo-3 (−) and ccz-1 (−) single and glo-3 ; ccz-1 double mutants, indicating that it can associate with gut granules independent of GLO-1 GEF activity ( Figure 7 ). In support of the model that WHT-2 assists in the GEF-independent localization of GLO-1 , wht-2 (+) activity is necessary for the association of GLO-1 (D132A) with gut granules in glo-3 (−) mutants ( Figure 7 ). However, wht-2 (+) activity is not similarly required for the association of the GLO-1 fast exchange mutant with gut granules when ccz-1 activity is disrupted ( Figure 7 ). If WHT-2 promotes the GLO-1 GEF-independent localization of GLO-1 (D132A), then wht-2 (−) would be expected cause to the loss of the GLO-1 fast exchange mutant from gut granules in ccz-1 (−) mutants. As a possible explanation for this difference we considered that CCZ-1 , but not GLO-3 , functions as part of the GEF for RAB-7 ( Nieto et al. 2010 ; Poteryaev et al. 2010 ; Morris et al. 2018 ). Targeting rab-7 with RNAi in glo-3 (−) ; wht-2 (−) mutants did not enhance the gut granule association of the GLO-1 fast exchange mutant (Figure S5), suggesting that the loss of RAB-7 function is unlikely to underlie the difference between ccz-1 (−) and glo-3 (−) mutants. We utilized null alleles of wht-2 and glo-3 in this study. While ccz-1 ( ok2182 ) completely disrupts gut granule formation ( Delahaye et al. 2014 ; Morris et al. 2018 ), this strong loss-of-function allele retains a small amount of activity ( Nieto et al. 2010 ), which might enable some GLO-1 GEF activity to be present in ccz-1 (−) ; wht-2 (−) mutants. If WHT-2 functions redundantly with the GLO-1 GEF, then a small amount of GEF activity could be sufficient for the gut granule localization of GLO-1 (D132A) in this double mutant.

WHT-2 clearly functions in collaboration with other factors to localize GLO-1 . Our observation that WHT-2 associates with gut granules that lack GLO-1 in glo-3 ( kx38 ) mutants indicates that the mere presence of WHT-2 is not sufficient to recruit or stabilize the majority of GLO-1 ( Figure 4 ) ( Morris et al. 2018 ). For the most part, gut granules appear to be properly formed in wht-2 (−) mutants ( Figure 2 ), in contrast to glo-1 (−) mutants, which lack these organelles ( Hermann et al. 2005 ; Morris et al. 2018 ). In our model, other factors such as the GEF activating GLO-1 are functioning in wht-2 (−) mutants. These would promote the gut granule localization of a small cohort of GLO-1 , which we cannot detect in wht-2 (−) that supports the biogenesis of gut granules.

There is currently no precedent for how an ABC protein could affect Rab localization. However, the simultaneous localization of WHT-2 and GLO-1 on gut granules suggests a number of possibilities. They include WHT-2 promoting the localization of GLO-1 to gut granules prior to GEF activation; for example, by facilitating the delivery of GLO-1 to membranes from the cytoplasm. WHT-2 might promote interactions between activated GLO-1 and its effectors, which could enhance the association of GLO-1 with gut granules. Finally, WHT-2 could stabilize the association of active GLO-1 with gut granules by inhibiting the inactivation and extraction of GLO-1 from gut granules.

Transport-dependent and -independent functions of WHT-2

ABC transporters have established roles transporting pigments and other optically active molecules into endosomal organelles. For example, the precursor of the red pigment that accumulates in yeast vacuoles of ade2 mutants is transported by the ABCC protein YCF1 ( Chaudhuri et al. 1997 ), the White ABCG protein transports pigments into Drosophila LROs ( Sullivan and Sullivan 1975 ), and the Bmwh3 ABCG transporter transports uric acid into silkworm urate granules ( Kômoto et al. 2009 ). The formation of optically active birefringent material within gut granules is delayed in wht-2 (−) null mutants, suggesting that WHT-2 functions similarly ( Figure 1 and Table 1 ) ( Currie et al. 2007 ). While WHT-2 clearly affects the appearance of birefringent granules, we favor the interpretation that WHT-2 does not directly transport this material, or its precursors, into the gut granule lumen. ABC proteins couple ATP binding and hydrolysis with substrate transport ( Lewinson and Livnat-Levanon 2017 ; Ford and Beis 2019 ). Mutations in the Walker A motif of ABC transporters that disrupt ATP hydrolysis destroy their transport activity, including pigment transport by YCF1 and White ( Ewart et al. 1994 ; Falcón-Pérez and Dell’angelica 2002 ). In contrast, pretzel stage embryos robustly accumulated birefringent material within the gut granules of two different WHT-2 Walker A motif point mutants predicted to lack transport activity ( Table 1 ). If WHT-2 functioned to directly transport substrates involved in the generation of birefringent crystals within gut granules, then the WHT-2 ATPase mutants should more closely resemble wht-2 ( ok2775 ) . While we cannot rule out the possibility that the WHT-2 point mutants have some residual ATPase and transport activity, we think it unlikely given the extensive studies of analogous mutations in other ABCG transporters ( Azzaria et al. 1989 ; Müller et al. 1996 ; Wemmie and Moye-Rowley 1997 ; Hou et al. 2000 ; Ozvegy et al. 2002 ; Henriksen et al. 2005 ; Zhang et al. 2006 ). If WHT-2 does not directly transport the material that generates birefringent granules, then why is the appearance of birefringent gut granules delayed when the ATPase activity of WHT-2 is disrupted? We propose that this stems from defective association of GLO-1 with gut granules in the WHT-2 point mutants and resulting alterations in gut granule biogenesis, which disrupt the processes creating birefringent granules. Additionally, why is the GLO-1 fast exchange mutant, which can associate with gut granules, unable to restore birefringent gut granules in wht-2 (−) null mutants ( Figure 7 and Table 3 )? This mutant robustly bypasses the requirement for the GLO-1 GEF in gut granule biogenesis ( Figure 7 , Table 3 , and Table 4 ), which is expected, given its predicted effects on GLO-1 nucleotide exchange. However, our data suggest that WHT-2 functions differently than GLO-3 and CCZ-1 to affect GLO-1 localization, and the GLO-1 (D132A) that associates with gut granules in wht-2 (−) mutants could have limited functionality that manifests itself in defective accumulation of birefringent material.

By whatever mechanism WHT-2 promotes the localization of GLO-1 , it appears to be dependent upon WHT-2 membrane transport activity. We found that two different WHT-2 Walker A point mutations, which inhibit ATPase and membrane transport by homologous ABC proteins ( Azzaria et al. 1989 ; Müller et al. 1996 ; Wemmie and Moye-Rowley 1997 ; Hou et al. 2000 ; Ozvegy et al. 2002 ; Henriksen et al. 2005 ; Zhang et al. 2006 ), disrupted the steady-state association of GLO-1 with gut granules ( Figure 9 ). The WHT-2 (K74R) mutant affected GLO-1 localization similarly to the wht-2 (−) null mutant, whereas low levels of GLO-1 associated with gut granules in the WHT-2 (K74M) point mutant ( Figure 9 ). Consistent with the point mutants having slight differences in activity, WHT-2 (K74M) mutants accumulated birefringent material to a greater extent than WHT-2 (K74R) ( Table 1 ), which correlates with their effects on GLO-1 localization.

Membrane transport is the best understood function of eukaryotic ABC proteins and the substrate specificity of ABC transporters typically varies by subfamily ( Tarling et al. 2013 ; ter Beek et al. 2014 ). Mammalian ABCG proteins transport lipid sterols (ABCG1, ABCG4, ABCG5, and ABCG8) and hundreds of different small molecules with diverse structures (ABCG2) ( Kerr et al. 2011 ; Woodward et al. 2011 ; Horsey et al. 2016 ). Phylogenetic analyses point to WHT-2 , as well as the rest of the WHT proteins in C. elegans , being most closely related to the Drosophila White, Scarlet, and Brown ABCG proteins ( Sheps et al. 2004 ). These transporters move guanine- and tryptophan-derived pigments into pigment granules ( Sullivan et al. 1979 ; Lloyd et al. 1998 ; Mackenzie et al. 1999 ). We think it unlikely that the localization of a Rab is affected by the presence or absence of small molecules inside the organelle lumen, and favor the possibility that sterols are transported by WHT-2 . ABC transporters have well-characterized functions removing lipids from the cytoplasmic leaflet of cellular membranes ( van Meer et al. 2006 ; Tarling et al. 2013 ). Differences between the lipid composition of cytoplasmic and luminal membrane leaflets regulates vesicle budding, tubule elongation, and membrane fusion ( van Meer et al. 2008 ; Johansen et al. 2012 ; Scott et al. 2014 ; Wickner and Rizo 2017 ), as well as the membrane association of Rabs ( Kulakowski et al. 2018 ). WHT-2 might therefore modify the cytoplasmic leaflet of gut granules to promote the localization of GLO-1 .

Our results also suggest that WHT-2 has membrane transport-independent functions. Foremost among these is the robust formation of birefringent granules in both K74 catalytic point mutants ( Table 1 ). WHT-2 function also affects the localization of RAB-7 ( Figure 5 ), an endolysosomal Rab GTPase closely related to GLO-1 ( Elias et al. 2012 ; Klöpper et al. 2012 ; Rojas et al. 2012 ). GLO-3 , likely through its activation of GLO-1 , prevents the association of RAB-7 with gut granules ( Morris et al. 2018 ). wht-2 (−) null mutants completely disrupt the mislocalization of RAB-7 to gut granules in glo-3 (−) mutants ( Figure 5 ), whereas the WHT-2 Walker A mutants do not ( Figure 8 ). The wht-2 point mutants similarly do not cause the reduction in gut granule size and the mislocalization of LMP-1 seen with the wht-2 (−) null mutant in glo-3 ( kx38 ) ( Figure 5 and Figure 8 ). WHT-2 is not the only ABC protein suggested to have transport-independent activities. The yeast ABCC protein Ycf1p has a membrane transport-dependent function in lysosome membrane fusion and a transport-independent role in SNARE localization ( Sasser et al. 2013 ). The transport-independent activity of WHT-2 and other ABC proteins could result from direct interactions with other proteins ( Paumi et al. 2009 ; Lee et al. 2011 ; Babu et al. 2012 ; Sasser et al. 2012 , 2013 ).

ABC superfamily proteins have diverse functions, most of which are focused at cellular membranes ( Higgins 1992 ). They are best known for membrane transport activities that affect a range of important physiological processes ( Holland et al. 2003 ). However, it is becoming clear that ABC proteins can play a significant role in organelle formation ( Brazill et al. 2001 ; Schaheen et al. 2006 ; Schroeder et al. 2007 ; Kawai et al. 2009 ; Bergam et al. 2018 ). ABC transporters can physically interact with Rabs ( Swiatecka-Urban et al. 2005 , 2007 ; Saxena and Kaur 2006 ; Saxena et al. 2006 ; Ferrándiz-Huertas et al. 2011 ), as well as SNAREs and tethers that promote membrane fusion ( Naren et al. 1997 ; Cormet-Boyaka et al. 2002 ; Bilan et al. 2004 ; Sasser et al. 2013 ; Snider et al. 2013 ). Our work provides support for an ABC protein in localizing Rab GTPases, key regulators of membrane dynamics ( Mizuno-Yamasaki et al. 2012 ; Barr 2013 ; Pfeffer 2013 ). Further studies of WHT-2 , as well as other ABC proteins that localize to and are involved in the biogenesis of gut granules, will provide insights into the underappreciated roles that these conserved proteins play in membrane and protein trafficking.
