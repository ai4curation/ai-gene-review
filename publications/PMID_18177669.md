The translocase of the outer mitochondrial membrane (TOM) complex is the general
entry site into the organelle for newly synthesized proteins. Despite its
central role in the biogenesis of mitochondria, the assembly process of this
complex is not completely understood. Mim1 (mitochondrial import protein 1) is a
mitochondrial outer membrane protein with an undefined role in the assembly of
the TOM complex. The protein is composed of an N-terminal cytosolic domain, a
central putative transmembrane segment (TMS) and a C-terminal domain facing the
intermembrane space. Here we show that Mim1 is required for the integration of
the import receptor Tom20 into the outer membrane. We further investigated what
the structural characteristics allowing Mim1 to fulfil its function are. The N-
and C-terminal domains of Mim1 are crucial neither for the function of the
protein nor for its biogenesis. Thus, the TMS of Mim1 is the minimal functional
domain of the protein. We show that Mim1 forms homo-oligomeric structures via
its TMS, which contains two helix-dimerization GXXXG motifs. Mim1 with mutated
GXXXG motifs did not form oligomeric structures and was inactive. With all these
data taken together, we propose that the homo-oligomerization of Mim1 allows it
to fulfil its function in promoting the integration of Tom20 into the
mitochondrial outer membrane.

Karn Keywords: GXXXG motif; Mim1; mitochondria; protein import; TOM complex Introduction Almost all mitochondrial proteins are synthesized in the cytosol and must be imported into mitochondria. There are two known translocases in the outer mitochondrial membrane —the translocase of the outer mitochondrial membrane (TOM) complex and the topogenesis of mitochondrial outer membrane β-barrel proteins (TOB) complex. 1–4 Precursors targeted to all four compartments of mitochondria [outer membrane, intermembrane space (IMS), inner membrane and matrix] are recognised in an early step of their import by the TOM complex. This initial recognition is mediated in most cases by the receptor proteins Tom20 and Tom70.
5 Both proteins are anchored to the outer membrane via a single αhelical transmembrane segment (TMS) at their N-terminus, whereas their functional part is facing the cytosol.
6,7 The precursor proteins are then relayed from the receptor proteins to the TOM core complex, which is composed of five subunits (Tom40, Tom22, Tom5, Tom6 and Tom7).
8–10 The central component of the latter complex is the essential protein Tom40, which forms the protein-conducting channel. 11–14 The TOM complex is required for its own biogenesis. However, detailed understanding of the insertion and assembly pathways of its components is still missing. Precursors of mitochondrial β-barrel proteins are recognised initially by the receptors of the TOM complex and then translocated through the TOM import pore. 15–17 From the TOM complex, β-barrel precursors are relayed to another complex in the outer membrane, which was termed the TOB complex or SAM complex (for sorting and assembly machinery).4,18–20 This latter complex mediates the insertion of β-barrel protein into the outer membrane. The major component of the TOB complex is the essential protein Tob55 (also named Sam50/Omp85).
19–21 Two further proteins with undefined functions, Tob38 (Tom38/ Sam35) and Mas37 (Tom37/Sam37), were identified as subunits of the TOB complex.
20,22–24 It has been recently suggested that Mdm10 can also function as a subunit of the TOB complex. Mdm10 was reported to be required for the assembly of Tom40 into the TOM complex but to be dispensable for the membrane insertion of other β-barrel proteins.
25 Recently, an outer membrane protein named Mim1 (mitochondrial import protein 1) (or Tom13) was identified as an additional assembly factor of the TOM complex. 22,26 Deletion of the protein also resulted in changes in mitochondrial morphology 27 and in accumulation of nonimported mitochondrial precursor proteins.
28 These effects are thought to be secondary to the main function of Mim1 in the biogenesis of the TOM complex. Mim1 has one putative TMS with a cytosolic N-terminal domain and a C-terminal domain that faces the IMS. Depletion of Mim1 abrogates assembly of the TOM complex and results in accumulation of Tom40, the major constituent of the TOM complex, in a low-molecular-mass species. Interestingly, Mim1 is a constituent neither of the TOM complex nor of the TOB complex; 22,26,29 rather, it appears to be a subunit of another, yet unidentified, complex.
Many questions as to the molecular function of Mim1, its oligomeric state and the contribution of its domains to the function of the protein remain to be answered. Here we report that the N- and C-terminal domains of Mim1 are crucial neither for the function of the protein nor for its own biogenesis. Rather, the TMS is the functional domain of the protein. We further show that two G XXXG motifs within the TMS of Mim1 are instrumental for the homo-oligomerization of the protein. Such oligomerization is crucial for the function of Mim1 in the biogenesis of the TOM complex.
Results The N- and C-terminal domains of Mim1 are crucial neither for the function of the protein nor for its biogenesis We investigated the structural characteristics that allow Mim1 to fulfil its function. Sequence homology analysis revealed that the central TMS of Mim1 is highly conserved among fungi, whereas both the N- and C-terminal domains are variable ( Fig. 1a and Refs. 22,26). To test whether this conservation reflects the functional importance of these domains, we introduced the cDNAs encoding either Neurospora crassa Mim1 or Schizosaccharomyces pombe Mim1 into yeast expression vectors. Expression of the proteins was under the control of the original Saccharomyces cerevisiae Mim1 promoter (in pRS315 vector) or the strong TPI (triosephosphate isomerase) promoter (in pRS426 vector). We transformed these vectors into S. cerevisiae cells lacking Mim1 and tested the capacity of these proteins to complement the function of Mim1. The growth was compared with that of cells harbouring either plasmid encoding the S. cerevisiae Mim1 or an empty plasmid as a negative control. Mim1 from N. crassa could rescue the growth phenotype of Δmim1 cells only partially and upon its overexpression ( Fig. 1 b). We can exclude the possibility that the lower complementation capacity of N. crassa Mim1 resulted from low levels of expression since expression was verified by Western blotting (data not shown). In contrast, cells harbouring S. pombe Mim1 did not present any growth phenotype under all tested conditions ( Fig.
1b and other data not shown). We cannot explain the difference between the S. pombe and N. crassa proteins. However, it appears that major variations in both the cytosolic and IMS domains of Mim1 can be tolerated.
To study further the importance of the various domains of Mim1, we constructed Mim1 variants where either amino acid residues 1 –34 [Mim1( ΔN)] or amino acid residues 76 –113 [Mim1( ΔC)] were deleted ( Fig. 1 c). The third variant was constructed by deleting both domains, leaving only the TMS of the protein [Mim1(TM)]. These truncated forms of Mim1 were tested for their ability to complement the function of full-length Mim1. Deletion of either domain alone did not result in any growth alteration under all the tested conditions ( Fig. 1 d and other data not shown). The variant containing only the TMS could not complement the function when expressed under the endogenous promoter but could do so when it was overexpressed ( Fig. 1 d).
Thus, neither the N-terminal domain nor the C-terminal domain of Mim1 seems to play a crucial role in the function of the protein. We then tested whether the capacity of the truncated Mim1 variants to sustain growth correlates with their ability to support the biogenesis of the TOM complex.
Mitochondria from strains harbouring full-length Mim1 or its truncated variants were isolated, and the TOM complex was analyzed by blue native (BN)-PAGE. As expected, in mitochondria lacking Mim1, the low-molecular-mass species of the TOM complex was observed.
26 In contrast, this species was observed at very low levels or not at all upon overexpression of the Mim1 truncated variants ( Fig.
1e). Likewise, the steady-state levels of Tom20 and Tom40 in mitochondria harbouring the truncated version were similar to those in wild-type (WT) mitochondria ( Fig. 1 f). As seen in Fig. 1 f, Mim1( ΔN) can be detected at levels similar to those of the full-length protein. A band corresponding to Mim1( ΔC) can also be observed, albeit in minor intensity. Although we used an antibody raised against full-length Mim1, it is indicative that most of the epitopes reside in the C-terminal domain of Mim1 (data not shown). Mim1(TM) is very hydrophobic and probably does not contain any epitopes. Thus, our ability to detect Mim1( ΔC) and Mim1(TM) was very limited. Taken together, our results suggest that neither the N-terminal domain nor the C-terminal domain of Mim1 is crucial for the function of the protein and for its targeting to and insertion into the mitochondrial outer membrane.

673Mim1 Functions in an Oligomeric Form Mim1 forms dimers in the mitochondrial outer membrane Previous studies have demonstrated that Mim1 is a component of a high-molecular-mass complex of about 200 kDa.
22,26 To investigate the oligomeric state of Mim1 in this complex, we employed a chemical cross-linking approach. Intact mitochondria were treated with a cross-linking reagent:
disuccinimidyl glutarate (DSG), disuccinimidyl suberate or 1,5-difluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene. Mim1containing cross-linked products were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunodecoration. One major band corresponding to an apparent molecular mass of 28 kDa was detected after treatment of mitochondria with these three cross-linking reagents ( Fig. 2 a).
Since the molecular mass of Mim1 is 12.8 kDa, this cross-linking product represents most probably a homodimer of Mim1 (see below). We also constantly observed a weak band with an apparent molecular mass of 40 kDa that could correspond to a trimer of Mim1 ( Figs. 2a and 4c and d ).
To further demonstrate that Mim1 has the potential to form dimers, we repeated the crosslinking experiments with mitochondria isolated from cells lacking full-length Mim1 and overexpressing Mim1( ΔN). The addition of the cross-linking reagent DSG to these mitochondria resulted in the formation of one dominant cross-linking adduct with a molecular mass of 16 kDa ( Fig. 2 b). The predicted molecular mass of Mim1( ΔN) is 8.9 kDa.
Thus, the cross-linking adduct represents a dimer of Mim1(ΔN). These results strongly argue against the possibility that the cross-linking adduct of 28 kDa, which is observed with full-length Mim1, contains in addition to Mim1 another protein that by chance has (like Mim1) a molecular mass of 13 kDa. In addition, we analyzed the cross-linking pattern of Mim1 in mitochondria containing both full-length Mim1 and Mim1( ΔN). Besides the expected homodimers of Mim1 and Mim1( ΔN), we could also detect cross-linking adducts that represent heterodimers of these two forms ( Fig. 2 b). These results suggest that the ability of Mim1 to form dimers is independent of the N-terminal domain of the protein.

These cells grew like WT cells (Ref. 26 and other data not shown); therefore, the tagged Mim1 variants appear to be fully functional. Careful examination of the sequence of the TMS of Mim1 revealed that this stretch contains two consecutive G XXXG/A motifs encompassing residues 57–61 and 63–67 (Fig. 3a). GXXXG and G/AXXXG/ A motifs are known to mediate helix –helix interactions within biological membranes.
30,31 We investigated the function of these motifs by mutating them and analyzing the effect of these mutations on the growth of the resulting yeast strains. First, we mutated only one motif by exchanging Gly61 with the bulky residue leucine (Mim1-G61L; Fig. 3b). This change did not cause a growth phenotype ( Fig. 3b).
We reasoned that the second motif might be sufficient to support oligomerization even when the first motif is mutated. Therefore, in addition to the above mutation, we replaced both residues Gly63 and Ala67 with Ile residues (resulting in Mim1-LII; Fig. 3 a). This variant was not able to complement the function of native Mim1 under all conditions tested (Fig. 3b and other data not shown).
The reduced growth rate in the strain containing Mim1-LII led us to monitor the steady-state levels of outer membrane proteins in this strain. Like mitochondria from the ΔMim1 strain, organelles containing Mim1-LII had significantly reduced levels of Tom20 and moderately reduced amounts of Tom40 and porin ( Fig. 4 a and b). Occasionally, upon inspection of isolated mitochondria, we observed a minor fraction of Mim1 that is cleaved by proteases during the isolation procedure. Interestingly, in the case of Mim1-LII, the portion of cleaved protein is highly increased ( Fig. 4 a). Thus, Mim1-LII probably has an altered topology that makes it more susceptible to proteolytic cleavage.
We further analyzed by chemical cross-linking whether mutating the dimerization motifs affected the oligomeric structure of Mim1. The Gly61-to- Leu61 single mutation did not affect the dimerization of Mim1 ( Fig. 4 c). In contrast, no dimer of Mim1-LII could be observed ( Fig. 4d). To investigate whether the Mim1-LII variant is able to build oligomeric structures, m itochondria harbouring this variant were isolated and analyzed by BN- PAGE. Mim1-containing complexes could not be detected ( Fig. 4 e). Thus, these motifs are crucial for the ability of Mim1 to form oligomeric structures.
Furthermore, immunodecoration of the BN-PAGE membrane with antibody against Tom40 revealed that part of the Tom40 molecules in these mutated mitochondria migrated as a low-molecular-mass species ( Fig. 4 d). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the ability of Mim1 to form oligomers is essential for the function of the protein in the biogenesis of the TOM complex.
Mim1 is required for the optimal integration of Tom20 into the outer membrane Mim1 was initially found in a high-throughput screen to have an undefined role in the import of precursor proteins into mitochondria.
28 A more detailed analysis revealed that Mim1 is crucial for the biogenesis of the TOM complex. Depletion of Mim1 abrogates assembly of the TOM complex and results in accumulation of Tom40, the major constituent of the TOM complex, in low-molecular-mass species ( Figs. 1e and 4e and Refs. 22,26). However, we constantly observed that upon deletion of Mim1, the steady-state level of Tom20 is reduced to a higher extent as compared with Tom40 ( Fig. 4 a and b and Refs. 22,26). Can the assembly phenotype in the absence of Mim1 be explained by the lower amounts of assembled Tom20 in the TOM complex? Model et al. reported that Tom20 is crucial for stable organization of Tom40 channel units into larger assemblies.
32 Thus, we compared by BN-PAGE analysis mitochondria lacking Tom20 with those deleted in Mim1.
Figure 5 a shows that a much larger portion of the Tom40 molecules migrated in the low-molecularmass species in the absence of Tom20 as compared with the situation when Mim1 was deleted.

To investigate whether Mim1 is crucial for the assembly of Tom20 into the TOM complex, we performed co-immunoprecipitation analysis. WT mitochondria and mitochondria lacking Mim1 were solubilized with the mild detergent digitonin, and the ability of antibodies against Tom40 to precipitate Tom20 and that of antibodies against Tom20 to co-precipitate Tom40 were monitored. 676 Mim1 Functions in an Oligomeric Form The exposed domains of Mim1 on both sides of the outer membrane do not appear to be crucial neither for targeting of the protein to mitochondria nor for insertion of the protein into the outer membrane.
Our finding that overexpression of the TMS of Mim1 is sufficient to complement the function of the fulllength protein suggests that the TMS is the functional part of the protein. This conclusion is in line with the observation that in contrast to the exposed domains of the protein, the TMS is highly conserved. In addition to being the functional domain of Mim1, the TMS also mediates the molecular organization of the protein. We were able to localize the oligomerization element of Mim1 within the TMS of the protein. Two sequential GXXXG/A motifs that are essential for the oligomerization were identified. These two motifs are highly conserved among Mim1 homologues, and mutations in both motifs rendered Mim1 inactive and hindered its oligomerization. G XXXGi sa strong transmembrane helix-packing motif that is frequently involved in high-affinity homo-oligomerization.
30 Worth noting, G XXXG motifs are found in various cellular membranes. For example, GXXXG motifs within the TMS of subunits e and g are involved in the dimerization/oligomerization of the mitochondrial A TP synthase complex, 34–36 and such motifs within the TMS of the amyloid precursor protein are critical for the etiology of the A β42 peptide. 37 Mim1 provides the first example of such a motif in an outer mitochondrial membrane protein and offers a special example of two sequential motifs where both appear to be important for dimerization.
Does the Mim1-containing complex harbour further proteins besides Mim1? Our results suggest that the basic structural unit of Mim1 is composed of homodimers that form a higher structural assembly of about 200 kDa. Cross-linking experiments and genetic screens designed to identify interacting partners of Mim1 (data not shown) propose that these complexes may solely contain Mim1 molecules.
Such putative homo-oligomers of Mim1 might create a local environment in the outer membrane that facilitates the biogenesis of Tom20.

32 Furthermore, it has been previously reported that Tom20 is required for efficient biogenesis of the TOM core components Tom40 and Tom22.
17,38,39 With all these data taken together, we propose that the observed phenotypes of ΔMim1 cells, such as altered mitochondrial morphology, import inhibition of precursor proteins and hampered assembly of newly synthesized subunits of the TOM complex, are secondary to the primary effect on the integration of Tom20 into the outer membrane. Despite extensive efforts, we could not detect any stable interaction between Mim1 and Tom components (data not shown). Therefore, we speculate that Mim1 exerts its role via an indirect interaction with precursor molecules of Tom20.
A successful integration of Tom20 into the outer membrane required initial recognition, insertion into the membrane and assembly into the TOM complex. As Mim1 does not function as a classical receptor for precursor proteins, but rather within the membrane, we can exclude its involvement in the initial recognition at the cytosolic surface of the organelle. Our experiments suggest that the capacity of Tom20 molecules to insert into the outer membrane is hampered by the absence of Mim1 but that their ability to assemble into the TOM complex is not affected. Therefore, we propose that Mim1 is involved mainly in the membrane integration of Tom20 precursors.
We have previously developed a specific proteolytic assay in order to study the insertion of Tom20 into the membrane.
33 This assay is based on the acquisition of the correct topology of Tom20 upon its insertion into the membrane and the subsequent assembly into the TOM holo complex. Using this assay and based on previous findings, 40 we have proposed that the precursor of Tom20 is inserted into the outer membrane by a process that is mediated by the TOM core complex. However, when proteoliposomes containing reconstituted TOM core complex were used in the proteolytic assay, only a rather inefficient insertion of Tom20 was observed.
33 This suggests that the ability of the TOM core complex alone to allow Tom20 to acquire its correct topology is quite limited. Based on our current results, the absence of Mim1 in our reconstituted system can explain, at least partially, the low efficiency of Tom20 import in this system.
In ongoing experiments, adding maltose-binding protein–Mim1 fusion protein to lipid vesicles did not restore efficient insertion of Tom20 into these vesicles (data not shown). Thus, full reconstitution of TOM complex in combination with Mim1 is an aim of our future studies.
In summary, the present study documents important insights into the structure –function relationships of Mim1. Mim1 forms homo-oligomers via GXXXG motifs within its TMS. This oligomerization is essential for its function in facilitating the biogenesis of the TOM complex.
Materials and Methods Yeast strains and growth methods Standard genetic techniques were used for the growth and manipulation of yeast strains. 41 The S. cerevisiae WT strain YPH499 was used. The GAL10–MIM1 strain was constructed as described before. 26 The mim1 null strain was constructed by replacing the MIM1 open reading frame with the HIS3 cassette by homologous recombination. For dilution assays, cells were grown to log phase in synthetic medium lacking leucine (SD − leu) or synthetic medium lacking uracil (SD − Ura) and diluted in the same medium to an OD 600 of 0.5. Cells were then diluted in 10-fold increments, and 3 μl of each was spotted onto the indicated solid media.
Cloning of MIM1 homologues and variants The sequences encoding Mim1( ΔN) (lacking first 102 bp), Mim1( ΔC) (lacking last 114 bp) and Mim1(TM) (lacking first 102 bp and last 114 bp) were cloned by PCR from yeast genomic DNA using primers based on the published sequence. The PCR products were inserted into the yeast expression vector pRS315-LEU2. The PCR product was flanked upstream and downstream by PCR products representing 300 bp of the 5 ′-untranslated region and 3 ′-untranslated region, respectively. The PCR products encoding the Mim1 variants were also introduced into the multicopy yeast plasmid pRS426xTPIp-URA3 (kind gift of Dr. K. Dietmeier). The Mim1 gene from N. crassa was cloned as described before, 42 whereas the Mim1 open reading frame from S. pombe was amplified by PCR from an S. pombe cDNA library (kind gift of Dr. N.
Bonnafoey). Both PCR products were introduced into the yeast expression vector as described above.
Mim1-G61L and Mim1-LII were generated using a QuickChange Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Stratagene) according to the manufacturer's protocol. As a template for the PCR, the plasmid pGEM4-Mim1 containing the MIM1 coding sequence was used.
Biochemical and cell biological methods Mitochondria were prepared by differential centrifugation as described previously.
43 Radiolabeled precursor proteins were synthesized in rabbit reticulocyte lysate in the presence of [ 35S]methionine (MP Biomedicals) after in vitro transcription by SP6 polymerase from pGEM4 vectors containing the cDNA of interest. Import experiments were performed in an import buffer containing 250 mM sucrose, 0.25 mg/ml of bovine serum albumin, 80 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl 2, 10 mM Mops –KOH, 2 mM NADH and 2 mM A TP , pH 7.2.
BN gel electrophoresis was performed basically as described before. 17,44 Briefly, mitochondria (50 μg) were lysed in 30 μl of detergent-containing buffer (0.5 –1.0% digitonin in 20 mM Tris –HCl, 0.1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 50 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol and 1 mM PMSF, pH 7.4). After incubation at 4 °C for 15 min and a clarifying spin (15 min at 20,600 g), 5 μl of sample buffer [5% (w/v) Coomassie brilliant blue G-250, 100 mM Bis –Tris and 500 mM 6-aminocaproic acid, pH 7.0] was added, and the mixture was analyzed on a 6 –13% gradient BN gel.
For pull-down assays, mitochondria were dissolved in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris –HCl, 20 mM KCl, 1 mM PMSF, 678 Mim1 Functions in an Oligomeric Form 10 mM imidazole and 1% digitonin, pH 8.0). After a clarifying spin (20 min at 125,000 g), the supernatants were incubated with Ni–NTA beads. For chemical cross-linking experiments, mitochondria were resuspended in import buffer and then incubated with the chemical cross-linker DSG, disuccinimidyl suberate or 1,5-difluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene for 30 min on ice. The cross-linking reagents were added from 100-fold stock solution in dimethyl sulfoxide.
Next, glycine (0.1 M, pH 8.8) was added to quench excess of the cross-linker and mitochondria were reisolated and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunodecoration.
For co-immunoprecipitation experiments, mitochondria were dissolved in buffer containing 1% digitonin for 30 min at 4 °C. The lysed material was centrifuged (15 min at 20,000g), and the supernatant was incubated with antibodies coupled to protein A –Sepharose beads. At the end of the binding reaction, the protein A –Sepharose beads were washed with detergent-containing buffer and bound proteins were eluted with sample buffer and subjected to SDS-PAGE. The gels were blotted and immunodecorated with antibodies against mitochondrial proteins.
