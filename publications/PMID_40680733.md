Adequate delivery of thyroid hormones to the brain is crucial for normal
neurological development. MCT8 and OATP1C1, two solute carrier (SLC)
transporters, mediate the passage of thyroid hormones across the blood-brain
barrier and into the central nervous system. Mutations in MCT8 result in
Allan-Herndon-Dudley syndrome (AHDS), an X-linked birth defect characterized by
neurodevelopmental impairments and peripheral hyperthyroidism, whereas OATP1C1
deficiency is linked to brain hypometabolism and progressive neurodegeneration.
Here, we report cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of MCT8 and OATP1C1
bound with the active thyroid hormone triiodothyronine (T3) and the prohormone
thyroxine (T4) at 2.9 and 2.3 Å resolutions, respectively. Combined with
functional studies, we elucidate their distinct thyroid hormone recognition and
transport mechanisms and explain disease mutations. Although extracellular
allosteric sites are not a common feature of SLC transporters, we identify one
in OATP1C1. Collectively, these findings illuminate key aspects of thyroid
hormone transport, a fundamental process in development and disease.

INTRODUCTION

Thyroid hormones, consisting of the prohormone thyroxine (T4) and the active product 3,5,3′-triiodothyronine (T3), are essential regulators of development and metabolism1,2. Originating from the thyroid gland, these circulating hormones enter the target cells through a carrier-mediated process to ultimately activate thyroid receptors (TRs) for gene expression2,3. MCT8 and OATP1C1 are two membrane transporters of the major facilitator superfamily (MFS) responsible for passage of thyroid hormones across the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and into neurons and glial cells3–5. Thyroid hormone deficiency during crucial developmental stages can cause permanent brain damage, emphasizing the critical importance of having adequate thyroid hormone levels during central nervous system development6–9.

MCT8 belongs to the solute carrier family 16 (SLC16) of monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs). The 14 SLC16 members typically mediate proton-coupled transport of small monocarboxylate metabolites including lactate, pyruvate and ketone bodies as exemplified by MCT1/2/3/410. Unlike MCT1–4, MCT8 represents the most specific thyroid hormone transporter identified up to date. It mediates the flux of T3 and T4 across the plasma membrane through facilitated diffusion independent of proton or sodium gradient4,11–13. In the brain, MCT8 is responsible for thyroid hormone uptake across the BBB3,14,15. Mutations in MCT8 lead to Allan-Herndon-Dudley syndrome (AHDS), an X-linked congenital disorder with severe neuropsychomotor defects resulting from cerebral hypothyroidism and chronic peripheral thyrotoxicosis due to elevated serum T3 concentrations6–8.

OATP1C1 is a brain-specific T4 transporter of the SLCO (former SLC21) family of organic anion transporting polypeptides (OATPs)5, which has at least 11 members in human that transport a broad spectrum of endogenous and exogenous amphipathic anion substrates including various drugs16. While other members in this family often display substrate promiscuity causing pharmacokinetic drug–drug interactions (DDIs)17, OATP1C1 selectively facilitates T4 transport over T35,18. In mice, OATP1C1 partially overlaps with MCT8 in T4 transport across the BBB19,20. Although Mct8 knockout mice recapitulate the abnormal endocrine profiles seen in AHDS, they lack the neurological deficits observed in patients, likely due to higher OATP1C1 expression in the mouse BBB and subsequent conversion of T4 into T3 by type 2 deiodinase (DIO2)21–23. Indeed, when both Mct8 and Oatp1c1 are eliminated, the resulting phenotype in mice closely resembles the full spectrum of AHDS, including the associated neurological impairments19. In the human brain, OATP1C1 is sparsely expressed at the BBB but is predominantly found in astrocytes, where it primarily mediates T4 uptake to nourish target neural cells3,15. A case of OATP1C1 disease mutation has been reported9. It gives rise to profound cerebral hypometabolism and neurodegenerative symptoms. This clinical finding, together with MCT8 deficiency, underscores the physiological importance of having both transporters in maintaining brain thyroid hormone homeostasis.

A major problem in AHDS pathophysiology that is not fully resolved is the origin of excessive serum T3 and reduced serum T4, which may be associated with increased hepatic type 1 deiodinase (DIO1) activity in converting T4 into T3, or impaired thyroid hormone degradation by type 3 deiodinase (DIO3) in the brain3,22–24. Furthermore, tissue hyperthyroidism observed in AHDS suggests that peripheral organs rely on transporters other than MCT8, for thyroid hormone uptake21,22. Although several candidate thyroid hormone transporters, including MCT10 and the L-type amino acid transporters LAT1 and LAT2, have been proposed3, their physiological roles have not been convincingly established. MCT10 is the only other MCT family member known to transport thyroid hormones12; however, studies in knockout mice suggest that MCT10 primarily facilitates T3 efflux in peripheral tissues rather than cellular uptake in absence of MCT83,25. Similarly, LAT1 and LAT2 have not been definitively linked to thyroid hormone transport in vivo, likely due to their broad substrate specificity and inhibition by physiological amino acid concentrations3. Therefore, the identities of thyroid hormone transporters underlying peripheral hyperthyroidism in AHDS remain incompletely understood. Intriguingly, recent genome-wide association studies have identified SLC17A4 as a thyroid hormone transporter implicated in thyroid hormone homeostasis26, raising the possibility that additional, yet undiscovered, transporters may contribute to peripheral pathogenicity in AHDS.

Despite extensive studies on the functional characterization and pathophysiological roles of MCT8 and OATP1C1, the precise molecular mechanisms governing their substrate binding and transport remain elusive. Here, we present high-resolution cryo-EM structures of human MCT8 and OAPT1C1 bound with T3 and T4, at 2.9 Å and 2.3 Å resolution, respectively. Combining mutagenesis and functional assays, we demonstrate that MCT8 and OATP1C1 exhibit distinct thyroid hormone recognition patterns, conferring high substrate specificity and explaining disease mutations in AHDS. These structures serve as a structural framework for predicting additional thyroid hormone transporters that may share similar substrate recognition principles. Additionally, we have determined the cryo-EM structure of OATP1C1 bound with estrone 3-sulfate (E1S), a prototypical substrate for OATPs, at 2.6 Å resolution, and with estrone 3-glucuronide (E1G) at 3.13 Å resolution. These structures unexpectedly reveal an extracellular allosteric regulatory site, highly conserved across OATPs. We further demonstrate that E1G acts as an allosteric ligand that exclusively binds to this site and inhibits OATP1C1 transport activity. Together, our findings open avenues for designing allosteric modulators targeting OATPs, with broader implications for therapeutic modulation of SLC transporters.

RESULTSStructure and mechanism of thyroid hormone transport by MCT8The low molecular mass of MCT8 (~50 kDa, excluding the disordered regions) and its limited soluble structural features pose significant challenges for cryo-EM structure determination due to two main factors: the weak signal produced by its small size and the large mass of the disordered detergent micelle surrounding its transmembrane region, which substantially reduces the signal-to-noise ratio and causes overfitting to the detergent micelle during cryo-EM data processing. Although the heterogeneous detergent micelle introduces noise, it also provides a strong low-resolution constraint that aids in determining reliable particle orientations. To leverage the structural features of the detergent micelle at low resolution while minimizing overfitting its noise at high resolution, we developed a method utilizing composite masks during 3D classification and refinement27 (see Methods). The composite mask separated the protein from the detergent micelle and during each iteration of 3D reconstruction, the signals from the detergent micelle were low-pass filtered, leaving the protein signal unmodified. In addition to challenges posed by its small size, the MCT8-T3 complex exhibited a pronounced preferred orientation, with an overabundance of particles in the top views, resulting in anisotropic resolution. To overcome this, we implemented an orientation “rebalancing” approach, selectively discarding particles from the most overrepresented orientations. This reduced the dataset from 256,934 particles to a final subset of 59,661 particles, effectively mitigating the anisotropy. Importantly, to accurately estimate the resolution during processing, Fourier shell correlation (FSC) calculations were restricted to the protein region defined by the mask. As a result, this refined processing strategy enabled us to determine the MCT8–T3 complex structure at 2.9 Å resolution (Figures 1 and S1; Table S1).The overall architecture of MCT8 adopts a canonical MFS fold consisting of 12 transmembrane (TM) helices folded into two 6TM bundles, namely the N-terminal domain (NTD, TM1–6) and the C-terminal domain (CTD, TM7–12), which are related through a 2-fold pseudo-symmetry axis perpendicular to the membrane in an outward-open conformation (Figure 1C). Plasma membrane localization of MCT1–4 requires single-pass transmembrane chaperon proteins Basigin or Embigin by forming a polar interaction of an Asn or His residue on TM6 (e.g., N187 in MCT1) with a membrane-embedded acidic Glu residue of Basigin/Embigin10,28,29. In MCT8, N187 is replaced by V272, suggesting MCT8 does not reply on these chaperones to reach the cell surface (Figure S2).MCT8 is nearly entirely buried in the membrane. In its outward-open conformation, the NTD and CTD enclose a central cavity forming an exclusive opening towards the extracellular space, suggesting thyroid hormones can only access MCT8 through this cell surface entry (Figure 1D). T3 binds to the lower portion of the cavity with the carboxyl group located approximately at the midpoint of the membrane and the two iodophenyl rings further extended to reach the bottom of the hydrophobic cavity. Thyroid hormones are chemically distinctive among endocrine signaling molecules owing to their covalent incorporation of large hydrophobic iodine atoms whose van der Waals radii are approximately 3 times that of a carbon atom (Figure 1A). Our structure reveals that the shape of T3 precisely matches the binding site in MCT8, which is predominantly lined by several aromatic and hydrophobic residues from both the NTD and CTD that tightly surround these three iodine atoms (Figures 2A and 2B). There is also extra room near I428, and the size and chemical environment are suitable to accommodate a fourth iodine atom in T4. This shape complementarity explains why MCT8 exclusively transports thyroid hormones but does not distinguish between T3 and T44 (Figures 2A, 2B and S1I).Members of the MCT family transport a variety of monocarboxylate substrates. A structural comparison reveals that the carboxyl group of T3 in our structure aligns with lactate in MCT1, likely indicating a common binding position for monocarboxylates28,30 (Figures 2E–2G). In MCT1, the negatively charged carboxyl group of lactate is tightly clamped by two positively charged residues, K38 from TM1, and R313 from TM8, indicating cooperation between the NTD and CTD in substrate recognition (Figure 2F). D309 acts as a proton switch, which forms a salt bridge with R313, supporting its conformation in binding lactate (Figure 2F). Protonation of D309 disrupts such interaction, decoupling the NTD and CTD, and leading to a conformational transition to an inward-open state, enabling lactate transport28. In MCT8, there is only one positively charged residue that interacts with the carboxyl group of T3, namely R371, which is equivalent to R313 in MCT1 (Figures 2E and 2G). In contrast, K38 is substituted with N119 in MCT8, losing the salt bridge from the NTD (Figure 2G). Instead, the hydroxyl group of Y335 from TM7, stabilizes the carboxyl group of T3 through hydrogen bond formation (Figure 2E). As D309 is not conserved in MCT8, our structure raises the question of whether there is a functional analogue that can potentially serve as a proton sensor in MCT8. We notice that the only negatively charged residue inside the cavity, D424 from TM10, is in close vicinity to D309 in MCT1 (Figures 2E and 2G). However, its distance to R371 is approximately 4.5 Å, seemingly incapable of a similar role as D309 in MCT1 (Figures 2E and 2F). D424 forms a hydrogen bond with the hydroxyl group of Y335, positioning it in relay to interact with the carboxyl group of T3 (Figure 2E), which is less sensitive to the protonation state. It is noteworthy that R371, Y335 and D424 are all located within the CTD, different from MCT1 which involves charged residues from both the NTD and CTD (Figures 2E and 2F). To summarize, our structural findings are consistent with the generally accepted idea that MCT8 functions as a uniporter and that the transport of thyroid hormones does not require a pH gradient3,4,11–13.To validate our structural findings, we assessed the cellular uptake of T3 and T4 using HEK293 stable cell lines expressing either the wild-type (WT) or MCT8 mutants together with the intracellular thyroid hormone-binding protein mu-crystallin (CRYM) to prevent the efflux of thyroid hormones, as MCT8 mediates bidirectional flux12,13 (Figures 2C and 2D). Surface biotinylation assays confirmed that all tested mutants exhibited surface expression levels similar to that of the WT (Figure S3A). R371C and D424N mutations have been identified as pathogenic variants in AHDS31,32. Substituting R371 with either Ala or Cys significantly reduces the uptake of both T3 and T4 (Figures 2C and 2D). A similar reduction in uptake was observed for Y335F. Moreover, replacing D424 with either Asn or Ala completely abolished transport activity, consistent with previous findings33. As a control, Y339F, which is adjacent to D424 (Figure 2B), did not alter transport activity (Figures 2C and 2D). These results confirm the crucial roles of these residues identified in our structural analysis for thyroid hormone recognition. We then swapped two key residues between MCT8 and MCT1 and examined their functional consequences. N119K, which restores the absent salt bridge for monocarboxylate recognition, fully inhibited transport activity (Figures 2C, 2D and 2G). One possible explanation is that the enhanced binding of thyroid hormones may lock MCT8 in the outward-open conformation, preventing transport. Additionally, A432 from TM10, located at the bottom of the substrate binding cavity (Figure 2B), is replaced by F375 in MCT1 (Figure S2), which would potentially clash with the outer ring of T3. Surprisingly, A432F had only a mild effect on thyroid hormone uptake (Figures 2C and 2D), suggesting greater tolerance for modifications around this distal hydroxyl moiety compared to the critical requirement for the carboxylate terminal of thyroid hormones.MCT10 is the only member of the MCT family, aside from MCT8, that transports thyroid hormones3,12. MCT10 shares 58% sequence identity with MCT8 within the transmembrane domain and demonstrates a substrate preference for T3 over T412. Structural mapping reveals that MCT10 possesses a slightly smaller substrate binding cavity, with F213 and S236 in MCT8 replaced by Y184 and T207, respectively (Figures S1I and S2). Notably, mutating these two residues in MCT10 to their counterparts in MCT8 has been shown to enhance its T4 transport ability due to an enlarged cavity34. Conversely, we introduced mutations to reduce the cavity size in MCT8 and evaluated their impact on substrate selectivity. Mutating I428 to a bulky Phe residue restricts the unoccupied space that could accommodate the extra iodine on T4 (Figure 2B), significantly decreasing T4 transport while having a lesser effect on T3 (Figures 2C and 2D). Collectively, these results elucidate the molecular determinants underlying the differential recognition of thyroid hormones between MCT8 and MCT10.Structure and mechanism of T4 transport by OATP1C1The structure of human OATP1C1 bound with T4, determined at 2.3 Å resolution, exhibits a similar MFS outward-open conformation with an extracellular Kazal domain, inserted between TM9 and TM10 (Figures 3A and S4; Table S1). This domain, a conserved structural module commonly found in serine protease inhibitors, is also present in all OATP transporters35–37, although its exact role within OATPs remains unclear. The long extracellular loop connecting TM3 and TM4 (ECL2, residues 141–183), is absent in the electron density map. Notably, this loop contains two cysteines, C154 and C178, which form disulfide bonds with C480 from the Kazal domain and C624 from an extended hairpin loop between TM11 and TM12, respectively, according to homology with OATP1B1 structures36,37 (Figure 3A). Consequently, the NTD and CTD are linked through disulfide bonds in OATPs, which is unusual among MFS transporters16. However, the high flexibility of this loop does not appear to hinder the conformational changes associated with the rocker-switch mechanism characteristic of MFS transport cycles. Indeed, the C178S/C624S double mutant shows only a mild effect on the uptake of [125I]-T4 compared to the WT, suggesting that these interdomain disulfide bonds are dispensable for transporter function (Figures 3E and S3B).Unlike MCT8, whose cavity is sealed within the membrane, OATP1C1 in its outward-open conformation features a central cavity with a large lateral opening in the outer leaflet of the membrane, formed by TM2 and TM11 between the two halves (Figures 3A, S4M and S4N). Previously, lipid-like densities were observed within this lateral entrance in OATP1B136, likely representing a common feature among OATPs. This feature suggests that T4 can enter its translocation path either from the extracellular opening or by diffusion through this lateral gate due to its amphipathic properties. In our structure, an additional, narrower lateral opening is seen between TM5 and TM8 on the opposite side, traversed by a sterol molecule (Figures 3A, 3B, S4O and S4P). The density fits best with cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS), which was added during early protein purification steps (Figure S4J). The sterol rings of CHS pack against the aromatic side chain of F240 from TM5 (Figure 3A), suggesting that membrane lipids may play a role in regulating the alternating conformation transitions necessary for transport. The F240A mutation completely abolishes T4 uptake, highlighting the importance of maintaining the integrity of this lateral gate (Figures 3E and S3B).The central cavity in OATP1C1 exhibits both electropositive and hydrophobic surface characteristics, a conserved feature among OATPs consistent with their role in transporting amphiphilic organic anions36,37 (Figure 3B). Given the broad range of chemically distinct compounds transported by OATPs, this central cavity likely mostly filters out organic cations and accounts for substrate promiscuity as seen in OATP1B136. In contrast, T4 primarily binds horizontally to a smaller side pocket formed by TM7, TM8, and TM10 in the CTD, a site previously shown to engage estrone 3-sulfate (E1S) in OATP1B1 and less conserved among OATPs36,37 (Figures 3A–3D). The outer diiodophenol ring of T4 is deeply inserted into this pocket, while the amino carboxylic end remains exposed to the central cavity. Although the current 2.3 Å map clearly resolves the hydroxyl group and four individual iodine atoms of T4, poor electron density was observed for the alanine side chain (Figure 3C), suggesting that OATP1C1 does not specifically interact with the negative charge of T4, which is instead loosely enveloped by the electropositive environment. Additional lipid-like densities were revealed beneath T4 at the bottom of the central cavity (Figure S4I). While the exact identity of these densities remains unknown, it is possible that certain lipophilic molecules could be co-transported with T4.The T4 binding pocket is predominantly composed of hydrophobic residues that tightly enclose the outer diiodophenol ring of T4, while the inner ring is partially exposed (Figure 3D). This structural arrangement indicates that the two iodine substitutions on the outer ring are more critical for thyroid hormone binding. Reverse triiodothyronine (rT3), a T4 metabolite, differs from T3 by having two iodine atoms on the outer ring and one on the inner ring (Figure 1A). Consistent with structural predictions, OATP1C1 exhibits the highest transport efficiency for T4 and rT3, while showing markedly reduced activity for T35,18.In addition to hydrophobic interactions, the hydroxyl group of T4 on the outer ring forms a hydrogen bond with the positively charged K376 from TM7, as well as additional hydrogen bonds with N399 from TM8 and S562 from TM10 (Figure 3D). These three polar residues are positioned in the innermost part of the binding pocket. The K376A mutation completely abolished [125I]-T4 uptake activity without altering the surface expression level compared to WT (Figures 3E and S3B). In contrast, the N399A mutation led to reduced cell surface expression and a significant decrease in T4 uptake (Figures 3E and S3B). Notably, K376 is unique to OATP1C1 and is not present in other OATPs (Figure S5A). Among the eight OATP transporters we tested, only OATP1C1 displayed robust T4 uptake activity (Figure S5B), underscoring the critical role of K376 in recognizing T4 as the substrate. Therefore, OATP1C1 and MCT8 employ distinct substrate recognition mechanisms for thyroid hormones. OATP1C1 primarily interacts with the hydroxyl group of the hormone through hydrogen bond formation, whereas MCT8 recognizes the carboxylate terminal at the opposite end. These interactions, along with hydrophobic contacts, serve as their respective binding determinants.An extracellular allosteric regulatory site in OATP1C1As an attempt to explore an alternative conformation of OATP1C1, we expressed and purified the F240A mutant (OATP1C1F240A). This mutation was designed to eliminate the bound CHS seen in the outward-opened conformation of the WT structure, which would otherwise block the rotation between the NTD and the CTD, thereby preventing the conformational transition. We prepared the sample in the presence of estrone 3-sulfate (E1S) and solved its structure at 2.6 Å resolution (Figures 4A and S6; Table S1). Unlike the previously reported inward-open conformation of E1S-bound OATP1B1 structures36,37, OATP1C1F240A, however, maintained an outward-open conformation similar to the WT, with weaker detergent-like electron density observed at the former CHS binding site (Figure S6J). E1S was found to bind the same side pocket within the transmembrane domain as T4, with nearly identical pocket-forming residues except for minor side chain rotameric adjustments (Figures 4A, 4C and 4D). The sulfate group of E1S was buried, forming polar interactions with K376, N399 and S562—three residues that also coordinate the hydroxyl group of T4 in the WT structure (Figures 4C and 4D). In addition to reducing [125I]-T4 transport, mutating these residues individually also impaired the uptake of [3H]-E1S (Figures 4E and S3B), further confirming their importance in recognizing polar moieties from diverse substrates. Notably, despite OATP1C1 and OATP1B1 adopting distinct conformations when bound to E1S, superimposition of their CTD structures revealed that E1S binds in different orientations within their cavities (Figure S6K–S6M). This variation is attributed to differences in the pocket-forming residues across OATPs, while the general amphiphilic nature of the pockets is preserved. Among the eight OATP transporters tested, OATP1C1 expression led to only a modest increase in [3H]-E1S uptake, in contrast to the most robust uptake observed with OATP1B1 and OATP2B1 (Figure S5C).The most surprising finding is the identification of two additional E1S molecules bound within the rearranged near-extracellular region of the NTD besides the trafficking E1S located within the transmembrane domain (Figures 4A and 4B). All helices in the NTD, except TM5, contribute to the binding site formation, along with ECL1–3. In the absence of E1S, the upper segment of TM1 bends toward TM3 and TM4 through hydrophobic interactions as seen in the T4-bound structure (Figures 5A and 5B). R73, positioned at the extracellular end of TM1, appears to act as an anchor to further stabilize this bent helical conformation, by forming water-mediated hydrogen bonds with Q130 from TM3, D252 from ECL3 and W274 from TM6, as well as a direct hydrogen bond with the carbonyl oxygen of E134 from ECL2 (Figure 5B). E1S binding breaks these interactions, causing the dramatic outward movement of this bent segment of TM1, transforming it into a straight helix, with the most pronounced Cα displacement of more than 10 Å observed for R73 (Figures 5C and S6Q). ECL1 also undergoes conformational rearrangement, accompanied by slight rigid body movements of ECL2 and ECL3, and side chain rotameric switches of W187 and F191 on TM4 (Figure S6Q). These conformational changes collectively create a hydrophobic cavity with a wide extracellular opening, wherein two molecules of E1S bind simultaneously (Figures 4B and 5C). The steroid ring A of one E1S molecule packs against the ring D of the other, leaving their negatively charged sulfate groups pointing towards the aqueous environment in opposite directions (Figure 5C). The steroid core structures are largely shielded by hydrophobic residues, while the edges remain exposed to the solvent. Unlike the previously reported inward-open conformation of E1S-bound OATP1B1 structures36,37, OATP1C1F240A, however, maintained an outward-open conformation similar to the WT, with weaker detergent-like electron density observed at the former CHS binding site (Figure S6J). All helices in the NTD, except TM5, contribute to the binding site formation, along with ECL1–3. In the absence of E1S, the upper segment of TM1 bends toward TM3 and TM4 through hydrophobic interactions as seen in the T4-bound structure (Figures 5A and 5B). R73, positioned at the extracellular end of TM1, appears to act as an anchor to further stabilize this bent helical conformation, by forming water-mediated hydrogen bonds with Q130 from TM3, D252 from ECL3 and W274 from TM6, as well as a direct hydrogen bond with the carbonyl oxygen of E134 from ECL2 (Figure 5B). E1S binding breaks these interactions, causing the dramatic outward movement of this bent segment of TM1, transforming it into a straight helix, with the most pronounced Cα displacement of more than 10 Å observed for R73 (Figures 5C and S6Q). ECL1 also undergoes conformational rearrangement, accompanied by slight rigid body movements of ECL2 and ECL3, and side chain rotameric switches of W187 and F191 on TM4 (Figure S6Q). These conformational changes collectively create a hydrophobic cavity with a wide extracellular opening, wherein two molecules of E1S bind simultaneously (Figures 4B and 5C). The steroid ring A of one E1S molecule packs against the ring D of the other, leaving their negatively charged sulfate groups pointing towards the aqueous environment in opposite directions (Figure 5C). The steroid core structures are largely shielded by hydrophobic residues, while the edges remain exposed to the solvent. This contrasts with E1S, which engages both sites simultaneously. In summary, we uncovered an extracellular allosteric site in OATP1C1 for small molecule binding, which, to our best knowledge, is unique among all the SLC transporter structures solved up to date. Furthermore, we validate its regulatory role by demonstrating that an allosteric inhibitor binding to this site modulates transporter activity.

An extracellular allosteric regulatory site in OATP1C1

As an attempt to explore an alternative conformation of OATP1C1, we expressed and purified the F240A mutant (OATP1C1F240A). This mutation was designed to eliminate the bound CHS seen in the outward-opened conformation of the WT structure, which would otherwise block the rotation between the NTD and the CTD, thereby preventing the conformational transition. We prepared the sample in the presence of estrone 3-sulfate (E1S) and solved its structure at 2.6 Å resolution (Figures 4A and S6; Table S1). Unlike the previously reported inward-open conformation of E1S-bound OATP1B1 structures36,37, OATP1C1F240A, however, maintained an outward-open conformation similar to the WT, with weaker detergent-like electron density observed at the former CHS binding site (Figure S6J). E1S was found to bind the same side pocket within the transmembrane domain as T4, with nearly identical pocket-forming residues except for minor side chain rotameric adjustments (Figures 4A, 4C and 4D). The sulfate group of E1S was buried, forming polar interactions with K376, N399 and S562—three residues that also coordinate the hydroxyl group of T4 in the WT structure (Figures 4C and 4D). In addition to reducing [125I]-T4 transport, mutating these residues individually also impaired the uptake of [3H]-E1S (Figures 4E and S3B), further confirming their importance in recognizing polar moieties from diverse substrates. DISCUSSIONAlthough fundamental for physiological processes, free serum concentrations of thyroid hormones remain remarkably low, typically in the picomolar range3. This raises a critical question: how do cells efficiently take up these hormones at such minimal concentrations? In present work, we determined high-resolution cryo-EM structures of two essential brain thyroid hormone transporters, MCT8 and OATP1C1, bound with T3 and T4, respectively. Our work elucidates the structural basis for the highest specificities of these transporters towards thyroid hormones among known transporters, although their detailed recognition patterns vary. In MCT8, the shape of thyroid hormones precisely matches the bottom portion of the central substrate cavity that does not discriminate between T3 and T4. Such a high degree of shape complementarity observed in MCT8 may account for its high affinity as a thyroid hormone transporter. Our structural and mutagenesis analyses support the previous notion that MCT8 functions as a uniporter and explains disease mutation in AHDS (Figure 7A). In contrast, T4 binds to a smaller side pocket in OATP1C1 and remains partially exposed. The electropositive central cavity in OATP1C1 may further compensate for the binding by attracting electronegative thyroid hormones through its large extracellular and lateral entrances, thereby increasing their local concentration to facilitate transport. The D252N mutation in OATP1C1, located in ECL3 (Figure S5A), has been identified as a clinical case causing brain-specific hypometabolism and neurodegeneration9. Our structure suggests that this mutation does not alter the transporter’s function but it has been reported to impair its surface expression9, thereby reducing T4 uptake. It has remained controversial whether OATPs require an energy-driven force or a counter ion for transport38–44. Further studies utilizing proteoliposome reconstitution are necessary to fully understand their precise transport mechanisms.The T3 analog 3,3’,5-triiodothyroacetic acid (TRIAC, Figure 1A) is currently in clinical trials for patients with AHDS45,46. As a thyroid receptor agonist, TRIAC can cross the BBB independently of MCT8, offering therapeutic potential to rescue MCT8 deficiency. Structural analysis of MCT8 reveals that key residues involved in recognizing the alanine group of T3 are unable to establish optimal interactions with TRIAC’s shorter acetic side chain, explaining why MCT8 does not transport TRIAC47. Although it is conceivable that OATP1C1 could transport TRIAC due to its lack of specificity for this carboxyl terminal, OATP1C1 is not expressed in the human BBB15. Recent screening efforts have identified several candidate transporters capable of mediating TRIAC uptake, including some that also transport thyroid hormones48,49. Expanding these screening strategies could help uncover additional thyroid hormone transporters, which may contribute to peripheral hyperthyroidism associated with AHDS. The structures of MCT8 and OATP1C1 complexed with thyroid hormones provide a structural template to inform the systematic prediction of such transporters, collectively advancing our understanding of tissue-specific thyroid hormone homeostasis and revealing potential therapeutic targets.We identified an extracellular allosteric site in OATP1C1 that is conserved across the OATP family (Figure S5A). This region has been implicated in pH sensing and bicarbonate regulation in certain OATP members37,39 (Figure 7B), and we demonstrated its potential for small-molecule modulation. Given the pivotal role of OATPs in mediating drug-drug interactions (DDIs)17, it will be important to investigate further how this extracellular site may contribute to DDIs. Detailed functional characterization of its regulatory role could have broader implications, potentially extending to other SLC transporters, and could pave the way for innovative strategies in the development of allosteric therapeutics targeting these transporters.

LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDYWhile our MCT8 structure and functional assays offer mechanistic insights into AHDS mutations affecting T3 recognition such as R371C and D424N, we note that other reported AHDS mutations may prevent MCT8 protein trafficking to the plasma membrane, likely due to impaired protein folding. These defects in protein trafficking or folding are not directly explained by our structural data. Our structural analysis reveals the shape and chemical complementarities that make MCT8 an ideal, highly specific thyroid hormone transporter. However, we cannot absolutely rule out the possibility that MCT8 could also transport other unidentified substrates whose absence contributes to the severe symptoms of AHDS, particularly certain amphiphilic monocarboxylate molecules.

The data processing procedures for cryo-EM datasets of MCT8-T3, OATP1C1-T4, OATP1C1F240A-E1S and OATP1C1F240A-E1G complexes are summarized in Figures S1, S4, S6 and S7, respectively. Unless otherwise noted, the data processing method is consistent across all three datasets. The beam-induced image motion within dose-fractionated movies was corrected using MotionCor251. The motion correction was conducted with 2× binning to achieve target pixel size of 0.83 Å (MCT8-T3 and OATP1C1-T4) or 0.82 Å (OATP1C1F240A-E1S and OATP1C1F240A-E1G). CTFFIND452 was used to determine the defocus value and astigmatism of each motion-corrected micrograph. Micrographs with a resolution better than 4 Å, as reported by CIFFIND4, were selected for further data processing. Particle picking was performed using Topaz53. The data processing was carried out using RELION454.

For all datasets, particles picked using Topaz were extracted into 96×96 pixels (2× binned from 192×192 pixels in original motion-corrected micrographs) for several rounds of 2D classification and selection. Subsequently, the selected particles were re-extracted into 256×256 pixels without binning for further processing. An initial model was generated using the ab-initio reconstruction in cryoSPARC55 except for OATP1C1F240A-E1S/E1G that utilized the cryo-EM 3D reconstruction of OATP1C1-T4 as its initial model. Iterative rounds of 2D classification and 3D classification were performed until a small population of highly homogeneous particles were obtained for 3D auto-refinement. To achieve optimal data processing results, a modified version of RELION4 with implementation of the composite masking method27 (https://github.com/jiangjiansen/relion_composite_masks) was employed for 3D classification and 3D auto-refinement. A composite mask includes a regular reference mask (shown in magenta in Figures S1, S4, S6 and S7), that covered only the protein, and a second mask (shown in grey in Figures S1, S4, S6 and S7) that covered the detergent micelle. Particle polishing and CTF refinement were conducted before the final high-resolution 3D reconstructions.

Cryo-EM images of MCT8-T3 particles exhibited a preferred orientation in which most particles were at the top views, leading to overestimated and anisotropic resolutions. To address this issue, excess particles on the top views were randomly discarded after particle polishing to obtain a more uniform orientation distribution (Figure S1). This subset of particles with “rebalanced” orientations were sent to cryoSPARC55,56 for the final 3D reconstruction using non-uniform 3D refinement.

Cellular uptake assaysHEK293 cells stably expressing transporters of interest were cultured in either 12-well or 24-well plates. Prior to experiments, the cells were washed twice with warm DMEM medium (Corning). For [125I]-T3 uptakes, the cells were incubated with a final concentration of 0.5 nM of [125I]-T3 (Revvity, part number NEX110X100UC) in DMEM supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) BSA at 37 °C for 15 mins and washed three times with DMEM supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) BSA and 10 nM cold T3. For [125I]-T4 uptakes, the cells were incubated with a final concentration of 0.5 nM of [125I]-T4 (Revvity, part number NEX111X100UC) in DMEM supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) BSA at 37 °C for 30 mins and washed three times with DMEM supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) BSA and 10 nM cold T4. For [3H]-E1S uptakes, the cells were incubated with a final concentration of 0.18 μM of [3H]-E1S (Revvity, part number NET203250UC) and varying concentrations of E1G in DMEM at 37 °C for 15 mins. Following incubation, cells were washed three times with DMEM supplemented with 4.2 μM cold E1S. After washing, the cells were lysed in TBST buffer [50 mM Tris, pH 7.5; 150 mM NaCl; 0.2% Triton X-100] and mixed with 4 mL ULTIMA GOLD™ liquid scintillation cocktails (Revvity). The radioactivity readings were recorded using a Beckman Coulter LS6500 liquid scintillation counter. A standard curve was generated using known quantities of [125I]-T3, [125I]-T4 or [3H]-E1S to convert counts per minute into the respective uptake amounts. Results were presented as mean ± SEM of three independent experiments.As noted above, [125I]-T3 and [125I]-T4 cellular uptake assays were conducted in the presence of 0.1% (w/v) BSA, similar to established protocols12,13. Under these conditions, a substantial portion of the radioactive substrates were bound to BSA. Although E1G inhibits OATP1C1-mediated transport of [3H]-E1S, it did not appear to inhibit [125I]-T4 transport under these assay conditions. One plausible explanation is that high concentrations of steroids such as E1G compete with [125I]-T4 for BSA binding, thereby increasing the free (unbound) fraction of [125I]-T4 available for transport. In contrast, the [3H]-E1S inhibition assay was performed without BSA and therefore did not exhibit this issue. Attempts to perform [125I]-T4 uptake assays without BSA; however, resulted in high background signals likely caused by nonspecific binding of T4 to the cell membrane, making the data difficult to interpret reliably.
