Abstract

We propose an approach for the identification of mutant genes for rare diseases in single cases of unknown etiology. All genes with rare biologically significant variants sorted from individual exome data are tested further for profiling of their spatial-temporal and cell/tissue specific expression compared to that of their paralogs. We developed a simple bioinformatics tool (“Essential Paralogue by Expression” (EPbE)) for such analysis. Here, we present rare clinical forms of early ataxia with cerebellar hypoplasia. Using whole-exome sequencing and the EPbE tool, we identified two novel mutant genes previously not associated with congenital human diseases. In Family I, the unique missense mutation (p.Lys258Glu) was found in the LRCH2 gene inherited in an X-linked manner. p.Lys258Glu occurs in the evolutionarily invariant site of the leucine-rich repeat domain of LRCH2. In Family II and Family III, the identical genetic variant was found in the CSMD1 gene inherited as an autosomal-recessive trait. The variant leads to amino acid substitution p.Gly2979Ser in a highly conserved region of the complement-interacting domain of CSMD1. The LRCH2 gene for Family I patients (in which congenital cerebellar hypoplasia was associated with demyelinating polyneuropathy) is expressed in Schwann and precursor Schwann cells and predominantly over its paralogous genes in the developing cerebellar cortex. The CSMD1 gene is predominantly expressed over its paralogous genes in the cerebellum, specifically in the period of late childhood. Thus, the comparative spatial-temporal expression of the selected genes corresponds to the neurological manifestations of the disease.

Introduction

Early-onset ataxia is a rare heterogeneous group of diseases that manifests as motor developmental delay from infancy and impaired coordination. In many cases, early-onset and congenital ataxia are accompanied by intellectual disability and the involvement of other organs and tissues. Congenital pathologies inherited in an X-linked recessive manner caused by cerebellar hypoplasia in the embryonic period, leading to abnormal coordination without intellectual disability, were described in a small number of publications [ 1 , 2 ]. In one Danish family, X-linked spinocerebellar ataxia 1 (SCAX1) disease is caused by a mutation in the ATP2B3 gene (MIM: 300014) encoding a plasma membrane Ca 2+ ATPase involved in the active transport of calcium ions from neuronal cells [ 3 ]. In another case of the large Buryat family, a pathogenic variant was found in the mitochondrial ABCB7 transporter (MIM: 300135) involved in iron transport and inclusion in the heme group [ 2 , 4 ]. Most autosomal-recessive cases of early-onset ataxia with cerebellar hypoplasia are complicated by intellectual disability, especially diseases with infantile onset, whereas with the later development of pathology, intellectual disability is less pronounced, as in patients with autosomal recessive spinocerebellar ataxia 6, 19 and 24 (SCAR6, SCAR19 and SCAR24) [ 5 – 7 ]. However, the clinical picture of the listed autosomal-recessive diseases differs from that in the cases presented here.

In this work, we investigate new clinically similar cases of ataxia with cerebellar hypoplasia and onset in infancy or puberty. In the first studied family (Family I) with two affected brothers, the clinical presentation of ataxia in infancy was accompanied by additional signs of demyelinating polyneuropathy. Two new genetic variants in the FAAH2 (MIM: 300654) and LRCH2 genes were revealed. FAAH2 encodes fatty acid amide hydrolase 2, which is involved in the catabolism of numerous bioactive lipids and fatty acid amides [ 8 ]. Our further analysis predicted that variants in FAAH2 are unlikely to be pathogenic. LRCH2 encodes a leucine-rich repeat and calponin homology domain-containing protein and is expressed in the developing brain, especially in the regions responsible for motor functions. It has not previously been associated with any pathology. There is only one mention without details of variant rs1333479504 in acceptor region in patient with global development delay [ 9 ]. We suppose that a novel variant in a highly conserved region of eight leucine-rich repeats (LRR8) in LRCH2 is a genetic cause in our cases of congenital ataxia with cerebellar hypoplasia and demyelinating polyneuropathy.

In the other two unrelated families (Family II and Family III), the first symptoms of the disease were seen in late childhood or puberty: the disease was characterized by motor impairment and discoordination. In both families, a nonsynonymous substitution in the CSMD1 gene (MIM: 608397) was identified, the functions of which are associated with innate immunity and neuronal development but are not clearly understood [ 10 – 12 ]. CSMD1 encodes CUB and Sushi Multiple Domains 1 protein and is associated with schizophrenia [ 13 ], but there is no information about congenital pathology caused by its defects. Here, we first report the link between the CSMD1 and cerebellum development and present this gene as a candidate factor for congenital autosomal-recessive cerebellar ataxia.

Methods

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Research Center of Neurology, and all participants provided informed consent. Blood samples of all the subjects were obtained and genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using a QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Exome library preparation and sequencing for Families I and II were performed by Genetico (Moscow, Russia). Library preparation and exome enrichment were performed using a TruSeq Exome kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Exome library preparation and exome enrichment for Family III were performed using SureSelect Human All Exon V (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed on a HiSeq 2000/2500 System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Raw reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh37/hg19 using bwa mem software with an average depth of coverage of 112-135X for Families I and II. For Family III, an average depth of coverage of 17X was obtained, although even distribution of the alignment was achieved in view of the excess amount of DNA taken for exome library preparation. In all libraries, more than 98% of the target exome loci were sequenced. The variants were called and annotated by GATK and VEP software [ 14 , 15 ]. The validation of candidate variants was performed using Sanger sequencing on a 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). The prediction of protein spatial structure was performed by Phyre2 and iTasser software [ 16 , 17 ]. The alignment of predicted models was obtained using The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (Version 1.2r3pre, Schrödinger, LLC). Paralog expression analysis was carried out using an online program “Essential Paralogue by Expression” (EPbE) developed by us. The EPbE program is written in R programming language.

Discussion

In this study, a genetic analysis was performed in families with a rare form of congenital ataxia, for which we developed an approach based on the comparative analysis of paralogs, including the analysis of published data of functional studies and the analysis of expression in developing cerebellum and other brain regions by a specially written EPbE program. In the first investigated Family I, two rare variants were selected, located on the X chromosome at a great distance of 57 Mb from each other. The first variant identified in the FAAH2 is located extremely close to another previously identified pathogenic variant in one Caucasian patient with symptoms of ataxia and autism [ 26 ]. Patients from two families share common symptoms: motor development delayed, ataxia, dysarthria, tremor and muscle hypotonia. The ability to learn is preserved in both cases. The main difference is the absence of autism, anxiety, depression and psychogenic seizures. Moreover, cerebellar hypoplasia and demyelinating polyneuropathy were revealed in our patients. Whereas in the case of Canadian authors, only prominent cisterna magna and delayed myelination were mentioned. Differences in the clinical picture of patients could be associated with a different effect of p.Ala458Ser and p.Lys460Thr substitutions on the FAAH2 protein, because the second is less conserved. The significance of FAAH2 gene for development is controversial. In one study nonsense variant of p.W392* was found in patient with lethal phenotype (Zellweger syndrome) [ 52 ]. But in another study the lack of the C-terminal region of FAAH2, as result of nonsense variant of p.E432*, may not lead to clinical manifestations [ 53 ]. Moreover, our analysis showed that FAAH2 probably plays an insignificant role in the embryonic period of cerebellar development. Thus, we believe that FAAH2 is not the major factor in the development of cerebellar hypoplasia in Family I.

The second novel variant, identified in the Family I in the LRCH2 . To date, no family case has been described with a congenital pathology caused by a defect in this gene. Due to the paralog expression approach, it was possible to assess the significance of LRCH2 in the embryonic development of the cerebellum. Thus, the period of the highest gene expression and low expression of its paralogs in the embryonic cerebellum coincides with the development of pathology in patients. Additionally, in the prenatal period, high expression of LRCH2 was observed in comparison with paralogs in other parts of the brain involved in the control of motor movements related to cortical sensorimotor circuits and frontostriatal circuits (Figs. 4A , S6 , and S8 ). Single-cell expression data from adult mice showed that LRCH2 is expressed particularly in interneurons, confirming its involvement in motor circuits in the brain [ 47 ] (Fig. S12 ). However, there were no obvious neurological changes in these areas in the patients. This is possibly associated with a strong compensatory effect or an identified variant that only partially affects the function of the gene product. The expression of LRCH2 in the dorsal root ganglion and Schwann cells confirms its involvement in generalized demyelinating lesions in patients from Family I [ 48 , 49 ]. On the other hand, one of the main causes of demyelinating polyneuropathies is autoimmune diseases. Although there are practically no data on the functions of LRCH2 , the LRCH family is involved in the formation of immunity and cell migration [ 29 ].

In two other unrelated Families II and III with a later onset of the disease (in adolescence) but a clinical picture rather similar to that of the Family I, we revealed a novel genetic candidate, the CSMD1 gene. Our analysis revealed that the manifestation of the disease coincides with a period of decreased expression of paralogs of the CSMD1 and its increased expression in comparison with them in the cerebellum. However, differences in the expression of CSMD1 and its paralogs in the cerebral cortex were also observed at this age (Fig. S7 ). It is also quite possible that the high expression of the paralogs in the prenatal period plays a major role in the formation of the brain, and the lack of the CSMD1 in older age leads to fewer disorders. In support of this hypothesis, a patient with an earlier manifestation has a more severe course of the disease.

Interestingly, the analysis of the functions of the CSMD1 and LRCH2 and their paralogs revealed same biological processes, innate immunity, cytoskeleton formation and cell migration [ 27 , 29 ]. The CSMD family have high similarity, are located in the growth cone and are involved in neuronal growth, dendrite branching and the formation of new synapses [ 11 , 33 , 50 ] Interestingly, in both genes, variants are found in extracellular domains involved in interactions with other cellular factors and, most likely, disrupting it. Thus, a disruption in the interaction of CSMD1 with the main component of the C3 compliment because of pathogenic variants may be a result of an increased autoimmune response and premature neuron death and underdevelopment [ 12 ]. A pathogenic variant in the LRCH2 is also located in a region of a protein that is likely to suppress the immune response [ 29 , 31 ]. Since the both genes are highly conserved at the evolutionary and population levels and the absence of public data about homo- and hemizygous mutations lead to a disruption of the reading frame, it is quite possible that a complete absence of them may not be compatible with life. It cannot be ruled out that, due to the presence of expression in different brain regions, other rare variants in these genes can lead to pathologies associated with lesions of not only motor functions but also cognitive functions.

This study identified new genetic factors that can lead to rare forms of early-onset ataxia with cerebellar hypoplasia. The identified variants are located in highly conserved regions of the protein-coding sequence of the LRCH2 and CSMD1 genes, for which hereditary pathologies have not been previously described. Thus, these genes and the genetic variants in these genes are of interest for further study aimed at understanding the molecular biological basis of the formation of motor functions in the brain.
