# Lineage-specific requirements of Alx4 function in craniofacial and hair development.

BACKGROUND: Disruption of ALX4 causes autosomal dominant parietal foramina and
autosomal recessive frontonasal dysplasia with alopecia, but the mechanisms
involving ALX4 in craniofacial and other developmental processes are not well
understood. Although mice carrying distinct mutations in Alx4 have been
previously reported, the perinatal lethality of homozygous mutants together with
dynamic patterns of Alx4 expression in multiple tissues have hindered systematic
elucidation of the cellular and molecular mechanisms involving Alx4 in
organogenesis and disease pathogenesis.
RESULTS: We report generation of Alx4f/f conditional mice and show that
tissue-specific Cre-mediated inactivation of Alx4 in cranial neural crest and
limb bud mesenchyme, respectively, recapitulated craniofacial and limb
developmental defects as found in Alx4-null mice but without affecting postnatal
survival. While Alx4-null mice that survive postnatally exhibited dorsal
alopecia, mice lacking Alx4 function in the neural crest lineage exhibited a
highly restricted region of hair loss over the anterior skull whereas mice
lacking Alx4 in the cranial mesoderm lineage exhibited normal hair development,
suggesting that Alx4 plays partly redundant roles in multiple cell lineages
during hair follicle development.
CONCLUSION: The Alx4f/f mice provide a valuable resource for systematic
investigation of cell type- and stage-specific function of ALX family
transcription factors in development and disease.

Introduction

The Aristaless-related homeobox (ALX) genes encode DNA-binding transcription factors containing a Paired-like homeodomain and a highly conserved C-terminal OAR domain, named after the Drosophila Otp, Aristaless, and Rax proteins1,2. In addition, heterozygous deletion or disruption of ALX4 has been associated with parietal foramina (OMIM 609597), a defect in ossification of the skull vault11,15,16. The cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying the ALX-related developmental disorders are not well understood yet5,17.

Alx4 was first discovered in mouse and expressed mainly in the developing craniofacial region and limb buds in mouse embryos8,18. Alx4 expression was also detected in various mesenchymal condensations, such as the dermal papilla of hair follicles19. Results and DiscussionThe mouse Alx4 gene spans four exons, with the homeobox sequence split into exon2 and exon3. The exon2 is 311 bp long and flanked by two large introns. Deletion of exon2 is expected to cause frameshift and the resultant mutant mRNA, if translated, would generate a nonfunctional truncated protein lacking both the homeodomain and the OAR domain. F1 mice carrying the 5’ loxP insertion were intercrossed and F2 mice homozygous for the 5’ loxP insertion were detected at Mendelian ratio and did not show any phenotypic abnormality. Following sequencing verification, F2 mice homozygous for the 5’ loxP insertion were intercrossed to generate zygotes for CRISPR/Cas9-mediated introduction of the 3’ loxP site into intron2 (see Experimental Procedures section). Founder mice were crossed to C57BL/6J inbred mice to generate mice heterozygous for the Alx4f allele containing correctly integrated 5’ loxP (intron1) and 3’ loxP (intron2) sequences in cis, verified by direct sequencing of long PCR products spanning both integration sites (see Experimental Procedures section). Subsequently, Alx4f/f mice were generated from intercrossing Alx4f/+ male and female mice (Fig. 1E, F). Results and Discussion

The mouse Alx4 gene spans four exons, with the homeobox sequence split into exon2 and exon3. The exon2 is 311 bp long and flanked by two large introns. Deletion of exon2 is expected to cause frameshift and the resultant mutant mRNA, if translated, would generate a nonfunctional truncated protein lacking both the homeodomain and the OAR domain. Thus, our strategy to generate the Alx4 conditional allele in mice is to insert a pair of loxP sites into intron1 and intron2, respectively, using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing25,26 (Fig. 1A–D).

Initial attempt at introducing both loxP sites through sequential electroporation of C57BL/6J zygotes resulted in founder mice that produced F1 progeny carrying only one correctly integrated loxP sequence at either the intron1 (5’ loxP) or intron2 (3’ loxP) location. F1 mice carrying the 5’ loxP insertion were intercrossed and F2 mice homozygous for the 5’ loxP insertion were detected at Mendelian ratio and did not show any phenotypic abnormality. Following sequencing verification, F2 mice homozygous for the 5’ loxP insertion were intercrossed to generate zygotes for CRISPR/Cas9-mediated introduction of the 3’ loxP site into intron2 (see Experimental Procedures section). Founder mice were crossed to C57BL/6J inbred mice to generate mice heterozygous for the Alx4f allele containing correctly integrated 5’ loxP (intron1) and 3’ loxP (intron2) sequences in cis, verified by direct sequencing of long PCR products spanning both integration sites (see Experimental Procedures section). Subsequently, Alx4f/f mice were generated from intercrossing Alx4f/+ male and female mice (Fig. 1E, F). The Alx4f/f mice were indistinguishable phenotypically from their wildtype and heterozygous littermates, indicating that the loxP sites do not interfere with Alx4 gene expression or function in the absence of Cre-mediated recombination.

To validate that Cre mediated recombination can effectively inactivate the Alx4f allele, we crossed the Alx4f/+ mice with EIIa-Cre transgenic mice27. Crossing the F1 Alx4f/+;EIIa-Cre progeny with C57BL/6J inbred mice resulted in generation of mice heterozygous for the Alx4 exon2 deletion allele (Alx4ΔE2) segregated from the EIIa-Cre transgene. Sequencing of PCR genotyping products demonstrated successful deletion of exon2 in the Alx4ΔE2 allele. Crossing Alx4ΔE2/+ heterozygous mice with Alx4lst−2J/+ mice (The Jackson Laboratory) generated Alx4ΔE2/lst−2J trans-heterozygous mice, which exhibited defect in skull ossification and preaxial polydactyly on all four limbs (Fig. 2A–F) similar as previously reported for Alx4lst−2J/lst−2J mutants21. Three out of 9 Alx4ΔE2/lst−2J trans-heterozygous pups exhibited gastroschisis at birth (data not shown), a body wall defect previously reported in Alx4−/− mice8. The Alx4ΔE2/lst−2J trans-heterozygous mice that survive postnatally (3 out of 23 mice in three litters) exhibited smaller body size and dorsal alopecia (Fig. 3), similar as previously reported for Alx4lst−2J/lst−2J mice21. These data indicate that Cre-mediated deletion of the floxed exon2 completely inactivates the function of the Alx4f allele.

To further validate that Alx4f/f mice enable tissue-specific analysis of Alx4 function, we crossed Alx4f/f mice with Prrx1-Cre transgenic mice, which express Cre specifically in the developing limb bud mesenchyme and a subset of craniofacial mesenchyme cells28. All Alx4f/f;Prrx1-Cre mice survive postnatally and exhibited partial hair loss over the anterior fontanel of the skull and the limbs, in addition to preaxial polydactyly of all four limbs (n=6) (Fig. 4A, B). Analysis of skeletal preparations of neonates showed that the Alx4f/f;Prrx1-Cre mice were born with a large anterior fontanel, preaxial polydactyly, and shortened radius and tibia bones (n=6) (Fig. 4C–F). The hair and skeletal defects in Alx4f/f;Prrx1-Cre mice correlated well with the patterns of Prrx1-Cre expression in embryonic craniofacial and limb mesenchyme28, indicating that the Alx4f/f mice provide a valuable resource for genetic dissection of cell autonomous functions of Alx4.

Whereas much of the craniofacial bones and cartilages arise from neural crest derived mesenchyme cells, the parietal bones are derived from cranial mesoderm29. We crossed Alx4f/f mice with Wnt1-Cre transgenic mice30 to investigate the effect of neural crest lineage-specific inactivation of Alx4 on skull morphogenesis. All Alx4f/f;Wnt1-Cre mice survive postnatally (n=14). Analysis of skeletal preparations of neonates showed a large gap between the paired frontal bones (n=6) (Fig. 5A, B). Micro-CT analysis of the craniofacial bones at postnatal day 15 (n=6 each for control and mutant samples) showed that the Alx4f/f;Wnt1-Cre mice had hypoplastic frontal bones with an ossification center at the anterior midline of the frontal suture separate from the bilateral frontal bones (Fig. 5C–I). In addition, the Alx4f/f;Wnt1-Cre mice exhibit shortened and flattened nasal bones compared with the Alx4f/+;Wnt1-Cre littermates (Fig. 5G–I).

Since patients heterozygous for loss of ALX4 function exhibit parietal foramina11,16 and since Alx4−/− mice showed defects in ossification of the parietal bones8, we investigated whether Alx4 function is required in the cranial mesoderm lineage for parietal bone development. The Mesp1-Cre mice exhibits specific Cre activity throughout the cranial mesoderm-derived tissues including the parietal bones29. We generated Alx4f/f;Mesp1-Cre mice and found that they were born with a larger gap at the midline between the bilateral parietal bones compared with Alx4f/+;Mesp1-Cre littermates (Fig. 6A, B) (n=8 each for mutant and control samples). By four weeks of age, the midline gap between the parietal bones in the Alx4f/f;Mesp1-Cre mice is filled (Fig. 6C, D) (n=4 each for mutant and control samples), indicating that Alx4 deficiency in the cranial mesoderm lineage caused a delay in parietal bone ossification.

Previous studies showed that FND2 patients had almost complete alopecia whereas both Alx4lst/lst and Alx4lst−2J/lst−2J mutant mice exhibited dorsal alopecia6,21,24. Since hair follicle development and maintenance are known to depend on reciprocal epithelial-mesenchymal interaction and since Alx4 is primarily expressed in the mesenchymal condensations around developing hair follicles, it has been suggested that Alx4 likely primarily act in the mesenchymal compartment to regulate hair follicle development6,19,31,32. Consistent with our hypothesis that deletion of exon2 abolishes Alx4 gene function, we found that Alx4ΔE2/ΔE2 mice exhibit dorsal alopecia (n=4) (Fig. 7A, B). However, we found that Alx4f/f;Wnt1-Cre mice showed a restricted patch of hair loss over the anterior skull (n=10) (Fig. 7C, D) whereas the Alx4f/f;Mesp1-Cre mice exhibited normal hair (n=13) (Fig. 7E, F). It has been shown that Alx4 is also highly expressed in the outer root sheath epithelial cells surrounding the developing hair, in addition to the dermal papilla19. Further study is required to determine if Alx4 expression in the epithelial compartment plays a crucial role in hair growth and regeneration.

In summary, we have successfully generated Alx4f/f conditional mice and demonstrate that these mice provide a valuable resource for investigation of the cellular and molecular mechanisms involving Alx4 in development and disease.

Experimental ProceduresGeneration of mice carrying an Alx4f conditional allele using CRISPR/Cas9 mediated genome editingThe detailed methods for design and syntheses of single-guide RNAs and donor oligonucleotides, and the procedures for generating genome-edited founder mice have been described previously33. Briefly, sgRNA target sites for insertion of loxP sites in the intron1 (sgRNA1 target sequence AGAGGGGTCTTTAGCAGGTG) and intron2 (sgRNA2 target sequence TTGCGTGGTGTTTAGCTGAG) regions, respectively, of the Alx4 gene were selected according to the on- and off-target scores from the CRISPR design web tool (http://CRISPOR.org)34. The oligonucleotide donor templates with asymmetrical homologous arms flanking the loxP sequence were designed according to Paix et al.35. The sgRNAs and donor oligo templates (IDT Ultramers) were chemically synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Coralville, IA, USA). To form the ribonucleoprotein complex (RNP), the individual sgRNA (60 ng/μl) was mixed with the Cas9 protein (IDT; 80 ng/μ;l) in Opti-MEM (ThermoFisher) and incubated at 37°C for 15 min. The donor oligo template was added to the respective RNP at the final concentration of 500 ng/μ;l. The zygotes from superovulated C57BL/6J inbred female mice were first electroporated with 7 μ;l RNP/donor mix targeting the intron1 site on ice using a Genome Editor electroporator (BEX; 30V, 1ms width, and 5 pulses with 1s interval). Two minutes after electroporation, zygotes were moved into 500 μ;l cold M2 medium (Sigma), warmed up to room temperature, and cultured in KSOM (CytoSpring) in a CO2 incubator at 37°C. Four hours later, the zygotes were electroporated again to target the intron2 site and subsequently transferred into the oviductal ampulla of pseudopregnant CD-1 female mice. Although two founder mice carrying loxP integrations at the intron1 and intron2 sites were generated, no progeny from either founder carried both 5’ and 3’ loxP sites in the same allele. Following Sanger sequencing verification, F1 male and female mice hemizygous for the 5’ loxP insertion in intron1 were intercrossed to generate F2 homozygotes. The zygotes from intercrossing homozygous F2 mice were then collected to reintroduce the 3’ loxP insertion into intron2 via electroporation using the sgRNA2 RNP/donor mix, followed by embryo transfer into pseudopregnant CD-1 females for birth. The offspring carrying both 5’ and 3’ loxP insertions in cis were then identified by PCR and Sanger sequencing. The PCR primers are Alx4flox-P1 (5’ GTGTCAGCAGTGAGCAGTCAGGG’ 3) and Alx4flox-P4 (5’ GAGAAAGCAGGGCCACTGAACCGC 3’). Generation of mice carrying an Alx4f conditional allele using CRISPR/Cas9 mediated genome editingThe detailed methods for design and syntheses of single-guide RNAs and donor oligonucleotides, and the procedures for generating genome-edited founder mice have been described previously33. Briefly, sgRNA target sites for insertion of loxP sites in the intron1 (sgRNA1 target sequence AGAGGGGTCTTTAGCAGGTG) and intron2 (sgRNA2 target sequence TTGCGTGGTGTTTAGCTGAG) regions, respectively, of the Alx4 gene were selected according to the on- and off-target scores from the CRISPR design web tool (http://CRISPOR.org)34. The oligonucleotide donor templates with asymmetrical homologous arms flanking the loxP sequence were designed according to Paix et al.35. The sgRNAs and donor oligo templates (IDT Ultramers) were chemically synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Coralville, IA, USA). To form the ribonucleoprotein complex (RNP), the individual sgRNA (60 ng/μl) was mixed with the Cas9 protein (IDT; 80 ng/μ;l) in Opti-MEM (ThermoFisher) and incubated at 37°C for 15 min. The donor oligo template was added to the respective RNP at the final concentration of 500 ng/μ;l. The zygotes from superovulated C57BL/6J inbred female mice were first electroporated with 7 μ;l RNP/donor mix targeting the intron1 site on ice using a Genome Editor electroporator (BEX; 30V, 1ms width, and 5 pulses with 1s interval). Two minutes after electroporation, zygotes were moved into 500 μ;l cold M2 medium (Sigma), warmed up to room temperature, and cultured in KSOM (CytoSpring) in a CO2 incubator at 37°C. Four hours later, the zygotes were electroporated again to target the intron2 site and subsequently transferred into the oviductal ampulla of pseudopregnant CD-1 female mice. Although two founder mice carrying loxP integrations at the intron1 and intron2 sites were generated, no progeny from either founder carried both 5’ and 3’ loxP sites in the same allele. Following Sanger sequencing verification, F1 male and female mice hemizygous for the 5’ loxP insertion in intron1 were intercrossed to generate F2 homozygotes. The zygotes from intercrossing homozygous F2 mice were then collected to reintroduce the 3’ loxP insertion into intron2 via electroporation using the sgRNA2 RNP/donor mix, followed by embryo transfer into pseudopregnant CD-1 females for birth. The offspring carrying both 5’ and 3’ loxP insertions in cis were then identified by PCR and Sanger sequencing. The PCR primers are Alx4flox-P1 (5’ GTGTCAGCAGTGAGCAGTCAGGG’ 3) and Alx4flox-P4 (5’ GAGAAAGCAGGGCCACTGAACCGC 3’).
