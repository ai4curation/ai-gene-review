ABSTRACT

Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) bodies are thought to function in plant defense against insects and pathogens. Recently, a new type of ER body referred to as “leaf ER bodies” (L-ER bodies) was identified in Arabidopsis rosette leaves. L-ER bodies accumulate two β-glucosidases, namely PYK10 and BGLU18, which are characteristic of previously described constitutive ER bodies and inducible ER bodies, respectively. However, it is unclear how the biogenesis of L-ER bodies, which are similar to both constitutive and inducible ER bodies, is regulated. In the present study, we show that the biogenesis of L-ER bodies is regulated by both jasmonate (JA)-dependent and -independent pathways. Confocal imaging analysis revealed the presence of L-ER bodies in the JA insensitive mutant coronatine insensitive 1–1 ( coi1-1 ), which lacks the JA receptor COI1. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction analysis revealed that the expression of BGLU18 mainly depends on the JA signaling pathway while that of PYK10 does not. In addition, expression of the ER body related genes NAI1, NAI2 , and TSA1 was reduced in the coi1-1 mutant relative to the wild type. Taken together, these findings suggest that JA signaling is not necessary for the formation of L-ER bodies, while it is partially required for gene expression of L-ER body components.

KEYWORDS: Arabidopsis thaliana, BGLU18, ER body, JA signaling, PYK10

Text

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) body is a spindle-shaped organelle derived from the ER found in the order Brassicales. The ER body accumulates β-glucosidases that hydrolyze glucosinolates into toxic compounds that function in plant defense against herbivory. In Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis), two types of ER bodies have been identified: constitutive ER bodies and inducible ER bodies. Constitutive ER bodies exist in the epidermal cells of whole seedlings, while inducible ER bodies are induced by wounding or methyl jasmonate (MeJA) treatment in Arabidopsis rosette leaves. 1 The constitutive and inducible ER bodies each accumulate a distinct β-glucosidase: PYK10/BETA-GLUCOSIDASE 23 (BGLU23) and BGLU18, respectively. 2 Notably, the biogenesis of constitutive and inducible ER bodies is differentially regulated. The biogenesis of constitutive ER bodies is controlled by the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) type transcription factor NAI1 in seedlings. 3 NAI1 also controls gene expression of PYK10 and NAI2. PYK10 is a major component of constitutive ER bodies, while NAI2 is required for the formation of constitutive ER bodies from the ER. 4 By contrast, the biogenesis of inducible ER bodies and expression of BGLU18 do not depend on NAI1. 2 Recently, TONSOKU-ASSOCIATING PROTEIN 1 (TSA1), a NAI2 homolog in Arabidopsis, was revealed to play a role in the biogenesis of inducible ER bodies. 5 As the expression of TSA1 is not regulated by NAI1, it was assumed that there were unidentified transcription factor(s) regulating the expressions of BGLU18 and TSA1 downstream of the JA signaling pathway to function in the biogenesis of inducible ER bodies. 6

In addition to the two types of ER bodies described above, a third type of ER body referred to as “leaf ER bodies” (L-ER bodies) was recently identified in Arabidopsis rosette leaves. 7 L-ER bodies occur in three types of epidermal cells: leaf marginal cells, epidermal cells covering the midrib, and giant pavement cells ( Figure 1a ). L-ER bodies accumulate two β-glucosidases, namely BGLU18 and PYK10, which are characteristic of inducible ER bodies and constitutive ER bodies, respectively. The expression of these two β-glucosidases in L-ER bodies is differentially regulated; the expression of PYK10 depends on NAI1, as is the case for constitutive ER bodies, while the expression of BGLU18 does not depend on NAI1. The formation of L-ER bodies depends on NAI1, as is the case for constitutive ER bodies. However, it remains unclear whether L-ER bodies form via induction by JA, similar to the formation of inducible ER bodies.

Figure 1.

To investigate whether L-ER body formation depends on JA signaling, we used the JA insensitive mutant coi1-1 , which lacks the JA receptor CORONATINE INSENSITIVE 1 (COI1) 8 and exhibits defects in JA perception and signaling. Microscopic analysis revealed the presence of L-ER bodies in three types of epidermal cells of coi1-1 rosette leaves, similar to the wild-type (WT) ( Figure 1b ). This result indicates that the biogenesis of L-ER bodies does not depend on the JA signaling pathway, unlike the biogenesis of inducible ER bodies. To confirm the absence of inducible ER bodies in the coi1-1 mutant, rosette leaves of WT and coi1-1 plants were wounded using a toothpick and epidermal regions near the wound sites were then observed. Shortly after wounding, inducible ER bodies were not found in the peripheral regions around the wound site in either plant ( Figure 1c ). At 25 hours after wounding, inducible ER bodies were observed around the wound site in rosette leaves of WT plants but not in in coi1-1 mutant plants ( Figure 1c ). These results demonstrate that inducible ER bodies are not formed by wound stress in coi1-1 mutant. This finding is consistent with a previous report showing that inducible ER bodies were not formed in rosette leaves of the coi1 mutant treated with MeJA. 1

To determine the expression of genes involved in the biogenesis of ER bodies in the coi1-1 mutant, we performed real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis. The expression level of BGLU18 was remarkably reduced in the coi1-1 mutant relative to the WT ( Figure 2a ), whereas the expression level of PYK10 was similar ( Figure 2a ). These results suggest that BGLU18 expression depends mainly on the JA signaling pathway, whereas PYK10 expression does not. The expression levels of NAI1 and NAI2 decreased by nearly 50% in the coi1-1 mutant compared with the WT ( Figure 2a ). Considering that NAI2 expression is regulated by NAI1, this result suggests that the NAI1 pathway is partially under the control of the JA signaling pathway. Furthermore, the expression of TSA1 decreased to ~20% in the coi1-1 mutant compared with the WT ( Figure 2a ), suggesting that regulation of TSA1 gene expression is mostly under the control of the JA signaling pathway.

Figure 2.

Based on the above findings, we propose a novel model of L-ER body biogenesis ( Figure 2b ). Previously, we demonstrated that NAI1 is required for the biogenesis of L-ER bodies and expression of PYK10 . 7 In the present study, we showed that the formation of L-ER bodies is independent of the JA signaling pathway. However, the JA signaling pathway affects the expressions of several genes involved in L-ER body biogenesis. Expression of BGLU18 and TSA1 may be regulated by an unknown transcription factor(s) downstream of the JA signaling pathway. Interestingly, the JA signaling pathway partially affected the NAI1 pathway. This relationship may be important for the accumulation of the two β-glucosidases, namely BGLU18 and PYK10, in the same L-ER body. Overall, we conclude that the JA signaling pathway is not necessary for the biogenesis of L-ER bodies, although it is involved in the production of some L-ER body components.
