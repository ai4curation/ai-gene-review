Autophagy-associated protein 13 (ATG13) plays a pivotal role in regulating the
assembly of the autophagy initiation complex. However, the function and
mechanism of action of ATG13 during influenza virus infection remain
incompletely understood. This study elucidates that ATG13 restricts influenza A
virus (IAV) replication while potentiating the activation of the type I
interferon (IFN-I) pathway. Mechanistically, coimmunoprecipitation assays
revealed that ATG13 interacts with MAVS and recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligase
ASB1 to catalyze K63-linked polyubiquitination at the K348 residue, thereby
amplifying the downstream antiviral signalling cascade. Furthermore, through
dual-luciferase reporter assays and pharmacological inhibition of autophagy, we
confirmed that this novel immunomodulatory function of ATG13 is independent of
the canonical autophagy pathway. The present study identifies ATG13 as a novel
host restriction factor against IAV and reveals a nonautophagic role for ATG13
in antiviral innate immunity.

Introduction

The innate immune system constitutes the host's primary defense against viral infection through the use of specific pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) for different viruses, which activate interferon (IFN) pathways to restrict viral replication [1,2]. The RIG-I-like receptor (RLR) family mediates the detection of RNA viruses through specialized members: retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) for short double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) and melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5) for long dsRNA replication intermediates. In addition to these primary sensors, laboratory of genetics and physiology 2 (LGP2) has been established as an important modulator of RLR signalling dynamics [3–5]. RIG-I, a crucial viral RNA sensor, is activated upon ligand binding and recruits the downstream mitochondrial adaptor protein mitochondrial antiviral signalling protein (MAVS). This interaction induces MAVS oligomerization and promotes the assembly of the TBK1 (TANK-binding kinase 1)/IKKϵ (IκB kinase epsilon) complex, which in turn facilitates the nuclear translocation of the transcription factors IRF3 (interferon regulatory factor 3) and NF-κB (nuclear factor kappa B), thereby initiating interferon production [6]. Upon sensing viral DNA, cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) activates the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) to trigger the TBK1-IRF3 signalling axis, leading to the nuclear translocation of IRF3 and the expression of type I interferons [7,8].

Influenza A virus (IAV), a member of the Orthomyxoviridae family, is a segmented negative-strand RNA virus that continues to pose a considerable threat to global public health [9]. The genome of the virus in question consists of eight distinct RNA segments, with antigenic differences in the hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) proteins serving as the primary basis for the classification of influenza virus subtypes [10]. The high mutation rate of influenza virus hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) proteins during replication leads to antigenic shift in IAV. This poses considerable challenges for the prevention and treatment of influenza. As demonstrated in our previous studies, both H7N9 and H9N2 subtype IAVs have been shown to suppress the host interferon pathway by degrading MAVS [11,12]. H1N1-IAV nucleoprotein (NP) has been shown to inhibit interferon activity by inducing mitochondrial autophagy to degrade MAVS [13]. Furthermore, the IAV membrane protein (M2) has been reported to increase MAVS activation and amplify the interferon pathway. These findings elucidate the intricate regulatory effects of IAV infection on the host interferon pathway.

The autophagy-related (ATG) protein family is found in eukaryotic cells and is central to autophagy. It regulates the breakdown and recycling of materials inside cells, maintaining their balance [14,15]. In yeast, the autophagy initiation core complex is composed of Atg1, Atg13, Atg17, Atg29, and Atg31. In mammals, the ULK1 (Unc-51-like autophagy activating kinase 1) complex is composed of ULK1, FIP200/RBCC1, ATG13, and ATG101 [16–19]. Both complexes are crucial to autophagy initiation [20]. The ATG13 protein is a key regulator of autophagy and possesses an N-terminal domain with a phospholipid-binding site. This site binds phosphatidic acid (PA), phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PtdIns3P) and phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate (PtdIns4P) via four conserved amino acids [21]. The N-terminal HORMA domain binds ATG101. The C-terminus comprises three domains: an RB1-inducible coiled-coil 1 (RBCC1) binding site, an LC3-interacting region (LIR) domain that interacts with the ATG8 family, and a ULK1/2 binding site [22]. Studies have shown that ATG family proteins regulate the interferon pathway. For example, ATG4B degrades TBK1 via Atg8 and GABARAP, preventing overactivation and maintaining immune balance [23,24]. In black carp, ATG16L1 blocks MAVS-mediated signalling [25]. These findings suggest that ATG family proteins influence viral replication via autophagy, although research into nonautophagy functions is limited.

The ankyrin repeat and SOCS box (ASB) family is a group of proteins that contain ankyrin repeats and SOCS boxes. In humans, this family has 18 members that function in E3 ubiquitin ligase complexes, where they regulate protein degradation. ASB1 is a key member whose structure has two functional domains. The N-terminal ankyrin repeat sequence allows ASB1 to interact with other proteins and regulate various cellular processes. The C-terminal SOCS box recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex by binding to Elongin B/C and Cullin5. This structure then allows ASB1 to bind to the complex, and target proteins can be ubiquitinated and degraded. ASB1 specifically binds with Cullin5 and Rbx2 to form the Cullin5-RING E3 ligase complex (CRL5), which is involved in substrate ubiquitination [26,27].

Previous studies have demonstrated that ATG13 has antiviral functions through the inhibition of viral replication. Our earlier research further confirmed that ATG13 suppresses viral replication by inducing type I interferon activity [28–30]. Our research revealed that ATG13 inhibits IAV replication. Overexpression of ATG13 increases the levels of IFNB1 and other interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) caused by IAV while also promoting the activation of IRF3. Mechanistically, ATG13 enhances MAVS activation through K63-linked polyubiquitination at K348 of the MAVS protein, a process involving the E3 ligase ASB1. We have identified a novel mechanism by which the autophagy-associated protein ATG13 activates the interferon pathway through nonautophagic functions, thereby inhibiting IAV replication.

ResultsATG13 inhibits influenza virus replicationTo determine the function of ATG13 during influenza A virus (a single-stranded negative-sense RNA virus) infection. HEK293 T cells were infected with two representative influenza virus strains, A/chicken/Hong Kong/G9/1997 (H9N2) and A/Puerto Rico/8/34 (H1N1). The results suggested that ATG13 expression is enhanced during IAV infection (Figure 1A, B). Concurrently, our study demonstrated that both Sendai virus (SeV) and polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (Poly (I:C)) upregulated the expression of ATG13 (Figure S1A, B), indicating that RNA virus infection triggers ATG13 induction. And then we involved the overexpression of ATG13 in HEK-293 T cells, followed by infection with H9N2-IAV and H1N1-IAV. Subsequently, alterations in viral titres were measured. The results demonstrated that transient expression of ATG13 led to a significant reduction in the viral titres of both subtypes in HEK-293 T cells (Figure 1C). As IAV primarily targets respiratory and pulmonary epithelial cells, human non-small cell lung carcinoma cells (A549) were infected with both strains. In accordance with previous findings, transient expression of ATG13 in A549 cells led to a comparable reduction in viral titres for both influenza subtypes (Figure 1D). To further elucidate the impact of ATG13 on the replication of influenza, an ATG13-knockdown A549 cell line was established and subsequently infected with H9N2 and H1N1 influenza viruses. Compared with that in negative control cells, the knockdown of ATG13 resulted in a significant increase in the titre of the influenza virus in A549 cells (Figure 1E). To verify whether ATG13 has the same function in immune cells, human monocytic leukemia cell lines (THP-1) were infected with both viruses following ATG13 knockout (KO). The results indicated that, in comparison with WT cells, ATG13-KO cells exhibited increased influenza virus titres (Figure 1F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the ATG13 protein has an inhibitory effect on influenza virus replication.

Coimmunoprecipitation and immunoblotting analysisThe HEK-293 T cells were cultivated to achieve 80–90% confluence, after which they were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and collected into centrifuge tubes using a cell scraper. The subsequent step involved the lysis of the cells in a buffer composed of NP-40 and ice. This step lasted for 30 min. Ultimately, the immunoprecipitated complexes and whole-cell lysates were subjected to conventional Western blotting for protein transfer, with protein detection performed using the aforementioned antibodies.
