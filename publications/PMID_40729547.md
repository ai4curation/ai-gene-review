The polycomb repressive-deubiquitinase (PR-DUB) complex removes ubiquitin from
lysine residue 119 on histone H2A (H2AK119Ub) in humans. The PR-DUB is composed
of two central protein factors, the catalytic breast cancer type 1
susceptibility protein (BRCA1)-activating protein 1 (BAP1) and one of three
additional sex combs-like 1-3 (ASXL1-3) proteins. A plant homeodomain (PHD) at
the C terminus of ASXL proteins is recurrently truncated in cancer, was
previously proposed to recognise epigenetic modifications on the N-terminal tail
of histone H3 and was recently shown to bind an auxiliary set of PR-DUB
interactors, named methyl CpG-binding domain proteins 5 (MBD5) and 6 (MBD6).
Here, we demonstrate that the ASXL PHD domain lacks features required for
histone tail recognition and is unable to bind histone H3 epigenetic marks.
Modelling the structure of the ASXL PHD using AlphaFold3 suggests that the
domain has an atypical fold and that the isolated ASXL PHD can chelate a single
zinc ion in vitro, compared with the two ions conventionally bound by PHD
domains. Alternatively, we show that the ASXL PHD-MBD5 and PHD-MBD6 complexes
are stable in vitro. A composite zinc-binding site was shown to form at the
interface between the ASXL2 PHD and MBD6 MBD domains, and is required for stable
complex formation. Overall, these data suggest an unconventional pairing of
domains coordinate key functions of the PR-DUB-a noncanonical PHD domain from
ASXL proteins partners with MBD5 or 6, which were themselves misannotated
because they cannot bind to methylated DNA.

Introduction

Chromatin, the structure used to efficiently store DNA, is formed with DNA wrapped around an octamer of histone proteins to form a nucleosome. Two copies of four histone proteins, H2A, H2B, H3 and H4, interact within the nucleosome core. The N‐ and C‐terminal tails from histone proteins protrude from the nucleosome and are accessible for covalent chemical modification [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Attachment of ubiquitin to histone H2A at K119 limits gene expression through chromatin compaction and is characteristic of repressed developmental genes [6, 7, 8, 9]. H2AK119Ub deposition is regulated by polycomb group proteins, being attached by the really interesting new gene (RING) E3 ubiquitin ligase polycomb repressive complex 1 (PRC1 [8, 10, 11, 12]) and removed by the PR‐DUB [13, 14, 15].

The PR‐DUB has regulatory functions in the cell cycle, cellular development and DNA damage response, and determines short‐term changes to gene expression (reviewed in Refs [7, 16, 17, 18]). The core of the human PR‐DUB is composed of a catalytic deubiquitinase, BAP1, and one of three regulatory ASXL proteins (mutually exclusive, interchangeable). The BAP1 deubiquitinase is comprised of two conserved functional domains; the ubiquitin C‐terminal hydrolase (UCH) confers histone protein deubiquitination, whereas the UCH L5‐like domain (ULD) interacts with the deubiquitinase‐associated domain (DEUBAD) of ASXL proteins [13, 19, 20]. Interaction with the ASXL DEUBAD is required to form a composite ubiquitin‐binding site, shown by crystallisation of the Drosophila PR‐DUB in 2018, and later in 2019 [15, 21]. Recently, the structure of the human PR‐DUB bound to ubiquitinated nucleosome was resolved by cryo‐electron microscopy. In complex with chromatin, BAP1 and ASXL are positioned to restrict the PR‐DUB towards removal of H2AK119Ub and not other histone marks [22, 23]. ASXL1–3 also contain a DNA‐binding HB1, ASXL, restriction endonuclease‐helix‐turn‐helix (HARE‐HTH) domain at their N terminus and a C‐terminal PHD finger, thought to bind histone modifications (Fig. 1A) [24].

ASXL1–3 are recurrently truncated in human haematological malignancies, particularly acute myeloid leukaemia. ASXL mutations typically occur in the last exon and truncate the protein, causing loss of the PHD domain (Fig. 1A) [25]. Often, ASXL truncation products are expressed, escape nonsense‐mediated decay [26] and interact to form aberrant versions of the PR‐DUB [25, 27, 28]. The mutant PR‐DUB has altered protein interactions, increased stability and deubiquitinase activity, and facilitates widespread removal of H2AK119Ub [27, 28, 29, 30]. Given the ASXL PHD is repetitively lost in cancer, the domain may have an important functional role. This is further reiterated by the observation that similar inherited truncations of ASXL1 (Bohring–Opitz syndrome [31]), ASXL2 (Shashi–Pena syndrome [32]) and ASXL3 (Bainbridge–Ropers syndrome [33]) cause developmental disorders.

PHD domains conventionally possess a ‘binuclear treble‐clef’ zinc finger fold, where cysteine and histidine residues chelate two structurally integral zinc ions in a Cys4‐His‐Cys3 structural motif [34, 35, 36]. Typically, PHD domains bind the terminal alanine (A1), arginine (R2) and lysine (K4) residues of the histone H3 N‐terminal tail to selectively recognise histone modification state (Fig. 1B). Most commonly, PHD domains bind either unmodified, di‐ or tri‐methylated H3K4, where H3K4me2 and H3K4me3 specify active gene expression states (Fig. 1B) [36, 37, 38]. The histone methyl specificity of the ASXL PHD is not currently clear [24, 39]. Here, we use a variety of methods to show the ASXL PHD domains are unlikely to bind modified or unmodified histone tails. Instead, AlphaFold modelling and subsequent validation with recombinant protein showed that the domain has an atypical fold. Through study of the recently characterised interaction between the ASXL PHD domains and methyl CpG‐binding domains 5 (MBD5) and 6 (MBD6 [40]) we found the MBD proteins form a stable complex with the ASXL PHD, and that an additional zinc‐binding site is shared between them. Accordingly, MBD5 and MBD6 appear to be essential to PR‐DUB function and their loss as interaction partners is likely causative in ASXL truncation‐driven disease.

ResultsThe sequence of the ASXL PHD argues against the canonical PHD domain functionTypically, PHD domains bind terminal residues of the histone H3 N‐terminal tail (A1, R2 and K4) through conserved elements of the PHD domain fold (Fig. 1B) [36, 37, 38]. The residues that specify histone‐tail recognition are well understood and can be identified on the primary sequence of a classical PHD [38]. The organisation of residues within a conventional PHD domain is displayed for inhibitor of growth 2 (ING2) on the left‐hand side of Fig. 1C. For comparison, the amino acid sequence of the ASXL2 PHD (residues 1397–1433) is modelled alongside (Fig. 1C, right). While the Cys4‐His‐Cys3 zinc chelation motif typical of PHD domains is evident for the ASXL PHD [24, 36], most other residues that function in histone binding are missing. For instance, the ASXL PHD domains lack an aromatic cage, the feature required to bind histone methyl modifications, with few aromatic amino acids conserved (pink [36, 38, 41]). A highly conserved tryptophan—characteristic of PHD domains that bind H3K4 methyl marks (purple, outlined [41])—is also noticeably missing [24]. Some other PHD domains bind unmodified histone H3, but still require the ‘bilobed brace’—a hydrophobic region that binds the N‐terminal alanine (A1) of histone H3 [24, 38]. The PHD domains of ASXL1–3 all lack the ‘bilobed brace’, shown by the ‘expected position of bilobed brace’ label in Fig. 1C [24].Prior reports of histone methyl‐binding [24, 39] are therefore contradictory to the sequence features readily identifiable in the ASXL PHD domains. An assortment of protein domains bind zinc to increase stability [45, 46]. Of these, several chelate two zinc ions through the ‘binuclear treble‐clef’ fold predicted for the ASXL PHD. Common binuclear treble‐clef domains include the PHD, RING, LIN‐1, Isl‐1 and MEC‐3 (LIM) and myeloid translocation protein 8, Nervy and DEAF1 (MYND) zinc fingers, which each possess a distinct structure, function and fold [34, 35, 47]. Overall, the properties of the PHD‐MBD spectrum represent a marked improvement in PHD domain behaviour in the presence of MBD6. This contrasted markedly with quality control total correlation spectroscopy (TOCSY) and nuclear overhauser effect spectroscopy (NOESY) data collected for the isolated ASXL2 PHD domain (not shown), which lacked resonances in the structured regions and had fewer cross‐peaks than expected, potentially due to lack of a stable structure or due to self‐association.

Having observed PHD‐MBD co‐purification and NMR spectra consistent with complex formation in solution, we predicted the structure of the ASXL2 PHD‐MBD6 protein complex using AlphaFold3 (Fig. 4C,D). The predicted protein–protein interface had a buried surface area of 633 Å2, substantial relative to the total surface area of either domain (3313 Å2 for the ASXL2 PHD, 4728 Å2 for the MBD domain of MBD6, Fig. 4C). The interface PTM (predicted template modelling, iPTM) score for the complex was 0.7, and the PAE plot for interdomain contacts had a predicted distance error of generally less than 5 Å, indicative of a confident prediction (Fig. 4D).

In assessing the ASXL2‐MBD6 model, we noticed that residues Cys60 and Cys62 on MBD6 were positioned near Cys1400 and Cys1402 at the N terminus of the ASXL2 PHD (Fig. 5A). Given the proximity of these zinc‐chelating residues, we considered the possibility that a shared, composite zinc‐binding site may exist at the interface between the two proteins. Further AlphaFold modelling supported this observation, where two zinc ions were modelled onto the complex in Fig. 5B. Inclusion of zinc further increased the iPTM score of the complex (0.8), while the PAE plot in Fig. 5C shows an increase in model confidence relative to the initial model (Fig. 4D) [48, 49]. A tetrahedral zinc‐chelating site was predicted at the region described, with contributing residues from both interacting proteins (schematic, stick representation in Fig. 5D). Zinc‐binding was subsequently compared between the isolated ASXL2 PHD‐MBD6 complex and the constituent proteins (Fig. 5E), using the PAR‐based zinc assay established in Fig. 3G [52]. In contrast to the ASXL2 PHD (1.1 zinc ions released per molecule, on average) and MBD6 MBD domain proteins by themselves (0.22 zinc ions released per molecule, on average), the ASXL2 PHD‐MBD6 complex released approximately two zinc ions per molecule, on average (1.8, Fig. 5E). Accordingly, the ASXL PHD likely assembles a composite zinc‐binding site when bound to the MBD domain of MBD6.

To further characterise the ASXL‐MBD protein complex, we performed native mass spectrometry of the heterodimer purified with an affinity tag only on the ASXL2 PHD domain and SE chromatography (Fig. 5F). The most prominent spectral peak cluster corresponds to the expected mass of the PHD‐MBD heterodimer, within a mass error of 0.05 Da. Several additional peak clusters were observed, corresponding to each dissociated subunit. Each species appeared to have additional zinc ions associated with it relative to zinc ion measurements in solution (Figs 3G and 5F), which may arise due to the binding of ions in the process of liquid‐to‐gas phase transition. The ASXL2 PHD peak had either one (744.16 m/z, +6 charge state) or two zinc ions (754.81 m/z, +6 charge state), and the MBD domain of MBD6 had species with zero (1191.77 m/z, +6 charge state) or one (1202.42 m/z, +6 charge state) zinc associated. The most prominent heterodimer peak (1304.72 m/z, +9 charge state) corresponds to the molecular weight of the PHD‐MBD complex with three zinc ions. Heterogeneity of zinc association, and oxidised species also evident, mean that exact numbers of zinc ions present should be viewed with some caution. AlphaFold3 modelling of the heterodimer complex with three or more zinc ions failed to show a convincing third zinc‐binding site (not shown). Overall, our native mass spectrometry data reiterate that a stable heterodimeric complex between the ASXL2 PHD and MBD6 MBD domain is formed in solution and involves zinc binding.

Finally, to specifically probe the proposed composite zinc‐binding site between the ASXL2 PHD and MBD6 MBD domains, we performed site‐directed mutagenesis of residues involved. Using the co‐expression system used to produce samples for NMR in Fig. 4B, we generated single‐ and double‐mutants of Cys1400 (C1400A) on the ASXL2 PHD and Cys60 (C60A) on the MBD6 MBD (Fig. 5G). For effective visualisation of the PHD and MBD domains on SDS/PAGE, we retained the SUMO‐tag on the ASXL2 PHD and compared elution profiles of wild‐type and mutant complexes by analytical SE chromatography (Fig. 5G). The single‐ and double‐mutant proteins showed reduced protein complex formation, with all mutant complexes lacking the distinct ASXL2‐MBD6 complex peak seen for the wild‐type protein (highlighted, Fig. 5G). Analysis of peak fractions by SDS/PAGE corroborated this disruption, with mutant proteins showing disrupted ratios of PHD:MBD domains and delayed elution of the predominant SUMO‐PHD fractions. Thus, residues at the composite zinc‐binding site predicted by AlphaFold3 are essential to the stable association of the ASXL PHD and MBD5/6 MBD domains. Given the apparent flexibility of the ASXL N terminus in our AlphaFold model of the isolated PHD domain (Fig. 3), the composite zinc‐binding site likely increases the overall stability of the ASXL PHD N‐terminus and mediates stable binding to MBD5 and MBD6.

To the contrary, we have shown that the ASXL PHD domain is missing a complement of sequence features that are integral to histone methyl‐binding (Fig. 1). ITC (Fig. 2A,C) and an ELISA (Fig. 2D, Table S1) of histone methyl modifications were subsequently used to demonstrate that the ASXL PHD is not able to bind common histone marks in vitro.

The ASXL PHD has previously been suggested to be atypical, where Aravind and Iyer identified that the domain lacks the bilobed brace that is required to bind the end of the histone H3 N‐terminal tail. This feature was hypothesised to allow the ASXL PHD to bind histone modifications further up the histone tail, like H3K27me3 [24]. Contrastingly, peptide pulldown assays performed by Park et al. [39] showed broad specificity for the ASXL2 PHD to all of unmodified histone H3, H3K4me2 and H3K4me3. PHD domains typically are sensitive and selective to particular histone modification states, meaning the observed specificity to a broad range of modifications is unusual [36, 37, 38]. According to the current study, prior characterisations of the ASXL PHD overstate the ability of the domain to bind histone methyl marks. Instead, the ASXL PHD appears to have an atypical role in PR‐DUB complex regulation. In support of an unusual function for the ASXL PHD, the fold of the domain is irregular (Fig. 3). Most notably, the ASXL PHD is able only to chelate a single zinc ion even though the domain sequence contains various potential residues that could chelate two zincs (Fig. 3). As a result, the N terminus of the isolated ASXL PHD was variably modelled in AlphaFold3 (Fig. 3).

During the course of this work, Tsuboyama et al. [40] uncovered that the PHD domains of ASXL1–3 incorporate MBD5 and MBD6 into the PR‐DUB complex. We could recapitulate the binding of MBD5 and MBD6 with recombinant ASXL2 PHD (Fig. 4A) and show that the protein complex is folded in solution (Fig. 4B). AlphaFold modelling was later used to predict the protein interface, where a large buried surface area relative to the total protein surface area was observed (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, zinc‐chelating residues on the ASXL PHD, MBD5 and MBD6 were shown to be proximal (Fig. 5A–C) and to chelate an additional zinc ion (Fig. 5E) when in complex with one another. Further studies using intact mass spectrometry and site‐directed mutagenesis reiterated that the ASXL‐MBD5/6 protein complex binds additional zinc ions and that a composite zinc‐binding site is formed between the two proteins (Fig. 5F,G). Various other protein complexes bind zinc at their interface to improve stability and to provide sensitivity to intracellular concentrations of zinc [58].

A mechanistic understanding of MBD5 and MBD6 protein function is elusive. It has been shown that MBD5 and MBD6 bind the ASXL PHD domain in a mutually exclusive fashion [40, 55] and that the MBD proteins co‐localise with key components of the PR‐DUB to chromatin [40]. Furthermore, MBD5 and MBD6 each bind chromatin independently of the PR‐DUB [40, 55]. MBD6, for instance, can independently co‐localise to double‐stranded DNA damage during the DNA damage response [55]. Our current study further brings MBD5 and MBD6 into focus as key interactors of the ASXL PHD. We anticipate that future structural and biochemical studies will enable further insight into the ASXL PHD–MBD protein axis and clarify the functions of MBD5 and MBD6 within the PR‐DUB complex. It may be interesting to investigate whether other protein interactors can bind the ASXL PHD in a fashion that is complementary to, or independent of, MBD5 and MBD6.

In summary, this study provides novel insight into the functions of the ASXL PHD domain. We show that the ASXL PHD is missing key functional residues and is unable to bind common histone modifications in vitro. Extensive AlphaFold modelling and validation through zinc‐binding assays demonstrate that the structure of the ASXL PHD is unusual but supported in complex with MBD5 and MBD6 through binding of an additional zinc ion at the domain N terminus. The latter finding highlights that MBD5 and MBD6 are key ASXL PHD interactors that likely contribute to ASXL truncation‐driven disease.
