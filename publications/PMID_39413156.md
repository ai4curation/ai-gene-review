## Full Text

Abstract

The opportunistic pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa infects the airways of people with cystic fibrosis (CF) and produces a virulence factor Cif that is associated with worse outcomes. Cif is an epoxide hydrolase that reduces cell-surface abundance of the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) and sabotages pro-resolving signals. Its expression is regulated by a divergently transcribed TetR family transcriptional repressor. CifR represents the first reported epoxide-sensing bacterial transcriptional regulator, but neither its interaction with cognate operator sequences nor the mechanism of activation has been investigated. Using biochemical and structural approaches, we uncovered the molecular mechanisms controlling this complex virulence operon. We present here the first molecular structures of CifR alone and in complex with operator DNA, resolved in a single crystal lattice. Significant conformational changes between these two structures suggest how CifR regulates the expression of the virulence gene cif . Interactions between the N-terminal extension of CifR with the DNA minor groove of the operator play a significant role in the operator recognition of CifR. We also determined that cysteine residue Cys107 is critical for epoxide sensing and DNA release. These results offer new insights into the stereochemical regulation of an epoxide-based virulence circuit in a critically important clinical pathogen.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is an opportunistic pathogen that infects immunocompromised patients. It can cause acute and chronic airway infections, particularly in patients with underlying lung disease. For example, P. aeruginosa chronically colonizes the majority of adult patients with cystic fibrosis (CF) and is a major contributor to respiratory failure in these patients ( 1 ). While infections by P. aeruginosa are not as frequent in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or non-CF bronchiectasis, these infections are associated with high rates of mortality ( 2–5 ). The success of P. aeruginosa in establishing persistent lung infections reflects the fact that it harbors diverse mechanisms of genetic adaptation and antibiotic resistance ( 1 ). For example, the bacteria secrete virulence factors that manipulate host physiology, subvert host defenses, and favor colonization ( 1 , 6 ).

Previous studies have uncovered a mechanistically distinct secreted virulence factor from P. aeruginosa that can reduce the cell-surface abundance of the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) in airway epithelial cells ( 6 ) and degrade pro-resolving signals typically formed during the response to airway infection ( 7 ). The virulence factor responsible for these effects, the CFTR inhibitory factor (Cif), is also capable of reversing pharmacological rescue of the most common disease-associated CFTR mutation in human airway epithelial cells ( 8 ). Cif is an epoxide hydrolase, an enzyme that converts an oxirane ring into a vicinal diol ( 9 , 10 ), and its hydrolase activity is required for its effects on CFTR ( 11 ). Epoxides are cyclic ethers with a three-atom ring and play important physiological roles ( 7 , 12 , 13 ). Within cells, Cif accelerates degradation of CFTR by inhibiting its post-endocytic deubiquitination ( 14 ). Cif thus promotes bacterial colonization, as demonstrated in a mouse model of acute pneumonia ( 15 ). Cif also hydrolyzes the epoxy-polyunsaturated fatty acid 14,15-epoxyeicosatrienoic acid (14,15-EET), a signal required for the formation of the pro-resolving mediator 15-epi-lipoxin A 4 . Consistent with these mechanistic observations, higher levels of Cif protein in pediatric airway samples correlated with worse lung function and increased hyperinflammatory signaling ( 7 ).

Exposure to epibromohydrin (EBH) and other synthetic epoxides potently upregulates cif expression in P. aeruginosa , suggesting that it is part of an epoxide-responsive circuit ( 16 ). Indeed, the sequence of Cif is homologous to a protein that was earlier found to enable a soil-dwelling pseudomonad to degrade environmental epichlorohydrin ( 17 ). cif is part of an operon that also includes a divergently transcribed gene cifR (Figure 1; top row), which exhibits sequence homology to TetR family transcriptional regulators (TFRs). Expression of the cif gene is repressed when CifR binds to the operator site within the cif promoter segment (Figure 1; middle row), and clinical strains exhibiting high cif expression often show reduced levels of cifR expression ( 16 ). Furthermore, EBH disrupts CifR binding to the intergenic promoter region, permitting transcription of cifR and the three divergently transcribed genes including cif ( 16 , 18 ) (Figure 1; bottom row). Cif-mediated hydrolysis of the epoxide (Figure 1; bottom right) has the potential to reset the transcriptional state. Thus, based on sequence and functional analysis, CifR appears to be a TetR repressor and to represent the first reported epoxide-sensitive bacterial transcriptional regulator.

To resolve the molecular basis for CifR-operator recognition and lay a foundation for understanding the epoxide responsiveness, we solved the X-ray crystal structure of CifR in complex with double-stranded (ds) operator DNA at 2.6 Å resolution. Despite the length of the operator sequence, the overall structure turned out to involve a single CifR dimer binding per operator site, with unusual protein:DNA contacts at the periphery. Surprisingly, the lattice of the selenomethionine derivative used for phase determination was found to harbor a DNA-free CifR molecule caged within a bulk-solvent channel. Thus, we were able to determine both the DNA-bound and DNA-free structures of CifR from a single lattice, revealing conformational changes associated with DNA binding and suggesting a hypothesis for epoxide-mediated release of DNA relying on a critical cysteine residue.

Materials and methods

Strains and plasmids

Escherichia coli DH5α cells were used for cloning. BL21-derived Rosetta 2 (DE3) cells were used for protein expression. The cifR gene (PA14_26140; UniProt ID: A0A0H2ZCS5) and its derivative mutants were cloned in plasmid pET-16b with a 10-His tag followed by a human rhinovirus 3C protease cleavage site between the tag and the N-terminus of the recombinant proteins ( 16 ). Mutagenesis of cifR was performed using the Pfu Turbo DNA Polymerase (Agilent), and mutants were verified by Sanger sequencing. Primers used for cloning are listed in Supplementary Table S1 . Cysteine mutants are described by the three-letter subscript following CifR, corresponding to the amino acids at positions 99, 107 and 181, respectively. Wild-type protein containing all three cysteines is CifR CCC . In this nomenclature, for example, mutation C99T/C181R is CifR TCR .

Protein expression and purification

Cells transformed with the relevant expression plasmid (either from freshly transformed cells or from a glycerol stock) were first grown as a starter culture in 50 ml of Lysogeny Broth (LB) with 100 μg/ml ampicillin and 34 μg/ml chloramphenicol at 37°C in Erlenmeyer flasks overnight. Ten milliliters of this starter culture were inoculated into 1 l LB with ampicillin and chloramphenicol and cultivated at 37°C in 2 l Erlenmeyer flasks. Protein expression was induced when OD 600 reached a value of 0.4–0.6 by addition of isopropyl β- d -1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 0.5 mM and was allowed to continue overnight at 16°C. Cells were collected by centrifugation, resuspended in 25 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 10 mM imidazole pH 8.5, and then lysed using a microfluidizer (Model M-110L, Microfluidics, USA) equipped with an H10Z interaction chamber submerged in ice. Following sequence optimization (see Results for details), CifR variants were subsequently purified by immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) using HisPur Ni-NTA agarose resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, supplemented with varying concentrations of imidazole using a step-elution protocol. The imidazole concentration was 10 mM in the binding buffer, 100 mM in the washing buffer and 500 mM in the elution buffer. Two hundred microliters of 4 mg/ml hexahistidine-tagged 3C protease were added to the 40 ml eluted fraction pool. The mixture was dialyzed against 2 l of 25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM dl -dithiothreitol (DTT) and 40 mM imidazole pH 8.5 overnight at 4°C. This sample was reapplied to Ni-NTA agarose resin to capture any uncleaved protein along with the 3C protease. The flow-through fraction was concentrated and centrifuged to remove any precipitated protein. The supernatant was subjected to size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) at 4°C using a Superdex 75 26/600 column equilibrated with Buffer A (25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl and 1 mM DTT).

To obtain selenomethionine (SeMet) labeled protein, we inhibited the methionine (Met) biosynthesis pathway by adding high concentrations of isoleucine, lysine, and threonine ( 19 ). Starter cultures were grown in Terrific Broth (TB) supplemented with appropriate antibiotics at 37°C, until the OD 600 reached a value of 3–4. Cells were pelleted at 2000 × g for 10 min, resuspended in modified M9 minimal medium ( 20 ). The culture was incubated at 37°C for 1 h to minimize the amount of residual methionine. Amino acids were added to the culture to reach final concentrations of 100 μg/ml each of selenomethionine, lysine, phenylalanine and threonine, and 50 μg/ml each of isoleucine, leucine, and valine. Cells were transferred to an incubator at 20°C, incubated for a further 30 min, and then protein expression was induced by addition of 0.5 mM IPTG, followed by incubation at 20°C overnight.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

Protein:DNA binding reactions were carried out in 10 μl reaction volumes containing varying concentrations of DNA and protein (see figures for details) in 25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl and 0.5 mM DTT. Epibromohydrin (EBH) and N -ethylmaleimide (NEM) were initially dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at 1 M. Serial dilutions were prepared with DMSO, and 0.5 μl of each dilution of EBH or NEM was added to an aliquot of the protein:DNA mix. The reaction was incubated at RT for 30 min. Three microliters of loading dye (50% [w/v] glycerol, 0.04% [w/v] cyanole xylene) were added, and the samples were loaded on a 12% acrylamide Tris/borate/EDTA gel. Electrophoresis was performed for 100–130 min at 80 V to separate protein-bound DNA and free DNA at room temperature. The gel was stained with SYBR Gold Nucleic Acid Gel Stain (Invitrogen) and imaged using a Gel Doc™ XR+ Gel Documentation System (Bio-Rad). Quantification was performed using Image Studio (Ver. 5.5). Least-square fits were performed in Excel using logistic equations, and imposing a maximum exponent of 2, to determine the EC 50 or IC 50 of protein:DNA binding and epoxide inhibitory effect, respectively. In the case of CifR TCR , the binding or the inhibition reached 100% which was assigned as the 'max' value. For other mutants, the maximum values were set at the highest protein or EBH concentration of the test, accordingly, since saturation was not reached at our highest test concentration; in these cases, a symbol of ‘>’ was placed prior to the calculated values of the EC 50 and IC 50 . Crystallization and data collection

The concentrations of the protein dimer and DNA duplex in crystallization screens were 100 and 125 μM, respectively. Initial screening was carried out for CifR mixed with DNA fragments varying from 23 to 36 bp using at least 10 commercially available screens. Crystals were obtained under >50 conditions and were assayed for diffraction quality. The conditions that yielded the best diffraction were further optimized by varying pH and precipitant concentration. For the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L native co-crystal, the best dataset was obtained from a crystal grown in 0.1 M Tris pH 8.3, 0.2 M MgCl 2 and 16% [ w/v ] polyethylene glycol (PEG) 4000, conditions that were also used to obtain CifR TSR :dsDNA 26L co-crystals. For the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L SeMet co-crystal, the best condition was 0.1 M Tris pH 7.5, 0.2 M MgCl 2 , 10% [w/v] PEG 1000, 10% [w/v] PEG 8000. The final SeMet and native datasets of CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L used for structure determination were collected at NSLS-II beamline 17-ID-1. Diffraction data were collected on an Eiger 9M detector through 360° of total rotation using the NSLS-II AMX beamline's vector collection mode ( 22 ) in 0.2° frames at a detector distance of 200 mm. For the SAD experiment using the SeMet crystals, the optimal peak energies were determined from X-ray absorption scans. The native dataset for the CifR TSR :dsDNA 26L co-crystal was collected at NSLS-II beamline 17-ID-2 on an Eiger 16M detector through 360° of total rotation using vector collection mode in 0.2° frames at a detector distance of 300 mm. Diffraction data processing and structure refinement

Datasets were indexed, integrated and scaled internally using the XDS package ( 23 ). We initially attempted to determine phases by molecular replacement (MR) using published structures of other TFRs ( Supplementary Figure S1 ). In all these cases, TFRs form homodimeric complexes in the absence of DNA ( 24–28 ). However, the structures of TFR:DNA complexes show two fundamentally distinct stoichiometries, with a single operator binding either one or two TFR dimers ( Supplementary Figure S1 ). The Matthews coefficient ( 29 ) of our crystal was consistent with a single DNA duplex bound to either one (∼67% solvent) or two (∼41% solvent) CifR dimers in the ASU. However, MR failed to return an acceptable Solution in either stoichiometry. Therefore, we turned to experimental phasing based on SeMet substitution. Optimized SeMet crystals diffracted to 3.0 Å resolution. Experimental phases were determined using the SeMet SAD dataset with PHENIX_Autosol ( 30 ), and were used as the basis for initial model building of a CifR TCR dimer bound to a dsDNA 26L duplex. The structure was refined by iterative rounds of manual fitting using COOT ( 31 ) alternating with rigid-body, conjugate-gradient, real-space, and/or individual B-factor refinement with TLS (one chain per group) and protein and nucleic acid secondary-structure restraints using PHENIX_Refine ( 30 ). As described below (Results), during this process, an additional, DNA-free (apo-)CifR monomer (Chain E) was identified in the asymmetric unit, modeled, and refined. Grouped occupancy refinement was performed for chain E (apo-CifR) to confirm full occupancy. The asymmetric unit contained three CifR monomers and one 26 bp DNA duplex.

Given differences in unit cells, initial phases for the higher-resolution native datasets were obtained by molecular replacement using the experimentally phased and refined CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L structure as a starting model. The apo-CifR could not be modeled in the native dataset, due to substantially weaker electron density ( Supplementary Figure S2 ). For structural comparisons, we used the higher-resolution native structures of the chains A and B (DNA-bound CifR TCR dimer) together with the SeMet structure of chain E (apo-CifR TCR ). The N-terminal two residues of Chain A (native dataset), four residues of Chain B (native), and nine residues of Chain E (SeMet) could not be modeled due to disordered electron density. PyMol (Schrödinger, LLC) was used for least-squares superpositions and for visualization of structures and of difference electron-density maps. Electrostatic surface potential was calculated using the APBS plugin ( 32 ).

Analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC)

Sedimentation velocity was determined using a Beckman Coulter ProteomeLab XL-A ultracentrifuge. The reference cell chamber was loaded with 410 μl of Buffer A. The sample chamber was loaded with 400 μl of either protein, DNA, or a protein:DNA mixture. The amount of sample was adjusted to yield an absorbance at 280 nm ( A 280 ) of approximately 0.4. In the protein:DNA mixture, the DNA concentration was chosen to yield a 1.25-fold molar excess relative to the concentration of protein dimer. The samples were spun at 35 000 rpm in an An-60 Ti rotor at 20°C with reads every 3 min for at least 200 scans. Data from the first 100 scans were analyzed.

The sedimentation coefficients ( s ) were obtained by fitting a continuous c ( s ) distribution to experimental AUC traces using sedfit ( 34 ). Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)

Protein:DNA mixtures were prepared in Buffer A, with protein concentrations of 3, 2 or 1 mg/ml, together with DNA duplex at concentrations equivalent to a 1:1 molar ratio of DNA duplex:protein dimer for each mixture. The mixture was subjected to SEC using a Superdex 200 10/300 column to confirm the formation of protein:DNA complex. A control sample was prepared without protein and with DNA duplex at a concentration of 325 μM. The samples were measured at the LiX beamline ( 38 ) at the National Synchrotron Light Source II (NSLS-II), using the standard conditions for protein solution scattering. For each sample, the scattering data were averaged from three 3-s exposures. Buffer subtraction was based on the water peak intensity ( 39 ). Data were analyzed over a q range of 0.02–0.25 Å −1 using PRIMUS ( 40 ). Guinier plots were found to be linear, consistent with minimal levels of protein aggregation, and were used to determine the radius of gyration ( R g ) for each sample. Across varying sample concentrations, R g values were consistent within 0.1 Å, suggesting that neither the overall structure nor the oligomeric state are strongly dependent on concentration in this range. Theoretical SAXS curves were calculated from crystal structures using the FoXS webserver ( 41 , 42 ). Ten independent molecular envelopes were calculated from the 3 mg/ml SAXS data using DAMMIF, averaged using DAMAVER and then refined using DAMMIN ( 43–45 ). PDB structures were aligned with the molecular envelope using SUPCOMB ( 46 ).

Results

Double cysteine mutations and the presence of operator DNA stabilize CifR

To investigate the regulation of CifR at the molecular level, we needed to address the lack of solubility of recombinantly expressed 10His-CifR ( 16 ). Most of the wild-type protein pelleted during high-speed centrifugation (Figure 2B ), and the protein that could be purified continued to aggregate. We suspected that protein solubility could be improved by decreasing the number of cysteine residues in the construct. We performed multiple sequence alignment by using Position-Specific Iterative Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (PSI-BLAST) and analyzed the top hits that were TFRs and CifR homologs (938 total). Sequence similarity with other TFRs suggests that CifR is composed of a relatively conserved N-terminal DNA-binding domain with a typical HTH motif and a more variable C-terminal ligand-binding domain, which contains all three of the cysteine residues found in CifR (Figure 2A ). Cys107 is found in 52% of 938 TFR sequences analyzed, whereas Cys99 and Cys181 are each found in <3% (Figure 2A ). We therefore investigated replacement of Cys99 and Cys181 with threonine and arginine, which are respectively the most prevalent residues at their positions in the TFR sequence alignment.

Figure 2.

While a C99T mutation alone (CifR TCC ) did not improve initial solubility (Figure 2C ), the double-cysteine mutations (C99T/C181R; or CifR TCR ) substantially increased the fraction of protein remaining in the clarified lysates (Figure 2D ). Immobilized metal-affinity and size-exclusion chromatography yielded pure protein with a size corresponding to a CifR TCR monomer in the presence of reducing agent as determined by SDS-PAGE (Figure 2E ). The SEC elution profile of CifR TCR was consistent with the formation of a protein dimer in the absence of DNA (with a calibrated molecular mass of ∼40 kDa; Figure 2F and G ). Either the dsDNA 36 or dsDNA 26L ( Supplementary Table S1 ) formed protein:DNA complexes with CifR TCR as confirmed by SEC (Figure 2F and G ). Apo-CifR TCR was prone to crosslinking as a dimer in the absence of reducing agent (Figure 2E ), with slow precipitation occurring over time, while a mixture of CifR TCR and either dsDNA 36 or dsDNA 26L did not show visible precipitation. These results imply that the presence of its operator DNA could stabilize CifR TCR .

Operator ‘footprinting’ and crystallization of the CifR TCR :operator complex

Our previous studies had identified two distinct operator DNA sites for CifR in the P. aeruginosa genome (Figures 1A and 3A ) ( 16 ). Both sites are located in the intergenic region between cifR and morB , the gene located at the 5′ end of the cif operon ( 16 ). For each site, full CifR:DNA binding affinity was observed with a 27 bp construct (Figure 3A , sequence in bold) and lost with a 16–25 bp oligonucleotides ( 16 ). To screen for the best DNA construct to form a well-ordered crystal lattice packing, we tested a series of dsDNA constructs varying from 36 to 20 bp in length. We only focused on the operator DNA site which is proximal to cifR since this site showed 10-fold higher binding affinity compared to the one proximal to morB ( 16 ). We also tested two DNAs with cohesive 2 bp overhangs, designed to stabilize potential lattice interactions. We measured the binding affinity of each of these DNA constructs (Figure 3A and Supplementary Table S1 ) for CifR TCR by EMSA and found that CifR TCR could bind to DNAs with length varying between 36 and 23 bp with comparable affinities (Figure 3B ). As we progressively shortened the DNA to 20 bp, we observed complex formation only at progressively higher concentrations (Figure 3B ). Based on these results, we concluded that dsDNA 23 contains the minimum binding-site footprint for CifR, and we took 23 bp as the lower boundary on the length of dsDNA for crystallization studies.

Initial vapor-diffusion crystallization screening trials were set up with distinct mixtures of CifR TCR with each of the different lengths of DNA that form complexes (Figure 3B ) at a 1.25-fold molar excess of DNA. Crystals of varying quality were obtained with most of the constructs tested, as shown in Figure 3C . Complexes with the dsDNA 36 , dsDNA 27 and dsDNA 26L constructs produced crystals with good size and morphology, with crystallization conditions shown in Supplementary Table S2 . The crystals obtained with the dsDNA 26L construct yielded a native dataset at 2.6 Å resolution.

Co-crystallization of two protein conformations: CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex and apo-CifR TCR

Given the failure of multiple molecular replacement approaches, we proceeded with experimental phasing by SeMet substitution. Following expression and purification through IMAC and SEC, an average Se incorporation of ∼76% was determined by MALDI-TOF. A dataset with a resolution of 3.0 Å was obtained and analyzed to determine initial experimental phases. The crystal lattice is formed in one orientation by head-to-tail contacts between DNA molecules, forming extended helices with CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complexes oriented alternately up and down along the extended DNA helices (Figure 4A ). The resulting rows of protein:DNA complexes pack at right angles to extend the lattice in a second dimension, contacting each other through protein:protein interactions (Figure 4A , right panel). Consistent with the relatively high (∼67%) solvent content calculated for a single CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex per ASU, this arrangement creates relatively large channels that run the length of the crystal lattice (Figure 4B , top left) and that span paired ASUs related by two-fold axes associated with the P 2 1 2 1 2 space group. These channels contained weak, but connected experimental electron density, as well as a subset of the original Se sites identified by Phenix Autosol on either side of the two-fold axis, but not accounted for by the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex ( Supplementary Figure S2A ). We were able to model this additional density as a DNA-free (apo-)CifR TCR dimer that was apparently trapped between adjacent CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complexes (Figure 4B , top right) and aligned along a crystallographic symmetry axis. The asymmetric unit (ASU) in the SeMet lattice is thus composed of a CifR TCR dimer bound to dsDNA 26L together with an additional apo-CifR TCR monomer, which forms a dimer with its crystallographic symmetry mate (PDB ID: 6NSR). The solvent content of this hybrid ASU was estimated at 54%. It is important to note that the overall electron density for the ‘stowaway’ apo-CifR TCR protein remained substantially weaker than that of CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex, even after model building and refinement and has poorly resolved side-chain density as well as an unresolved region spanning residues 96 through 118. This is also reflected in the refined parameters of the apo-CifR TCR model (chain E), which yielded an average B-factor higher than either of the DNA-bound monomers (chains A and B; Table 1 ).

Figure 4.

No direct interactions were observed between apo-CifR TCR and either of the DNA duplexes, even though from one perspective (Figure 4B , 90° rotation, bottom right), this apo-CifR TCR dimer appears to bridge DNA duplexes in the lattice. In fact, the closest apo-CifR TCR residue is Arg26 from helix α1 whose guanidinium group is unresolved, suggesting mobility in the terminal portions of this side chain. Furthermore, as modeled the nitrogen (NH) group is 4.2 Å away from the 'OP1' oxygen group of the DNA backbone at dT4’. Instead, apo-CifR TCR interacts with adjacent protein molecules: direct polar contacts were observed between apo-CifR TCR and the CifR TCR protein in the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex (Figure 4B , bottom left). Tyr48 from helix α3 of apo-CifR TCR forms a hydrogen bond (2.6 Å) with the main chain of Phe51, which is at the loop between helices α3 and α4 in the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex. This Tyr48 and the main-chain amide of Lys54 from helix α4 of apo-CifR TCR , form polar contacts with Glu60 from helix α4 of CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex at 3.8 and 3.1 Å, respectively (Figure 4B , bottom left).

CifR TCR lacks polar contacts with DNA bases in the major groove in the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex

Detailed structural information for the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex was derived from the 2.6 Å native dataset. The overall structure of the CifR TCR :operator DNA complex is composed of one CifR TCR dimer and one dsDNA 26L duplex (Figure 5A , PDB ID: 6NSN). In each CifR protomer, three helices (α1–α3) constitute the DNA-binding domain (DBD, green in Figure 5A ), whereas helices α4–α9 constitute a potential ligand-binding domain (LBD, pale cyan) as postulated from the domain organization of TFRs. To track the distance between the two DBDs, we picked the two Pro45 residues in the HTH motifs which is the point of deepest intrusion into the DNA major groove. The distance between these two residues is 37.9 Å, allowing the motifs to sit in adjacent major grooves along one face of the DNA double helix. Meanwhile, the N-terminal extension that precedes the helix α1 in each CifR protomer inserts into the distal DNA minor groove on either side (Figure 5A ).

Figure 5.

Surprisingly, although operator specificity is usually encoded via base contacts in the major groove, CifR side chains only form polar contacts with the DNA backbone (Figures 5B ). Residues Leu33 from helix α2 and Tyr48 from helix α3 form hydrogen bonds with the phosphate groups of DNA bases dA17’ and dG16’, respectively, as does α4 helix residue Lys54 (Figure 5B ). Lys54 also forms a hydrogen bond network with three residues (Tyr28, Glu29 and Ala31) located at the loop between helices α1 and α2. In contrast, there are no polar contacts between the HTH motif and the DNA bases in the major groove, which is typically the site that determines sequence specificity for TFRs ( 47 ). Water molecules can also bridge contacts between protein residues and the major groove, as seen for example, in the ms6564:DNA complex ( 48 ). However, in the CifR complex, the two residues most deeply inserted into the major groove are Pro44 and Pro45 (Figure 5C ), with side chains that cannot form hydrogen bonds. In fact, the only water-mediated hydrogen bond is formed by Ala34 from the α2 helix, but it also contacts only the phosphate groups of dC15 and dA16 (Figure 5F ).

Although there is no direct polar contact between side chains of the HTH motif and any of the DNA bases in the major groove, the binding affinity to CifR TCR was much lower when we mutated four of those bases (dC15dT/dA16dC/dC17dT/dT18dG) (Figure 5G , second panel compared to the first). The half-maximal effective concentration (EC 50 ) was increased from 1.40 to 2.48 μM (Figures 5G and S3). These central sequences may permit the DNA to form a cavity with a specific shape in the major groove to adopt the HTH motif of CifR TCR . Also, the distances from C δ2 and C ϵ2 of Tyr48 to C 7 of dT18’ are 3.4 and 3.7 Å, respectively. The distance from C β of Pro44 to C 7 of dT18’ is 3.8 Å. It thus is likely that Tyr48/Pro44 and dT18’ form van der Waals interactions (Figure 5C and 5F ). The distances between C δ and C γ of Pro45 and C 7 of dT8 are 4.6 and 4.7 Å, respectively, which likely form additional weak van der Waals interactions given the dynamics of the complex in solution.

We observed a noticeable degree of bending and kinking along the DNA where CifR binds (Figure 5A ). DNA conformation analysis using x3DNA (DSSR) ( 49 , 50 ) suggests that significant distortion occurred at the protein:DNA interface (Figure 5F ). The two DNA major grooves interacting with HTH domain of CifR are enlarged by approximately 3 Å (Figure 5F , blue shadow) while the two DNA minor grooves interacting with Arg6 are narrowed by 0.5–1 Å (Figure 5F , orange shadow) compared to standard B-form DNA. The operator DNA at the center is displaced approximately 1 Å towards the protein whereas the two termini are approximately 1 Å further away from the protein. Altogether, these results indicate that protein contact triggers DNA distortion in CifR-operator DNA complex.

Minor groove contacts provide additional sequence specificity

In contrast to interactions involving the major groove, we observed significant protein:DNA base interactions in the minor grooves (Figure 5D and F ). Structurally, the two external DNA minor grooves are rich in AT sequences. They are thus narrower by at least 2 Å than the minor grooves in the center of the dsDNA 26L . Nevertheless, as noted above, on both ends of the operator site, they accommodate the Arg6 side chain, which interacts with multiple bases (dT4, dT5, dT6 and dA23’). We tested the importance of these interactions by mutating the dT4-dA23’ and dT4’-dA23 to GC base pairs ( Supplementary Table S1 ) and we found that these substitutions led to significant decrease of complex stability (Figure 5G , third panel compared to first panel, with a EC 50 of 1.40 μM versus 2.84 μM).

In a reciprocal experiment, the CifR TCR R6A mutant lost the majority of its DNA binding capacity (Figure 5G , fourth panel), despite exhibiting similar biochemical behavior to CifR TCR , e.g. during purification. Only very weak binding could be detected, indicated by the formation of smear DNA at high protein concentration. Deletion of the entire N-terminal loop of 10 aa completely abolished DNA binding (Figure 5G , fifth panel). This may reflect additional interactions between bp 1–3 and the very N-terminal residues of CifR. For instance, in chain A, Arg4 reaches into the DNA minor groove as well and likely interacts with T2, based on the limited electron density. We calculated the surface electrostatic potential using the Adaptive Poisson-Boltzmann Solver (APBS) ( 32 ) and found the expected positive values along the DNA-binding surface. The N-terminal loop, which interacts with the DNA minor groove, exhibits very strong positive potential as well (Figure 5E ), primarily due to the presence of two arginine residues (Arg4 and Arg6). These results together suggest that minor groove contacts provide critical sequence specificity for CifR-operator recognition.

Our structural results suggest that CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex exists as one CifR protein dimer bound to a single operator DNA duplex, creating protein:DNA interactions both in the DNA major and minor grooves. To determine if the crystal structure of the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex represents the major component of the solution state, we analyzed the biochemical properties of this complex in solution. Theoretical SAXS curves were calculated and were found to fit very well to the experimental SAXS data from a 1:1 molar mixture of the two components (χ 2 = 0.39; Supplementary Figure S4A ). In addition, the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex structure fits into the molecular envelope calculated from the SAXS data with little additional density ( Supplementary Figure S4B ). SEC and AUC also provide shape-independent molecular mass estimation by applying Svedberg and Stokes–Einstein equations (see equation 3 ), yielding results consistent with the observed CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex (Table 2 , Figures 2F , G , Supplementary Figures S5 and S6 ). These results together confirm that the crystal structure of the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex represents the predominant form of the complex in solution, for both the 26 bp and the full-length 36 bp operator sequences.

Conformational change of CifR TCR in the presence and absence of DNA

The overall structure of apo-CifR TCR (Figure 6A , PDB ID: 6NSR) reflects a distinct conformation compared to that seen in complex with DNA. Helix α6 and adjacent loops, spanning residues 96–118, comprise a disordered segment that could not be modeled with confidence (Figure 6A , dotted line). A C α least-squares superposition of the apo-CifR TCR dimer and DNA-bound CifR TCR dimer yields an RMSD of 6.9 Å (Figure 6B ). The RMSD of the individual ligand-binding domain (helices α5, α7, α8, α9) is 0.76 Å (Figure 6C ), while that of each DNA-binding domain (helices α1 – α3) from each monomer is 0.69 Å (Figure 6D ). Thus, the structure of each individual domain is conserved. However, their relative orientation has changed. The RMSD for the α4 helix, which connects the two domains, is 2.1 Å, primarily reflecting its enhanced curvature in the apo structure (Figure 6D ). As a result, the two HTH motifs are further apart from the dimer axis in the absence of the DNA: the distance between the Pro45 residues in each of the two HTH (55.5 Å; Figure 6A ), has increased 17.6 Å relative to the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex (Figure 5A ).

Figure 6.

As noted above, while the electron density for helix α6 in DNA-bound CifR TCR is of high quality, we were not able to confidently build in the model for this segment in the apo-CifR TCR (Figure 6E and F ). However, the DNA bound conformation clearly does not fit into the observed electron density (Figure 6F ), which suggests that significant conformational changes in this region accompany DNA binding. The weakness of the corresponding electron density in the apo-protein structure, most likely reflects conformational flexibility in the absence of bound operator DNA.

Cys107 plays a role in ligand recognition

To explore the ligand sensing mechanism, we again applied multiple sequence alignment to predict critical epoxide-sensing residues in CifR. As we observed from the sequence alignment of CifR homologs, the DNA-binding domain is relatively well conserved among TFRs (Figure 2 ). We mapped the conserved residues to the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex structure and found that most of these residues are located at the interface formed among helices α1–α3 and the N-terminus of helix α4 ( Supplementary Figure S7 ).

In contrast, the C-terminal LBD shows very little conservation throughout (Figure 2 , Supplementary Figure S7A ). This polymorphism may correspond to the wide variety of ligands that are recognized by TFRs. However, there is a small group of residues in the LBD that are conserved in over 40% of sequences. These include Lys54, Leu57, Gly106, Cys107, Gly149, Ala161 and Gly170. The residues fall into three groups: the α4 helical residues, the C-terminal residues, and the ligand-binding residues.

First, the conserved residues Lys54 and Leu57 are located in helix α4 which forms a connection between the DNA-binding domain (helices α1–α3) and the ligand-binding domain (helices α5–α7) for most TFRs ( 51 ). As discussed above, in the structure of CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex, Lys54 forms a polar contact with the DNA backbone as well as with three residues (Tyr28, Glu29 and Ala31) located at the loop between the α1 and α2 helices (Figure 5B ). Leu57 forms hydrophobic interactions with Leu36 and Leu47 from α2 and α3, respectively ( Supplementary Figure S7B ). Thus, these two residues probably have a role in maintaining the relative positions of the DNA-binding domain and helix α4.

Second, the most C-terminal conserved residues (Gly149, Ala161 and Gly170) are either located in helix α8 or in the loop between helices α7 and α8. The α8 helix constitutes the protein dimerization interface (Figure 5A ). Therefore, these three conserved residues may well be important for protein dimerization. In particular, Gly170 resides at the dimer interface, approximately 4.0 Å away from the corresponding Gly170 of the other monomer ( Supplementary Figure S7C ).

The third group consisted of the remaining two conserved residues, Gly106 and Cys107. These residues are adjacent to the α6 helix (Figure 7A and B ), which is part of the ligand-binding triangle of most TFRs ( 51 ), composed of helices α5–α7. Gly106 takes on a glycine-specific main-chain torsional configuration, which may be necessary to achieve the observed side-chain orientation of Cys107. Given its position at the center of the LBD and the interesting chemistry of sulfhydryl groups, we hypothesized that Cys107 might play a role in ligand recognition. In the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex structure, we observed positive density peaks in the difference map near the thiol group of Cys107 (Figure 7C , panel i). We were able to model the cysteine-sulfenic acid (CSO) for chain A and cysteine–sulfinic acid (CSD) for chain B (Figure 7C , panel ii), two cysteine modifications that occurred frequently in certain crystallization conditions ( 52 ), into the electron density of CifR TCR . No negative density peaks are observed in the calculated electron density maps from refined coordinates containing aforementioned cysteine modifications (Figure 7C , panel iii). We also observed that high positive surface potential was found at this pose (Figure 7E ). Computational geometry using CASTp ( 53 ) revealed this pocket has 91 and 41 Å 3 in volume for chain A and chain B, respectively, likely to accommodate potential ligands. These observations are consistent with the fact that sulfoxide modifications usually occur at reactive cysteines located in a hydrophilic environment ( 52 ). Although we discuss below the potential role of other cysteine modifications in CifR regulation, based on this crystal structure, these specific oxidation events are not incompatible with DNA binding.

Figure 7.

To investigate the role of Cys107 in epoxide sensing, we replaced Cys107 with a serine residue (named CifR TSR ). To ensure that the mutation did not fundamentally disrupt the structural integrity of the CifR transcription factor, we crystallized the CifR TSR :dsDNA 26L complex and determined its structure at a resolution of 2.8 Å (PDB ID: 6NSM, Table 1 ). The structure is very similar to that of the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex, with an RMSD (0.16 Å) comparable to the estimated coordinate error of the structure itself (0.36 Å). The side chains pointing into the ligand-binding pocket of these two structures are shown in Figure 7F . The Ser107 hydroxyl group has slightly reoriented compared to the oxidized thiol group of Cys107; χ 1 shifts by approximately 26° accompanied with a slight translation which leads to a displacement of the γ atom by 0.7 Å. No positive density in the difference map near the hydroxyl group of Ser107 in CifR TSR was observed (Figure 7D ). These alterations resulted in a ligand binding pocket of CifR TSR with a comparable steric structure but a distinct chemical environment.

To address the role of Cys107 in operator recognition and ligand sensing, we tested the binding capability of CifR TSR to the operator DNA and its response to the epoxide. CifR TSR displays a slightly reduced binding affinity for the operator DNA compared to CifR TCR (Figures 7G and S8); the EC 50 increased from 1.40 to 1.94 μM. Surprisingly, the CifR TSR :dsDNA 26L complex is much more resistant to the disruption by addition of EBH (Figures 7H and S9), with a half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC 50 ) increasing from 1.84 to >20 μM. These results imply that residue Cys107 is likely involved in epoxide sensing. When we introduced the C107S mutation (CifR CSC ) into the wild type CifR, this mutant binds DNA as well as CifR TSR (Figures 7G and S8) and was also resistant to epoxide (Figures 7H and S9). These results excluded the role of Cys99 and Cys181 in epoxide response. Surprisingly, replacing Cys107 with threonine (CifR TTR ) led to a complete loss of DNA binding capability (Figure 7G and S8). The difference between serine and threonine is the addition of one γ-methyl group, a relatively subtle stereochemical modification. In addition to mutating Cys107, we applied the cysteine-modifying compound N -ethylmaleimide (NEM) to the CifR TCR :dsDNA 26L complex. Even at the lowest concentration tested (0.5 mM), it completely disrupted the DNA-binding capacity of CifR TCR (Figure 7I ). These covalent or mutagenetic modifications of Cys107 thus may structurally mimic the ligand-bound state, which leads to a conformational change and release from the DNA. Taken together, these results indicate that the conserved Cys107 is likely involved in ligand recognition and the transduction of ligand-binding to DNA release for this novel epoxide-sensitive regulator.

Discussion

CifR:operator interactions

TetR family transcriptional regulators (TFRs) are found across a wide range of bacterial species and in response to different ligands regulate diverse physiological functions, including drug resistance, metabolism, and signaling processes ( 47 ). Compared to the large number of known family members, only a relatively small number of TFRs have been characterized mechanistically ( 54–58 ). As outlined below, the structures of CifR alone and in complex with DNA share some fundamental features of the TFR family, but also illuminate some novel characteristics. Collectively, the structures of TFR:DNA complexes reveal two fundamentally distinct stoichiometries, with a single operator duplex binding either one or two TFR dimers (e.g. TetR in Figure 8A and QacR in Figure 8B ). Sites as short as 14–15 bp appear to represent the minimum footprint that can comfortably accommodate a single TFR dimer (Figure 8C ) ( 24 ), but can also accommodate two TFR dimers (e.g. SlmA; Figure 8C ) ( 25 , 47 ). Longer operator sequences are observed, and can harbor either one (e.g. DesT; Figure 8C ) or two (e.g. QacR; Figure 8C ) dimer-binding sites.

Figure 8.

The operator DNA sequences of most TFRs form inverted repeats (Figure 8C ). Among known structures with two dimers bound to an operator, the axis of each dimer almost always aligns not with the center of the overall inverted repeat, but rather with the centers of less symmetric embedded repeats located to either side. In either case, sequence repeats generally reflect the 2-fold rotational symmetry of the TFR dimer ( 59 ). The centers of the dual embedded repeats are highlighted with grey shadows in Figure 8C and the embedded repeats are shown in detail in Supplementary Figure S10 . The presence of embedded repeats in the operator DNA thus can serve as a clue for a two-dimer binding mode. Indeed, mutagenesis to create an embedded repeat has been leveraged to engineer enhanced affinity of protein:operator interactions in the DarR system ( 60 ).

Compared to most other TFR operator DNAs (Figure 8C ), the cifR -proximal and morB -proximal sites do not show strong embedded-repeat signatures (Figure 8D ). They are relatively long and show considerable sequence divergence from each other (Figure 8D ). However, not all dual-site operators show clear embedded repeat signatures (e.g. CprB and DarR; Figures 8C ), and in the case of DarR binding to the wild-type operator, one of the dimers actually aligns with the center of the main inverted repeat, while the other is offset by 3 bp ( 60 ). Thus, it was difficult to predict based on sequence alone whether one or two CifR dimers would form a complex with the operator. However, our structural and biochemical data clearly show that apo-CifR TCR is a dimer and that the CifR TCR :DNA complex is composed of one dimer bound to one dsDNA duplex.

Since these structures were first determined, there have been significant advances in the computational modeling of protein complexes ( 61–63 ). Since the structures reported here have been publicly available for training, CifR is not a strong test case for the ability to predict TFR conformations or their interactions with DNA. However, we were surprised to note that the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database ( 64 ) still predicts a monomeric structure for the PA14 CifR sequence, despite having identified homology to the deposited PDB structure. We confirmed that the current version of AlphaFold can successfully predict a dimeric structure for the CifR sequence ( Supplementary Figure S11 ), although in the absence of DNA it predicts a conformation closest to the DNA-bound conformation, differing primarily at the N-terminal recognition motif. Indeed, it is widely recognized that the accurate computational modeling of protein:DNA complexes remains challenging for a number of reasons. Nonetheless, recent advances raise the prospect of more accurate predictions in the near future ( 65 , 66 ). For these studies, paired structures of DNA-binding proteins alone and in complex with physiologically relevant operator sites, as seen here for CifR, will provide important opportunities for AI training and validation.

Lack of major-groove hydrogen-bond interactions

TFR HTH motifs are usually positively charged, which facilitates operator binding given the negatively charged phosphate backbone of DNA. The expected positive surface potential was observed along the DNA-binding surface of CifR TCR with enhanced positive charge at the N-terminal loop, which interacts with the DNA minor groove (Figure 5E ). In addition to general electrostatic attraction, the specific recognition of operator sequences usually involves the formation of hydrogen bonds between DNA bases and protein residues. The HTH motifs of TFRs typically insert into the DNA major groove, where base-specific hydrogen-bonding partners are more fully exposed for stereochemical recognition. Consistent with the canonical view, mutations of the bases in the DNA major groove near the HTH motif led to significant reductions in the binding affinity of CifR TCR (Figure 5G , second gel). This indicates that the major-groove sequence plays a role in CifR TCR recognition.

However, compared to the operator-bound structures of other TFRs, the CifR:DNA complex is distinguished by a lack of hydrogen bonds between the HTH motif residues and DNA bases in the major groove. One reason is that the two residues most deeply inserted into the major groove are prolines, which are hydrophobic residues. This may explain why the two operator DNA sequences of CifR exhibit only very weak inverted repeats (Figure 8D ), as they are not strongly constrained by individual stereochemical contacts.

One potential source of additional stereochemical specificity involves hydrophobic van de Waals interactions between the two proline residues and adjacent DNA bases. Another possibility is that the DNA sequence forms a specific cavity or permits DNA bending to accommodate the HTH motif of CifR TCR . The dsDNA 26L duplex in the complex was slightly bent compared with standard B-form DNA. Such deformation is typically sequence-dependent, and if required for transcription-factor binding, represents a form of ‘indirect readout’ ( 67–69 ).

Compensatory minor-groove interactions

In addition to evidently indirect recognition in the DNA major groove, we found that the N-terminal extension inserts into the DNA minor groove, where Arg6 interacts directly with multiple bases (Figure 5D ), and the minor groove narrows noticeably. Rohs and colleagues have reported that a narrowed minor groove is often associated with AT-rich sequences, like those found in dsDNA 26L , and that such narrowing is frequently associated with binding of arginine side chains ( 70 ). Altogether, the combination of the hydrophobic or shape-recognition mechanism at the major groove and the direct base-sequence read-out at the minor groove appear to constitute the complete recognition mechanism between CifR TCR and its operator DNA.

In fact, CifR is not the only TFR:DNA complex that exhibits specific base interactions at the minor groove. Six previously determined TFR:DNA structures show a comparable interaction in the DNA minor groove as well. This list includes the global nitrogen regulator AmtR ( 71 ), a master regulator ms6564 ( 48 ), the efflux pump regulators SimR ( 27 ) and EilR ( 72 ), the quorum-sensing regulators CprB ( 26 ) and LuxR ( 58 ). For AmtR, SimR, CprB and LuxR, arginine residues are also found to interact with the bases in the minor groove ( Supplementary Figure S12A–D ). Contacts are mediated by an arginine and a serine in ms6564 and by a tyrosine residue for EilR ( Supplementary Figure S12E and F ).

We believe that many TFRs may exploit this recognition strategy. The sequence alignment of 938 TFRs (Figure 2A ) shows that Arg6 is relatively conserved. Also, Le et al. have analyzed the sequences of 12 715 non-redundant TFRs and found that 72% of them have an N-terminal extension longer than 11 amino acids ( 27 ). Furthermore, residues upstream of the HTH motif are often not resolved in TFR complex structures, including several where a longer operator DNA binds a single homodimer, leaving the minor groove exposed at either end. BioQ was co-crystallized in complex with a 19-bp operator, and the footprint of the TFR covers the length of the duplex, which presumably represents the extent of a canonical TetR binding site. In comparison, KstR was crystallized as a single dimer in complex with a 26-bp duplex. Although the 5′ and 3′ base pairs did not appear to form direct protein contacts, the first 30 aa of KstR could not be located in the experimental electron density. The DesT:operator complex was co-crystallized using a 31-bp construct. However, several DNA bp were not resolved on either end of the duplex, and three N-terminal side chains are missing in the structure. These results together suggest that the interaction between N-terminal extension and DNA minor groove could be a relatively common type of interaction among TFRs. Because electrostatic interactions are not directional, in some cases, N-terminal residues may be able to contribute to affinity without requiring a defined binding poise.

Co-crystallization of CifR-DNA complex and apo-CifR

A very unusual feature of our structural results is that we were able to capture a DNA-binding protein in two different states within a single crystal lattice. CifR TCR retained some tendency to precipitate in the absence of DNA but retained much higher solubility and stability in the presence of DNA, like many DNA binding proteins. Fundamentally, crystallization requires regular lattice packing, which usually requires samples to be homogenous. In fact, in an attempt to drive the thermodynamic binding equilibrium towards complex formation, we had mixed the CifR TCR protein with a molar excess of dsDNA 26L , and were therefore surprised to discover an additional, DNA-free dimer (apo-CifR TCR ) caged in the bulk solvent channel of the lattice determined by the CifR TCR -DNA complexes. Residues Tyr48 and Lys54, which are involved in DNA-binding, are therefore free in apo-CifR TCR , are located at the interface with the adjacent DNA-bound CifR TCR molecule (Figure 4B , bottom left). Thus, our hypothesis is that the lattice-binding site was able to compete with DNA for the binding of apo-CifR TCR . DNA binding may also have been disfavored by the crystallization buffer, which required the presence of 200 mM magnesium chloride, providing a relatively high ionic strength, to form well diffracting crystals. We however have no explanation for why the electron density of apo-CifR TCR is more prominent in the crystals formed by the SeMet labeled protein ( Supplementary Figure S2A versus Supplementary Figure S2B ). No methionine side chains directly participate in CifR crystal lattice formation, although SeMet labeling is known to affect protein solubility and crystal stability ( 73 , 74 ).

In this context, it is important to consider that lattice packing can potentially introduce biased conformations for proteins. Given the flexibility of α4 helix in CifR, it is possible that unbound CifR has multiple conformations. The apo-structure that was captured in the bulk solvent channel is likely to represent one of the possible conformations of the apo-CifR, but may not be the predominant species. In any case, the dual-structure lattice suggested the existence of conformational dynamics associated with the CifR structure and provided us with the unexpected opportunity to visualize the conformational effects of interactions with the operator site.

CifR conformational changes associated with DNA binding

There are both local and global structural differences between the apo and DNA-bound forms of CifR. Specific changes are observed in helices α4 and α6 of the ligand-binding domain, which are otherwise well conserved. The α4 helix connects the ligand-binding domain and HTH motif. In both structures, α4 is bent in the middle, but the angles of curvature are noticeably different. The position of the C-terminal half of α4 relative to the ligand-binding domain is conserved between the apo and the DNA-bound states (Figure 6C ), as is the position of the N-terminal half relative to the DNA-binding domain (Figure 6D ). This flexing at the middle of the α4 helix is the key to the conformational rearrangement between the domains. The motion of one domain to another as a rigid body pivot about α4 helix which acts as a hinge. Our hypothesis is that bending of the α4 helix mediates conformational crosstalk between the effector and DNA binding sites that ultimately controls c if operon transcription.

Among the nine helices in apo-CifR TCR , α6 exhibits the poorest electron density, which suggests flexibility in the absence of ligand or DNA; as a result, no model was built for this segment (Figure 6F ). As shown above, apo-CifR TCR can form dimers in the absence of reducing agent (Figure 2E ). It is likely that the flexibility of the α6 helix enables the embedded Cys107 side chains of each monomer to interact covalently. Within the PDB, the structure with the closest sequence identity to CifR is ID 2i10, a putative TFR. Even though the structure was determined at a resolution of 2.0 Å, part of the α6 helix is missing in the refined structure, whereas the other helices are all very well defined experimentally. This implies that flexibility of helix α6 could be a shared characteristic among CifR homologs.

Conformational changes involving both helices have also been observed in other TFR structures. In the case of TetR, the α6 helix opens its C-terminal turn upon ligand binding, which in turn shifts the position of the α4 helix ( 75 ). For QacR, ligand binding leads to a movement of the α6 helix, which causes a pendulum motion of the α4 helix ( 76 ). In both cases, these changes are communicated to the DNA-binding domains. However, this is not the unique mechanism for allosteric regulation. For CprB, effector binding reorganizes the dimerization interface, which in turn alters the relative position of the two HTH motifs and leads to DNA release ( 26 , 77 ). For CifR, the dimer interface remains well conserved between the two determined conformations (Figure 6B ).

Potential biomedical relevance

Overall, our study revealed the molecular underpinnings of an epoxide-responsive bacterial virulence circuit. Epoxides play important roles in cell physiology. They can be generated during catabolic processes ( 78 ) and are also used as chemical signals between microbes (e.g. fosfomycin) ( 12 , 13 ). In human cells, polyunsaturated fatty acids can be enzymatically epoxidated, yielding compounds that play diverse roles in immune system signaling ( 7 ). Our earlier work showed that microbes can regulate transcription of a virulence circuit in response to the presence of epoxides. In P. aeruginosa , CifR controls the expression of the cif operon, which encodes Cif, a secreted epoxide hydrolase that dysregulates host immune responses and facilitates infection. We are developing small-molecule and nanobody inhibitors of Cif EH activity ( 11 , 79 , 80 ). However, the availability of structural and biochemical information on CifR may provide opportunities to block the ability of P. aeruginosa to recognize epoxides, mimicking the behavior of the C107S effector-insensitive mutant. This would suppress epoxide-mediated expression not only of cif , but also of the two other members of the operon: a major facilitator symporter homolog ( mfs ) and a morphinone reductase ( morB ) ( 16 ). While their roles have not been characterized in P. aeruginosa , MFSs are often involved in critical bacterial physiological processes ( 81 , 82 ). We performed neighboring sequence-context analysis of cif-cifR operon with the effector gene cif as the search query using WebFlags ( 83 ). We found that three genes ( cif , cifR and mfs ) in this operon are conserved while morB is not ( Supplementary Figure S13 ), suggesting that morB is specific to the operon found in P. aeruginosa . Furthermore, prokaryotic transcription factors can be engineered to activate established cellular response pathways in response to new small-molecule signals ( 84 , 85 ). Our characterization of the CifR epoxide-sensing circuit may expand this toolbox to include a new class of chemical scaffolds. Overall, the molecular structures of CifR helped us to understand its mechanism of operator recognition and uncovered critical residues for ligand sensing. These results offer new insights into the stereochemical regulation of an epoxide-based virulence circuit in critically important clinical pathogen.
