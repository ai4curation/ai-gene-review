IMPORTANCE: Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is an aggressive 
malignant neoplasm with an increasing need for precision therapeutics. 
Cancer-testis antigens (CTAs) represent promising targets given their aberrant 
tumor expression and otherwise localized expression to immune-privileged tissues 
with no (testis-restricted) or minimal (testis-selective) expression in other 
human body sites. Despite their potential, limited studies rigorously evaluate 
CTAs as therapeutic targets in HNSCC.
OBJECTIVE: To orthogonally validate and present specific CTAs as potential 
precision immuno-oncologic targets in both previously untreated (de novo) and 
recurrent HNSCC tumors.
DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS: This was a cross-sectional study conducting 
multiomic analyses on a single academic tertiary care center's tumor registry 
from 2018 to 2023, with validation using publicly available transcriptomic 
datasets. A total of 33 tumor samples from patients with HNSCC, including both 
de novo and radiation-recurrent tumors, were analyzed. Data were analyzed from 
August to December 2024.
MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES: The primary outcome was the identification and 
rigorous validation of specific CTAs with tumor-specific expression in HNSCC.
RESULTS: This study analyzed 33 HNSCC institutional tumor specimens, including 
25 de novo and 8 radiation-recurrent tumors. Of 33 included patients, 25 (76%) 
were male, 8 (24%) were female, and the median (range) age was 61 (29-87) years. 
Tumor subsites included the oral cavity (24 [73%]), larynx (7 [21%]), and 
oropharynx (2 [6%]). Tumors were primarily T4a (23 [70%]), with nodal 
involvement in 16 (48%). Initial analysis of bulk RNA-sequenced institutional 
data and single-cell RNA-sequenced external data identified several CTAs (DKKL1, 
SPANXB1, SPANXD, and ACTL8) upregulated in recurrent tumors. An expanded 
reanalysis revealed that CTAs were expressed across HNSCCs, including robust 
expression not only in radiation-recurrent disease but also in de novo tumors. 
Immunohistochemistry was performed on ACTL8 to confirm transcriptional level 
findings at the protein level, which showed moderate focal cytoplasmic staining 
in tumor tissue. To refine the tumor-specific CTA list, an exclusionary analysis 
using single-cell RNA-sequenced data from normal oral mucosa was conducted, 
removing any CTAs with any expression in normal tissue. This resulted in a final 
list of 23 testis-restricted and 44 testis-selective CTAs specific to HNSCC, of 
which 14 CTAs overlapped across all transcriptomic datasets. Finally, using 
CopyKAT, CTAs were specifically enriched in malignant epithelial populations 
compared with benign populations within the same patients with HNSCC.
CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE: In this study, a set of 23 testis-restricted and 44 
testis-selective CTAs were orthogonally validated in multiple tumor datasets. Of 
these, a core set of 14 CTAs were consistently detected across all datasets. 
Their tumor specificity and broad expression bolster their potential as 
promising precision immuno-oncologic targets for future T-cell receptor 
engineering efforts.

INTRODUCTION

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is a significant public health concern, with 70,000 estimated new cases and 15,000 deaths annually in the United States.1 While the prognosis for Human Papillomavirus (HPV)-associated HNSCC has improved, leading to treatment de-escalation strategies, similar progress has not been achieved for HPV-negative HNSCC. Despite advances such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI), the management of HPV-negative disease remains challenging. Standard of care, typically involving surgery followed by post-operative radiotherapy, often leads to a considerable decline in post-treatment quality of life (QOL).2,3 Unfortunately, many patients with locoregionally advanced HPV-negative HNSCC continue to face poor outcomes, with 5-year survival rates below 50%.4 These persistently high rates of morbidity and mortality prompt the urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches that not only improve survival but also enhance QOL.

Recent efforts have increasingly focused on identifying novel precision targets that can be leveraged for therapy. Among these, cancer-testis antigens (CTAs) have emerged as potential candidates. CTAs are a group of tumor-associated antigens (TAA) whose expression is typically restricted to human germline tissue, particularly the testis.5,6 Early autologous typing studies aimed at testing T cell neo-antigen specificity against melanoma, led to the discovery of the first CTA (MAGEA1) aberrantly expressed in cancer.6 Since then, over 200 CTAs have been discovered through in vivo, in silico, and transcriptomic analyses across various malignancies.7,8 A subset of CTAs has been shown to be immunogenic, capable of triggering both humoral and cellular immune response in cancer patients.9 Testicular immune-privilege, in combination with tumor-specific expression patterns, renders CTAs as highly attractive targets for immunotherapy. To this end, peptide-based vaccines, lentiviral vector-based vaccines, and engineered T cells against CTAs have been the focus of multiple clinical trials that show clinical promise.10–13

While various CTAs have been identified as targets for other malignancies, limited studies rigorously evaluate specific CTAs in HNSCC. Moreover, to date, clinical trial data published on the efficacy of CTAs in HNSCC focus on single agent therapy. This poses a clinical challenge given that our study finds variable expression of different CTAs in different patients. Lack of investigation and genomic heterogeneity associated with HNSCC mandates further study into which specific CTAs are most actionable.

To address this, we first performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses through our institutional tumor registry, and identified upregulation of CTAs in radiation-recurrent HNSCCs. To further define the CTA landscape in HNSCC, we expanded our analysis and show robust CTA expression not only in radiation-recurrent disease, but also in previously untreated (de novo) tumors. We then orthogonally validate these findings through external datasets, including single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). We further show that the CTAs identified in our study are absent in normal oral mucosa, providing critical evidence for their potential to be leveraged in the design of targeted therapeutics. Finally, to address whether our findings extend beyond non-matched patient tumors, we demonstrate that CTAs are preferentially enriched in predicted malignant cell populations as compared to benign epithelial cells within the same patient biopsy specimens. The CTAs identified in this study outline new potential avenues for targeting molecular aberrations in HNSCC, with the goal of improving treatment outcomes for patients facing this aggressive malignancy.

CTA identification and characterization

Due to the identified upregulation of CTAs, the Cancer-Testis Antigen Database (CTdatabase)8 was employed to determine physiological expression patterns. Each identified CTA was subcategorized as being localized to immune-privileged tissues, either with no expression in other human body sites (testis-restricted) or minimal expression (testis-selective). Designing therapies against antigens with unconfirmed expression patterns poses significant risk of off-target immunotoxicity and adverse effects on healthy, non-cancerous tissues. Therefore, CTAs with unknown or unconfirmed expression in other human tissues were deemed unsuitable for further study.

In initial institutional bulk RNA-seq analysis, genes were considered exploratory CTA candidates if they showed a log2 fold change (Log2FC) ≥ 1 and a nominal p-value < 0.05. These exploratory candidates were then carried forward for rigorous validation using external datasets.

For downstream analysis assessing CTA expression across the HNSCC landscape, expression was considered present if normalized expression exceeded the following thresholds: for bulk RNA-seq data (institutional cohort and TCGA), log2(RSEM + 1) expression values > 1 in a given tumor sample; for scRNA-seq data, log-normalized expression > 1 in at least one epithelial cell cluster per tumor sample.

Single-cell data pre-processing, integration and clusteringFor re-analysis of external scRNA-seq datasets, gene expression matrices were acquired through the Gene Expression Omnibus database and re-processed using Seurat (v5.1.0). First, quality control was performed by excluding cells with fewer than 200 or more than 7500 features, eliminating low-quality cells or doublets, respectively. Cells with mitochondrial gene expression exceeding 10% were also filtered out to exclude dying or dead cells. Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) was performed using 50 dimensions to address batch effects. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the 4,000 most highly variable features identified by the “vst” method. Clustering was then performed using the Louvain algorithm and were then visualized on the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plot. Cluster cell types were identified by unsupervised differential expression gene (DEG) analysis using the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test, combined with canonical marker gene annotation. Given the focus on squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) in this study, further downstream analysis was specifically performed on the epithelial cell cluster. Epithelial cells were identified using known markers such as EPCAM, KRT5, and KRT14. DEG analysis was performed comparing de novo and recurrent tumors using appropriate FDR correction (FDR < 0.05 and log fold change ≥ 1).

RESULTSExploratory analysis identifies certain CTAs upregulated in radiation-recurrent versus de novo HNSCCsTumor biopsy samples were categorized based on whether patients presented with a de novo HNSCC or underwent biopsy for an in-field recurrence in a previously irradiated tumor bed at the time of salvage surgery. eTable 1 in Supplement 1 details patient demographics and tumor characteristics for the selected twenty-five de novo and eight radiation-recurrent samples.Exploratory DEG analysis of bulk RNA-seq data comparing de novo and radiation-recurrent HNSCC tumors identified multiple CTAs with nominal upregulation in recurrent samples (Figure 1A and eTable 2 in Supplement 1). The CTdatabase was subsequently used to isolate only those CTAs with known expression patterns in other human tissues. This yielded DKKL1 (Log2FC = 1.46, p = 0.039) as the only testis-restricted CTA, and ACTL8 (Log2FC = 4.41, p = <0.001) as the only testis-selective CTA upregulated in recurrent tumors based on exploratory thresholds (Log2FC ≥ 1, nominal p-value < 0.05) (Figure 1B). Two additional CTAs, ELOVL4 and TMEM108, were also found to be upregulated but did not meet criteria for consideration as meaningful immunotherapy targets due to their unconfirmed expression patterns.Given high expression of ACTL8 in our institutional tumor registry, we validated transcriptional findings at the protein level via immunohistochemistry (IHC). ACTL8 exhibited moderate cytoplasmic staining in a focal pattern (Figure 1C). The stained cells appear to follow the architecture of epithelial tumor nests. Surrounding stromal regions and connective tissue lack significant staining. Positive controls (testis) and negative controls (skin) validated staining specificity.Although adjusted p-values did not reach statistical significance, this likely reflects the underrepresentation of radiation-recurrent tumors (8 of 33 total samples) in our cohort. Because multiple hypothesis testing corrections (such as FDR) are sensitive to group size and variance, the relatively small number of radiation-recurrent tumors may have limited power to detect statistically significant differential expression. Despite this imbalance, candidate CTAs showed nominal upregulation, suggesting a potentially meaningful signal that warrants further investigation.Single-cell RNA-seq confirms significant epithelial upregulation of select CTAs in recurrent versus de novo HNSCCsTo validate exploratory findings from bulk RNA-seq and to determine the intra-tumoral origins of CTAs at single-cell resolution, we re-analyzed an external scRNA-seq dataset (GEO Accession GSE2349332)21 of de novo and recurrent HNSCCs. eTable 3 in Supplement 1 outlines the ten samples selected from a total fifty-two samples in the dataset for analysis. These samples were selected to include oral cavity tumors with adequate data quality, and equal representation of de novo and locoregional recurrent tumors. The selected tumor samples were integrated, clustered, and cell-type identities were determined using unsupervised clustering analysis and marker gene annotation (Figure 2A). DEG analysis using Log2FC ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 as thresholds identified two testis-restricted CTAs, SPANXB1 (Log2FC = 4.79, FDR = 0.006) and SPANXD (Log2FC = 5.05, FDR = 0.006), and two testis-selective CTAs, PLAC1 (Log2FC = 4.20, FDR < 0.001) and AKAP3 (Log2FC = 2.16, FDR = 0.003), as significantly upregulated in recurrent versus de novo tumors (Figure 2B). Interestingly, the testis-restricted CTAs showed virtually no expression in de novo tumors, while the testis-selective CTAs were significantly upregulated in recurrent tumors but also expressed at higher levels in de novo tumors compared to testis-restricted CTAs.CTA expression is broadly detected across de novo and recurrent HNSCCsWhile our initial findings suggest CTAs may have value as immunotherapy targets in recurrent HNSCCs, we extended our analysis to determine if CTAs may serve as potential immuno-oncologic targets in previously untreated patients. We conducted a comprehensive re-analysis using normalized expression values of 1) institutional bulk RNA-seq data; 2) external HNSCC scRNA-seq data; and 3) TCGA. We screened for expression of only those CTAs listed in the CTdatabase with known physiologic expression.In the institutional bulk RNA-seq dataset (n = 33 tumors), we observed several testis-restricted CTAs (including MAGEA1, MAGEB2, SPANXB1) and testis-selective CTAs (such as MAGEA3, MAGEA4, ACTL8) with normalized expression values exceeding 1. Expression was assessed in aggregate across de novo and recurrent tumors. In the scRNA-seq data (n = 10), many of these same CTAs were observed within the epithelial cluster, and additionally identified new testis-selective CTAs (such as CCDC36 and ROPN1) that were not detected in the institutional bulk RNA-seq data. Expression was considered present if normalized counts exceeded 1 in at least one distinct epithelial cluster per tumor sample. We validate that select CTAs are exclusively expressed within malignant epithelial cells of biopsied tumors. Additionally, to our knowledge, this is the first study to provide crucial evidence identifying the absence of certain HNSCC CTAs in normal oral mucosa. This absence has significant implications on the design of immunotherapies aimed at minimizing on-target, off-tumor cytotoxicity, and help future efforts in the selection of targets for precision therapies, such as TCR-engineered T cells.In validating CTA expression across different datasets spanning 610 tumors, we find remarkable overlap of specific CTAs between institutional bulk RNA-seq, external scRNA-seq datasets, and TCGA, all derived from non-matched HNSCC tumors. In our institutional cohort of de novo tumors, we find high expression of testis-selective ACTL8. To investigate this further, we validated expression of ACTL8 via IHC, which revealed moderate focal cytoplasmic staining, confirming expression at the protein level. Supporting our findings, prior in vitro studies conducted on the PCI-13 cell line with transfected ACTL8 revealed that knockdown of ACTL8 inhibited cell proliferation, invasion and migration.25 This effect was attributed to ACTL8’s suppression of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which regulates cell cycle progression, growth, and survival.25 Building on our findings in de novo tumors, analysis of recurrent tumors reveals significant upregulation of genes specifically from the testis-restricted SPANX family. Their presence in a more aggressive tumor type correlates with other studies in which SPANX family genes were found to accelerate the metastasis of ER-negative breast cancer cells. To date, clinical trials testing TCR-engineered therapies against CTAs in HNSCC focus on single agent therapy.27–29 Given the breadth of CTAs we identify with varying expression levels in different tumor samples, our data suggests that a precision approach is required. Considering the significant genomic heterogeneity that has long defined HNSCC, such an approach will be critical for scaling TCR-engineered therapies to match each patient’s unique antigenic landscape. Large libraries of targeted CTA therapies may be required for optimal outcomes, matching patient-specific expression profiles to a therapeutic choice. In validating CTA expression across different datasets spanning 610 tumors, we find remarkable overlap of specific CTAs between institutional bulk RNA-seq, external scRNA-seq datasets, and TCGA, all derived from non-matched HNSCC tumors. In particular, fourteen CTAs were consistently expressed across all three datasets from diverse tumor contexts and patient populations. This convergence of multi-omic data bolsters the biological relevance of CTAs in HNSCC, and minimizes the attributions of our findings to technical artifact or random expression patterns.

The biological function of CTAs has increasingly been studied. While some CTAs are known as bystander genes with aberrant expression in tumors, others may also play an active role in oncogenesis. However, these classifications are not always distinct. Certain CTAs, such as those from the MAGE family, have been characterized as bystander genes but have also been implicated in tumor progression once expressed.23,24 Given this, the tumor-specific expression of CTAs we find may be exploited for therapeutic purposes in one of two ways.

First, if CTAs act as drivers of oncogenesis and proliferation, targeted inhibitors can be developed. In our institutional cohort of de novo tumors, we find high expression of testis-selective ACTL8. To investigate this further, we validated expression of ACTL8 via IHC, which revealed moderate focal cytoplasmic staining, confirming expression at the protein level. Supporting our findings, prior in vitro studies conducted on the PCI-13 cell line with transfected ACTL8 revealed that knockdown of ACTL8 inhibited cell proliferation, invasion and migration.25 This effect was attributed to ACTL8’s suppression of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which regulates cell cycle progression, growth, and survival.25 Building on our findings in de novo tumors, analysis of recurrent tumors reveals significant upregulation of genes specifically from the testis-restricted SPANX family. Their presence in a more aggressive tumor type correlates with other studies in which SPANX family genes were found to accelerate the metastasis of ER-negative breast cancer cells. Additionally, higher expression levels in breast cancer tumors were correlated with poorer prognosis.26 While this does shed light on the potential role of SPANX CTAs in promoting tumor aggression, their true in vivo role in HNSCC remains yet to be determined.

Second, if CTAs function as bystander genes with tumor-restricted expression and their peptide fragments are presented by HLA class I, they may serve as targets for HLA-restricted TCR-engineered therapies. To date, clinical trials testing TCR-engineered therapies against CTAs in HNSCC focus on single agent therapy.27–29 Given the breadth of CTAs we identify with varying expression levels in different tumor samples, our data suggests that a precision approach is required. Considering the significant genomic heterogeneity that has long defined HNSCC, such an approach will be critical for scaling TCR-engineered therapies to match each patient’s unique antigenic landscape. Large libraries of targeted CTA therapies may be required for optimal outcomes, matching patient-specific expression profiles to a therapeutic choice. As such, transcriptomic methods to screen for specific CTA expression levels may need to be employed in the pretreatment setting. More practically, PCR or IHC analyses on available tumor tissue can be utilized for evaluating expression levels, as we demonstrate with ACTL8.

While our analysis orthogonally validates CTA expression patterns, we encounter significant heterogeneity in CTA expression among individual malignant clones within each tumor. This variability limits our understanding of the percentage of malignant cells expressing a given CTA. Bulk RNA-seq lacks the resolution to capture this detail, and scRNA-seq is limited by technical dropout resulting from stochastic inefficiency of mRNA capture.30 As sequencing technologies advance, approaches like long-read sequencing or improvements in single-cell technology may help bridge this gap.31,32 Additionally, while the use of external datasets in our study provides opportunity for validation and cross-comparison of findings, they do pose some limitations. Although CTAs identified in the external scRNA-seq dataset align with our institutional bulk RNA-seq data, performing scRNA-seq on the same institutional cohort of patients would have minimized variability between findings. Furthermore, available scRNA-seq HNSCC datasets lack patient-matched tumor recurrent samples. This hindered our ability to track molecular changes over time within the same tumors and complicated the distinction between inter-patient variability and true longitudinal changes associated with recurrence.

In conclusion, we present a core set of fourteen CTAs from a broader group of twenty-three testis-restricted and forty-four testis-selective CTAs, all orthogonally validated and consistently expressed in both de novo and recurrent HNSCCs. These findings may allow us to exploit the aberrancy of CTAs within HNSCCs, while leveraging their immune-privileged expression patterns as promising precision immuno-oncologic targets. Future studies should focus on developing targeted therapeutics towards HNSCC, such as HLA-matched TCR-engineered T cells, against the CTAs we present. Additionally, confirming CTA expression in the metastatic disease setting would further elucidate their potential as systemic targets. Establishing consistent immunogenicity of CTAs aberrantly expressed in de novo, recurrent, and metastatic settings could open the door to revolutionizing outcomes for a patient population in dire need of novel therapies.
