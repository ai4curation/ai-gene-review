Neurodegenerative diseases, which pose significant challenges for effective
treatment, often involve risk variants of lysosomal gene products that disrupt
lysosomal function, leading to the accumulation of indigestible materials and
damage to brain cells. The lysosome is a degradative organelle and a signaling
hub that senses nutrient availability. How lysosomal dysfunction contributes to
neurodegenerative diseases is an important open question. In this study, we
identified CLN3 (ceroid lipofuscinosis, neuronal 3), an endolysosomal protein
that is linked to Batten disease, as an evolutionarily conserved protein that
facilitates lysosomal chloride efflux. Additionally, we report that a natural
compound with anti-inflammatory properties-the curcumin analog C1, which is a
TFEB (transcription factor EB) activator-could enhance CLN3 activity and improve
lysosomal function. These findings provide new insight into the role of CLN3 in
lysosomal ion homeostasis and raise the possibility that modulation of the
TFEB-CLN3 signaling axis may hold therapeutic potential for lysosomal storage
disorders.

Introduction

Lysosomes are responsible for degrading macromolecules and damaged organelles to recycle nutrient components1,2. Mutations of genes encoding lysosomal proteins can lead to lysosomal storage diseases (LSDs) and are associated with age-related disorders, including neurodegenerative diseases3–6 and cancer7,8. Batten disease, also known as neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (NCL), is a neurodegenerative LSD that shortens lifespan9. It is caused by mutations of 13 different genes in the NCL family with similar disease linkage rather than sequence similarity. Batten disease typically manifests during childhood or adolescence with an incidence rate ranging from 1 in 14,000 to 1 in 1,000,000 and is characterized by progressive cognitive, motor and visual decline10,11. While 13 NCL genes have been identified, the physiological functions of their gene products remain largely unknown, making it difficult to develop rationally designed targeted therapies12,13. Recent work has identified mutations in the CLCN6 gene, encoding a lysosomal chloride channel, that cause a new form of NCL14. This discovery underscores the importance of chloride channels in lysosomal physiology and suggests that dysregulated Cl− conductance may represent a pathogenic mechanism in LSDs. It is important to determine the identity, regulation, and physiological impact of other NCL-related lysosomal chloride channels.

The distribution of ions across the lysosomal membrane is asymmetric, a feature essential for lysosomal function15. Lysosomes not only have a high concentration of H+ in the lumen but also serve as crucial intracellular reservoirs for metal ions including Ca2+, Fe2+ and Zn2+ ions15,16. Ion movement across the lysosomal membrane leads to changes in membrane potential (ΔΨ), which impacts lysosomal ion homeostasis and contributes to lysosome-related diseases17,18. However, how an abnormal ion distribution across the lysosome membrane contributes to LSDs is not well understood. Hence, elucidating the relationship between lysosomal ion homeostasis and neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis is essential in identifying novel therapeutic targets.

In this study, we screened members of the NCL family for potential ion channel function. Our experiments provide the first direct evidence that CLN3, a protein previously associated with Batten disease, mediates chloride efflux from endolysosomes. Furthermore, we discovered that activation of the TFEB transcription factor could enhance CLN3 currents and partially restore abnormal lysosomal storage in primary culture of embryonic fibroblasts from Cln3+/− mice, suggesting TFEB functions upstream of CLN3. Our findings raise the possibility that the nutrient-sensitive TFEB-CLN3 signaling pathway may be implicated in the pathophysiology of LSDs.

ResultsThe Batten disease-associated protein CLN3 is essential for endolysosomal chloride currentsTo investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying Batten disease, we conducted a functional screen for candidate proteins associated with neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis. We focused our attention on proteins predicted to have at least one transmembrane (TM) segment (a characteristic shared by all known ion channels). We explored these candidates using patch-clamp electrophysiological recording from endolysosomes, with the bath and pipette solutions containing all major inorganic ions (Figure 1A). We found CLN3 significantly increased the outward-rectifying currents when it was overexpressed in HEK293T cells (Figure 1B). This outward current could arise from positive charges moving into the lumen or negative charges moving out. Substitution of the principal cations (K+, Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+) with N-methyl-D-glucamine (NMDG+), a large organic monovalent cation known to be impermeable to most ion channels, did not significantly alter the outward current in cells with overexpression of CLN3 (Figures 1C and 1D). The pipette solution included Cl− as the only anion, raising the question whether Cl− ions contributed to this outward current. Indeed, reduction of the [Cl−] concentration in the pipette solution from 150 mM to 1 mM abolished the outward current (Figures 1C and 1D), suggesting that CLN3 mediates an endolysosomal Cl− conductance.To explore the contribution of CLN3 to endogenous Cl− currents, we employed the CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing to generate CLN3 knockout cell lines in HEK293T and Neuro-2a cells (Figures S1A, 1E and 1F). Given that CLN7 (MFSD8) is another lysosomal chloride channel implicated in endolysosomal chloride conductance19, we further generated CLN7 knockout and CLN3/CLN7 double knockout cell lines to evaluate their individual contributions and potential interplay in regulating endogenous lysosomal chloride currents (Figure S1A). Patch-clamp recordings revealed that CLN7 knockout caused a moderate reduction in chloride currents in endolysosomes, whereas CLN3 knockout resulted in a more pronounced decrease (Figures S1B–S1F). Confocal imaging confirmed that CLN3 and CLN7 localize to lysosomes independently (Figures S1G and S1H). Patch-clamp recording from endolysosomes showed that overexpression of CLN3 in CLN7 knockout cells elicited smaller chloride currents compared to CLN3 overexpression in wild-type control cells. Similarly, CLN7 overexpression in CLN3 knockout cells produced reduced currents relative to CLN7 overexpression in wild-type control cells (Figures S1I–S1N). Collectively, these results suggest that CLN3 and CLN7 may act in parallel, but CLN3 is the main contributor to lysosomal chloride currents under our conditions.To gain insight into the molecular characteristics of CLN3, we next examined its sequence, topology, evolutionary conservation, and expression profile. CLN3 shares no significant sequence similarity to any other protein with known function, aside from a distant resemblance to the equilibrative nucleoside transporter family SLC29 and fatty acid desaturases20,21. Using deep neural networks and a DeepTMHMM model method22, human CLN3 (hCLN3) is predicted to have eleven transmembrane segments, indicating it is indeed a membrane protein (Figure 1G). In addition, amino acid sequence analysis reveals that CLN3 is evolutionarily conserved among multiple species, with 86% identity between human and mouse, and 51% identity between human and zebrafish23,24. Furthermore, RNA-sequencing databases show that hCLN3 is broadly expressed in various tissues, including colon, lung, skin and other organs (Figure 1H)25–30, suggesting CLN3 may play a role in fundamental cellular processes across different tissues. Lastly, we investigated the subcellular localization of CLN3 and observed that EGFP-tagged CLN3 puncta co-localized with the lysosome marker LAMP2 (Figure 1I) and early endosome marker Rab5 (Figure 1J)31–33, supporting a role for CLN3 in regulating the endolysosomal system.Next, we assessed whether CLN3 is sufficient to mediate chloride conductance in a non-lysosomal membrane context. For this test, we redirected CLN3 to the plasma membrane by mutating its lysosomal targeting motif, as previously described34. Confocal imaging confirmed its predominant localization at the plasma membrane (Figure S2A). Whole-cell recordings of HEK293T cells expressing CLN3 targeted to the plasma membrane revealed robust chloride currents, comparable to those in HEK293T cells expressing lysosomal CLN3 (Figures S2B–S2D). These results demonstrate that CLN3 maintains its chloride channel activity independent of the lysosomal environment, establishing its sufficiency as an autonomous chloride-conducting channel.CLN3 exhibits properties of chloride channelsPrevious studies have shown that certain chloride channels exhibit pH sensitivity35–37. Given that endosomes and lysosomes possess acidic luminal environments, we assessed the pH sensitivity of CLN3. Using a ramp protocol, we conducted experiments on Cln3 knockout (KO) Neuro-2a cell lines, which have larger endogenous lysosomal chloride currents than those in HEK293T cell (Figures 1E and 1F, S1B and S1C). Unlike wild type (WT) cells, Cln3 KO cells showed no sensitivity of endolysosomal currents to pH changes, whereas endolysosomal currents of WT cells displayed peak activity at pH 4.6, corresponding to the optimal lysosomal luminal pH (Figures 2A–2C). Similar conclusions were obtained using a step protocol (Figures 2D and 2E). Taken together, our results showed that CLN3 currents were regulated by luminal pH with the peak activity at around the range of optimal lysosome luminal pH, indicating that CLN3 activity aligns with the physiological conditions of endolysosomes (pH 4.6).We further tested 4,4′-Diisothiocyanatostilbene-2,2′-disulfonate (DIDS), niflumic acid (NFA), and 5-Nitro-2-(3-phenylpropylamino)benzoic acid (NPPB), which are well-established chloride channel inhibitors38. Our experiments demonstrated that application of each of these inhibitors to the bath solutions effectively inhibited CLN3-mediated currents in endolysosomes, further indicating that CLN3 is a chloride channel (Figures S3A–S3F).Critical residues in the putative pore affect anion selectivity in the CLN3 channelWhereas our findings support the notion that CLN3 forms a chloride channel, it is important to demonstrate the involvement of CLN3 in specifying the ion selectivity of this chloride channel. To approach this question, we first used AlphaFold to predict whether CLN3 has the necessary domains and motifs typical of ion channels, such as pore-lining regions responsible for the selectivity of permeant ions. In addition, we leveraged a database of mutations identified in patients with neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis. Combined with AlphaFold predictions, we selected specific mutations (Y52A, E295K, R334C) for further study and mapped them onto the predicted topology of CLN3 (Figures 3A and S3G)39.Next, we examined whether these three mutations affect lysosomal chloride currents. We first tested whether protein localization is affected by these mutations through live imaging experiments and observed that the mutant proteins still exhibited substantial localization in endolysosomes (Figures 3B and 3C). As a functional test, we performed electrophysiological recordings of whole-endolysosomal currents and found that each of the mutations reduced Cl− currents compared to that of wild-type CLN3 (Figures 3D–3H). It is worth noting that two of these three mutations, E295K and R334C, are known pathogenic mutations40,41. Therefore, our findings provide a potential mechanistic explanation for the clinical symptoms associated with these two CLN3 mutations.Our AlphaFold model predicts that these residues are positioned near the putative pore (Figure 3A), raising the possibility that mutation of these residues may alter the ion selectivity. To test this notion, we measured the permeability for I− or Br− normalized by the permeability for Cl− (the permeability ratios) for the Y52A, E295K, and R334C mutations. While the R334C mutation did not alter the permeability ratios, both Y52A and E295K mutations decreased the permeability for Br− or I− normalized by the permeability for Cl− (Figures 3I–3M). These findings suggest that CLN3 is a pore-forming subunit of the proton-activated Cl− channel, with Y52 and E295 likely located near the ion-conducting pathway.To further assess whether the putative pore residues contribute to inhibitor binding, we tested the sensitivity of the Y52A, E295K, and R334C mutants to 100 μM DIDS. While DIDS reduced wild-type CLN3-mediated currents to 28.7% of baseline levels (Figures S3A and S3D), all three mutants exhibited attenuated inhibition by DIDS, retaining 47.4%, 44.6%, and 52.9% of the currents, respectively (Figures S3H–S3M). These findings suggest that these residues not only shape anion selectivity but also influence blocker interaction, providing further evidence that CLN3 is integral to the ion conduction pathway.Zebrafish Cln3 also encodes an endolysosomal chloride channel but with distinct propertiesCross-species studies are essential for uncovering the conserved functions of lysosomal proteins, as they provide insights into their fundamental biological roles. Zebrafish, a widely used vertebrate model, offers genetic tractability and exhibits functional conservation with humans. When expressed in HEK293T cells, zebrafish CLN3 is also localized in lysosomes and endosomes (Figures 4A and 4B), similar to the localization of the human CLN3 protein. The conservation of the protein localization indicates that CLN3 may possess a conserved function among species shown in the phylogenetic tree (Figure 4C). To further explore the potential functional conservation, we characterize the channel properties of zebrafish CLN3.First, expression of zCLN3 in HEK293T cells led to a significant increase in endolysosomal chloride currents, as evidenced by electrophysiological recordings (Figures 4D–4F). This observation strongly supports the hypothesis that CLN3 serves an evolutionarily conserved function as a chloride channel in lysosomes. Notably, the activation kinetics of zCLN3 currents were considerably slower than those of hCLN3 currents (Figures 4G and 4H), suggesting species-specific variations in channel gating mechanisms.In addition to kinetic differences, we found that zCLN3 channels exhibited a different ion selectivity profile compared to their human counterpart. Unlike hCLN3, which shows a clear preference for Cl− over I−, zCLN3 channels demonstrated a reduced preference for Cl− over I− (Figures 4I–4K). This shift in ion selectivity further highlights the functional divergence between zebrafish and human CLN3 chloride channels. Taken together, these findings confirm that the role of CLN3 as a lysosomal chloride channel is evolutionarily conserved. The presence of species-specific variations in channel gating, conductance and ion selectivity reflects important functional differences that may have implications for understanding species-specific differences in CLN3-related pathologies.To further evaluate the evolutionary conservation of disease-associated CLN3 mutations, we introduced the corresponding substitutions (Y39A, E304K, and R343C, equivalent to human Y52A, E295K, and R334C) into the zebrafish ortholog (Figure S4A). Patch-clamp recordings showed that these mutations markedly reduced chloride current amplitudes and altered anion selectivity as compared to those of wild-type zebrafish CLN3 (Figures S4B–S4F, S4G–S4K). The resulting functional deficits closely mirrored those observed with the human mutations, supporting the evolutionarily conserved pathogenic mechanisms across species. Moreover, these findings demonstrate that zebrafish CLN3 shares conserved subcellular localization and chloride channel activity with its human counterpart, notwithstanding species-specific differences in gating and ion selectivity. These conserved phenotypes of disease-associated mutations support zebrafish CLN3 as a relevant model for lysosomal dysfunction.CLN3 is essential for lysosomal functionsGiven that CLN3 functions as an outward-rectifying Cl− channel, we hypothesized that its loss would lead to chloride accumulation in the lysosomal lumen, thereby disrupting lysosomal ion homeostasis. To directly assess this possibility, we generated a construct encoding the chloride sensor EYFP-H148Q/I152L fused to the lysosomal targeting sequence of Lamp2. Confocal imaging confirmed that the sensor was localized to lysosomes (Figures S5A and S5B). HEK293T cells were transfected with the lysosome-targeted Cl− sensor, and luminal chloride levels were assessed in WT, CLN3 knockout, and CLN3-rescued conditions. We found that the fluorescence intensity of this chloride sensor was markedly reduced in CLN3-knockout cells compared to control cells, consistent with increased chloride accumulation in the lysosomal lumen leading to fluorescence quenching. Importantly, stable re-expression of CLN3 in knockout cells fully restored the fluorescence signal to levels comparable to controls (Figures 5A and 5B). Whereas these results indicate that CLN3 plays a critical role in maintaining lysosomal Cl− homeostasis, and its loss indeed leads to abnormal chloride accumulation within lysosomes, it is important to bear in mind that lysosomal pH can affect the fluorescence intensity of chloride sensors, and pH differences between genotypes could partially influence the measured chloride signal.Having found that CLN3 deficiency leads to Cl− accumulation in the lysosomal lumen, we next considered the physiological consequences of disrupted chloride homeostasis. Given that previous studies have shown that Cl− acts as a counter-ion for maintaining lysosomal pH42, we hypothesized that impaired Cl− efflux would lead to excessive H+ accumulation and result in a decrease in luminal pH. To test this hypothesis, we imaged LysoSensor, a lysosomal pH indicator, in HEK293T cell, and found that knocking out CLN3 reduced the luminal pH in both fed and starved cells. This decrease in lysosomal pH was partially rescued by transfecting the cells with CLN3 (Figures 5C–5F). Similar results were also observed when lysosomal pH was precisely measured by using the ratiometric dye Oregon Green 488 Dextran (Figure 5G).Furthermore, we examined the effect of two mutations, CLN3-Y52A and CLN3-E295K that altered the ion channel properties of CLN3, including current amplitude and anion selectivity (Figures 3E, 3F, 3H, 3J, 3K and 3M). Cells expressing CLN3 with these mutations exhibited a similar reduction in lysosomal pH to that seen in CLN3-knockout cells (Figures 5H and 5I). LysoSensor imaging confirmed that neither mutation could restore the lysosomal pH to levels observed in CLN3-knockout cells expressing wild-type CLN3 (Figures 5H and 5I). Consistent results were obtained when lysosomal pH was independently measured using the ratiometric dye Oregon Green 488 Dextran (Figure 5J). In addition, emerging evidence indicates that their function also varies with luminal Cl− levels43,44, raising the question of whether lysosomal enzyme activity depends on CLN3 function. We measured the activity of cathepsin L, a lysosomal enzyme commonly used as an indicator for lysosome functions, and observed a decrease in cathepsin L activity in cells lacking CLN3 (Figures 5K and 5L). Notably, stable re-expression of CLN3 in Cln3-knockout cells significantly restored normal lysosomal cathepsin L activity (Figures 5K and 5L).Protein degradation within lysosomes is crucial for maintaining cellular proteostasis. We incubated Neuro-2a cells with DQ-BSA, a fluorescent probe used to indicate the lysosome degradation capability, and found that the fluorescence intensity was markedly reduced in Cln3-knockout cells compared to control cells, while the signal of this fluorescent reporter was fully restored in knockout cells stably re-expressing CLN3 (Figures 5M and 5N). It thus appears that the protein degradation process is impaired in cells lacking CLN3. To further investigate whether CLN3 deficiency affects the completion of protein degradation, we assessed the clearance of p62, an autophagic substrate45–48. We found that intracellular p62 levels in Cln3-knockout cells were significantly higher than those in control cells, a phenotype that was significantly rescued by stably re-expressing CLN3 in Cln3-knockout cells (Figures S6A–S6D). Together, these data indicate that CLN3 plays a critical role in maintaining lysosomal homeostasis. Moreover, CLN3-overexpressing cells generated significantly larger endolysosomal chloride currents under the same conditions (Figures S7E and S7F), indicating that CLN3 function is regulated by nutrient availability. Treatment with the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin did not affect endolysosomal chloride currents, likely due to its partial inhibition of mTORC1 activity52–54 and the presence of mTOR-independent TFEB activation pathways55 (Figures S8A and S8B). Consistent with these previous findings, whereas the TFEB-specific activator Curcumin analog C1 markedly increased TFEB nuclear translocation and the TFEB inhibitor Eltrombopag reduced TFEB nuclear localization (Figures S8C–S8F), rapamycin treatment failed to promote TFEB nuclear translocation (Figures S8G and S8H).To further investigate whether the lysosomal nutrient signaling factor TFEB is involved in CLN3 regulation, we tested for the response to Curcumin analog C1 or Eltrombopag (Figure 6A). To confirm that the observed effects were indeed CLN3-dependent, we treated CLN3-KO cells with Curcumin analog C1 and found that it failed to increase endolysosomal chloride currents (Figures S9A–S9F). This result indicates that the enhancement of chloride conductance by Curcumin analog C1 requires functional CLN3. We then analyzed the mRNA expression of CLN3 under drug treatment conditions. We found that the CLN3 expression was increased by Curcumin analog C1 but reduced by Eltrombopag (Figures 6F and 6G), changes that could underlie the effect of these TFEB modulators on CLN3-mediated endolysosomal chloride currents. These findings suggest that TFEB is involved in regulating CLN3 expression level. Furthermore, we also found that loss of CLN3 function led to decreased TFEB expression, while overexpressing CLN3 did not affect TFEB protein levels or nuclear translocation, indicating that CLN3 may play a necessary but not sufficient role for TFEB expression (Figures 6H and 6I, S8I and S8J). This finding suggests that CLN3 is a critical component of the TFEB regulatory network, and the loss of CLN3 may disrupt upstream or auxiliary mechanisms essential for maintaining TFEB expression.To further explore the function of CLN3 in vivo, we examined a Cln3 knockout mouse line generated via CRISPR-Cas9 technology (Figures 6J and 6K)56. Consistent with previous findings, CLN3-deficient mice exhibited neurodegenerative features including accumulation of ATP synthase subunit C (SCMAS)57–59, increased autofluorescence, and activation of astrocytes and microglia marked by GFAP and Iba1 proteins60,61, respectively (Figures 6L–6O). Western blot analysis confirmed accumulation of SCMAS, GFAP, and Iba1 in Cln3 knockout mouse brain (Figures S10A–S10F). However, heterozygous Cln3 (Cln3+/−) mice did not exhibit this phenotype in our experiments (data not shown). We also observed that primary mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) derived from Cln3−/− mouse embryos exhibited autofluorescence and SCMAS accumulation (Figures S10G–S10J). These data corroborate lysosomal dysfunction and neuroinflammatory activation in both brain tissue and cellular models of CLN3 deficiency. Notably, primary culture of mouse embryonic fibroblasts isolated from Cln3+/− pregnant mice showed SCMAS accumulation (Figure 6P), establishing it as a useful in vitro model to further investigate the TFEB-CLN3 signaling axis and its contribution to lysosomal dysfunction. Interestingly, we observed that treatment with Curcumin analog C1 led to a noticeable reduction in SCMAS accumulation in Cln3+/− MEFs, whereas no such effect was observed in Cln3−/− MEFs. These results suggest that the presence of residual CLN3 may be necessary for Curcumin analog C1 to exert its modulatory effect on lysosomal homeostasis (Figures 6P–6R). To further assess lysosomal functional restoration, we measured lysosomal pH across genotypes after Curcumin analog C1 treatment. Consistent with our findings regarding SCMAS clearance, Curcumin analog C1 treatment elevated lysosomal pH in WT and Cln3+/− MEFs but had no effect in Cln3−/− MEFs (Figures S11A and S11B), reinforcing the requirement of functional CLN3 for TFEB-mediated lysosomal repair. In summary, our results demonstrate that CLN3 is a key target downstream of TFEB signaling. Curcumin analog C1 could activate TFEB to enhance CLN3 expression and channel activity, thereby providing mechanistic insight into the regulation of lysosomal homeostasis. While these findings highlight the TFEB-CLN3 axis as an important molecular pathway to modulate pathology, further in vivo studies are necessary to evaluate its therapeutic potential for Batten disease.

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying Batten disease, we conducted a functional screen for candidate proteins associated with neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis. We focused our attention on proteins predicted to have at least one transmembrane (TM) segment (a characteristic shared by all known ion channels). This outward current could arise from positive charges moving into the lumen or negative charges moving out. To test whether CLN3 is involved in lysosomal nutrient signaling, we subjected wild-type (non-transfected) cells to starvation conditions and observed a slight increase in endolysosomal chloride currents (Figures S7A and S7B), however, knocking out CLN3 eliminated this response (Figures S7C and S7D). Treatment with the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin did not affect endolysosomal chloride currents, likely due to its partial inhibition of mTORC1 activity52–54 and the presence of mTOR-independent TFEB activation pathways55 (Figures S8A and S8B). Consistent with these previous findings, whereas the TFEB-specific activator Curcumin analog C1 markedly increased TFEB nuclear translocation and the TFEB inhibitor Eltrombopag reduced TFEB nuclear localization (Figures S8C–S8F), rapamycin treatment failed to promote TFEB nuclear translocation (Figures S8G and S8H).

To further investigate whether the lysosomal nutrient signaling factor TFEB is involved in CLN3 regulation, we tested for the response to Curcumin analog C1 or Eltrombopag (Figure 6A). Compared to the control, treatment with Curcumin analog C1 increased CLN3-mediated endolysosomal chloride currents (Figures 6B and 6D), while treatment with Eltrombopag reduced these currents (Figures 6C and 6E). To confirm that the observed effects were indeed CLN3-dependent, we treated CLN3-KO cells with Curcumin analog C1 and found that it failed to increase endolysosomal chloride currents (Figures S9A–S9F). This result indicates that the enhancement of chloride conductance by Curcumin analog C1 requires functional CLN3. We then analyzed the mRNA expression of CLN3 under drug treatment conditions. We found that the CLN3 expression was increased by Curcumin analog C1 but reduced by Eltrombopag (Figures 6F and 6G), changes that could underlie the effect of these TFEB modulators on CLN3-mediated endolysosomal chloride currents. These findings suggest that TFEB is involved in regulating CLN3 expression level. Furthermore, we also found that loss of CLN3 function led to decreased TFEB expression, while overexpressing CLN3 did not affect TFEB protein levels or nuclear translocation, indicating that CLN3 may play a necessary but not sufficient role for TFEB expression (Figures 6H and 6I, S8I and S8J). This finding suggests that CLN3 is a critical component of the TFEB regulatory network, and the loss of CLN3 may disrupt upstream or auxiliary mechanisms essential for maintaining TFEB expression.

To further explore the function of CLN3 in vivo, we examined a Cln3 knockout mouse line generated via CRISPR-Cas9 technology (Figures 6J and 6K)56. Consistent with previous findings, CLN3-deficient mice exhibited neurodegenerative features including accumulation of ATP synthase subunit C (SCMAS)57–59, increased autofluorescence, and activation of astrocytes and microglia marked by GFAP and Iba1 proteins60,61, respectively (Figures 6L–6O). Western blot analysis confirmed accumulation of SCMAS, GFAP, and Iba1 in Cln3 knockout mouse brain (Figures S10A–S10F). However, heterozygous Cln3 (Cln3+/−) mice did not exhibit this phenotype in our experiments (data not shown). We also observed that primary mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) derived from Cln3−/− mouse embryos exhibited autofluorescence and SCMAS accumulation (Figures S10G–S10J). These data corroborate lysosomal dysfunction and neuroinflammatory activation in both brain tissue and cellular models of CLN3 deficiency. Notably, primary culture of mouse embryonic fibroblasts isolated from Cln3+/− pregnant mice showed SCMAS accumulation (Figure 6P), establishing it as a useful in vitro model to further investigate the TFEB-CLN3 signaling axis and its contribution to lysosomal dysfunction. Interestingly, we observed that treatment with Curcumin analog C1 led to a noticeable reduction in SCMAS accumulation in Cln3+/− MEFs, whereas no such effect was observed in Cln3−/− MEFs. These results suggest that the presence of residual CLN3 may be necessary for Curcumin analog C1 to exert its modulatory effect on lysosomal homeostasis (Figures 6P–6R). To further assess lysosomal functional restoration, we measured lysosomal pH across genotypes after Curcumin analog C1 treatment. Consistent with our findings regarding SCMAS clearance, Curcumin analog C1 treatment elevated lysosomal pH in WT and Cln3+/− MEFs but had no effect in Cln3−/− MEFs (Figures S11A and S11B), reinforcing the requirement of functional CLN3 for TFEB-mediated lysosomal repair. In summary, our results demonstrate that CLN3 is a key target downstream of TFEB signaling. Curcumin analog C1 could activate TFEB to enhance CLN3 expression and channel activity, thereby providing mechanistic insight into the regulation of lysosomal homeostasis. While these findings highlight the TFEB-CLN3 axis as an important molecular pathway to modulate pathology, further in vivo studies are necessary to evaluate its therapeutic potential for Batten disease.

Generation of CLN3/CLN7 knockout cell linesTo disrupt CLN3 gene in HEK293T and Neuro-2a cells, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene deletion was employed. The human CLN3, CLN7 and mouse Cln3 target sequences, AGGCTCGCGGCGGCGCTTTT[CGG], CCTGCGGAACGAAAGTGAAC[AGG] and TGGGAAGTTCTGCGGGCTCG[TGG] (with CGG, AGG and TGG as the PAM motif), were cloned into the plentiCRISPR-V2 vector, respectively. Lentiviruses transfection was used to generate gene-modified cell lines as described before84. Briefly, lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with the plentiCRISPR-V2 vector, along with lentiviral packaging (psPAX2) and envelope (pMD2.G) plasmids using jetOPTIMUS (Polyplus) transfection reagent. The plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G were gifts from Dr. Didier Trono (Addgene plasmid # 12260 ; http://n2t.net/addgene:12260 ; RRID:Addgene_12260) and (Addgene plasmid # 12259 ; http://n2t.net/addgene:12259 ; RRID:Addgene_12259). Two days post-transfection, the medium containing lentiviral particles was harvested and used to infect fresh HEK293T cells in antibiotic-free medium supplemented with 8 μg/mL polybrene. One day post-infection, the cells were subjected to selection with 3 μg/mL puromycin for 48 hours. Single-cell clones were isolated via Fluorescence Activated Cell Sorting (FACS)-sorted into 96-well plates and sequenced to confirm successful CLN3 knockout.

In brief, cells were seeded into 35 mm culture dishes with thin glass bottoms and incubated with Oregon-Green 488-conjugated Dextran (250 μg/mL, Invitrogen, D7170) overnight.
