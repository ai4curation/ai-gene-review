Neurons establish specific synapses based on the adhesive properties of
cell-surface proteins while also retaining the ability to form synapses in a
relatively non-selective manner. However, comprehensive understanding of the
underlying mechanism reconciling these opposing characteristics remains
incomplete. Here, we have identified Side-IV/Beat-IIb, members of the Drosophila
immunoglobulin superfamily, as a combination of cell-surface recognition
molecules inducing synapse formation. The Side-IV/Beat-IIb combination
transduces bifurcated signaling with Side-IV's co-receptor, Kirre, and a
synaptic scaffold protein, Dsyd-1. Genetic experiments and subcellular protein
localization analyses showed the Side-IV/Beat-IIb/Kirre/Dsyd-1 complex to have
two essential functions. First, it narrows neuronal binding specificity through
Side-IV/Beat-IIb extracellular interactions. Second, it recruits synapse
formation factors, Kirre and Dsyd-1, to restrict synaptic loci and inhibit
miswiring. This dual function explains how the combinations of cell-surface
molecules enable the ranking of preferred interactions among neuronal pairs to
achieve synaptic specificity in complex circuits in vivo.
