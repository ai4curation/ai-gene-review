Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) significantly impacts
patient's quality of life and complicates cancer treatment. Neuroprotectin D1
(NPD1)/protectin D1 (PD1), derived from docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), exhibits
analgesic actions in animal models of inflammatory pain and neuropathic pain.
GPR37, a receptor for NPD1/PD1, is known to regulate macrophage phagocytosis and
inflammatory cytokine expression, but its role in primary sensory neurons and
CIPN remains poorly understood. We found Gpr37 mRNA expression in both neurons
and macrophages in mouse dorsal root ganglia (DRG), furthermore, GPR37 is
downregulated by the chemotherapy agent paclitaxel. Gpr37 mRNA was notably high
in neonatal mouse DRG neurons. In contrast, Gpr37l1 is primarily expressed by
satellite glial cells in DRG. Chemotherapy-induced neuropathic pain symptom
(mechanical allodynia) resolved within seven weeks in wild-type mice, but it
persisted in Gpr37 knockout mice, highlighting GPR37's role in acute-to-chronic
pain transition. Consistently, intra-DRG knockdown of Gpr37 in naive animals was
sufficient to induce mechanical allodynia. In primary DRG cultures, NPD1
facilitated neurite outgrowth of sensory neurons in the presence of paclitaxel,
in a GPR37-dependent manner. NPD1 treatment also mitigated mechanical allodynia
and prevented the loss of intraepidermal nerve fibers in hind paw skins in
wild-type mice undergoing chemotherapy, but these protective effects are absent
in Gpr37 knockout mice. Finally, spatial transcriptomics analysis revealed
macrophage and neuronal expression of GPR37 in human DRG. Our findings indicate
that GPR37 deficiency drives pain chronicity in CIPN. This study also
underscores the potential of NPD1 in safeguarding against sensory neuron
degeneration and neuropathic pain in CIPN through GPR37.

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) is a prevalent issue in clinical oncology, affecting 30–80% of cancer patients undergoing treatment with neurotoxic chemotherapy agents (1, 2). CIPN limits treatment dose and impairs the quality of the patient’s life. With the increasing survival rate of cancer patients, CIPN is becoming a more pressing issue and significantly increases the burden of healthcare (3), with very limited treatment options (2). Paclitaxel (PAX) is one of the widely used chemotherapy drugs to treat solid cancers such as ovarian, breast, and non-small cell lung cancer (4). Clinically, PAX-induced neurotoxicity manifests as sensory neuropathy, with patients reporting sensations of numbness, tingling, and burning pain, often distributed in a “glove and stocking” pattern. The loss of intraepidermal nerve fibers (IENFs) has been demonstrated after PAX treatment (5). Despite recent progress in elucidating neuronal and immune regulations of CIPN (6–12), its mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

G protein-coupled receptor 37 (GPR37) was initially identified as a parkin-associated endothelin-like receptor (13). GPR37 is widely expressed in various brain regions and has been implicated in various diseases, including Parkinson’s disease (14), autism (15, 16), depression (17), and bipolar disorder (18). GPR37 has also been studied for its role in regulating glial physiological functions, such as dopamine signaling (16, 19) and oligodendrocyte myelination (20, 21). The neuroprotective and glioprotective factors prosaposin and prosaptide (e.g., TX14 peptide) were identified as the ligands of the GPR37 and GPR37L1 (22), with TX14 shown to reduce neuropathic pain (23). However, their precise binding sites remain unknown, classifying these receptors as orphan GPCRs (24). Recently, we identified GPR37 as a novel receptor for neuroprotectin D1 (NPD1), also known as protecine D1 (PD1) (25). NPD1 activates GPR37 in macrophages, enhancing phagocytosis and promoting the resolution of inflammatory pain (26). Additionally, we identified the anti-malaria drug artesunate as an agonist of GPR37 (27), with GPR37 activation in macrophages providing protection against bacterial infections and sepsis (27–29). GPR37 also confers protection against ischemic stroke (30). Recent research further implicates GPR37 in dopamine neuron degeneration and neuroinflammation in Parkinson’s disease (31). Moreover, GPR37 activation by NPD1 or artesunate alleviates bone cancer pain in multiple mouse models (32). Given the established neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory roles of NPD1 in neuropathic pain following nerve trauma (33), we hypothesized that GPR37 plays a crucial protective role in regulating CIPN through GPR37 signaling.

Chronic pain imposes a significant personal and economic burden, impacting over 30% of the global population (34). The transition from acute to chronic pain occurs through distinct pathophysiological and histopathological stages (35, 36). Sensory neurons and macrophages in DRG play a critical role in the initiation and maintenance of chronic pain (37). However, the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying acute-to-chronic pain transition in CIPN remain poorly understood. In this study, we demonstrate that GPR37 is a key regulator of the acute-to-chronic pain transition following PAX-induced peripheral neuropathy. We further show that NPD1 protects against chemotherapy-induced neuronal degeneration and neuropathic pain via GPR37, providing new insights into the role of GPR37 signaling in CIPN pathophysiology.

4.DiscussionMultiple risk factors contribute to the transition from acute to chronic pain, including genetic, psychological, autonomic, and environmental factors (36, 56). Our study identified GPR37 loss-of-function as a potential risk factor for the development of chronic pain following CIPN (Fig. 9A–C). In the PAX models of CIPN, the duration of neuropathic pain (especially mechanical pain) varied based on the total dose and injection frequency (42). In our models, involving single injection of 6 mg/kg of PAX (Model One) and four injections of 2 mg/kg PAX administered every other day (Model Two), we observed acute pain in Model One (5–7 weeks) (39) and chronic pain in Model Two (>7 weeks) (40). Thus, Model One, with single PAX injection is ideal for studying the transition from acute to chronic pain due to loss of function. We found that compared to WT mice Gpr37 knockout mice failed to recover from neuropathic pain after 7 weeks, underscoring the critical role of GPR37 in protecting CIPN. Mechanistically, we observed a time-dependent downregulation of GPR37 in DRG at 1 and 2 weeks post-PAX treatment, followed by a recovery at 6 weeks (Fig. 2C). This dynamic loss and recovery of GPR37 expression corresponded with the development and resolution of CIPN in our models. Additionally, specific siRNA-mediated knockdown of Gpr37 in the DRG was sufficient to induce acute mechanical allodynia for several days in naïve mice (Fig. 5), in further support of a protective role of GPR37 in DRG.NPD1/PD1, derived from omega-3 fatty acids, specifically docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), is a member of specialized pro-resolving mediator (SPM) superfamily (57, 58). NPD1 exhibits analgesic actions in animal models of inflammatory and neuropathic pain after tissue injury, infection, and nerve injury (27, 33, 59). Acute inflammation is known to generate SPMs, which promote the resolution of inflammation and pain (60). Application of SPMs has been shown to promote physiological restoration, altering the traditional concept of tissue recovery from a passive to an active process driven by specific cells and active products (61, 62). Specific GPCRs have been identified as receptors for SPMs (58, 63). Recently, we discovered that GPR37 is a receptor for NPD1, and NPD1 facilitates macrophages phagocytosis, a crucial step of inflammation resolution via GPR37. Furthermore, GPR37 mediates NPD1’s analgesic and anti-inflammatory actions in mouse inflammatory pain model (26). GPR37 is known to have neuroprotective effects in neurodegenerative diseases such as brain trauma, stroke, and spinal cord injury (22, 29, 30, 64–67). Our follow up study revealed that the NPD1/GPR37 axis confers protection against infection-induced pain and sepsis through macrophage signaling (27). In this study, we demonstrated a key role of GPR37 in axonal outgrowth and nerve regeneration in mouse DRG neurons. Notably, Gpr37 mRNA is highly expressed in DRG neurons of juvenile P4 mice, suggesting its involvement in neuronal development (Fig. 1F).While it is generally believed that PAX induces peripheral neuropathy through direct effects on neurons—including cell bodies, axons, and intraepidermal nerve terminals—PAX is also known to activate macrophages, prompting the release of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as TNF and CXCL1, via TLR9 signaling (11). Chemotherapy, including PAX, was shown to trigger infiltration of immune cells, including macrophages, which contribute to neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and neuropathic pain (7, 12, 68, 69). Our results showed that GPR37 is also expressed by macrophages in mouse and human DRG. Furthermore, GPR37 is co-expressed with CD163 in human DRG. Recently research identified CD163+ macrophages as an integral component of the DRG-neurovascular unit, and CD163+ macrophages play an important role in pain resolution (70). Given its role in regulating macrophage phagocytosis and cytokine expression, including downregulation of TNF and IL-1β and upregulation of IL-10 (26), GPR37 may also protect axonal degeneration by controlling the local environment of inflammation. Multiple risk factors contribute to the transition from acute to chronic pain, including genetic, psychological, autonomic, and environmental factors (36, 56). Our study identified GPR37 loss-of-function as a potential risk factor for the development of chronic pain following CIPN (Fig. 9A–C). In the PAX models of CIPN, the duration of neuropathic pain (especially mechanical pain) varied based on the total dose and injection frequency (42). In our models, involving single injection of 6 mg/kg of PAX (Model One) and four injections of 2 mg/kg PAX administered every other day (Model Two), we observed acute pain in Model One (5–7 weeks) (39) and chronic pain in Model Two (>7 weeks) (40). Thus, Model One, with single PAX injection is ideal for studying the transition from acute to chronic pain due to loss of function. We found that compared to WT mice Gpr37 knockout mice failed to recover from neuropathic pain after 7 weeks, underscoring the critical role of GPR37 in protecting CIPN. Mechanistically, we observed a time-dependent downregulation of GPR37 in DRG at 1 and 2 weeks post-PAX treatment, followed by a recovery at 6 weeks (Fig. 2C). This dynamic loss and recovery of GPR37 expression corresponded with the development and resolution of CIPN in our models. Additionally, specific siRNA-mediated knockdown of Gpr37 in the DRG was sufficient to induce acute mechanical allodynia for several days in naïve mice (Fig. 5), in further support of a protective role of GPR37 in DRG.

NPD1/PD1, derived from omega-3 fatty acids, specifically docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), is a member of specialized pro-resolving mediator (SPM) superfamily (57, 58). NPD1 exhibits analgesic actions in animal models of inflammatory and neuropathic pain after tissue injury, infection, and nerve injury (27, 33, 59). Acute inflammation is known to generate SPMs, which promote the resolution of inflammation and pain (60). Application of SPMs has been shown to promote physiological restoration, altering the traditional concept of tissue recovery from a passive to an active process driven by specific cells and active products (61, 62). Specific GPCRs have been identified as receptors for SPMs (58, 63). Recently, we discovered that GPR37 is a receptor for NPD1, and NPD1 facilitates macrophages phagocytosis, a crucial step of inflammation resolution via GPR37. Furthermore, GPR37 mediates NPD1’s analgesic and anti-inflammatory actions in mouse inflammatory pain model (26). GPR37 is known to have neuroprotective effects in neurodegenerative diseases such as brain trauma, stroke, and spinal cord injury (22, 29, 30, 64–67). Our follow up study revealed that the NPD1/GPR37 axis confers protection against infection-induced pain and sepsis through macrophage signaling (27). In this study, we demonstrated a key role of GPR37 in axonal outgrowth and nerve regeneration in mouse DRG neurons. Notably, Gpr37 mRNA is highly expressed in DRG neurons of juvenile P4 mice, suggesting its involvement in neuronal development (Fig. 1F).

While it is generally believed that PAX induces peripheral neuropathy through direct effects on neurons—including cell bodies, axons, and intraepidermal nerve terminals—PAX is also known to activate macrophages, prompting the release of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as TNF and CXCL1, via TLR9 signaling (11). Chemotherapy, including PAX, was shown to trigger infiltration of immune cells, including macrophages, which contribute to neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and neuropathic pain (7, 12, 68, 69). Our results showed that GPR37 is also expressed by macrophages in mouse and human DRG. Furthermore, GPR37 is co-expressed with CD163 in human DRG. Recently research identified CD163+ macrophages as an integral component of the DRG-neurovascular unit, and CD163+ macrophages play an important role in pain resolution (70). Given its role in regulating macrophage phagocytosis and cytokine expression, including downregulation of TNF and IL-1β and upregulation of IL-10 (26), GPR37 may also protect axonal degeneration by controlling the local environment of inflammation. The GPR37-mediated signaling facilitates restoration and regeneration by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, enhancing debris clearance, and promoting the production of anti-inflammatory factors (26) (Fig. 9B).

5.ConclusionOur findings have highlighted the critical role of GPR37 in the transition from acute to chronic pain after chemotherapy treatment. Loss of GPR37 led to chronic pain following CIPN, while activation of GPR37 by its legend NPD1 protects against the development of CIPN. Mechanistically, NPD1 protects against paclitaxel-induced nerve degeneration in dissociated DRG neurons, prevents the loss of epidermal nerve fibers in vivo, and mitigates paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain in both sexes through GPR37 signaling (Figs. 9A, 9C). Additionally, NPD1 may attenuate neuropathic pain and nerve degeneration by modulating macrophage signaling (Fig. 9B). Targeting the NPD1/GPR37 pathway holds promise for the development of new therapeutics aimed at preventing and treating CIPN.
