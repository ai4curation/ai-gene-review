Mitochondria export Ca2+ via Na+/Ca2+ exchange machinery (mito-NCX) to regulate
intracellular Ca2+ signalling and mitochondrial Ca2+ homeostasis. TMEM65 has
recently been implicated as essential for mito-NCX, but its mechanisms and roles
remain unclear. Here we show that TMEM65 depletion severely impairs mito-NCX.
TMEM65 is highly expressed in the heart and brain but absent in the liver,
correlating with mito-NCX activity in these tissues. Biochemical and functional
analyses reveal that TMEM65 forms a homodimer, containing plausible
ion-coordinating residues critical for function. Heterologous expression of
TMEM65 induces Na+/Ca2+ exchange in cells lacking native mito-NCX activity.
Moreover, purified, liposome-reconstituted TMEM65 exhibits key mito-NCX
features. We further identify the binding site for CGP-37157, a potent, widely
used mito-NCX inhibitor. Finally, TMEM65 deletion elevates mitochondrial Ca2+
and primes mitochondria to permeability transition. These findings firmly
establish TMEM65 as the protein mediating mito-NCX, offering a new therapeutic
target for diseases associated with mitochondrial Ca2+ dysregulation.

Introduction

Mitochondria utilize a Ca2+ transport system to shuttle Ca2+ across the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM)1, 2. This system enables mitochondria to modulate the spatiotemporal dynamics of cytosolic Ca2+ signals, and to generate Ca2+ signals within the matrix to regulate oxidative phosphorylation and cell-death pathways3–6. Mitochondrial Ca2+ transport regulates various physiological processes3–6, including cardiac and skeletal muscle contraction, insulin secretion, neurotransmission, and immune cell activation. Abnormal mitochondrial Ca2+ transport has been implicated in several pathological conditions3, 4, such as neurodegeneration7–9, heart failure10–12, cancer13–15, and ischemia-reperfusion injury16–18, highlighting its broad significance in human health.

The mitochondrial Ca2+ transport system comprises three main pathways1, 2, 4, 6: the mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter (hereafter, the uniporter), a multi-subunit Ca2+ channel that facilitates the influx of cytoplasmic Ca2+ into the matrix, and the Na+/Ca2+ and H+/Ca2+ exchangers, which mediate Ca2+ efflux from the matrix to the cytoplasm. Extensive research over the past decades has identified the uniporter’s core subunits and established its fundamental mechanisms19–34. However, the molecular identities and mechanisms of the Ca2+-efflux exchangers remain elusive. This knowledge gap hinders our understanding of how mitochondria regulate vital Ca2+ signaling processes both within the organelle and in the cytosol, and limits the development of clinical interventions for the diseases mentioned above.

Here, we present our work on mitochondrial Na+/Ca2+ exchange (mito-NCX), initially described by Carafoli and colleagues in the 1970s35. They demonstrated that isolated mitochondria release Ca2+ when exposed to Na+ or Li+, distinct from the Li+-irresponsive plasma-membrane Na+/Ca2+ exchange. Subsequent studies established that mito-NCX is the primary pathway for mitochondrial Ca2+ extrusion in excitable cells, including cardiomyocytes and neurons36, 37. In contrast, some non-excitable cells, such as hepatocytes, predominantly utilize H+/Ca2+ exchange (mito-HCX) for mitochondrial Ca2+ efflux36, 38. Pharmacological studies have identified benzothiazepines and benzodiazepines as mito-NCX inhibitors39, 40, notably the Ca2+ channel blocker diltiazem and the more potent inhibitor CGP-37157, a signature tool for pharmacological studies of mito-NCX.

The NCLX protein (SLC8B1/SLC24A6), a member of the Ca2+/cation antiporter (CaCA) protein superfamily41, has been suggested to mediate mito-NCX42. However, it is unclear whether NCLX binds Na+, since it lacks multiple Na+-coordinating residues found in canonical CaCA Na+/Ca2+ exchangers, such as cardiac NCX143, 44. Moreover, studies on NCLX gene deletion have reported variable effects on mitochondrial Ca2+ efflux, ranging from significant reductions (70–90%)12, 45, 46 to modest or marginal changes (<60%)47–49. By comparison, it has been reliably observed that deletion of MCU, the uniporter’s pore-forming subunit, eliminates >90% of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake16, 17, 23, 50, 51. Critically, there is no evidence that NCLX expression in heterologous systems reconstitutes mito-NCX, nor that CGP-37157 directly targets NCLX. These uncertainties suggest that the role of NCLX in mito-NCX requires further investigation.

The TMEM65 protein plays critical roles in brain and skeletal muscle function52. Recent studies have further shown that TMEM65 knockdown (KD) suppresses mito-NCX activity52–54, indicating an important role for TMEM65. This effect, however, could be explained by several molecular scenarios: TMEM65 may serve as an activator or essential auxiliary component of the mito-NCX, promote mito-NCX protein expression or maturation, directly mediate mito-NCX, or indirectly regulate mito-NCX by influencing the driving force for Na+/Ca2+ exchange. Indeed, it has been proposed that TMEM65 stimulates Na+/Ca2+ exchange mediated by NCLX54, but this is complicated by uncertainties regarding NCLX function. Our study aims to elucidate the mechanisms of TMEM65 and define its specific roles in mito-NCX.

ResultsEssential roles of TMEM65 in mito-NCXThe full-length human TMEM65 protein consists of 240 amino acid residues and has a molecular weight of 25.5 kDa. Bioinformatic analyses55 predicted a 55-residue N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS), which is likely cleaved following mitochondrial import. Consistently, Western blot analyses of HEK-cell subcellular fractions showed that TMEM65 is enriched in mitochondria and migrates at ~20 kDa, indicative of MTS removal (Fig. 1a). Previous studies have identified TMEM65 as an IMM protein56. Our results corroborate this classification, showing that TMEM65 is resistant to extraction by both alkaline Na2CO3 (Extended Data Fig. 1a) and digitonin (Extended Data Fig. 1b), hallmark features of integral IMM proteins57.To investigate TMEM65’s function, we manipulated its expression in human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK) cells either by overexpression (OE) or by stable KD using two distinct short-hairpin RNAs (KD1 and KD2). Both TMEM65-KD cell lines exhibited TMEM65 levels reduced to less than 10% of those in wild-type (WT) cells (Fig. 1b). A standard assay to quantify mito-NCX activity in digitonin-permeabilized cells is presented in Fig. 1b, showing that after abolishing mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake with the uniporter inhibitor Ru360, the addition of 10-mM Na+ induced Ca2+ efflux. This efflux, representing mito-NCX activity, was subsequently inhibited by 5-μM CGP-37157 (Fig. 1b). Notably, mito-NCX was strongly enhanced by TMEM65 OE and suppressed by TMEM65 KD (Fig. 1c), confirming that TMEM65 plays a crucial role in mito-NCX. In contrast, mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake, known to be mediated by the uniporter, was unaffected by changes in TMEM65 expression (Extended Data Fig. 2a–b).Mito-NCX has two distinctive properties. First, while Li+ is a poor substrate for plasma-membrane Na+/Ca2+ exchangers, mito-NCX can effectively utilize inward Li+ gradients to drive Ca2+ efflux35. Second, mito-NCX is highly active in excitable cells but undetectable in the liver36–38, an observation we have confirmed (Extended Data Fig. 3). Here, we show that TMEM65 KD impairs mitochondrial Li+/Ca2+ exchange (Fig. 1d). Moreover, tissue analyses revealed a mitochondrial TMEM65 expression pattern across species: high levels in excitable tissues (cerebral cortex and heart) and negligible levels in the liver, as observed in both mice (Fig. 1e) and healthy human organ donors (Fig. 1f). This expression profile closely aligns with the known activity pattern of mito-NCX36–38 (Extended Data Fig. 3). Collectively, these findings further reinforce the connection between TMEM65 and mito-NCX.Molecular architecture of TMEM65TMEM65 is predicted to possess three transmembrane helices (TMH1–3), with its N- and C-termini on opposite sides of the IMM. To determine TMEM65’s transmembrane orientation, we expressed C-terminally 1D4-tagged IMM proteins (either the uniporter’s MCU subunit, its EMRE subunit, or TMEM65) in HEK cells, prepared mitoplasts (submitochondrial vesicles with ruptured outer membranes), and treated the samples with Proteinase K. The protease digested the 1D4 tag attached to EMRE but not MCU (Fig. 2a), consistent with their established orientation where the C-termini of EMRE and MCU reside within the intermembrane space (IMS) and matrix, respectively23, 27, 29. Proteinase K cleaved TMEM65’s C-terminal 1D4 tag, indicating that TMEM65 adopts an orientation with the N-terminus in the matrix and the C-terminus in the IMS (Fig. 2b).Given that TMEM65 contains only three TMHs, which are apparently insufficient to constitute a transmembrane substrate pathway, it likely needs to oligomerize to potentially function as a transporter. Alternatively, if TMEM65 exists as a monomer, it probably acts as a regulator of mito-NCX. Co-immunoprecipitation (CoIP) experiments demonstrated that immobilized, 1D4-tagged TMEM65 can pull down FLAG-tagged TMEM65 (Fig. 2c), providing evidence for TMEM65 oligomerization. However, no interaction between TMEM65 and NCLX was detected using CoIP (Fig. 2c), blue-native PAGE (BN-PAGE) (Extended Data Fig. 4a–b), fluorescence size-exclusion chromatography (FSEC) (Extended Data Fig. 4c), or mass spectrometry (see Supplementary Information). Moreover, NCLX overexpression does not alter TMEM65-induced mito-NCX activity (Extended Data Fig. 4d). While we recognize the limitations of negative results, these observations, nonetheless, raise questions about TMEM65’s previously proposed role as an NCLX activator54.We then performed glutaraldehyde cross-linking, a commonly used method to determine protein oligomeric states58. The results were unambiguous: over a time course of 45 minutes, glutaraldehyde completely shifted the 20-kDa TMEM65 monomer band to a position of ~40 kDa with no other obvious higher oligomer bands (Fig. 2d), indicating a dimeric architecture of TMEM65. Of note, the observation that TMEM65 is uniformly oriented with its N-terminus in the matrix (Fig. 2a-b) implies that the two TMEM65 subunits are arranged parallel to each other, forming a parallel homodimer.Corroborating our biochemical assays, in silico analysis of TMEM65 using AlphaFold59 predicts a parallel dimer structure (Fig. 2e), with high confidence scores clearly above monomeric or higher oligomeric forms (Extended Data Fig. 5). The two protomers are aligned at the same level with respect to the predicted lipid-bilayer boundary, compatible with a TMEM65 dimer residing within the membrane. Importantly, the six TMHs of the predicted dimer enclose an interfacial aqueous pathway, which is accessible from the matrix and narrows to a constricted region in the protein core near the bilayer center (Fig. 2e). This model thus exhibits features of a transporter in a “matrix-facing” conformation.Interestingly, four polar residues (D132, N163, S166, and D167), highly conserved in TMEM65 across species (Extended Data Fig. 6), are strategically located at the dimer interface, forming a ceiling for the matrix-accessible vestibule. All mutants failed to induce mito-NCX (Fig. 2f), underscoring the importance of these potential ion-binding residues. TMEM65 contains two Trp residues: W199 in TMH3 and W31 in the MTS, which is cleaved in the mature protein (Fig. 1a). If ion binding to TMEM65 induces conformational changes and/or shifts conformational distributions, as commonly observed in transporters, W199 might experience changes in chemical environments, leading to alterations in its fluorescence. Indeed, the addition of either 20-mM Na+ or 20-μM Ca2+ caused significant changes in Trp fluorescence (Extended Data Fig. 8b–d). Sf9 cells provide such a system—their mitochondria possess the uniporter (NCBI #XP_050557061) for Ca2+ uptake but lack mito-NCX activity (Fig. 3a). Expression of human TMEM65 in these cells produced robust mito-NCX, which was inhibited by CGP-37157 (Fig. 3a-b), without affecting mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake (Extended Data Fig. 2c–d). This result strongly suggests that TMEM65 directly mediates mito-NCX. Interestingly, co-expression of these uniporter proteins enhanced TMEM65-induced mito-NCX (Fig. 3a-b), probably because the uniporter increased Ca2+ content in the matrix, creating larger outward Ca2+ gradients to drive Ca2+ efflux. For this purpose, TMEM65 was purified using n-decyl-β-maltopyranoside (DM) (Extended Data Fig. 9a–b) rather than the lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG) and glyco-diosgenin (GDN) detergents used in previous experiments (Extended Data Fig. 8). To further validate this key finding, we investigated the mechanisms of CGP-37157 inhibition. Specifically, locating a CGP-37157 binding site on TMEM65 would offer additional compelling evidence for its direct role in mediating mito-NCX, analogous to how the identification of a Ru360-resistant S259 point mutant of MCU helped confirm its function as the uniporter’s pore-forming subunit27.CGP-37157 is a benzothiazepine. Since diltiazem also inhibits mito-NCX40, we reasoned that benzothiazepines might act on L-type Ca2+ channels and mito-NCX through similar mechanisms. A recent structural study revealed that diltiazem blocks CaV1.1 by binding to a pocket near the cytoplasmic end of the Ca2+ selectivity filter62 (Fig. 5b). This pocket is lined with multiple hydrophobic residues, including aromatic amino acids that interact with diltiazem’s benzene rings (Fig. 5b). Inspection of AlphaFold-predicted TMEM65 structures drew our attention to a similar hydrophobic site below the proposed cation-binding area in the central solvent-accessible cavity (Fig. 5c). Consistent with the literature39, CGP-37157 inhibits mito-NCX induced by WT TMEM65 with an IC50 of 1.1 μM (Extended Data Fig. 10b). Consistently, Ca2+ extraction using the chelator BAPTA under denaturing conditions in mitochondria isolated from TMEM65-KO cells revealed significantly greater total mitochondrial Ca2+ content than in WT cells (Fig. 6b). Taken together, our results suggest that TMEM65, as the protein responsible for mito-NCX, is essential for maintaining normal mitochondrial Ca2+ homeostasis and IMM potential while also playing a protective role against MPT, a critical event preceding apoptotic cell death (Fig. 6e).

Essential roles of TMEM65 in mito-NCX

The full-length human TMEM65 protein consists of 240 amino acid residues and has a molecular weight of 25.5 kDa. Bioinformatic analyses55 predicted a 55-residue N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS), which is likely cleaved following mitochondrial import. R=R2-R1T×103

where T (arbitrary units, AU) is the total signal increase caused by the initial addition of 15-μM CaCl2, and R1 and R2 denote the rates of Ca2+ efflux (AU/s) immediately before and after adding Na+, respectively. I(CGP)=R0-R3(CGP)R0-R1

R=R2-R1T×103

I(CGP)=R0-R3(CGP)R0-R1

IonQuant (version 1.27.12) was used for quantification, with MaxLFQ enabled, a minimum ion count of two, and match-between-runs enabled. The false discovery rate was set at 1% for both peptide and protein levels. MaxLFQ intensities were imported to Perseus (version. 2.0.10.0) and filtered with the following features: removing ‘reverse = +’, ‘potential contaminant = +’, and ‘only identified by site = +’. The data were log2 transformed and rows not identified in at least two replicates of one sample group were removed. Missing values were imputed from normal distribution (width = 0.3, downshift = 1.8, total matrix mode). A Student’s t-test was executed to examine protein groups with different intensities between the Exp and control groups. The t-test settings were as follows: ‘Permutation-based FDR’, ‘FDR = 0.05’, S0 = 0.1, ‘Report q-value’, ‘number of randomizations = 250’ and ‘-log10 p-value’. Protein abundance was estimated using spectral counting and label-free quantification (see Supplementary Text and Supplementary Table 1).
