Gene expression is regulated by controlling distinct steps of the
transcriptional cycle, including initiation, pausing, elongation, and
termination. Kinases phosphorylate RNA polymerase II (RNA Pol II) and associated
factors to control transitions between these steps and to act as central gene
regulatory nodes. Similarly, phosphatases that dephosphorylate these components
are emerging as important regulators of transcription, although their roles
remain less well understood. Here, we discover that the mouse PNUTS-PP1
phosphatase complex plays an essential role in controlling transcription pause
release in addition to its previously described function in transcription
termination. Transcription pause release by the PNUTS complex is essential for
almost all RNA Pol II-dependent gene transcription, relies on its PP1
phosphatase subunit, and controls the phosphorylation of factors required for
pause release and elongation. Together, these observations reveal an essential
new role for a phosphatase complex in transcription pause release and show that
the PNUTS complex is essential for RNA Pol II-dependent transcription.

Introduction

Cells must control gene expression to respond to changing cellular conditions and to create cell type-specific expression patterns that enable complex multicellular development. To achieve this, a series of mechanisms shape RNA Polymerase II (RNA Pol II)-dependent transcription to control gene expression1. For example, decades of biochemical investigation have revealed that transcription occurs in discrete and highly regulated phases, with recent cryo-EM structures providing atomic-resolution insight into the composition of transcription complexes characteristic of individual phases2-4. However, how these phases are integrated and regulated to control transcription in vivo remains poorly understood.

Based on our current understanding, the transcription cycle in metazoans can be roughly segregated into four distinct phases: initiation, pausing, elongation, and termination. Initiation is governed by the formation of the pre-initiation complex, whereby the general transcription factor TFIID nucleates on core promoter DNA leading to stepwise acquisition of additional general transcription factors. Assembly of the RNA Pol II pre-initiation complex is then influenced by the action of coactivators like Mediator. This culminates in activation of TFIIH, which, through its helicase XPB, melts promoter DNA whilst its kinase CDK7 phosphorylates the C-terminal repeat domain (CTD) of the largest subunit of RNA Pol II on serine 5 (Ser5P) as well as other transcription-associated factors5,6. This allows RNA Pol II to dissociate from the pre-initiation complex and enter into early elongation where DSIF, composed of SPT4/5, binds RNA Pol II. During early elongation, newly produced nascent RNA is capped, and NELF and Integrator complexes bind RNA Pol II causing pausing of transcription ~50-100 base pairs into the gene where additional regulatory signals ultimately define its destiny7-10. One fate of paused RNA Pol II is to terminate, aided by Integrator and Restrictor complexes, and most transcription initiation events end in promoter-proximal premature transcription termination6,11-15. Alternatively, the P-TEFb complex, which contains the CDK9 kinase, can phosphorylate pausing factors including NELF and Integrator causing their dissociation, while also phosphorylating SPT5 to promote productive elongation6,16. Processivity of early elongating RNA Pol II is then aided by SPT6 and the PAF1 complex. During productive elongation, kinases including CDK9/12/13 phosphorylate the CTD of RNA Pol II at serine 2 (Ser2P) allowing binding of additional factors that shape transcription and associated processes17. Finally, as RNA Pol II transcribes over the 3’ cleavage and polyadenylation site, RNA Pol II processivity is reduced, and the nascent RNA is cleaved. The RNA exonuclease XRN2 then binds to the uncapped RNA and translocates towards the slowing RNA Pol II where it contributes to transcription termination6,18. Importantly, each step of the transcription cycle must occur correctly to produce a mature functional mRNA and, therefore, are central points for transcription control.

A key regulatory influence dictating transitions between distinct phases of transcription is phosphorylation of regulatory factors and RNA Pol II itself6. Therefore, significant effort has been focused on identifying and characterising kinases that drive these phosphorylation events and defining how they affect RNA Pol II activity. Importantly, these phosphorylation events are dynamic and opposing phosphatases have also been proposed to regulate transcription19. For example, phosphoprotein phosphatase (PPP) family member PP2A interacts with Integrator to form the INTAC complex20,21. INTAC sustains the promoter-proximal pause state, and this corresponds to effects on phosphorylation of the RNA Pol II CTD and SPT521-26. Similarly, the PP4 phosphatase can dephosphorylate SPT5 in promoter-proximal regions to maintain pausing27,28. In contrast, the PP1 phosphatase functions with its adaptor protein PNUTS to dephosphorylate SPT5 in gene bodies and reduce RNA Pol II elongation rate for efficient termination at the end of genes29-33. Beyond the PPP family, other phosphatases can also regulate transcription, including FCP1, RPAP2, and SSU7219.

The dynamic nature of phosphorylation during key regulatory phases of transcription has made it challenging to define how kinases and phosphatases are staged and integrated to regulate transcription in vivo. However, rapidly acting kinase inhibitors and analogue-sensitive alleles coupled with techniques that measure primary effects on transcription have been particularly informative in demonstrating that CDK7 and CDK9 are involved in transcription initiation and pause release, respectively, and in identifying substrates that may explain how they control transcription34-44. In contrast, inhibitor-based approaches targeting phosphatases like PP2A and PP1 have been less informative because these enzymes form the catalytic core of multiple phosphatase complexes that have diverse and essential roles in both the cytoplasm and nucleus19. To overcome this, degron approaches targeting adaptor proteins unique to specific phosphatase complexes have yielded important insights. For example, PP2A within the INTAC complex was shown to regulate transcription pausing and premature transcription termination23,24. However, how other phosphatase complexes regulate transcription remains poorly understood.

Here we use degron technology, live-cell imaging, in vitro biochemistry, and quantitative genomic/proteomic approaches to discover an essential new role for the PNUTS/PP1 phosphatase complex in controlling transcription pause release. This mechanism is essential for RNA Pol II-dependent gene transcription, relies on PP1, and regulates phosphorylation of factors involved in promoting pause release and early elongation. Therefore, we discover a completely new transcription pause release control mechanism which is essential for gene expression.

ResultsThe PNUTS complex is essential for RNA Pol II-dependent transcriptionPNUTS was first discovered as a nuclear targeting subunit for PP1 phosphatases45. Later, it was found to form a multiprotein complex with TOX4, WDR82, and PP1 that is capable of binding to RNA and the CTD of RNA Pol II46-51. The PNUTS complex associates with RNA Pol II in gene bodies to slow transcription elongation by dephosphorylating SPT5 on its C-terminal repeat region 1 (CTR1), and this is important for transcription termination at the end of genes19,27,29,30,52-57. Beyond transcription termination, PNUTS has also been implicated in splicing, DNA repair, and other nuclear processes58-65. However, understanding the primary roles of the PNUTS complex has been challenging as it is essential for cell viability and current knowledge is mostly derived from experiments using slow perturbation techniques that cannot distinguish between primary effects and secondary consequences of cellular pathology. Moreover, quantitative methods have not been used to study how PNUTS influences transcription at the genome scale, further complicating our understanding of its role in gene regulation.To define how the PNUTS complex influences gene transcription, we used CRISPR Cas9-based genome engineering to homozygously insert a degron tag (dTAG) into the C-terminus of the endogenous Pnuts gene in mouse embryonic stem cells (ESCs)66 (Figure 1A). The dTAG did not affect PNUTS protein levels or formation of the PNUTS complex (Figure 1B). Upon treatment with dTAG13 compound, there was a rapid and near-complete loss of PNUTS protein within two hours, and major reductions in its complex partner protein TOX4 (Figure 1C). In contrast, WDR82 levels were only slight reduced, likely due to its presence in other nuclear protein complexes49. PP1 levels were unaffected, consistent with its high abundance and involvement in several nuclear protein complexes67.With this system to rapidly deplete the PNUTS complex, we set out to quantitatively investigate its effects on transcription using calibrated transient transcriptome sequencing (cTT-seq), which involves a short pulse of 4-thiouridine, purification of the labelled RNA, and massively parallel sequencing with spike-in calibration to quantitatively measure ongoing transcription68-70. We carried out cTT-seq in untreated PNUTS-dTAG cells and following two hours of dTAG13 treatment. Previous work implicated the PNUTS complex in transcription termination6,27,30,32, so we first analysed the transcription end site (TES) of genes. Consistent with previous findings, transcription in PNUTS-depleted cells failed to terminate appropriately and, in some cases, read-through transcription extended tens to hundreds of kilobases downstream of the gene (Figure 1D, 1E, and S1A).While defects in transcription termination after PNUTS depletion were anticipated, we also observed massively reduced cTT-seq signal across gene bodies, suggesting a major attenuation of transcription (Figure 1D and 1F). Consistent with this, metaplot and heatmap analysis of cTT-seq signal across all transcribed genes showed a profound reduction in transcription (Figure 1G and 1H, and S1B). Strikingly, quantifying the difference in cTT-seq signal upon PNUTS depletion, 11,350 genes (83.5% of transcribed genes) showed a greater than 1.5-fold reduction in transcription (Figure 1H and 1I). These observations were independent of 4-thiouridine labelling time and correspond to effects on nascent transcription (Figure S1E-J). RNA Pol II is also responsible for promoter upstream antisense transcription and following PNUTS depletion this was also reduced, albeit less so than genic transcription, while antisense transcription also failed to terminate appropriately (Figure 1J and 1K). Therefore, PNUTS is essential for transcription of genes in both the sense and antisense direction. Interestingly, a subset of genes (3138) showed increased transcription following PNUTS depletion (Figure 1I). When we examined these genes further, we found that they tended to be in the lowest expression decile (Figure S1C and S1D). When we visualised cTT-seq signal at these genes, it became evident that their increased transcription manifested from read-in transcription from more highly expressed upstream genes that had not terminated appropriately (Figure S1A, S1E and S1F). This demonstrates that PNUTS plays central roles in both transcription and termination of genes. Importantly, this effect is limited to RNA Pol II-transcribed genes, as we observe no effect on RNA Pol III-dependent transcription of tRNAs (Figure S1G and S1H). Therefore, PNUTS is essential for the transcription and termination of most, if not all, RNA Pol II-dependent genes.Entry into productive elongation relies on PNUTSOur cTT-seq analysis revealed a crucial role for the PNUTS complex in regulating gene transcription (Figure 1). In considering potential mechanisms underlying this effect, we hypothesised that profound reductions in transcription could be a secondary consequence of transcription termination defects. For example, if transcription was not terminated appropriately at the end of genes, RNA Pol II could become sequestered on chromatin making it unavailable for new rounds of transcription initiation. To explore this, we employed single particle tracking to quantify the chromatin binding dynamics of RNA Pol II in live cells and measure the fraction of bound and free molecules. To achieve this, we used genome engineering to homozygously insert a Halo-TAG into the Rpb1 gene, which encodes the largest subunit of the RNA Pol II, in the PNUTS-dTAG cell line (Figure 2A-C). We then used a photoactivatable Halo-dye and highly inclined laminated optical light sheet microscopy to capture single-molecule dynamics (Figure 2D). This showed that approximately 45% of RNA Pol II molecules were bound to chromatin, consistent with previous measurements (Figure 2E and 2F)71,72. After PNUTS depletion, the bound fraction of RNA Pol II did not increase, but was in fact reduced (Figure 2E and 2F). Therefore, the profound defects in gene transcription caused by PNUTS depletion are not a secondary consequence of defective transcription termination. Instead, PNUTS must control some other aspect of transcription.To explore how PNUTS might control transcription, we utilised calibrated chromatin-immunoprecipitation sequencing (cChIP-seq) to examine RNA Pol II distribution throughout the genome73. After PNUTS depletion, the levels of RNA Pol II in the gene body were majorly reduced (Figure 2G-2I, S2A and S2B) in line with reduced cTT-seq signal (Figure 1), although quantitatively comparing the levels of these reductions is challenging due the inherent differences in the signal-to-noise between these distinct techniques. Diminished RNA Pol II occupancy in gene bodies suggests the reductions in cTT-seq signal were not due to slowed or non-productive transcription elongation, which would have resulted in accumulation of RNA Pol II74. In contrast to reduced RNA Pol II signal in gene bodies, following PNUTS depletion there was an accumulation of RNA Pol II at the transcription start site (TSS) of genes (Figure 2G-2I). This was somewhat unexpected, given that PNUTS was previously shown to be enriched and function at the 3’ end of genes30,51. Therefore, we examined the localisation of PNUTS by cChIP-seq before and after dTAG13 treatment. In untreated cells, PNUTS localised over the gene body and was enriched at the 3’ end of genes, consistent with its role in regulating elongation speed and termination (Figure 2G-2I)30. Importantly, however, we also detected a peak at the 5’ end of genes and dTAG13 treatment resulted in near-complete loss of this signal (Figure 2G-2I). Together, this demonstrates that PNUTS associates with the 5’ end of genes and is required for RNA Pol II to transition from the 5’ end of genes into elongation.PNUTS is essential for transcription pause releaseWe next aimed to identify the step of the transcription cycle that PNUTS controls to exert its effects on transcription. Accumulation of RNA Pol II at the 5’ end of genes after PNUTS depletion could correspond to engagement of a pre-initiated form of RNA Pol II, or an initiated and elongating form of RNA Pol II that has paused but not transitioned into productive elongation. To explore this, we first carried out cChIP-seq examining the phosphorylation state of the C-terminal heptapeptide repeat (CTD) of RNA Pol II, as distinct stages of transcription are characterised by phosphorylation events on specific CTD residues6. For example, serine 5 phosphorylation (Ser5P) in the CTD, deposited by CDK7, is associated with RNA Pol II that has transitioned from pre-initiation into early elongation. In contrast, serine 2 phosphorylation (Ser2P), driven by the activity of CKD9, CDK12, and CDK13, is associated with exit of RNA Pol II from promoter-proximal pausing (Figure 3A). Importantly, cChIP-seq after PNUTS depletion revealed RNA Pol II at the 5’ end of genes was phosphorylated on Ser5, but lacked Ser2P (Figure 3B and Supplementary Figure S2C). Furthermore, Ser2P signal in the gene body was greatly reduced, consistent with transcription defects observed in cTT-seq (Figure 1). Therefore, without the PNUTS complex, RNA Pol II may be limited in exiting from promoter-proximal pausing and entry into productive elongation (Figure 3A).To explore this possibility in more detail, we profiled occupancy of RNA Pol II-associated factors known to demarcate specific phases of transcription. During the transition from pre-initiation into early elongation, DSIF, composed of SPT4/5, is loaded onto RNA Pol II as it transcribes towards the promoter-proximal pause site (Figure 3A and 3C)5,8,10. When we analysed SPT5 occupancy by cChIP-seq after PNUTS depletion, SPT5 was present at the 5’ end of genes suggesting that RNA Pol II had entered into early elongation (Figure 3C and S2D). Consistent with this, we also observed binding of TFIIS, which helps to restart backtracked RNA Pol II during the initiation/early non-processive elongation and poising phases of the transcription cycle (Figure 3A, 3C, and S2D)75-77. To determine if the accumulated RNA Pol II corresponded to a paused RNA Pol II, we examined binding of the promoter-proximal pausing factors NELF (NELF-B) and Integrator (INTS11) (Figure 3A, 3D and S2E)5,8,10. cChIP-seq demonstrated that they remained associated with RNA Pol II at the 5’ end of genes after PNUTS depletion. This suggests that in the absence of PNUTS, RNA Pol II initiates transcription and enters into early elongation, but does not efficiently transition beyond the pausing zone. Consistent with this, we could not detect binding of SPT6 and PAF1, which load onto RNA Pol II during the transition from pausing into productive elongation (Figure 3A, 3E, S2F and S2H). Finally, we examined CDK9 and BRD4, which are essential for the transition of promoter-proximally paused RNA Pol II into productive elongation (Figure 3A, 3F, and S2G)9. In the absence of PNUTS these factors still localised to the 5’ end of the gene and further biochemical characterisation confirmed that P-TEFb complexes remained intact (Figure S3A and S3B). Despite this, RNA Pol II did not efficiently transition into productive elongation. Therefore, we conclude that PNUTS regulates pause release to enable productive transcription elongation.PNUTS interaction with PP1 is essential for exit from promoter-proximal pausingPNUTS belongs to a large group of adapter proteins that regulate the targeting and activity of PP1 phosphatases20,67. The PNUTS complex also contains TOX4, whose function is poorly understood, and WDR82, which interacts directly with RNA Pol II49,78-81. Therefore, the effects that PNUTS has on RNA Pol II at promoter-proximal pause sites and transcription could be due to PP1-dependent or -independent functions. To determine if PNUTS requires interaction with PP1 to control transcription, we used a previously identified single substitution mutation in PNUTS (W401A) to disrupt its interaction with PP148. We engineered cells with an endogenous degron for rapid PNUTS depletion to also contain a transgene for doxycycline-inducible expression of PNUTS wildtype (WT) or PNUTS with the W401A substitution (Figure 4A and S4A). Rescue of PNUTS depletion with PNUTS WT led to reconstitution of the PNUTS complex (Figure 4B). In contrast, PNUTS W401A associated with TOX4 and WDR82, but not PP1α (Figure 4B).Having biochemically validated our rescue system, we carried out cTT-seq to examine transcription. PNUTS depletion alone caused dramatic reductions in transcription and defective termination as before (Figure 1), whereas expression of PNUTS WT significantly rescued these defects (Figure 4C, 4D, Supplementary Figure S4B and S4C). In contrast, expression of PNUTS W401A failed to rescue these transcription defects, effectively phenocopying the effects on transcription and termination observed following PNUTS depletion (Figure 4C, 4E, S4D, and S4E). To determine if the failure of PNUTS W401A to rescue transcription was due to an inability of RNA Pol II to enter into elongation, we carried out cChIP-seq for RNA Pol II in the rescue lines. RNA Pol II appeared to undergo normal pause release in the PNUTS WT rescue, but in the PNUTS W401A rescue it did not efficiently transition beyond the 5’ end of genes, with its occupancy reduced over gene bodies similarly to PNUTS depletion (Figure 4F, 4G, and S4F). cChIP-seq for PNUTS showed that PNUTS W401A localised appropriately to 5’ end of genes, albeit to lower levels than PNUTS WT (Figure 4F, 4H, and S4G). In agreement with PNUTS W401A being unable to promote the transition of RNA Pol II into productive elongation, PNUTS W401A no longer localised over the gene body (Figure 4F and 4H). We conclude that the PNUTS complex can engage with promoter proximally-paused RNA Pol II but is incapable of promoting pause release and entry of RNA Pol II into productive transcription elongation when it cannot interact with PP1. Therefore, PNUTS promotes pause release via a PP1-dependent mechanism.PNUTS regulates the phosphorylation of pause release and elongation factorsPNUTS requires its interaction with PP1 to enable pause release and transcription, suggesting it could function to regulate phosphorylation of proteins that control these steps of transcription. To explore this, we wanted to interrogate the nuclear phosphoproteome after PNUTS depletion. To achieve this, we isolated nuclei from cells and digested chromatin with nucleases to extract the total nuclear fraction from untreated cells and cells depleted of PNUTS for 30 minutes or 2 hours, with the view that these time points would allow us to capture early and persistent effects on phosphorylation (Figure S5A). We then proteolytically digested the nuclear fraction and used a tandem mass tag labelling approach to carry out quantitative proteomics (Figure 5A and 5B) and, after phosphopeptide-enrichment, phosphoproteomics (Figure 5C and 5D). Importantly, when we examined the nuclear proteome after PNUTS depletion, there were very few significant alterations, with PNUTS and its complex component TOX4 showing the largest changes (Figure 5B). Therefore, proteomic analysis suggests that PNUTS-dependent control of transcription does not result from secondary effects caused by an altered proteome.We then focused on the phosphoproteome (Figure 5C). In untreated cells, we identified 13,527 phosphosites on 3,236 proteins (Table S1). We then compared the phosphoproteome of untreated cells with those depleted of PNUTS for 30 mins and 2 hours using matched nuclear proteomes to correct for any alterations in protein levels (Figure 5D). This revealed 161 phosphosites on 131 proteins showing significant changes in phosphorylation at both time points, with 71 phosphopeptides showing decreases and 90 phosphopeptides showing increases in phosphorylation. We next focussed on peptides and proteins with increased phosphorylation, as these could correspond to PNUTS substrates. Importantly, sites showing increased phosphorylation were almost exclusively serine and threonine residues, consistent with the specificity of PP1 (Table S1). GO term analysis of proteins with increased phosphorylation after PNUTS depletion revealed significant enrichment for factors involved in mRNA processing, splicing, and positive regulation of transcription from RNA Pol II promoters (Figure 5E). Notably, within the group of proteins showing increases in phosphorylation were several factors that play central roles in promoter-proximal pause control and early transcription elongation including CDK9, the nuclear cap binding complex protein 1 (NCBP1), SPT6, and the PAF complex components PAF1 and LEO1 (Figure 5D)9,82. This demonstrates that PNUTS regulates the phosphorylation of factors that play important roles in pause release and elongation.We next wanted to explore whether putative substrates identified in phosphoproteomics could be direct targets for dephosphorylation by the PNUTS complex. To address this, we used a baculovirus expression system to reconstitute and purify a recombinant PNUTS complex composed of PNUTS, TOX4, WDR82, and PP1α (Figure 5F and S5B). We then performed in vitro phosphatase activity assays using a series of phosphorylated peptides (Figure 5G). As a positive control, we used a phosphorylated peptide corresponding to the CTR1 of SPT5, which is regulated by the PNUTS complex during transcriptional elongation and termination30. As a negative control, we used a phosphorylated peptide from a second site on SPT5 encompassing S664-P (human S666-P), which is regulated by INTAC/PP2A as opposed to PNUTS/PP127,30. From our phosphoproteomics data, we selected three sites phosphorylated on peptides corresponding to proteins involved in pause release and elongation: T21 on NCBP1, S347 on CDK9, and T1530 on SPT6H. Additionally, we included phosphopeptides corresponding to Ser5P and Ser2P on the CTD of RNA Pol II as it had been suggested that PNUTS-PP1 might also directly target these sites for dephosphorylation46,49. In vitro phosphatase assays demonstrated that the PNUTS complex dephosphorylated SPT5 CTR1, but not S664, in agreement with previously reported specificities (Figure 5G)27,30. Importantly, when we tested the putative substrates identified from our proteomics, we observed dephosphorylation of NCBP1-T21, CDK9-S347, and SPT6H-T1530. Conversely, we detected very little activity towards the Ser5P or Ser2P CTD peptides. Therefore, PNUTS can directly control the phosphorylation of proteins involved in regulating pause release and elongation.The PNUTS TND guides interactions with substrates and is required for pause releasePNUTS interacts with PP1 to enable release of RNA Pol II into productive elongation and factors involved in transcription pause release and elongation become hyperphosphorylated when PNUTS is depleted (Figure 5). This suggested that PNUTS might select substrates for PP1-dependent dephosphorylation to drive the effects on transcription. Recent in vitro biochemical work and structural analysis showed that PNUTS contains a TFIIS-like N-terminal domain (TND). The TND is composed of a right-handed bundle of alpha-helices that forms an interface which binds intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), called TND-interacting motifs (TIMs), found in other proteins49,83-85 (Figure 6A). There are more than a hundred nuclear proteins containing putative TIMs, including many with important roles in transcription83. This suggests that the TND could help guide the selection of substrates and thus how PNUTS regulates transcription. However, what factors PNUTS interacts with via its TND in vivo remains unknown.Therefore, to determine the complement of proteins that PNUTS interacts with and define which rely on the TND, we used existing structural information and AlphaFold predictions to explore the PNUTS TND and its TIM interaction interface (Figure 6A). Consistent with previous biochemical and structural observations, AlphaFold predicts the C-terminal side of the TND will act as the interaction interface for TIM-containing proteins. In addition, AlphaFold predicts that a small HEAT-repeat sub-domain packs up against the TND and forms the interaction site for TOX4. Based on this structural model, we designed a mutant of the TND (TND-M) with substitutions in key residues that should disrupt TIM interactions while leaving the structure of the TND and its ability to interface with the HEAT sub-domain and TOX4 intact (Figure 6A). We then engineered a new rescue line where we could simultaneously induce the expression of the PNUTS TND-M protein while depleting endogenous PNUTS (Figure 6B and S6A). Rescue with PNUTS TND-M enabled the formation of a core PNUTS complex containing TOX4, WDR82, and PP1α in a manner indistinguishable from WT rescue (Figure 6C). This suggests the TND-M retains the overall TND structure and supports efficient incorporation of TOX4 into the PNUTS complex.We next induced expression of PNUTS WT or PNUTS TND-M and performed immunoprecipitations from total nuclear extracts and used mass spectrometry to identify interacting factors. We first focussed on proteins which interact with PNUTS WT (Figure 6D and Table S2). Importantly, the core PNUTS complex components TOX4, WDR82, and PP1α/β/γ were among the most enriched. However, we also identified additional proteins, some of which contain putative TIMs. The majority of significant interacting factors could be segregated into five general groupings and included key transcription elongation factors (e.g. the PAF complex, SPT6, TFIIS, SCAF4/8, and IWS1), splicing regulatory factors (e.g. SRSF3/7/10, SON, and TRA2B), diverse RNA binding proteins, cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factors (e.g. SYMPK, CPSF1/2/3, CSTF1/2, WDR33, and FIP1), and the RNA Pol II complex itself. To discover which of these rely on the TND we compared the factors enriched in WT and TND-M purifications (Figure 6E, 6F, and Supplementary Table S2). In agreement with immunoprecipitation and western bot analysis (Figure 6C), the TND-M retained normal interactions with the core PNUTS complex components TOX4, WDR82, and PP1α/β/γ. Interestingly, almost all the cleavage and polyadenylation-related factors remained associated with PNUTS TND-M, indicating their interactions with PNUTS are TND-independent. In contrast, most splicing and RNA-binding factors were partially dependent on the TND for interaction with PNUTS. Strikingly, transcription elongation factors and RNA Pol II were completely dependent on the TND, demonstrating that the PNUTS complex requires its TND to efficiently interface with these proteins. Importantly, comparing proteins that interact with the PNUTS complex via its TND to proteins that showed increases in phosphorylation following PNUTS depletion (Figure 5D), we observed factors like SPT6, PAF1 and LEO1, consistent with these having TIMs and likely being direct targets of the PNUTS complex that are selected by the TND (Figure S6B).To understand if TND-mediated interactions were required for the effects PNUTS has on transcription, we carried out cTT-seq after PNUTS TND-M rescue and compared this to PNUTS WT rescue (Figure 7A, 7B, S7A, and S7B). Remarkably, the effects on transcription following PNUTS TND-M rescue were almost indistinguishable from PNUTS depletion, suggesting that TND interactions are essential for how PNUTS controls transcription, despite this mutant retaining its interaction with PP1. To understand how RNA Pol II is affected in the PNUTS TND-M rescue, we carried out cChIP-seq (Figure 7D, 7E, and S7C). Similar to both PNUTS depletion and the PNUTS W401A rescue (Figure 4F and 4G), RNA Pol II accumulated at the 5’ end of genes, consistent with a failure to efficiently release from promoter-proximal pausing. Finally, when we analysed PNUTS TND-M occupancy by cChIP-seq we found that, like PNUTS W401A (Figure 4F and 4H), it localised appropriately to the 5’ end genes, albeit to lower levels than PNUTS WT (Figure 7D, 7F, and S7D). This demonstrates that targeting of a PNUTS complex that still interacts with PP1 to the 5’ end of genes is not sufficient to drive pause release unless it can bind TND-interacting partners. Therefore, we propose that the PNUTS complex uses its TND to identify substrates which are then dephosphorylated by PP1 allowing for efficient transcription pause release and elongation. If either of these PNUTS complex functions is disrupted, RNA Pol II cannot efficiently exit the paused state and normal transcription cannot occur.

PNUTS is essential for transcription pause releaseWe next aimed to identify the step of the transcription cycle that PNUTS controls to exert its effects on transcription. Accumulation of RNA Pol II at the 5’ end of genes after PNUTS depletion could correspond to engagement of a pre-initiated form of RNA Pol II, or an initiated and elongating form of RNA Pol II that has paused but not transitioned into productive elongation. To explore this, we first carried out cChIP-seq examining the phosphorylation state of the C-terminal heptapeptide repeat (CTD) of RNA Pol II, as distinct stages of transcription are characterised by phosphorylation events on specific CTD residues6. For example, serine 5 phosphorylation (Ser5P) in the CTD, deposited by CDK7, is associated with RNA Pol II that has transitioned from pre-initiation into early elongation. In contrast, serine 2 phosphorylation (Ser2P), driven by the activity of CKD9, CDK12, and CDK13, is associated with exit of RNA Pol II from promoter-proximal pausing (Figure 3A). Importantly, cChIP-seq after PNUTS depletion revealed RNA Pol II at the 5’ end of genes was phosphorylated on Ser5, but lacked Ser2P (Figure 3B and Supplementary Figure S2C). Furthermore, Ser2P signal in the gene body was greatly reduced, consistent with transcription defects observed in cTT-seq (Figure 1). Therefore, without the PNUTS complex, RNA Pol II may be limited in exiting from promoter-proximal pausing and entry into productive elongation (Figure 3A).To explore this possibility in more detail, we profiled occupancy of RNA Pol II-associated factors known to demarcate specific phases of transcription. During the transition from pre-initiation into early elongation, DSIF, composed of SPT4/5, is loaded onto RNA Pol II as it transcribes towards the promoter-proximal pause site (Figure 3A and 3C)5,8,10. When we analysed SPT5 occupancy by cChIP-seq after PNUTS depletion, SPT5 was present at the 5’ end of genes suggesting that RNA Pol II had entered into early elongation (Figure 3C and S2D). Consistent with this, we also observed binding of TFIIS, which helps to restart backtracked RNA Pol II during the initiation/early non-processive elongation and poising phases of the transcription cycle (Figure 3A, 3C, and S2D)75-77. To determine if the accumulated RNA Pol II corresponded to a paused RNA Pol II, we examined binding of the promoter-proximal pausing factors NELF (NELF-B) and Integrator (INTS11) (Figure 3A, 3D and S2E)5,8,10. cChIP-seq demonstrated that they remained associated with RNA Pol II at the 5’ end of genes after PNUTS depletion. This suggests that in the absence of PNUTS, RNA Pol II initiates transcription and enters into early elongation, but does not efficiently transition beyond the pausing zone. Consistent with this, we could not detect binding of SPT6 and PAF1, which load onto RNA Pol II during the transition from pausing into productive elongation (Figure 3A, 3E, S2F and S2H). Finally, we examined CDK9 and BRD4, which are essential for the transition of promoter-proximally paused RNA Pol II into productive elongation (Figure 3A, 3F, and S2G)9. In the absence of PNUTS these factors still localised to the 5’ end of the gene and further biochemical characterisation confirmed that P-TEFb complexes remained intact (Figure S3A and S3B). Despite this, RNA Pol II did not efficiently transition into productive elongation. Therefore, we conclude that PNUTS regulates pause release to enable productive transcription elongation.

To explore this, we wanted to interrogate the nuclear phosphoproteome after PNUTS depletion. To achieve this, we isolated nuclei from cells and digested chromatin with nucleases to extract the total nuclear fraction from untreated cells and cells depleted of PNUTS for 30 minutes or 2 hours, with the view that these time points would allow us to capture early and persistent effects on phosphorylation (Figure S5A). PNUTS requires its interaction with PP1 to enable pause release and transcription, suggesting it could function to regulate phosphorylation of proteins that control these steps of transcription. To explore this, we wanted to interrogate the nuclear phosphoproteome after PNUTS depletion. The PNUTS TND guides interactions with substrates and is required for pause releasePNUTS interacts with PP1 to enable release of RNA Pol II into productive elongation and factors involved in transcription pause release and elongation become hyperphosphorylated when PNUTS is depleted (Figure 5). This suggested that PNUTS might select substrates for PP1-dependent dephosphorylation to drive the effects on transcription. Recent in vitro biochemical work and structural analysis showed that PNUTS contains a TFIIS-like N-terminal domain (TND). The TND is composed of a right-handed bundle of alpha-helices that forms an interface which binds intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), called TND-interacting motifs (TIMs), found in other proteins49,83-85 (Figure 6A). There are more than a hundred nuclear proteins containing putative TIMs, including many with important roles in transcription83. This suggests that the TND could help guide the selection of substrates and thus how PNUTS regulates transcription. However, what factors PNUTS interacts with via its TND in vivo remains unknown.Therefore, to determine the complement of proteins that PNUTS interacts with and define which rely on the TND, we used existing structural information and AlphaFold predictions to explore the PNUTS TND and its TIM interaction interface (Figure 6A). Consistent with previous biochemical and structural observations, AlphaFold predicts the C-terminal side of the TND will act as the interaction interface for TIM-containing proteins. In addition, AlphaFold predicts that a small HEAT-repeat sub-domain packs up against the TND and forms the interaction site for TOX4. Based on this structural model, we designed a mutant of the TND (TND-M) with substitutions in key residues that should disrupt TIM interactions while leaving the structure of the TND and its ability to interface with the HEAT sub-domain and TOX4 intact (Figure 6A). We then engineered a new rescue line where we could simultaneously induce the expression of the PNUTS TND-M protein while depleting endogenous PNUTS (Figure 6B and S6A). Rescue with PNUTS TND-M enabled the formation of a core PNUTS complex containing TOX4, WDR82, and PP1α in a manner indistinguishable from WT rescue (Figure 6C). This suggests the TND-M retains the overall TND structure and supports efficient incorporation of TOX4 into the PNUTS complex.We next induced expression of PNUTS WT or PNUTS TND-M and performed immunoprecipitations from total nuclear extracts and used mass spectrometry to identify interacting factors. We first focussed on proteins which interact with PNUTS WT (Figure 6D and Table S2). Importantly, the core PNUTS complex components TOX4, WDR82, and PP1α/β/γ were among the most enriched. However, we also identified additional proteins, some of which contain putative TIMs. The majority of significant interacting factors could be segregated into five general groupings and included key transcription elongation factors (e.g. the PAF complex, SPT6, TFIIS, SCAF4/8, and IWS1), splicing regulatory factors (e.g. SRSF3/7/10, SON, and TRA2B), diverse RNA binding proteins, cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factors (e.g. SYMPK, CPSF1/2/3, CSTF1/2, WDR33, and FIP1), and the RNA Pol II complex itself. To discover which of these rely on the TND we compared the factors enriched in WT and TND-M purifications (Figure 6E, 6F, and Supplementary Table S2). In agreement with immunoprecipitation and western bot analysis (Figure 6C), the TND-M retained normal interactions with the core PNUTS complex components TOX4, WDR82, and PP1α/β/γ. Interestingly, almost all the cleavage and polyadenylation-related factors remained associated with PNUTS TND-M, indicating their interactions with PNUTS are TND-independent. In contrast, most splicing and RNA-binding factors were partially dependent on the TND for interaction with PNUTS. Strikingly, transcription elongation factors and RNA Pol II were completely dependent on the TND, demonstrating that the PNUTS complex requires its TND to efficiently interface with these proteins. Importantly, comparing proteins that interact with the PNUTS complex via its TND to proteins that showed increases in phosphorylation following PNUTS depletion (Figure 5D), we observed factors like SPT6, PAF1 and LEO1, consistent with these having TIMs and likely being direct targets of the PNUTS complex that are selected by the TND (Figure S6B).To understand if TND-mediated interactions were required for the effects PNUTS has on transcription, we carried out cTT-seq after PNUTS TND-M rescue and compared this to PNUTS WT rescue (Figure 7A, 7B, S7A, and S7B). Remarkably, the effects on transcription following PNUTS TND-M rescue were almost indistinguishable from PNUTS depletion, suggesting that TND interactions are essential for how PNUTS controls transcription, despite this mutant retaining its interaction with PP1. To understand how RNA Pol II is affected in the PNUTS TND-M rescue, we carried out cChIP-seq (Figure 7D, 7E, and S7C). Similar to both PNUTS depletion and the PNUTS W401A rescue (Figure 4F and 4G), RNA Pol II accumulated at the 5’ end of genes, consistent with a failure to efficiently release from promoter-proximal pausing. Finally, when we analysed PNUTS TND-M occupancy by cChIP-seq we found that, like PNUTS W401A (Figure 4F and 4H), it localised appropriately to the 5’ end genes, albeit to lower levels than PNUTS WT (Figure 7D, 7F, and S7D). This demonstrates that targeting of a PNUTS complex that still interacts with PP1 to the 5’ end of genes is not sufficient to drive pause release unless it can bind TND-interacting partners. Therefore, we propose that the PNUTS complex uses its TND to identify substrates which are then dephosphorylated by PP1 allowing for efficient transcription pause release and elongation. If either of these PNUTS complex functions is disrupted, RNA Pol II cannot efficiently exit the paused state and normal transcription cannot occur.

Cell line generation

To allow for rapid depletion of the PNUTS protein, we introduced a homozygous C-terminal 3xT7-2xStrepll-dTAG tag into the endogenous Pnuts gene. The insertion of the T7 tag allowed for the efficient chromatin immunoprecipitation of PNUTS-dTAG and purification of the PNUTS complex. To allow for single particle tracking of RNA Pol II, we further engineered PNUTS-dTAG ESCs to expressed endogenously N-terminal tagged HaloTag-RPB1 (as described in113).

To be able to calibrate PNUTS-T7 ChIPseq experiments, we generated a HEK293T cell line carrying an N-terminal 3xT7-2xStrepII-dTAG tag at the endogenous Rpb1 gene.

For proteomic analysis, 40 micrograms of peptide were removed. Peptides were lyophilized and stored at −80°C until further use. For proteomic analysis, peptides were labelled with Tandem-Mass-Tagpro (TMTpro) reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific). Once labelling efficiency was confirmed to be at least 95%, each reaction was quenched by the addition of hydroxylamine to a final concentration of 0.25% for 10 minutes, mixed, acidified with TFA to a pH of about 2, and desalted over an Oasis HLB 10 mg plate (Waters). For phosphoproteomics, phosphopeptide enrichment was carried out using a Fe-NTA phosphopeptide enrichment kit (ThermoFisher) according to instructions provided by the manufacturer and desalted over an Oasis HLB 10 mg plate (Waters). Phosphopeptides were then labeled with TMTpro reagents and offline separated as described above. TMT-labeled peptides were analyzed on an Orbitrap Lumos mass spectrometer (ThermoScientific) equipped with an Easy-nLC 1200 (ThermoScientific). Quantification of LC-MS/MS spectra was performed using in-house developed software. The probability of phosphorylation site localization was determined by PhosphoRS108. Peptide intensities were adjusted based on total TMT reporter ion intensity in each channel, and log2 transformed. P-values were calculated using a two-tailed Student’s t-test in Perseus109.
