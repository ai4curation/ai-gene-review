Climate change is resulting in more frequent and rapidly changing temperatures
at both extremes that severely affect the growth and production of plants,
particularly crops. Oxidative stress caused by high temperatures is one of the
most damaging factors for plants. However, the role of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)
in modulating plant thermotolerance is largely unknown, and the regulation of
photorespiration essential for C3 species remains to be fully clarified. Here,
we report that heat stress promotes H2O2 accumulation in chloroplasts and that
H2O2 stimulates sulfenylation of the chloroplast-localized photorespiratory
enzyme 2-phosphoglycolate phosphatase 1 (PGLP1) at cysteine 86, inhibiting its
activity and promoting the accumulation of the toxic metabolite
2-phosphoglycolate. We also demonstrate that PGLP1 has a positive function in
plant thermotolerance, as PGLP1 antisense lines have greater heat sensitivity
and PGLP1-overexpressing plants have higher heat-stress tolerance than the wild
type. Together, our results demonstrate that heat-induced H2O2 in chloroplasts
sulfenylates and inhibits PGLP1 to modulate plant thermotolerance. Furthermore,
targeting CATALASE2 to chloroplasts can largely prevent the heat-induced
overaccumulation of H2O2 and the sulfenylation of PGLP1, thus conferring
thermotolerance without a plant growth penalty. These findings reveal that
heat-induced H2O2 in chloroplasts is important for heat-caused plant damage.

Introduction

Temperature is one of the most important environmental factors that affect the seasonal growth and geographic distribution of plants. Average global surface temperatures are predicted to increase by over 3°C by the end of the 21st century, and each degree Celsius increase in global mean temperature is predicted, on average, to reduce global yields of wheat (Triticum aestivum) by 6.0%, rice (Oryza sativa) by 3.2%, maize (Zea mays) by 7.4%, and soybean (Glycine max) by 3.1%, depending on the specific climate model used (Challinor et al., 2014; Smith et al., 2015; Raftery et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2017). High temperatures adversely affect almost all aspects of plant growth, development, reproduction, and yield by causing the accumulation of toxic by-products such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) and damaged proteins (Wang et al., 2018; Hendrix et al., 2023). To counteract the damage imposed by heat stress, plants use ROS-scavenging enzymes and heat shock proteins as mitigation mechanisms (Driedonks et al., 2015; Suzuki and Katano, 2018).

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is a major form of ROS that plays critical roles in plant growth and development, including seed germination, root development, photosynthesis, flowering, and senescence (Dunand et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2013, 2017; Guo et al., 2016; Yuan et al., 2017). Various environmental stresses, including heat stress, induce the overaccumulation of H2O2 in plant cells and thus reduce growth rates and productivity (Dat et al., 1998; Ivanchenko et al., 2013; Fu et al., 2023). Heat stress can disturb photosystems I and II as well as the electron transport chain, which constitute multiple potential sites for H2O2 production in chloroplasts (Waszczak et al., 2018). Heat stress also decreases the selectivity of ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco) for carbon dioxide (CO2) while increasing its affinity for O2 (Jordan and Ogren, 1984). The consequent alterations in CO2 and O2 concentrations affect the Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle, thus impairing the plastid electron transport chain (de Pinto et al., 2015).

H2O2 acts on proteins through sulfenylation, a reversible post-translational modification of thiol (–SH) into sulfenic acid (–SOH) at Cys residues that induces structural changes in proteins and alters their functions, affecting enzymatic activity, protein–protein interactions, and subcellular localization (Couturier et al., 2013). The biological functions of protein sulfenylation remain largely unexplored in plants, although thousands of sulfenylated proteins have been identified (Waszczak et al., 2014; Akter et al., 2015; De Smet et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2019). To date, the effects of sulfenylation on protein function have been analyzed for only a small number of plant proteins (Yuan et al., 2017; Bi et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022b; Fu et al., 2023). Because the generation of H2O2 is an unavoidable consequence of aerobic metabolism, plants have evolved a large array of H2O2-scavenging mechanisms, including non-enzymatic and enzymatic scavengers. Catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and glutathione peroxidase (GPX) are the main H2O2-scavenging enzymes in plants and are crucial for reducing toxic intracellular H2O2 levels (Niu and Liao, 2016). Peroxisome-located CAT directly converts H2O2 into H2O and O2 when H2O2 concentrations reach high levels (Li et al., 2015), and APX and GPX catalyze the reduction of H2O2 to water using reducing molecules as electron donors in the cytosol, chloroplasts, and mitochondria (Antunes et al., 2002; Margis et al., 2008; Cuypers et al., 2011). Both APX and CAT have been reported to regulate plant thermotolerance by preventing toxic levels of H2O2 accumulation, as evidenced by the greater heat-stress sensitivity of plants that lack APX1 or CAT2 activity (Vanderauwera et al., 2011; Ono et al., 2021). However, how excessive accumulation of H2O2 functions in plant heat-stress responses remains to be clarified.

The oxygenase reaction of Rubisco generates 2-phosphoglycolate (2PG), a toxic metabolite that inhibits the triose-phosphate isomerase and phosphofructokinase enzymes needed for CO2 assimilation (Somerville and Ogren, 1981; Ogren, 1984). Photorespiration therefore performs a vital function in plant growth by degrading 2PG and recovering a portion of the previously fixed carbon trapped in 2PG (Somerville and Ogren, 1981; Ogren, 1984; Artus et al., 1986; South et al., 2018). The breakdown of 2PG into glycolate is catalyzed by 2PG phosphatase (PGLP) in the entry step of the photorespiratory pathway. In mammals and yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), phosphoglycolate phosphatase removes 2PG generated by the fragmentation of DNA sugars initiated by free radicals and destroys other toxic metabolites, such as 4-phosphoerythronate and 2-phospho-L-lactate (Povirk, 2012; Beaudoin and Hanson, 2016). The Arabidopsis thaliana genome encodes two PGLP isoenzymes, each encoded by a single gene: one is located in the plastids (PGLP1, encoded by At5g36700) and the other in the cytosol (PGLP2, encoded by At5g47760). Loss-of-function mutation of PGLP2 is not associated with obvious phenotypes. By contrast, the pglp1 mutant is seedling lethal when grown in normal air but largely viable when maintained in a high-CO2 environment (Schwarte and Bauwe, 2007; Flügel et al., 2017), suggesting that PGLP1 is essential for plant survival in normal air. There have been very few reports on the functions of PGLP1 and 2PG; one study revealed that overexpression of PGLP1 increased photosynthesis and starch accumulation under heat stress (Timm et al., 2019), but how heat stress affects plant growth through PGLP1 and modulation of 2PG accumulation remains largely unknown.

Here, we describe how heat stress affects photorespiration through H2O2 accumulation. Heat stress triggers the accumulation of excess H2O2 in chloroplasts, stimulating the sulfenylation of PGLP1 and inhibiting its activity, thus leading to 2PG accumulation and inhibition of plant growth and development. In addition, suppression of H2O2 accumulation by targeting CAT2 to the chloroplasts confers thermotolerance.

ResultsHeat stress promotes H2O2 accumulation in chloroplastsH2O2 is involved in a wide range of environmental stresses, including heat stress (Wang et al., 2014). We therefore examined endogenous H2O2 accumulation in wild-type plants using 3,3ʹ-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining and confirmed that heat stress induces H2O2 accumulation (Figure 1A and 1B). The fluorescent markers HyPer and roGFP2 have recently been used as probes to assess H2O2 levels and localization in plants (Exposito-Rodriguez et al., 2017; Nietzel et al., 2019). The roGFP2 reporter is particularly promising because, unlike HyPer, it is not sensitive to pH. Heat stress was shown to increase oxidation in chloroplasts using roGFP2 as a redox sensor (Kumar et al., 2021). Because roGFP2 does not readily react with H2O2, yeast GPX Orp1 was fused to roGFP2 to enhance its specificity for H2O2 (Gutscher et al., 2009). This H2O2-specific roGFP2-Orp1 probe has been widely used to analyze H2O2 dynamics in intracellular compartments (Nietzel et al., 2019; Ugalde et al., 2021), but detection of H2O2 in chloroplasts under heat stress has not been explored. To test whether heat stress affects H2O2 levels in chloroplasts, we generated transgenic 35S::chl-roGFP2-Orp1 plants expressing chloroplast-targeted roGFP2-Orp1 under the control of the constitutive cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter. The chloroplast localization of the chl-roGFP2-Orp1 sensor was confirmed in the transgenic plants by confocal laser scanning microscopy, as reported previously (Fu et al., 2023). When subjected to heat stress, 35S::chl-roGFP2-Orp1 transgenic plants showed increased chl-roGFP2-Orp1 oxidation (OxD) (Figure 1C and 1D), indicating that heat stress promotes H2O2 accumulation in chloroplasts. To investigate whether heat-induced H2O2 accumulation in chloroplasts is related to light, 3-week-old 35S::chl-roGFP2-Orp1 plants were placed in the dark at 23°C for 24 h and transferred to incubators at 38°C in the dark for 24 h, after which the extent of roGFP2-Orp1 oxidation was examined. The results showed that light did not significantly affect the accumulation of chloroplastic H2O2 in heat-stressed plants (Figure 1E). The PGLP1 protein contains eight Cys residues, three of which (Cys-27, Cys-40, and Cys-43) are located within the chloroplast transit peptide and are thus removed following import into the organelle (Supplemental Figure 2) (Schwarte and Bauwe, 2007). To determine which of the remaining five Cys residue(s) in PGLP1 are the target(s) of H2O2 sulfenylation, we produced and purified recombinant mutated PGLP1 proteins (PGLP1-C86S, PGLP1-C232S, PGLP1-C239S, PGLP1-C302S, and PGLP1-C320S) in which each of the five Cys residues was individually replaced by a Ser residue using site-directed mutagenesis. The enzymatic activity of PGLP1-C86S, PGLP1-C232S, PGLP1-C239S, and PGLP1-C320S was lower than that of intact PGLP1 (Figure 2J). When incubated with H2O2
in vitro, PGLP1-C232S, PGLP1-C239S, and PGLP1-C320S recombinant proteins showed a further reduction in their activity, in contrast to PGLP1-C86S (Figure 2J). We also found that the Cys-302-Ser mutation completely abolished PGLP1 activity (Figure 2J), probably as a result of mutation-induced conformational changes. Furthermore, recombinant PGLP1-C86S appeared to be less sensitive to sulfenic acid modification by H2O2 than intact PGLP1 or the other mutated recombinant proteins PGLP1-C232S, PGLP1-C239S, PGLP1-C302S, and PGLP1-C320S (Figure 2K), suggesting that H2O2 sulfenylates PGLP1, likely at its Cys-86 residue, to inhibit its activity. We next labeled –SOH in recombinant PGLP1 protein with dimedone and identified the sulfenylated Cys residues using LC–MS/MS, as reported previously (Fu et al., 2023). Strangely, we did not identify any peptides containing labeled or unlabeled Cys-86 residues, despite various experimental optimizations, including the use of different proteases for protein hydrolysis. We even failed to synthesize two peptides (amino acid residues 64–91 and 78–91 of PGLP1) at GenScript (Nanjing, China), possibly owing to their high hydrophobicity, low isoelectric point, and β sheet structure. These attempts suggest that the characteristics of the Cys-86 residue may prevent it from being easily detected by LC–MS/MS.

DiscussionROS are essential for life and are involved in the regulation of almost all biological processes (Mittler, 2017; Smirnoff and Arnaud, 2019; Castro et al., 2021). Their production is mainly restricted to chloroplasts, mitochondria, and peroxisomes, and compartmentalization of ROS production and scavenging determines the biological functions of ROS in plants (Waszczak et al., 2018; Castro et al., 2021). Chloroplasts have been proposed to be an important source and target of H2O2, although direct evidence of H2O2 in chloroplasts following heat stress has been lacking (Allakhverdiev et al., 2008; Waszczak et al., 2018). We therefore generated 35S::chl-roGFP2-Orp1 transgenic lines to probe relative H2O2 levels in chloroplasts and demonstrated that heat stress promotes chloroplast H2O2 accumulation. Furthermore, we revealed that heat-induced H2O2 modifies the Cys-86 of chloroplastic PGLP1 and inhibits its activity, disrupting photorespiration and leading to 2PG accumulation. After exploring this mechanism, we limited chloroplast H2O2 accumulation by targeting the H2O2-scavenging enzyme CAT2 to the chloroplasts. The resulting 35S::chl-CAT2 plants were more tolerant of heat stress and showed no adverse growth effects under normal, non-stressed conditions.Chloroplasts and peroxisomes form direct points of contact at their outer membranes that are essential for their functions and for the transport of metabolites between the organelles (Oikawa et al., 2015). H2O2 has also been postulated to flow between chloroplasts and peroxisomes (Waszczak et al., 2018). In fact, peroxisomal ROS production is tightly linked to photosynthesis in photosynthetically active tissues (Foyer and Noctor, 2016). We previously demonstrated that elevated H2O2 levels in peroxisomes caused by the cat2 mutation lead to increased TSB1 sulfenylation in chloroplasts during pathogen infection (Yuan et al., 2017). Pretreating plants with low levels of H2O2 permits the survival of more green leaf tissue and leads to a higher quantum yield of photosystem II under heat stress compared with non-treated controls (Uchida et al., 2002). In addition, plants with mutations in RESPIRATORY BURST OXIDASE HOMOLOG B (RBOHB) and RBOHD have much lower H2O2 concentrations than their wild-type parents and show a significant thermosensitive phenotype (Larkindale et al., 2005). A slight increase in H2O2 may function as a signal that activates heat shock factor-dependent pathways and triggers APX-dependent heat response pathways to prevent accumulation of toxic levels of H2O2, thus protecting plants from high-temperature damage (Liu and Charng, 2013; Katano et al., 2018). CATs are most notably distinguished from other ROS-scavenging enzymes by not requiring a reductant, as they catalyze a dismutation reaction. In Arabidopsis, CAT1 is expressed mainly in pollen and seeds, CAT2 is expressed in photosynthetic tissues as well as roots and seeds, and CAT3 is associated with vascular tissues and leaves (Hu et al., 2010; Mhamdi et al., 2010). We therefore selected CAT2 to help degrade H2O2 in chloroplasts. CATs have a fast turnover rate but a much lower affinity for H2O2 than APX and GPX, indicating that they function well in vivo only over a critical threshold of H2O2 (Mittler and Zilinskas, 1991; König et al., 2002). Our results reveal that, under normal conditions, targeting CAT2 to chloroplasts in 35S::chl-CAT2 plants does not change H2O2 levels globally. However, when subjected to heat stress that might result in toxic accumulation of H2O2, this chloroplast-localized chl-CAT2 enzyme can block the overaccumulation of H2O2 induced by heat stress (Figure 5A–5C). Thus, manipulating chl-CAT2 protein levels in chloroplasts may help to mitigate the damage caused by toxic levels of H2O2 and thus confer plant thermotolerance. It has also been reported that overexpression of Gossypium hirsutum CAT1 in chloroplasts enhances tolerance to methyl viologen and salt stress, although the molecular mechanism is unclear (Luo et al., 2013). Installing synthetic glycolate metabolism pathways into tobacco chloroplasts stimulates growth and productivity in the field (South et al., 2019).
