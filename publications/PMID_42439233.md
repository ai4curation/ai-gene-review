Actin-related proteins (Arps) and actin-like proteins constitute a subfamily of 
actin-fold proteins that are evolutionarily and structurally related to 
conventional actin but functionally distinct. While actin is defined by its 
ability to form dynamic filaments, bind and hydrolyze ATP, and serve as a major 
cytoskeletal scaffold, Arps and actin-like proteins have evolved specialized 
roles in cytoplasmic and nuclear protein complexes. The Arp2/3 complex mediates 
branched actin nucleation, whereas the dynactin complex, containing Arp1 and 
Arp11, functions as an activator and cargo-adaptor for dynein-dependent 
intracellular transport. In the nucleus, multiple Arps, actin, and actin-like 
proteins are incorporated into large ATP-dependent chromatin-remodelling and 
histone-modifying complexes, where they regulate nucleosome positioning, histone 
variant exchange, and transcriptional accessibility. Beyond canonical Arps, 
mammals encode a range of non-canonical Arps and actin-like proteins, many of 
which exhibit tissue-specific expression, particularly in the testis, and are 
associated with spermiogenesis and male fertility. This review summarizes the 
current knowledge on the classification, structure-function relationship, and 
cellular roles of human Arps and actin-like proteins, as well as their links to 
human disease, including immunodeficiency, neurodevelopmental disorders, 
infertility, and cancer.
