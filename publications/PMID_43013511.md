Neurokinin B (NKB) signaling plays a central role in mammalian thermoregulatory
dysfunction associated with menopause, yet the underlying neural mechanisms
remain incompletely understood. Here, we establish Caenorhabditis elegans
thermotaxis as a genetically tractable system for investigating conserved
tachykinin signaling. We demonstrate that overexpression of nlp-58, the C.
elegans ortholog of mammalian NKB, induces abnormal thermotaxis behavior without
impairing the temperature response of the thermosensory neurons. NLP-58 is
expressed in multiple neurons, including the AIY interneurons, a central
integrative node of the thermosensory circuit. AIY-specific overexpression of
nlp-58 is sufficient to reproduce the thermotaxis defect, identifying AIY as an
important site of NLP-58-dependent behavioral modulation. Genetic analyses
further demonstrated that the thermotaxis behavior is mediated primarily through
the tachykinin receptor TKR-1, the homolog of mammalian tachykinin 3 receptor
(NK3R), acting in ASG and/or ASJ sensory neurons. We also identified a genetic
interaction between dhs-4, a lipid metabolic enzyme gene, and nlp-58, suggesting
that lipid metabolic pathway modulate tachykinin-dependent thermotaxis behavior.
Furthermore, endogenous NLP-58/TKR-1 signaling contributed to the age-dependent
decline in thermotaxis behavior. Finally, treatment with fezolinetant, a
selective NK3R antagonist used clinically for menopausal vasomotor symptoms,
partially improved the thermotaxis defect caused by nlp-58 overexpression.
Together, our findings identify the NLP-58/TKR-1 signaling pathway as a
modulatory component of thermotaxis behavior and provide a framework for
investigating conserved principles of tachykinin-dependent circuit modulation.

Introduction

Menopause is a physiological transition characterized by declining ovarian function and reduced circulating estrogen levels. One of the most common and distressing symptoms during this period is hot flashes: sudden sensations of heat often accompanied by flushing and sweating. These vasomotor symptoms affect over 70% of menopausal women and significantly impact quality of life and daily functioning [1–5]. Although hormone replacement therapy remains the most effective treatment, concerns about its long-term safety, including increased risks of cardiovascular disease and breast cancer, have prompted the search for alternative therapeutic targets [6,7].

Accumulating evidence from human studies and rodent models has identified neurokinin B (NKB), a member of the tachykinin peptide family, as a key mediator of vasomotor symptoms. Following estrogen withdrawal, NKB expression is markedly upregulated in the hypothalamus, and exogenous NKB administration in premenopausal women elicits hot flash-like symptoms [8–14]. These findings indicate that estrogen withdrawal is associated with enhanced NKB signaling, which in turn contributes to thermoregulatory dysfunction. Nevertheless, elucidating the molecular and neuronal mechanisms underlying this pathway in mammals remains challenging due to the complexity of hormonal feedback networks, limited experimental access to specific neural circuits, and difficulties in quantitatively assessing thermoregulatory response.

A genetically tractable model system for dissecting conserved tachykinin signaling mechanisms would facilitate mechanistic understanding [15–17]. Caenorhabditis elegans offers several advantages for studying neuroendocrine regulation of behavior: its nervous system is fully mapped, genetically tractable, and amenable to single-cell level manipulation and analysis. Importantly, C. elegans exhibits robust thermosensory response, in which animals migrate along temperature gradients toward their prior experienced cultivation temperatures, providing a well-established and quantitative paradigm for investigating temperature-driven behavioral responses [18–21]. Previous works have identified core components of the thermosensory circuit, including the AFD thermosensory neurons and the AIY interneurons, which integrate thermal information and encode temperature preference [19,22,23]. Previous studies have suggested that steroid-related signaling pathways influence thermotaxis behavior in C. elegans [24].

In this study, we used C. elegans thermotaxis to investigate the conserved function of the tachykinin-like peptide NLP-58 and its receptor TKR-1. We demonstrate that overexpression of nlp-58 induces abnormal, temperature-dependent thermotaxis behavior without disrupting primary thermosensory input, consistent with altered circuit modulation rather than sensory impairment. This phenotype is influenced by lipid metabolic pathways, revealing a functional link between neuropeptide signaling and lipid metabolism. Furthermore, through cell-specific overexpression and targeted rescue experiments, we identify the neurons that mediate NLP-58–dependent behavioral modulation and demonstrate that TKR-1 functions in specific sensory neurons that interface with the canonical thermosensory circuit.

Together, our findings uncover a conserved neuroendocrine mechanism linking lipid metabolism and temperature preference and establish C. elegans as a genetically tractable model for dissecting conserved neuropeptide signaling pathways relevant to mammalian neurokinin biology.

Hot flashes are among the most prevalent symptoms of menopause and substantially impair quality of life [4,5,42]. Recent studies have identified the tachykinin neuropeptide system, particularly neurokinin B (NKB) and its receptor NK3R, as a key contributor to menopausal vasomotor symptoms, and NK3R antagonists have shown clinical efficacy [8,9,12,43]. However, how altered tachykinin signaling modifies thermoregulatory neural circuits remains incompletely understood. In particular, distinguishing the effects of neuropeptide signaling on primary thermal sensation from those of other downstream processes and behavioral output remains an important challenge in complex mammalian systems.

In this study, we used C. elegans, in which thermosensory behavior can be analyzed at single-neuron resolution, to investigate how manipulation of tachykinin-like signaling modulates temperature-associated behavior. Temperature preference in C. elegans is regulated by a well-characterized circuit involving the AFD thermosensory neurons and downstream AIY interneurons [19,44]. We found that overexpression of nlp-58, which encodes a tachykinin-like neuropeptide related to mammalian NKB, showed thermotaxis behavioral defect without an apparent impairment of primary thermosensory responses. Cell-specific overexpression further showed that increased NLP-58 signaling from AIY is sufficient to reproduce the thermotaxis behavioral defect. Genetic analyses identified TKR-1 as the major tachykinin receptor contributing to this phenotype, and cell-specific rescue experiments demonstrated that TKR-1 expression in either ASG or ASJ sensory neurons is sufficient to restore the nlp-58OE phenotype in a tkr-1 mutant background. Together, these findings support a model in which excessive NLP-58/TKR-1 signaling modulates thermotaxis behavior downstream or in parallel with the primary thermosensory pathway rather than directly disrupting temperature sensing.

Our finding further suggests that AIY participates in thermotaxis on multiple functional timescales. The AFD–AIY pathway mediates rapid responses to changes in environmental temperature [45]. In contrast, our results suggest that NLP-58 signaling from AIY may provide an additional modulatory pathway that influences thermotaxis circuit output. ASG and ASJ are classically defined as sensory neurons and are not central components of the canonical AFD–AIY thermotaxis pathway. Nevertheless, TKR-1 expression in either ASG or ASJ was sufficient to reproduce the abnormal thermotaxis phenotype of nlp-58OE animals in the tkr-1 mutant background. ASJ has been reported to respond directly to temperature changes, suggesting that it possesses intrinsic thermosensory properties [46]. However, its specific contribution to thermotaxis behavior remains unclear. These observations are consistent with the possibility that ASG and ASJ participate in a modulatory pathway that influences thermotaxis outside the canonical AFD–AIY circuit. Because the present experiments demonstrate sufficiency but not necessity of TKR-1 function in these neurons, additional cell-specific loss-of-function experiments will be required to determine their endogenous contributions to NLP-58-dependent thermotaxis.

Our results also reveal a relationship between lipid metabolism and NLP-58 signaling. Loss of dhs-4, a gene involved in steroid hormone biosynthesis, altered thermotaxis behavior, and deletion of nlp-58 suppressed the dhs-4 mutant phenotype. Moreover, nlp-58 expression was increased in dhs-4 mutants as well as in animals lacking the nuclear hormone receptors genes nhr-69, nhr-121, and nhr-14. These genetic interactions suggest that DHS-4-dependent lipid metabolism and nuclear hormone receptor signaling can influence nlp-58 expression and thermotaxis behavior. Importantly, however, exogenous β-estradiol treatment did not significantly change nlp-58 expression in either wild-type or nuclear hormone receptor mutant backgrounds. Furthermore, the presence and physiological role of endogenous estrogen in C. elegans remain unresolved. Thus, our results do not establish estrogen as the endogenous signal linking DHS-4 or these nuclear hormone receptors to NLP-58. Instead, they support a broader model in which lipid metabolic and nuclear hormone receptor pathways modulate NLP-58 signaling, with the relevant endogenous ligand or metabolite remaining to be identified.

In addition to these regulatory mechanisms, our findings indicate that endogenous NLP-58/TKR-1 signaling contributes to age-dependent changes in thermotaxis behavior. Consistent with previous studies [32–34], the aged wild-type animals showed defect of the thermotaxis behavior. This age-dependent behavioral decline was significantly attenuated in nlp-58(nj428) and tkr-1(tm1756) mutants. In parallel, RNA-seq analysis revealed age-dependent increases in tkr-1 and tkr-2 transcript abundance, whereas nlp-58 itself did not show a significant age-dependent change (Fig 3A). These observations suggest that changes in tachykinin receptor signaling, rather than increased nlp-58 expression alone, may contribute to age-dependent modulation of thermotaxis.

The pharmacological experiments further support the involvement of TKR-1-dependent signaling in behavioral effects of NLP-58 overexpression. Fezolinetant, a selective antagonist of mammalian NK3R used clinically for menopausal vasomotor symptoms [47], partially improved the thermotaxis behavior of nlp-58OE animals, as supported by complementary analyses of animal distribution and the TTX index. Importantly, fezolinetant did not significantly alter thermotaxis in wild-type, tkr-1, or tkr-2 mutant animals. Moreover, the positive shift in the TTX index observed following fezolinetant treatment of nlp-58OE animals was no longer apparent in the tkr-1; nlp-58 OE background (Fig 8C). This finding is consistent with the pharmacological effect of fezolinetant depending, at least in part, on TKR-1 signaling. Previous biochemical studies have demonstrated that NLP-58-derived tachykinin-like peptides activate TKR-1 and TKR-2 [39,40]. However, whether fezolinetant directly interacts with or antagonizes TKR-1 has not been examined. Therefore, our results should not be interpreted as demonstrating that TKR-1 is a direct molecular target of fezolinetant. Direct pharmacological characterization of fezolinetant at TKR-1, for example using heterologous receptor assays, will be required to determine whether fezolinetant directly antagonized TKR-1.

Together, our findings identify the NLP-58/TKR-1 pathway as a modulatory component of the C. elegans thermotaxis circuit and reveal interactions among tachykinin-like neuropeptide signaling, lipid metabolic pathways, aging, and temperature preference. Additionally, we reveal a neuropeptide-dependent modulatory pathway in which NLP-58 signaling from AIY influences thermotaxis behavior through TKR-1 function in ASG and/or ASJ sensory neurons. Although mammalian thermoregulation and menopausal vasomotor symptoms involve physiological processes that are not recapitulated in C. elegans, the conservation of tachykinin signaling provides an opportunity to dissect fundamental principles of neuropeptide-mediated circuit modulation in a simple nervous system. Future studies combining cell-specific loss-of-function approaches with calcium imaging of ASG and ASJ during temperature stimulation in different nlp-58 and tkr-1 genetic backgrounds will be important for defining how NLP-58/TKR-1 signaling modulates thermosensory circuit activity. In parallel, direct pharmacological characterization of TKR-1 will be important for defining the endogenous circuit mechanism and determining the extent to which the pharmacological properties of mammalian NK3R signaling are conserved.

After two days, L4-stage animals were individually transferred onto 3.5 cm NGM plates (one worm per plate). After 20 hours, adults were removed and the plates were further incubated until progeny reached adulthood. To immobilize worms for counting, chloroform vapor was applied to the lid, and the plates were immediately sealed. The total number of progeny was then counted, confirming that all eggs hatched successfully in each assay.

The defecation cycle was measured under a stereomicroscope by recording the interval between successive defecation events. Measurements were performed using adult animals from Day 1 to Day 5.

Thermotaxis behavioral assayThermotaxis assays were performed as previously described [53,54]. Adult animals were cultivated on NGM plates at 23°C for three days. Animals were collected in NG buffer (0.3% NaCl, 1 mM MgSO4, 1 mM CaCl2, 25 mM KPO4, pH 6.0) and washed twice with the same buffer. Approximately 100–150 animals were placed at the center of a thermotaxis assay plate with a linear temperature gradient (17–23°C; 0.45–0.5°C/cm). Animals were allowed to move freely for 1 hour, after which locomotion was stopped using chloroform vapor. Each plate was divided into eight equal fractions, and the number of animals in each fraction was counted to quantify thermotaxis behavior.Thermotaxis behavior was quantified using a thermotaxis index (TTX index). Each fraction was assigned a score from 1 (coldest region) to 8 (warmest region), and the TTX index was calculated as the weighted average of animal distribution across the gradient using the following formula: TTX index = Σ(Si × Ni)/N, where Si represents the score of each fraction, Ni represents the number of animals in each fraction, and N represents the total number of animals scored. Differences in thermotaxis behavior between genotypes were evaluated by comparing TTX indices using the statistical tests indicated in each figure legend. Throughout this study, “abnormal thermotaxis” refers to a statistically significant difference in TTX index relative to wild-type animals, whereas “behavioral suppression” or “behavioral rescue” refers to a statistically significant improvement relative to the nlp-58OE animals.
