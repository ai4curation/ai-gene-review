The de novo generation of membrane contact sites (MCSs) between the phagophore 
and the endoplasmic reticulum exit sites (ERES) is important for the acquisition 
of the lipids necessary for phagophore elongation and autophagosome formation 
during autophagy. However, it is currently unclear how these MCSs are 
established. Here, we show that the TRAPPIII complex, the guanine nucleotide 
exchange factor of the Rab GTPase Ypt1, localizes to and regulates the formation 
of the MCS between the phagophore and the ERES. In particular, TRAPPIII and the 
lipid transfer protein Atg2 appear equally essential for the association of the 
phagophore with the ERES, TRAPPIII activation and Ypt1 activation onto the 
phagophore. Ypt1 redistributes over the entire surface of the phagophore and 
promotes its elongation through both stimulation of the local biosynthesis of 
phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate and recruitment of the downstream effectors 
Atg18 and Atg21. Our data suggest that de novo generation of the phagophore-ER 
MCSs and subsequent Ypt1 activation initiates phagophore elongation.

MainMacroautophagy (hereafter autophagy) is an intracellular catabolic pathway conserved among eukaryotes that is involved in numerous physiological functions, and its dysregulation has been linked to several human pathologies. Autophagy mediates the degradation of cytoplasmic material through its sequestration into double-membrane autophagosomes, which fuse with yeast and plant vacuoles or mammalian lysosomes, to expose their cargo to the hydrolytic interior of these organelles1. Autophagosomes are formed by nucleation, elongation and sealing of a membrane cisterna termed the phagophore. This structure is formed at a location adjacent to both the vacuole and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), called the phagophore assembly site (PAS), in yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae2–4, and in phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate (PtdIns3P)-enriched subdomains of the ER, termed omegasomes, in mammalian cells5–7. Yeast has been a key model organism for the identification and characterization of autophagy-related (Atg) proteins, which are organized in six functional clusters1,8. Although the Atg1/ULK kinase complex, the autophagy-specific phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K) complex I and Atg9/ATG9A-positive vesicles are central for the phagophore formation, the Atg2/ATG2–Atg18/WIPI4 complexes and the two ubiquitin-like (Ubl) conjugation systems are mainly involved in phagophore elongation and closure1,8. The origin of the membranes required for the phagophore nucleation remains to be fully understood; however, the ER appears to be the main lipid source for phagophore elongation8. The extremities of the phagophores form MCSs with the ER2,4,6,7, in particular, ERES2,4. Atg9 trimers, Atg2 and Atg18 localize at the phagophore extremity and have a key role in the phagophore–ER MCS establishment2,4,9,10. Atg9 distributes to the phagophore extremity where, together with PtdIns3P, it leads to Atg2 recruitment. Atg2 binding to Atg9 triggers Atg18 association through coincidence binding to Atg2 and PtdIns3P9. Inhibition of the Atg9–Atg2 interaction disrupts the formation of phagophore–ER MCSs, phagophore elongation and, ultimately, autophagy9. Importantly, Atg2 (and its mammalian counterparts ATG2A and ATG2B) transfers lipids between liposomes in vitro, which is enhanced by Atg18 (and its mammalian homolog WIPI4 (refs. 11–15)), and the lipid scramblase activity of Atg9/ATG9A trimers15. The current view is that the Atg9–Atg2–Atg18 module transfers lipids from the ER to the phagophore at the MCSs between these two organelles.Atg9-positive vesicles are a central element in PAS formation16,17. In vitro and in vivo experiments have suggested that these vesicles constitute the initial seed membrane that docks and forms a MCS with the ER18,19. These Atg9 membranes, before or after fusion with vesicles of different origin8, act as a platform to recruit the Atg machinery to promote phagophore nucleation and elongation. Another factor that has been connected to Atg9 is the transport protein particle (TRAPP) III complex20. TRAPP complexes are guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) that activate and thus recruit Rab GTPases at the Golgi by catalyzing the exchange of GDP for GTP21. Activated Rab GTPases recruit different downstream effectors, operating as molecular switches that regulate membrane trafficking and organelle biogenesis. There are two distinct TRAPP complexes, TRAPPII and TRAPPIII, which have a common set of subunits—Bet3, Bet5, Trs20, Trs23, Trs31 and Trs3322—which are sufficient for GEF activity in vitro23. Each one of them also possesses specific subunits: TRAPPII has Tca17, Trs65, Trs120 and Trs130, while TRAPPIII has Trs85 (refs. 24,25), which is essential to anchor TRAPPIII onto membranes26. Yeast TRAPPII is the GEF for Ypt31 and Ypt32 at the late Golgi/trans-Golgi network27, whereas TRAPPIII is the GEF for Ypt1/RAB1, at the early compartments of the Golgi system22. TRAPPIII and Ypt1, as well as their mammalian counterparts, are also involved in autophagy24,28–33. TRAPPIII is recruited to the PAS, possibly through Atg9 binding, and it is required for Ypt1 activation at this location20. TRAPPIII is also involved in ATG9A trafficking in mammalian cells31. However, some studies indicated that Trs85 and Ypt1 recruitment to the PAS is Atg9-independent24,34. These investigations and others have suggested that the Atg17–Atg29–Atg31 complex and Atg13, but not the other components of the Atg1 kinase complex (that is, Atg1 and Atg11), are involved in Ypt1 activation at the PAS34–36, although different conclusions have been reached regarding the role of Atg11 (refs. 35,36). These discrepancies are even more evident with respect to the involvement of TRAPPIII and/or Ypt1 in the association of other Atg proteins to the PAS20,24,28,35,36.In our study, we show that TRAPPIII, in coordination with Atg2, is a key determinant in the phagophore–ERES MCS formation and Ypt1 activation. Ypt1 is critical for the association of the PtdIns3P effectors Atg18 and Atg21 through the stimulation of local PtdIns3P synthesis. Given that Atg18 and Atg21 stimulate the activity of Atg2 and the two Ubl conjugation systems, our results suggest that Ypt1 coordinates the function of the Atg clusters, mediating lipid influx and phagophore elongation during autophagosome biogenesis.

As Atg8 is a late PAS marker protein3, we also examined the dynamic localization of Atg1–GFP and Atg14–GFP, which are part of the Atg1 kinase and PI3K complexes, respectively and considered early PAS marker proteins3. In most cases, Atg1-positive and Atg14-positive puncta also appeared next to the ER and the vacuole, and the number of puncta only proximal to the vacuole was higher at their disappearance (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b,c), confirming that the PAS associates with the ER as soon as it is formed and autophagosomes dissociate from the ER before fusing with vacuoles.

We also examined PAS formation in comparison to the ERES, visualized by endogenous GFP-tagging of the COPII subunit Sec23. Newly formed mCherry–Atg8-positive PAS occurred mainly between the ERES and vacuoles (Fig. 1c,d, Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Video 2), suggesting that the phagophore–ER MCSs are formed next to the ERES. Next, we focused on the Ubl conjugation systems, which mediate Atg8 covalent anchoring to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) in the phagophore1. Although the Ubl conjugation system components are recruited to the phagophore through their interactions with the Atg1 complex71, stimulation of Atg8 lipidation is mediated by the PtdIns3P effector Atg21, an Atg18 homolog18,71,72. We first analyzed Atg21–GFP association with the PAS and found it significantly reduced in atg2∆ and trs85Δ cells, but not in the atg18∆ knockout (Fig. 8b,c). We also examined PAS recruitment of Atg5–GFP and Atg16–GFP, which form a complex with Atg12 that, as an E3 ligase, guides Atg8 conjugation to PE1. Yeast Atg12–Atg5–Atg16 complex association with the PAS is modulated by Atg16 and Atg12 binding to Atg21 and the Atg1 kinase complex, respectively71. The N-terminal 56 amino acids of Atg12 mediate its interaction with the Atg1 kinase complex, and Atg12–Atg5–Atg16 complex is mainly recruited to the PAS by Atg21 in cells expressing the truncated Atg12NΔ56 variant71. Therefore, Atg5–GFP and Atg16–GFP localization to the PAS was investigated in strains expressing either Atg12 or Atg12NΔ56 (Extended Data Fig. 8). We observed that although TRS85 deletion strongly impaired it, ATG2 knockout slightly enhanced Atg5–GFP and Atg16–GFP recruitment to the PAS. The Atg12NΔ56 variant partially reduced the association of Atg16–GFP but not of Atg5–GFP in WT and atg2Δ cells (Extended Data Fig. 8). We concluded that TRAPPIII, but not Atg2, is critical for Ubl conjugation system recruitment to the PAS. The presence of the Ubl conjugation systems at the PAS does not mean that Atg8–PE is normally generated because even if it is not required for the Ubl conjugation system localization to the PAS71,73,74, Atg21 is still important for Atg8–PE generation18,72–74. We assessed Atg8–PE levels by quantifying mCherry–Atg8 signals in the experiment shown in Fig. 8b and observed that their intensity was strongly reduced when ATG2 or TRS85 was deleted but not in the atg18∆ knockout (Fig. 8d). This was not a result of PAS size reduction because no changes in mCherry–Atg8 puncta dimensions were measured (Fig. 8e). We reasoned that the constitutively active (GTP-bound) form of Ypt1 could bypass the Ubl conjugation system-associated defects of trs85∆ cells. To test this idea, we took advantage of the Ypt1Q67L mutant, which is defective in GTP hydrolysis75. Overexpression of this variant did not restore phagophore–ERES MCS formation (Extended Data Fig. 9a,b). By contrast, Ypt1Q67L restored PtdIns3P levels at the PAS in trs85∆ cells and thus Atg18 and Atg21 recruitment to this location (Extended Data Fig. 9c–h).

Our observations suggest that Ypt1 activation at the PAS regulates PtdIns3P synthesis, which in turn enhances the function of the Atg clusters mediating phagophore elongation.

DiscussionWe have uncovered a function of TRAPPIII in phagophore–ERES MCS formation whereby, together with Atg2, it regulates the timely activation of Ypt1 to modulate phagophore elongation. We spatiotemporally ordered and connected in vivo enzymatic activities that have separately been characterized in vitro. Phagophore–ERES MCSs have a central role in autophagosome biogenesis8. Although Atg9 and the Atg2–Atg18 complex are present on the phagophore extremity associated with the ER, ERES, as a subsection of the ER, are found at these MCSs2,4,9. Mostly based on in vitro reconstitution experiments10,37,38, it has been proposed that Atg2 proteins are the main factor tethering the phagophore to the ERES. However, in vivo investigations in yeast have shown that the PAS or phagophore association with the ER is unaffected in atg2∆ cells9. Moreover, live-cell imaging in mammalian cells has revealed that ATG2A mediates the firm docking of an early autophagosomal intermediate, probably to the ER, only after its formation39. These observations suggested the existence of additional factors mediating phagophore–ERES tethering. We have identified TRAPPIII as one of them. TRAPPIII prominently localizes to the edges of expanding phagophores (Fig. 1e,f). TRAPPIII binds Sec23, and this provides a possible mechanism for how TRAPPIII contributes to the phagophore–ERES MCS formation25,34,76. Atg2 may promote the establishment of the phagophore–ERES MCSs through a similar mechanism, as Atg2 directly or indirectly interacts with COPII components as well (Fig. 3)2. However, TRAPPIII and Atg2 do not depend on each other for recruitment to the PAS (Fig. 5a–d), but the interaction of Atg2 with the ERES requires functional TRAPPIII, and vice versa (Fig. 3). In agreement with previous studies20,28, we showed that Atg9 recruits TRAPPIII onto the phagophore (Fig. 5a,b). Moreover, the presence of Atg2 at the phagophore extremity and its interaction with Atg9, but not its membrane tethering and lipid transfer activities, are required for TRAPPIII’s GEF activity and thus the activation of Ypt1 at the phagophores (Fig. 6e,f). Interestingly, mammalian TRAPPC11 interacts with ATG2–WIPI4 in an ATG9A-dependent manner40, and ATG9A neighbors TRAPPIII77, suggesting that the functional association between these factors is conserved.The literature presents numerous contradictory results regarding TRAPPIII and Ypt1 function in Atg machinery assembly and regulation at the PAS, which is probably because of the different experimental conditions used20,24,28,34–36. We took advantage of the fact that ATG2 deletion specifically inhibits Ypt1 activation at the PAS (Fig. 6c,d) and systematically examined the PAS association of different Atg clusters to better understand Ypt1 function in autophagosome formation. Our results concur with and extend those obtained by fluorescence and biochemical analyses3 by showing that PAS recruitment of the Atg clusters mediating phagophore formation—including the Atg1 kinase and PI3K complexes and Atg9-positive vesicles—is not compromised in the absence of Atg2 or TRAPPIII (Fig. 7a,b, Extended Data Fig. 3a,b and Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). By contrast, Atg2 or TRAPPIII complex depletion impairs Atg18 and Atg21 recruitment (Figs. 5e,f and 8b,c). The PAS localization of these proteins requires the presence of PtdIns3P3,72–74,78. In vitro studies have revealed that mammalian RAB1A activates PI3K complex I68,69. Our results support this notion with in vivo data; that is, Atg2, TRAPPIII and, consequently, GTP-bound Ypt1 are required to enhance PtdIns3P levels at the PAS (Fig. 7a–d), which, in turn, are important for Atg18 and Atg21 recruitment. Our in vitro experiments suggest that higher levels of PtdIns3P also stimulate Atg2 lipid transfer activity directly and/or through an Atg18 switch (Fig. 8a and Extended Data Fig. 7a–c). Even when Ypt1 activation is impaired, there is some PtdIns3P at the PAS, which, possibly together with the binding to some components of Atg machinery, may allow the residual recruitment of Atg21 to this location. However, lower PtdIns3P levels are insufficient to support phagophore elongation. The data on the recruitment of Ubl conjugation system components are more complex to interpret because results differ depending on the analyzed protein and the deleted gene (Extended Data Fig. 8), possibly reflecting a Ypt1-independent interaction between the Ubl conjugation systems and TRAPPIII, which future investigations need to address. A very recent study has identified a third mechanism for Ubl conjugation system recruitment to the PAS, which involves the PKA-regulated interaction between Atg12 and the Atg1 kinase complex, highlighting that this step is far from being completely understood79. Nonetheless, the association of Atg8 with the PAS is severely impaired in both atg2∆ and trs85Δ mutants, inferring that even if present at the PAS, the activity of the two Ubl conjugation systems strongly depends on Atg21 recruitment, as previously suggested18,72–74,78. This result is also consistent with the notion that Atg2 transports the main PE pool required for Atg8 lipidation to the PAS18.Overall, our results support a model in which the phagophore–ERES MCSs and Ypt1 activation function as a regulatory hub that coordinates lipid influx into the phagophore with the autophagosome formation machinery (Fig. 8f). By increasing the PtdIns3P levels, Ypt1 triggers coordinated Atg18 and Atg21 recruitment, which activates the machineries involved in elongating the phagophore. Rab GTPases coordinate organelle functions and often their maturation by recruiting downstream factors other than tethering factors. Our findings suggest a similar scenario in autophagy, in which Atg9-positive vesicle docking to the ERES triggers the subsequent step of autophagosome biogenesis, particularly phagophore elongation, by targeting Ypt1 to the PAS or phagophore (Fig. 8f). The fact that Ypt1 distributes over the entire surface of elongating phagophores can explain how, upon recruitment, Ypt1 stimulates PI3K complex I, which localizes at the base of phagophores, where they make contact with the vacuole2,4. This distribution suggests that Ypt1 may regulate the activity and/or eventually the distribution over the phagophore of other Atg proteins, possibly explaining the detected interaction of Ypt1 with Atg1, Atg11, Atg17 and Atg23 (refs. 34–36). Protein detection and quantification of non-saturated images were performed using an Odyssey XF Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences) or ImageJ. Interaction of Atg2 with Atg9 in the co-immunoprecipitation experiments was quantified by dividing the intensity of Atg2 bands by that of Atg9 bands and expressing the obtained values relative to WT. Atg2–Atg18 binding in the APEX2-mediated proximity labeling experiment was calculated by quantifying the intensity of the corresponding bands divided by the intensity of biotinylation (anti-biotin immunoprecipitation blot). This ratio was then divided by the intensity of the corresponding input bands, previously normalized with the Pgk1 loading control, and expressed relative to the WT cells carrying Atg9–FLAG–APEX2 after biotin reaction. The percentage of Ape1 maturation was calculated by dividing the densitometric amount of mApe1 by that of total Ape1 (prApe1 + mApe1).
