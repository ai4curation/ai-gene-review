The primary cilium is a cellular organelle whose assembly and disassembly are
closely linked to the cell cycle. The centriole distal appendage (DA) is
essential for the early stages of ciliogenesis by anchoring the mother centriole
to the cell surface. Despite the identification of over twelve proteins
constituting the DA, including CEP83, CEP89, CEP164, FBF1, and SCLT1, their
specific functions in ciliary dynamics are not fully understood. Here, we
elucidate the precise role of DA proteins in ciliary assembly and disassembly.
While Cep89 mutant cells exhibit normal ciliogenesis, the kinetics of ciliary
disassembly is significantly delayed. Through siRNA-mediated knockdown of DA
proteins, we identified two functional subgroups within DA proteins: CEP83,
SCLT1, and CEP164, which are primarily essential for ciliary assembly and
centriole docking, and CEP89 and FBF1, which specifically regulate ciliary
disassembly. Notably, the depletion of CEP89 and FBF1 not only impedes ciliary
disassembly but also disrupts the Aurora A kinase signaling pathway, leading to
its downregulation and mislocalization at the basal body during serum-induced
cell cycle re-entry. These findings suggest that DA components can be
functionally categorized into two modules responsible for distinct aspects of
ciliary dynamics, with broad implications for cellular signaling, homeostasis,
and development.

Introduction

The primary cilia are microtubule-based organelles that function as membrane-encapsulated signaling hubs, arising from the basal body, a modified form of the centriole [3]. The centrosome, as the microtubule-organizing center, plays a crucial role in essential cellular processes, including mitosis, cell migration, and ciliogenesis. Throughout the cell cycle, centrioles perform distinct functions by organizing spindle poles during mitosis and transitioning into basal bodies to initiate cilia assembly during interphase [9]. The dynamic interplay between the centriole and basal body is tightly regulated, ensuring precise assembly and disassembly of primary cilia in synchrony with the cell cycle.

The assembly of primary cilia begins with the centriole maturation, marked by the hierarchical recruitment of appendage proteins [4, 21]. Subsequently, multiple steps of vesicle trafficking, docking, and centriole remodeling ensue. The basal body anchors to the plasma membrane and further extends microtubule doublets into the extracellular space, forming the primary cilium through the polymerization of alpha- and beta-tubulin heterodimers. During cell cycle progression, cilia disassembly is mediated by Aurora A kinase (AurA) [16, 17]. Upon activation, AurA phosphorylates histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6), leading to the destabilization and deacetylation of axonemal microtubules. This destabilization reduces the structural integrity of the microtubules that form the ciliary axoneme. Consequently, the axonemal microtubules undergo reabsorption, resulting in the disassembly of the primary cilium. This process ensures that the cell can proceed through the cell cycle unimpeded, as the presence of cilia is typically associated with a quiescent or differentiated state. Thus, the regulated disassembly of cilia is essential for normal cell cycle progression and cellular function.

During centriole maturation, distinct morphological changes occur at the distal ends of centrioles, where the vicinity of the triple microtubule transitions to a doublets. Two appendage structures form to distinguish mother centriole from daughter centrioles. Subdistal appendages play a role in anchoring microtubules (De Brabander et al., 1982; Piel et al., 2000), whereas distal appendages are critical for the recruitment of basal bodies to the membrane during ciliary assembly. The centriole distal appendages (DA) are attached to the mother centriole, acting as a specialized subcompartment essential for docking the basal body to the plasma membrane during ciliogenesis [22]. The composition of the DA was initially revealed by quantitative centrosome proteomics, identifying key components including CEP83, CEP89, CEP164, FBF1, and SCLT1 [21]. Knockdown studies in cultured cells and mouse models show that CEP83, CEP164, and SCLT1 are essential for ciliogenesis and basal body docking [2, 11, 12, 19], whereas hypomorphic FBF1 mouse model displays normal ciliogenesis, suggesting distinct roles for DA proteins in ciliary dynamics [24].

Here, we examined the specific functions of DA proteins in regulating ciliary dynamics. siRNA-mediated knockdown in cultured cells revealed gene-specific regulatory effects on ciliary assembly and disassembly. Specifically, CEP83, SCLT1, and CEP164 are essential for ciliary assembly by facilitating proper basal body docking. In contrast, CEP89 and FBF1 are crucial for disassembly, as their depletion delays this process, possibly through downregulation and mislocalization of AurA kinase. Our findings demonstrate the specialized roles of DA proteins in ciliary assembly and disassembly, with a particular link between CEP89/FBF1 and Aurora A kinase regulation in disassembly. This novel framework enhances our understanding of the molecular mechanism underlying ciliary homeostasis and has broader implications for the related cellular processes and diseases.

Results and discussionCEP89 is dispensable for ciliary assembly but essential for disassemblyTo further delineate the role of DA proteins in primary cilia homeostasis, we generated Cep89em1Dkl/KMPC (Cep89Mut) mice with a targeted deletion of exon 4 via CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome engineering as part of the Korean Mouse Phenotyping Consortium (KMPC) (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). These mice were born at expected Mendelian ratios and exhibited normal development. This implies that the absence of CEP89 does not affect cilia formation, which is important for transducing proper developmental signaling such as Hedgehog and Wnt. Given the DA’s established role in docking the basal body to the plasma membrane during cilogenesis, we analyzed cilia abundance in various tissues of Cep89Mut embryos at embryonic day 12.5 (E12.5) (Supplementary Fig. 1C). We observed a normal presence of cilia in epithelial cells of the neural tube and intestine, as well as in mesenchymal cells of the limb buds, comparable to that of wild-type littermates. Moreover, our data show that ciliary length in Cep89Mut MEFs (Fig. 1C) and mesenchymal cells from limb buds (Supplementary Fig. 2) is comparable to that of wild-type controls, indicating that CEP89 deficiency does not affect ciliary assembly or length in these cell types.

We next examined the effect of loss of Cep89 on ciliary assembly and disassembly dynamics in cultured mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells. Following 24 h of serum starvation to induce ciliogenesis, Cep89 mutant MEF cells displayed ciliogenesis levels comparable to that in control cells (Fig. 1A-C). However, upon adding serum to trigger cell cycle re-entry and ciliary disassembly, wild-type MEFs displayed a time-dependent decrease in ciliated cells, whereas the Cep89 mutant demonstrated a significant delay in this process (Fig. 1D-E). These results suggest that Cep89 deficiency selectively impairs ciliary disassembly without affecting assembly, highlighting its unique role in cilia homeostasis.The role of CEP89 in ciliary disassembly contrasts with its established function in ciliary assembly, as previously reported [20]. Their work demonstrated that CEP89 is critical for the assembly of primary cilia in RPE1 cells, specifically by promoting the formation of the ciliary vesicle at the distal appendages of the mother centriole. CEP89 and FBF1 regulate the Aurora A kinase level and basal body localization to promote ciliary disassemblyAurora A (AurA) is a well-known kinase that regulates M phase entry and cell cycle progression [13]. It also localizes to the basal body and plays a key role in ciliary disassembly by stimulating HDAC6-dependent tubulin deacetylation [16, 17]. To investigate whether AurA is affected by Cep89 or Fbf1 depletion, we treated NIH3T3 cells with siRNA specific for Cep89 or Fbf1 and induced ciliogenesis by serum starvation. Ciliary disassembly was then stimulated by adding serum, and cell extracts were collected at 0, 8, and 12 h after serum addition. Western blot analysis was performed to detect total AurA protein levels using an AurA-specific antibody. Interestingly, the knockdown of Cep89 or Fbf1 resulted in a significantly diminished AurA protein level compared to control cells (Fig. 4A-B). This reduction is consistent with previous reports that AurA levels fluctuate throughout the cell cycle. AurA expression is regulated at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels, with ubiquitin-proteasome-dependent degradation mediated by anaphase-promoting complex during mitosis, typically reaching its lowest level at the G1 phase [1, 8]. Our findings showed that AurA levels were lower at the 8-hour time point and increased after 12 h, which correlates with our cell cycle analysis (Fig. 3C). Specifically, the cell cycle progressed after serum addition, resulting in an increased S phase at 12 h. These results suggest that the reduced AurA kinase levels in Cep89 or Fbf1 knockdown cells might be due to an altered phase of the cell cycle. Also, it has been known that the knockdown of AurA induces cilia-dependent G0/G1 arrest in RPE1 cells [5]. Therefore, there are two possible mechanisms by which CEP89 and FBF1 might regulate AurA kinase levels. First, CEP89 and FBF1 might influence cell cycle progression, indirectly affecting AurA stability. Second, CEP89 and FBF1 could be directly involved in regulating AurA stability through mechanisms independent of cell cycle progression. To test this hypothesis, we stained for PCNA, a marker of the S phase, during ciliary disassembly. Co-staining for PCNA and cilia during serum stimulation, which induces cell cycle progression and cilia resorption, revealed a decrease in the number of PCNA-positive cells in Cep89 and Fbf1-deficient cells compared to control cells (Fig. 4C-D). Consistently with previous study, Cep89 and Fbf1 are involved in delaying cell cycle progression [23]. Furthermore, analysis of PCNA-positive cells with cilia showed a substantial increase in Cep89 and Fbf1-deficient cells compared to controls at all time points up to 12 h post-serum addition. This finding confirms that Cep89 and Fbf1 not only cause a modest delay in cell cycle progression but also independently delay ciliary disassembly.

Specifically, CEP89 and FBF1 contribute to ciliary disassembly by regulating Aurora A kinase stability and basal body localization

AurA is known to localize to the basal body, where it activates HDAC6 to promote ciliary tubulin deacetylation [17]. To examine the localization of AurA in siRNA-depleted cells, we performed immunofluorescent staining for AurA with gamma-tubulin antibody (Fig. 4E-F). Despite AurA kinase frequently localizing at the basal bodies, CEP89 or FBF1 knockdown cells exhibited significantly reduced AurA localization at basal bodies. Additionally, we confirmed that CEP89 regulates AurA in Cep89MUT MEFs (Supplementary Fig. 5A–D). These results suggest that CEP89 and FBF1 are crucial for maintaining AurA proteostasis and proper centriole localization.

In conclusion, our data demonstrate that CEP89 and FBF1 regulate the protein levels and basal body localization of AurA kinase, which is essential for promoting ciliary disassembly. Depletion of Cep89 or Fbf1 leads to reduced AurA levels and centriole localization, resulting in delayed ciliary disassembly and an altered cell cycle profile. These findings highlight the distinct roles of CEP89 and FBF1 in ciliary dynamics through regulating AurA kinase.

Our study elucidates the distinct roles of centriole distal appendage proteins in ciliary assembly and disassembly, with a particular focus on CEP89 and FBF1. While CEP89 and FBF1 are not essential for ciliary assembly, we demonstrate that they play crucial roles in ciliary disassembly. Knockdown of these proteins leads to delayed ciliary disassembly, likely through the downregulation and mislocalization of AurA kinase, a key regulator of ciliary disassembly. This delayed disassembly further results in altered cell cycle progression, emphasizing the importance of these DA proteins in cell cycle regulation. Our findings categorize DA components into two functional modules, with CEP83, SCLT1, and CEP164 being critical for ciliary assembly, and CEP89 and FBF1 specifically regulating disassembly (Fig. 4E). This functional distinction enhances our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying ciliary dynamics and their broader implications for cellular homeostasis and development.
