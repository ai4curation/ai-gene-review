## Full Text

Abstract

The highly conserved RCN family of proteins regulates the serine/threonine protein phosphatase calcineurin, which is required for the expression of genes involved in Ca 2+ -dependent processes, such as the control of memory, apoptosis, T cell activation, cell cycle, Ca 2+ -homeostasis, and skeletal and cardiac muscle growth and differentiation. However, RCNs regulate calcineurin through two paradoxical actions: they act as feedback inhibitors of calcineurin, whereas their phosphorylation stimulates calcineurin. Here we show that phosphorylation of yeast RCN, Rcn1, triggers degradation through the SCF Cdc4 ubiquitin ligase complex. Degradation of phosphorylated Rcn1 is required to mitigate inhibition of calcineurin by Rcn1 and results in activation of calcineurin activity in response to Ca 2+ as well as in reactivation of calcineurin in response to changes in Ca 2+ concentration. The SCF Cdc4 -dependent degradation required phosphorylation of Rcn1 by Mck1, a member of the GSK3 family of protein kinases, and was promoted by Ca 2+ . However, such degradation was counteracted by dephosphorylation of Rcn1, which was promoted by Ca 2+ -stimulated calcineurin. Thus, calcineurin activity is fine-tuned to Ca 2+ signals by mechanisms that have opposite functions. Our results identify the molecular mechanism of Rcn1 phosphorylation-induced stimulation of the phosphatase activity of calcineurin. The results provide insight into the mechanism involved in maintaining proper responses to Ca 2+ signals.

Keywords: calcium signaling, DSCR1/MCIP, feedback inhibitor, proteolysis

Calcineurin is a highly conserved Ca 2+ -dependent protein phosphatase that couples Ca 2+ signals to cellular responses, such as T cell activation, memory formation, apoptosis in neurons, and skeletal and cardiac muscle growth and differentiation ( 1 ). Precise regulation of calcineurin is important for a proper response to Ca 2+ signals. Aberrant calcineurin activity results in various disorders, such as osteoporosis ( 2 ), cardiac hypertrophy ( 3 , 4 ) and Down's syndrome ( 5 , 6 ). The mechanism responsible for activation of calcineurin in response to Ca 2+ signals appears to be conserved from human to yeast. Calcineurin is activated by Ca 2+ through calmodulin. Activated calcineurin then dephosphorylates substrate proteins. The well characterized substrates of calcineurin are members of the nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) family of transcription factors ( 7 ). Calcineurin dephosphorylates NFAT, leading to its nuclear translocation and subsequent activation of its target genes required for Ca 2+ -dependent processes. A prolonged Ca 2+ signal and the translocation of NFAT ensure sustained activation of gene expression. In yeast, calcineurin dephosphorylates the transcription factor Crz1/Tcn1 ( 8 , 9 ), thereby activating gene expressions required for Ca 2+ -dependent processes, such as Ca 2+ homeostasis, mating, sporulation, adaptation to high salt stress, and cell cycle progression from G 2 to M phase ( 1 , 10 ).

Calcineurin signaling is inhibited by an autoregulatory negative-feedback loop. Endogenous inhibitors known as the RCN family of proteins, whose expression depends on calcineurin, have been isolated ( 5 , 11 , 12 ). DSCR1 (Down's syndrome critical region 1) or MCIP (modulatory calcineurin-interacting protein), encoded in chromosome trisomy 21 in human, is highly expressed in the brain of Down's syndrome and is known to inhibit calcineurin through direct binding with the catalytic subunit of calcineurin ( 5 , 13 ). DSCR1/MCIP is also important in protection against cardiac hypertrophy ( 4 , 14 ), oxidative stress ( 15 ), and neurofibrillary tangle formation associated with Alzheimer's disease ( 16 ). Similarly, yeast Rcn1, a homolog of DSCR1/MCIP, inhibits calcineurin-dependent responses when overexpressed ( 11 ). However, RCNs regulate calcineurin through two paradoxical mechanisms ( 17 ): ( i ) inhibition of calcineurin signaling through an autoregulatory feedback mechanisms and ( ii ) stimulation of calcineurin after phosphorylation of RCNs by the GSK3 family of protein kinases.

Here we describe that phosphorylated Rcn1 is targeted for destruction by the SCF Cdc4 (Skp1, Cullin/Cdc53, F-box protein Cdc4) complex, which is one of the ubiquitin ligases required for ubiquitination of substrate proteins ( 18 – 20 ). The F-box protein Cdc4 recognizes phosphorylated substrates through its substrate-binding domain, and recruits it to the core SCF complex through the F-box motif ( 21 ). We therefore hypothesized that degradation of phosphorylated Rcn1 relieves the inhibition of calcineurin, thereby stimulating calcineurin. The results indicated that degradation of Rcn1 is required not only for stimulation of calcineurin after the addition of Ca 2+ but also for reactivation of calcineurin for changes in concentration of Ca 2+ . Degradation of Rcn1 is stimulated by the protein kinase Mck1 in a calcineurin-independent manner but is counteracted by calcineurin-dependent dephosphorylation, both of which are stimulated by Ca 2+ . Thus, we propose that the integrated control of the synthesis and degradation provides Rcn1 with the ability to fine-tune Ca 2+ signals.

Results

Isolation of Rcn1 as a Cdc4-Intercating Protein.

To identify substrates of the SCF Cdc4 complex, we used the two-hybrid method to identify proteins that interact with Cdc4, a substrate-recognition molecule in the SCF Cdc4 complex. To impede degradation of substrates during screening, the two-hybrid system was designed to stabilize substrates during the screening. Briefly, we used cdc4-1 temperature-sensitive mutants that are deficient in ubiquitin-dependent degradation of substrates as host cells ( 22 ), and a fragment of Cdc4 (Cdc4-dF) lacking the F-box motif but carrying the intact substrate-recognition domain was used as bait; the expression of reporter LacZ was monitored at the restriction temperature for the mutants ( Fig. 1 A ). We screened a yeast two-hybrid library and isolated Rcn1 ( 11 ) that showed a specific interaction with Cdc4 ( Fig. 1 A ), suggesting that Rcn1 is a substrate of the SCF Cdc4 complex.

Fig. 1.

Rcn1 Is Degraded Through the SCF Cdc4 Complex and Proteasome.

We first examined whether Rcn1 is degraded through the SCF Cdc4 complex by pulse–chase experiments [supporting information (SI) Materials and Methods ]. We expressed the HA-tagged Rcn1 (Rcn1-HA) from its endogenous promoter by the addition of 100 mM CaCl 2 for 15 min, followed by the addition of cycloheximide to inhibit protein synthesis, and determined the Rcn1-HA protein level by Western blot analysis ( Fig. 1 B ). Rcn1-HA was unstable in wild-type (t ½ ≈ 10 min) but was stabilized in cdc4-1 mutants. In contrast, a rapid degradation of Rcn1-HA was observed in grr1 Δ mutants defective in another F-box protein, GRR1 ( 18 , 20 , 23 ). These data demonstrate that Rcn1 is degraded after its expression and that Cdc4 is required for the degradation of Rcn1.

We next examined the involvement of proteasome in the degradation of Rcn1. Similar pulse–chase experiments indicated that Rcn1-HA was stabilized in pre1-1 mutants defective in one of the subunits of the 20S proteasome ( Fig. 1 B ). Consistently, degradation of Rcn1-HA was inhibited by the addition of MG132 ( Fig. 1 B ) ( 24 ), a proteasome inhibitor, indicating that Rcn1-HA was degraded by the proteasome. Taken together, these results indicate that Rcn1p is degraded through the SCF Cdc4 complex and proteasome.

The protein level of Rcn1-HA 15 min after the addition of CaCl 2 was decreased in cdc4-1 and pre1-1 mutants compared with that in wild-type cells ( SI Fig. 8 ), suggesting that Cdc4 and Pre1 are involved in the expression of Rcn1.

Phosphorylation of S113 Is Required for the Degradation of Rcn1.

The above results prompted us to further investigate the regulatory mechanism of Rcn1. Degradation through the SCF complexes is often triggered by phosphorylation of substrates ( 20 ). Rcn1 is reported to be phosphorylated at S113 by Mck1, which requires priming phosphorylation of S117 ( 17 ). We therefore constructed the serine-to-alanine mutant forms of Rcn1-HA (Rcn1-S113A-HA, Rcn1-S117A-HA, and Rcn1-S113/117A-HA) and monitored their degradation. Pulse–label experiments of Rcn1-S113A-HA expressed under the control of its own promoter revealed its stabilization ( Fig. 2 ). Consistently, Rcn1-HA was stabilized in mck1 Δ mutants, a deletion allele of MCK1 ( Fig. 2 ). As expected, Rcn1-S113/117A, as well as Rcn1-S117A, which is deficient in phosphorylation of S113, was also stabilized ( Fig. 2 ). The three mutant forms of Rcn1 lost interaction with Cdc4 in the two-hybrid context ( Fig. 1 A ). These data indicate that phosphorylation of S113 is required for degradation of Rcn1 through the SCF Cdc4 complex. Phosphorylation-dependent ubiquitination of Rcn1 was confirmed in vivo ( SI Fig. 9 ).

Fig. 2.

S113 Phosphorylation and Subsequent Degradation of Rcn1 Is Enhanced in Response to Ca 2+ .

We next explored the regulatory mechanisms of S113 phosphorylation. First, we used the mobility-shift assay to examine the phosphorylation state of Rcn1. We expressed Rcn1-HA or mutant forms of Rcn1-HA under the control of galactose-inducible GAL1 promoter in cdc4-1 mutants (to inhibit degradation of phosphorylated Rcn1-HA). We added FK506 ( 1 , 25 , 26 ) to inhibit possible dephosphorylation of Rcn1 by calcineurin. S113 was weakly phosphorylated in the absence of Ca 2+ ( Fig. 3 A ). However, the addition of CaCl 2 accelerated S113 phosphorylation: The Ca 2+ -dependent mobility shift of Rcn1-HA, which was due to phosphorylation, was observed 5 min after the addition of CaCl 2 ( Fig. 3 A and B ); Rcn1-S113A-HA did not show such mobility shift ( Fig. 3 A ). To examine the effect of Mck1 on phosphorylation of Rcn1, we introduced the mck1 Δ mutation into cdc4-1 mutants. Phosphorylation of S113 was not observed in cdc4-1 mck1 Δ double mutants ( Fig. 3 A ), indicating that Mck1 induces phosphorylation of S113. De novo protein synthesis was not required for the phosphorylation of S113 ( SI Fig. 10 ). These results indicate that Ca 2+ stimulates Mck1-dependent phosphorylation of S113 in a calcineurin-independent manner. In contrast, phosphorylation of S117 was apparent even in the absence of Ca 2+ ( Fig. 3 A ).

Fig. 3.

To confirm this conclusion, we examined whether Ca 2+ promotes the degradation of Rcn1. Pulse–chase experiments of Rcn1-HA expressed under the control of GAL1 promoter were performed with or without the addition of CaCl 2 ( Fig. 3 C ). The half-life of Rcn1-HA in the absence of Ca 2+ was 20–30 min. In contrast, the addition of CaCl 2 restored the degradation of Rcn1-HA (t ½ ≈ 10 min), with a kinetics similar to that observed by the pulse–chase experiments of Rcn1-HA expressed under the control of Ca 2+ -regulated RCN1 promoter ( Fig. 1 B ). Ca 2+ -stimulated degradation of Rcn1 required Mck1, Cdc4, and Pre1 ( Fig. 3 C ). Taken together, these results indicate enhanced degradation of Rcn1 through the SCF Cdc4 complex and proteasome in response to Ca 2+ . The results also indicate that Ca 2+ signaling does not only facilitate the expression of RCN1 but also the degradation of Rcn1.

Calcineurin Counteracts Phosphorylation and Degradation of Rcn1.

Phosphorylation of Rcn1 was barely detectable in the absence of FK506 (data not shown), suggesting that calcineurin dephosphorylates Rcn1. In addition, FK506 is shown to stabilize Rcn1 ( 11 ), suggesting that calcineurin-dependent dephosphorylation is involved in stabilization of Rcn1. To evaluate the contribution of calcineurin to the stability of Rcn1, we assessed the phosphorylation of S113 and the degradation of Rcn1 in cnb1 Δ cells (a deletion allele of CNB1 coding for the regulatory subunit of calcineurin and defective in calcineurin). Interestingly, phosphorylation of S113 became apparent in cdc4-1 cnb1 Δ double mutants even in the absence of Ca 2+ ( Fig. 4 A ), indicating that calcineurin could counteract Rcn1 phosphorylation even without the addition of Ca 2+ . Furthermore, degradation of Rcn1 was observed in cnb1 Δ mutants in the absence of Ca 2+ ( Fig. 4 B ). Such degradation was inhibited in cdc4-1 cnb1 Δ double mutants ( Fig. 4 B ). These results suggest that calcineurin counteracts the Mck1-induced phosphorylation of Rcn1, thereby inhibiting degradation of Rcn1 through the SCF Cdc4 complex. Taken together, Ca 2+ signals can regulate the protein level of Rcn1 through two opposite pathways, Mck1-dependent phosphorylation and calcineurin-dependent dephosphorylation of Rcn1.

Fig. 4.

SCF Cdc4 Complex-Dependent Degradation of Phosphorylated Rcn1 Stimulates Calcineurin After the Addition of Ca 2+ .

Given the fact that phosphorylated Rcn1 is degraded through the SCF Cdc4 complex and proteasome, we examined the role of the SCF Cdc4 complex and proteasome in calcineurin signaling. To analyze whether the SCF Cdc4 complex and proteasome are required for stimulation of calcineurin activity, we monitored the calcineurin-dependent transcription of RCN1 and PMC1 ( 9 , 10 ) after the addition of Ca 2+ . As shown in Fig. 5 A , the levels of both mRNAs were markedly decreased in cdc4-1 -, cdc53-1 - (a thermo-sensitive allele of CDC53 encoding one of the subunits of the SCF Cdc4 complex), and pre1-1 -defective mutants at their restriction temperatures ( Fig. 5 A ). To further investigate whether the enhanced activity of calcineurin is the result of Rcn1 degradation, we evaluated the effect of the stabilized form of Rcn1 (Rcn1-S113A-HA) on calcineurin signaling. Expression of Rcn1-S113A-HA decreased calcineurin-dependent transcription ( Fig. 5 B ), suggesting that degradation of S113-phosphorylated Rcn1 through the SCF Cdc4 complex and proteasome could allow stimulation of calcineurin. However, it is also possible that S113-phosphorylated Rcn1 itself may stimulate calcineurin. To examine which is the case, we analyzed calcineurin-dependent transcription in cdc4-1 mutants expressing Rcn1-S113A: Should phosphorylation of S113 and Cdc4-dependent degradation regulate calcineurin independently, the expression of Rcn1-S113A would markedly decrease calcineurin-dependent transcription in cdc4-1 mutants. However, expression of Rcn1-S113-HA did not substantially decrease calcineurin-dependent transcription in cdc4-1 mutants compared with a marked decrease in wild-type cells ( Fig. 5 C ), indicating that CDC4 is required for S113-phosphorylated Rcn1 to stimulate calcineurin. Consistently, Mck1, which phosphorylates S113, and Cdc4 functioned in the same regulatory pathway for calcineurin signaling ( Fig. 5 D ). The levels of calcineurin-dependent transcripts in cdc4-1 mutants were similar to those in mck1 Δ mutants. The introduction of cdc4-1 mutation into mck1 Δ mutants failed to produce a further reduction in calcineurin-dependent transcription in mck1 Δ mutants. Considered together, these results strongly suggest that degradation of phosphorylated Rcn1 through the SCF Cdc4 complex consequently stimulates calcineurin.

Fig. 5.

Degradation of Rcn1 Is Required for Reactivation of Calcineurin.

We next analyzed the physiological role of Rcn1 degradation after the initial activation of calcineurin. We hypothesized that degradation of Rcn1 is required for reactivation of calcineurin for changes in the concentration of Ca 2+ . To test this hypothesis, we added CaCl 2 again at various time intervals after the first treatment in rcn1 Δ cells expressing RCN1-HA under the control of its own promoter. No significant up-regulation of calcineurin-dependent transcripts of RCN1 and PMC1 was observed when CaCl 2 was readded 30 min after the initial addition ( Fig. 6 B ), when the amount of Rcn1-HA reached its maximal level ( Fig. 6 A ). In contrast, reactivation was evident when CaCl 2 was readded 120 min after the initial treatment ( Fig. 6 B ), at which time the Rcn1-HA protein level was significantly low ( Fig. 6 A ). This reactivation was not observed in rcn1 Δ mutants expressing the stabilized Rcn1-S113/117A-HA under the control of its own promoter ( Fig. 6 B ). Neither cdc4-1 mutants nor mck1 Δ mutants reactivated calcineurin-dependent transcription ( Fig. 6 B ). In these cells, initial Ca 2+ -treatment induced attenuated spikes of Rcn1-HA or Rcn1-S113/117A-HA, with the peak expression lower than that in rcn1 Δ mutants expressing Rcn1-HA, but the level remained high at 120 min when the CaCl 2 was readded ( Fig. 6 A ). These results suggest that the SCF Cdc4 complex-dependent degradation of Rcn1 is required for reactivation of calcineurin.

Discussion

RCNs act as feedback inhibitors of calcineurin. However, loss-of-function analyses of RCNs in various organisms suggest that RCNs may also function to facilitate calcineurin activity under certain conditions. For example, rcn1 Δ mutants showed reduced calcineurin activity ( 11 ). In Cryptococcus neoformans , cbp1 deletion mutants, defective in RCN1 homolog, also showed reduced calcineurin activity ( 27 ). Furthermore, DSCR1/MCIP1 −/− mice exhibited an impaired cardiac hypertrophic response to pressure overload ( 14 ). As Olson and colleagues ( 14 ) pointed out, although RCNs may stimulate calcineurin under certain conditions, calcineurin bound by RCNs is inactive ( 5 , 11 ). Therefore, a mechanism to relieve inhibition of RCNs should exist ( 14 ). Our results provide evidence of one such mechanism to relieve inhibition of calcineurin by Rcn1.

Rcn1 acts as a feedback inhibitor of calcineurin signaling in yeast ( 11 ). Therefore, precise regulation of calcineurin is accomplished by precise regulation of the Rcn1 protein level. To date, the expression of RCN1 is considered to be regulated by the transcription level ( 11 ). Here we report a mechanism that regulates the protein level of Rcn1 by showing that phosphorylated Rcn1 is degraded by the SCF Cdc4 complex and proteasome. Cells defective in the Rcn1 degradation system could account for disorders in calcineurin signaling as demonstrated by the following: ( i ) decreased transcription of RCN1 and PMC1 ( Fig. 5 ), ( ii ) decreased synthesis of Rcn1 ( SI Fig. 8 ), and ( iii ) deficiency in the reactivation of calcineurin ( Fig. 6 ).

Cunningham and colleagues ( 17 ) reported the importance of Mck1-induced phosphorylation of S113 for calcineurin stimulation. The authors suggested that the phosphorylated Rcn1 facilitates calcineurin, although the precise mechanism was not elucidated. Our data clearly show that Mck1-dependent phosphorylation targets Rcn1 for degradation through the SCF Cdc4 complex and releases the inhibition of calcineurin by Rcn1, resulting in stimulation of calcineurin. First, phosphorylation of S113 triggered degradation of Rcn1 ( Fig. 2 ). Second, the SCF Cdc4 complex and proteasome were required for stimulation of calcineurin ( Fig. 5 A and SI Fig. 8 ). Third, S113-phosphorylated Rcn1 required the function of Cdc4 to stimulate calcineurin ( Fig. 5 C ). Fourth, Cdc4 and Mck1 functioned in parallel for the regulation of calcineurin ( Fig. 5 D ).

These observations further suggest that phosphorylated S113 constitutes a phosphodegron that is recognized by Cdc4 ( 28 , 29 ). Phosphorylated S117 may also be a part of the phosphodegron, because phosphorylation of both S113 and S117 is required for ( i ) the degradation of Rcn1 ( Fig. 2 ) and ( ii ) the Cdc4-Rcn1 interaction in the two-hybrid system ( Fig. 1 A ). The FLIpS 113 PPApS 117 PPP sequence in Rcn1 is very similar to the phosphodegron in the c-jun oncoprotein, GEpT 239 PPLpS 243 P, in which both phosphates are required for recognition by Fbw7 ( 30 ), the human homolog of Cdc4. The S117 kinase that primes Mck1-dependent phosphorylation of S113 remains unknown. The proline-directed kinase, such as cyclin-dependent kinases or MAPKs, is likely to be involved in S117 phosphorylation.

We demonstrated that phosphorylation and therefore degradation of Rcn1 are counteracted by calcineurin ( Fig. 4 ). We, however, did not show that calcineurin directly dephosphorylates Rcn1. Vega et al. ( 31 ) demonstrated that calcineurin dephosphorylates MCIP/DSCR1 (mammalian RCN1 homolog) in vitro and that the GSK3 (mammalian Mck1 homolog) site, but not the priming site, is dephsophorylated. This evidence clearly support our conclusion that Rcn1 is dephosphorylated by calcineurin. Furthermore, it is most likely that calcineurin dephosphorylates phosphorylated S113 rather than that of S117.

Our model for the regulation of calcineurin signaling by Rcn1 is illustrated in Fig. 7 . First, Ca 2+ induces expression of RCN1 mRNA through activation of the Crz1/Tcn1 transcription factor ( 8 , 9 ) by calcineurin. Second, Ca 2+ /calcineurin promotes Rcn1 stabilization through likely calcineurin-mediated dephosphorylation of S113. Together, these two calcineurin-dependent mechanisms contribute an autoregulatory negative-feedback loop. Third, Ca 2+ acts to promote Rcn1 phosphorylation and degradation in a calcineurin-independent manner, a mechanism that promotes calcineurin signaling. Integration of these regulatory mechanisms would contribute to attain proper calcineurin activity. To understand these dual and seemingly opposite effects of Ca 2+ , a more quantitative understanding of the Ca 2+ dependence and/or timing of Ca 2+ signals is required.

Fig. 7.

The molecular mechanism that explains how Ca 2+ stimulates Mck1 function is unknown. Transcription of MCK1 is not up-regulated by Ca 2+ ( 17 , 32 ). Consistently, we observed that stimulation of Mck1 by Ca 2+ did not require de novo protein synthesis ( SI Fig. 10 ), suggesting that posttranslational modification of Mck1 stimulates its activity. Human Gsk3 kinases are inhibited by phosphorylation of S9, and their dephosphorylation stimulates kinase activities ( 33 ). However, Mck1 does not contain the serine residue that corresponds to S9 in Gsk3β ( 17 ).

Overproduction of feedback inhibitors is often observed in human disorders. For example, a number of tumors contain amplified or overexpressed Mdm2 ( 34 ), the feedback inhibitor of the p53 pathway ( 35 ). Degradation of Mdm2 can stimulate the p53 pathway ( 36 ). In addition, inhibitory Smads, feedback inhibitors of TGF-β signaling, are overexpressed in some types of tumors ( 37 , 38 ) and are degraded by ubiquitination ( 39 ). Therefore, degradation of feedback inhibitors might be a fundamental strategy in various processes that control cell signals.

Materials and Methods

Yeast Strain and Growth Conditions.

A complete list of yeast strains used in this study is provided in SI Table 1 . The strains were constructed and grown in accordance with standard protocols ( 40 ). Cycloheximide (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) was added to cultures at a final concentration of 50 μg/ml. FK506 (Fujisawa Pharma, Osaka, Japan) was added to the cultures at a final concentration of 0.75 μg/ml. MG132 (Peptide Institute, Osaka, Japan) was added at a final concentration of 50 μM.

Detection of Phosphorylated Rcn1.

cdc4-1 mutants harboring pGAL- RCN1-HA , pGAL- rcn1-S113A-HA , pGAL- rcn1-S117A-HA , or pGAL- rcn1-S113 / 117A-HA were grown to exponential phase in yeast extract/peptone/raffinose (YPR) medium at 30°C. Galactose was added, and the culture was further incubated at 30°C for 1 h, followed by further incubation at 37°C for 1 h. The culture was then treated with or without 100 mM CaCl 2 . The culture was treated simultaneously with FK506 to inhibit dephosphorylation of Rcn1 by calcineurin and was further incubated for 5 min before harvesting cells. Cells were lysed and immunoblotted with anti-HA (12CA5) antibody.
