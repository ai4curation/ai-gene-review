Single-strand breaks (SSBs) are one of the most commonly occurring endogenous
lesions with the potential to give rise to cytotoxic double-strand breaks (DSBs)
during DNA replication. To investigate how replication-dependent DSBs are
repaired, we employed Cas9 nickase (nCas9) to generate site- and strand-specific
nicks in the budding yeast genome. We found that nCas9-induced nicks are
converted to mostly double-ended DSBs during S phase. Repair of
replication-associated DSBs requires homologous recombination (HR) and is
independent of classical non-homologous end joining. Consistent with a strong
bias to repair these lesions using a sister-chromatid template, we observed
minimal induction of inter-chromosomal HR by nCas9. In a genome-wide screen to
identify factors necessary for the repair of replication-dependent DSBs, we
recovered components of the replication-coupled nucleosome assembly (RCNA)
pathway. Our findings suggest that the RCNA pathway is especially important to
repair DSBs arising from nicks in the leading-strand template through
acetylation of histone H3K56.

INTRODUCTION

Cells must faithfully replicate the genome and repair DNA damage in each cell cycle to maintain genome integrity. It is estimated that tens of thousands of single-strand breaks (SSBs or nicks) occur each day in human cells, making them one of the most common endogenous lesions.1,2 These lesions can arise from oxidative damage and as transient intermediates in DNA metabolic processes, such as topoisomerase I cleavage or base excision repair. SSBs have the potential to give rise to DSBs during replication by a mechanism that involves fork collapse. Several chemotherapeutic agents stabilize nicked DNA and therefore potentiate DSB formation. Unidirectional fork collapse at an SSB is proposed to result in a single-ended double strand break (seDSB) that is subsequently repaired by homologous recombination (HR) with the intact sister chromatid.3–6 Thus, investigating how SSBs are repaired has the potential to yield insight into the mechanisms of sister-chromatid recombination, which cannot be easily studied with endonucleases that cleave both sister chromatids.

Homology-directed repair of replication-independent DSBs initiates by the nucleolytic degradation of DSB ends to generate single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) overhangs in a process termed end resection.7 The Mre11-Rad50-Xrs2/NBS1 (MRX in budding yeast, MRN in human) complex and Sae2/CtIP catalyze the initial step of end resection by nicking internal to the 5′ end followed by chew back to the DSB end.7 In a second step, referred to as long-range resection, Exo1 and the Sgs1/BLM-Top3-Rmi1 (S/BTR)-Dna2 ensemble act redundantly to extend the tracts of ssDNA. Rad51 binds to the resulting 3′-overhangs and mediates homology search and strand invasion into a repair template, forming a displacement loop (D-loop).8 After extension by DNA polymerase δ, the extended invading end can be displaced by a DNA helicase for repair by synthesis-dependent strand annealing (SDSA). Alternatively, the D-loop is captured by the other break end to form a double-Holliday junction (dHJ). Dissolution of the dHJ intermediate by S/BTR generates non-crossover products, whereas cleavage by HJ resolvases has the potential to generate crossovers.8 In the absence of a second end for capture, extensive DNA synthesis occurs in the context of the D-loop by break-induced replication (BIR).9 BIR is highly mutagenic and requires the Pol32/POLD3 subunit of the DNA polymerase δ complex.10,11

Studies of replication-dependent DSBs have utilized the genotoxic agent, camptothecin (CPT), or enzymes that cleave one strand of DNA in a site-specific manner. CPT creates stabilized protein-bound SSBs by trapping Top1 cleavage complexes, and therefore potentiates replication-associated DSBs.12 To study this type of DNA damage in a site-specific manner, others have developed the Flp-nick system to introduce a single protein-bound nick in the Saccharomyces cerevisiae genome that is converted to a seDSB during S phase.13 Cell survival in response to Flp-mediated nicking is reduced in the absence of HR factors,4,13,14 consistent with the need for sister chromatid strand invasion to complete DNA replication. A minimal HO endonuclease recognition site that is mostly nicked by HO has been used to study the repair of replication-associated DSBs in a plasmid context.15,16 While these systems offer the ability to study a unique replication-associated break, both require introduction of an enzyme recognition site into the genome, and they lack strict strand specificity. By contrast, Cas9 nickase (nCas9) offers the ability to induce nicks in a site and strand-specific manner in the native genome with the versatile design of a single gRNA.

There are two versions of nCas9, depending on which nuclease site remains active. The Cas9D10A variant contains a point mutation in the RuvC domain and cleaves the gRNA target strand using the HNH domain, while the HNH domain of Cas9H840A is inactivated and it cleaves the non-target strand via the RuvC domain.17 Cas9 nickases has been used in genome editing, proving particularly useful in the paired nick strategy and prime editing.18,19 They has also been employed to study the structure and recombinogenic capacity of replication-associated breaks in mammalian cells.20–23 Genetic studies indicate that both nickases can stimulate intrachromosomal HR between repeat sequences with Cas9D10A showing higher activity.20,21,24 End-seq analysis in human cells has shown that nicks induced on the leading strand template by Cas9D10A are converted to mostly seDSBs during S-phase, whereas nicks on the lagging strand template generate double-ended DSBs (deDSBs).23

Here we establish use of Cas9 nickase in Saccharomyces cerevisiae to study repair of replication-associated DSBs. We show that nick induction with Cas9D10A leads to S-phase-dependent DSBs that require HR for repair. By systematic screening of the yeast non-essential gene deletion library, we identified the replication-coupled nucleosome assembly (RCNA) pathway as essential for repair. Previous screens in both yeast and humans have identified mutants of the RCNA pathway by their sensitivity to CPT, methyl methanesulfonate (MMS) and to unscheduled RNaseH2 expression, thereby implicating this pathway in maintaining genome integrity during replication stress.25–28 Our studies reveal that the RCNA pathway is especially important to facilitate repair of breaks that form on the leading strand template, whereas a nick on the lagging strand template is tolerated.

RESULTSCas9 nickase creates replication-dependent DSBsTo study the repair of broken replication forks, we engineered yeast strains expressing nCas9 with a gRNA designed to target a unique sequence in the genome. We generated two versions of nCas9, Cas9D10A and Cas9H840A, each with a single amino acid change to ablate one of the two Cas9 nuclease active sites.17 In our system, Cas9 or nCas9 is conditionally expressed by addition of β-estradiol to the growth medium and the gRNA is constitutively expressed from a Tyr-tRNA promoter.29 We designed four gRNAs to target sequences near the efficient, early firing ARS607 origin on chromosome VI (Figure 1A).13,30,31 Based on origin timing and proximity,30,31 gRNA1 and gRNA20 are predicted to direct Cas9D10A to the lagging strand template, whereas gRNA6 and gRNA14 should cause nicks on the leading strand template. To verify DNA cleavage by the gRNAs selected, each was expressed with Cas9 in asynchronous cultures and genomic DNA isolated for Southern blot analysis. In each case, >40% of the target site was cut after a 90-min Cas9 induction (Figure 1B and S1A).Based on recent data in human cells,23 we expected a Cas9D10A-induced nick on the leading strand template to generate a seDSB by replication fork convergence and a nick on the lagging strand template to be converted to a deDSB (Figure S1B). We synchronized MATa cells in the G1 phase with α-factor (t −60), induced expression of Cas9D10A with gRNA1 or gRNA14 for 60 min, released cells from the G1 arrest and isolated genomic DNA before release (t 0) and at 30 min intervals after release into S phase. Genomic DNA was then digested with FspI for Southern blot analysis (Figure 1C). DSBs were detected 30 min after release into S-phase consistent with replication dependence (Figure 1C, Figure S1C). We observed the expected seDSB with gRNA14, and a deDSB was generated at the lagging strand nick produced by gRNA1 (Figure 1C, D). Quantitation of the blots showed a reduction in break signal at 60 min and return of signal at 90 minutes for gRNA1, indicating repair and DSB formation in the subsequent cell cycle (Figure S1D). There is no apparent repair for the gRNA14 site. Determination of the ratio of DSB fragments confirmed DSB asymmetry for gRNA14 and symmetry for the gRNA1 site (Figure 1D).We performed a similar genomic DNA analysis for synchronized cells expressing Cas9D10A and gRNA6 or gRNA20 (Figure 1A, C). These guides target sequences 4 kb telomeric to ARS607 and 13 kb from ARS608, which is reported to be an inefficient origin.30,31 In agreement with the data obtained with gRNA1 and gRNA14, DSBs were detected 30 min after release from G1, indicating replication dependence (Figure 1C, S1C). However, we observed deDSBs for both leading and lagging strand nicks with these guides (Figure 1C, 1D). Break signals for gRNA6 and gRNA20 decreased at 60 min and increased again at 90 min, consistent with observations for gRNA1 of repair, re-nicking and DSB formation in the next cell cycle (Figure S1D). Since nicking the leading strand resulted in seDSB formation using one guide and deDSB formation using another guide, we designed a third guide targeting the leading strand telomeric to an efficient origin on chromosome XIII (ARS1332) and observed a deDSB signal (Figure 1D, S1E, F). Thus, in contrast to prevailing models of replication fork collapse at a nick, our data indicate that replication-dependent deDSBs are more common than previously thought.To explain the prevalence of deDSBs, we considered the possibility that replisome stalling at the lagging strand nick might allow time for a converging fork to arrive, converting the seDSB to a deDSB.32 To test this idea, we deleted annotated origins neighboring ARS607. Deletion of ARS606, an efficient origin located 31 kb centromere proximal to ARS607, did not change the deDSB signal observed using gRNA1 (Figure 1C, S1D). To verify that replication initiated from ARS606 can replicate the gRNA target sites, we expressed Cas9D10A with gRNA1 and gRNA14 in synchronized cells deleted for ARS607. As predicted if gRNA1 now directs Cas9D10A to the leading strand of a replication fork initiated at ARS606, a mostly seDSB pattern was observed (Figure S2A). We additionally employed a strain deleted for two annotated origins, ARS608 and ARS609 (17 kb and 57 kb telomeric to ARS607, respectively).33,34 Surprisingly, deDSBs were still detected in the strain expressing Cas9D10A with gRNA6 (Figure S2B, C). We note that there is one other confirmed origin and additional putative origins located telomeric to ARS607 (https://cerevisiae.oridb.org) that could be activated by replication fork collapse, as has been shown previously for an HO-endonuclease-induced DSB during S-phase.35 Likewise, the deDSB pattern at the gRNA11 site near ARS1332 may be due to fork convergence from activation of an inefficient origin that is 21 kb telomeric to ARS1332.Together, our data suggest that a nick on the leading strand template can generate a seDSB or deDSB due to fork collapse or fork convergence from a neighboring origin, respectively (Figure 1E). The replication-dependent deDSB signal from a lagging strand template nick could arise by continued translocation of the replicative helicase (CMG) on the leading strand template generating what would appear as a deDSB by lagging strand synthesis up to nick, as shown in a recent study using human cells and an in vitro reconstituted system.23 However, we cannot rule out a role for fork convergence at the lagging strand nick contributing to the deDSB signal. Notably, one end of a replication-dependent deDSB is predicted to be blunt and the other to have an overhang of up to the size of an Okazaki fragment.5Resection of replication-dependent DSBsTo investigate the extent of end resection, we modified a qPCR-based assay that is widely used to quantify resection of endonuclease-induced DSBs (Figure 2A).36,37 Resection of the replication-dependent DSB would generate ssDNA tracts that are resistant to in vitro digestion with restriction endonucleases that cleave within the indicated amplicons (blue and orange boxes) resulting in PCR amplification. Unresected DNA is cleaved by the restriction endonuclease resulting in no amplification with the indicated primers (horizontal arrows). In WT cells, we observed resection on both sides of the deDSB produced by Cas9D10A-gRNA6 in cycling cells (Figure 2B, S3). We found that the fraction of ssDNA increased in the rad51∆ mutant, consistent with hyper-resection resulting from decreased strand invasion to repair the deDSB, as noted previously for replication-dependent and independent DSBs.14,23,38Homologous recombination is essential for repair of replication-associated DSBsThe genomic blots indicate that Cas9D10A can generate replication-dependent single-ended or deDSBs. Repair of both types of breaks is expected to require end resection and Rad51-mediated invasion of the sister chromatid. However, a deDSB would have greater potential for repair by NHEJ and different modes of D-loop resolution compared with a seDSB. We tested sensitivity of WT cells and mutants defective for NHEJ (dnl4∆), end resection (mre11∆), and strand invasion (rad51∆) to induction of nCas9 or Cas9 by plating efficiency of cells on medium with or without β-estradiol. Cas9D10A induction with gRNA1, gRNA6, gRNA14 or gRNA20 did not impair growth of haploid WT or dnl4∆ cells, confirming that the DSBs detected by physical assays do not arise in G1 cells (Figure 3A). However, viability of mre11∆ and rad51∆ cells was reduced by ~1000-fold in the presence of the same gRNAs indicating that repair is highly dependent on HR. We note that not all gRNAs tested cause a severe growth defect in Mre11-deficient cells, which is likely due to variable cleavage by Cas9D10A (Figure S4A, B). Cas9H840A expression was slightly less toxic to HR-deficient strains compared to Cas9D10A, which is consistent with previous findings that Cas9H840A is not as effective as Cas9D10A in generation of replication-coupled DSBs and stimulation of HR (Figure 3A).20,21,23 For this reason, we primarily used Cas9D10A for subsequent analyses. A catalytically inactive Cas9 (dCas9) in combination with gRNA1 or gRNA6 did not confer sensitivity, confirming that the growth defect observed for HR-deficient strains is due to nicking and not simply Cas9 binding to DNA (Figure 3A). As expected, Cas9 expression with either gRNA1 or gRNA6 reduced viability of WT cells due to the inefficiency of error-prone NHEJ in yeast and was lethal in the absence of Dnl4.39 In budding yeast, the MRX complex is essential for NHEJ and, consequently, growth of mre11∆ was inhibited by Cas9 expression with both gRNAs. Although deDSBs are generated with Cas9D10A coupled to gRNA1, gRNA6 and gRNA20 (Figure 1), repair is still highly dependent on Rad51 suggesting that NHEJ repair of a replication-dependent DSB is inefficient. As noted above, one end of a replication-dependent deDSB is predicted to have a ssDNA overhang and to be less amenable to repair by canonical NHEJ.To assess the contribution of end resection in the repair of replication-coupled DSBs, we tested growth inhibition of cells lacking resection nucleases to Cas9D10A-induced nicking. We found that nuclease-dead mre11-H125N cells were only mildly sensitive to expression of Cas9D10A, indicating that collapsed fork repair does not depend on Mre11’s nuclease activity (Figure S4C). This finding is consistent with previous studies in yeast showing that clean DSB ends can be processed by Exo1 or STR-Dna2 in the absence of Mre11 nuclease activity.7 Mre11 may also be required due to its role in recruiting the long-range resection factors to DSB ends.40 Therefore, we tested sensitivity to Cas9D10A induction in exo1∆ sgs1∆ cells and observed a strong growth inhibition (Figure S4C). Deletion of either EXO1 or SGS1 alone did not cause a growth defect, consistent with redundancy between the long-range resection nucleases.7 The role of long-range resection is not immediately obvious, given that it is dispensable for HR between closely linked repeats.41 However, long-range resection may be required for efficient cohesion loading at a collapsed fork, since it has recently been shown that long-range resection is required for recruitment of the cohesin loader, Scc2, at DSBs.42A study using Flp-nick to generate seDSBs showed that BIR is largely dispensable for repair.4 BIR requires the non-essential subunit of DNA polymerase δ, Pol32/POLD3, and to a lesser degree, the Pif1 helicase.11,43 Elimination of Pol32 or nuclear Pif1 (pif1-m2) had little effect on survival in the presence of a gRNA1 or gRNA6 nick, with a slightly stronger phenotype for pol32∆ (Figure 3B). Even for gRNA14, which generates mostly seDSBs, Pol32 is not required (Figure S4D). Thus, BIR does not contribute significantly to repair of the Cas9D10A-induced seDSBs or deDSBs. Previous studies have shown that HJ resolution is important for the repair of replication-dependent seDSBs.4,6 Similarly, we found that growth of a mus81∆ yen1∆ mutant was severely impaired after Cas9D10A expression with gRNA1 or gRNA6 (Figure 3C). The role of Mus81-Mms4 and/or Yen1 is likely to cleave the D-loop intermediate or HJ formed by second end capture at a deDSB. Therefore, repair of replication-associated DSBs does not require extensive BIR synthesis or Mre11 nuclease activity, but does require the MRX complex, long-range end resection, and HJ resolvases.Cas9 nickase stimulates sister chromatid recombinationTo determine whether Cas9 nickases can induce HR, we employed an ade2 direct repeat assay to compare recombination frequencies in response to Cas9 or nCas9 expression. A gRNA was designed to target the I-SceI cut site within the ade2-I allele (Figure 4A). Log-phase cells were plated on medium with or without β-estradiol and the Ade+/− phenotype assessed by the colony color of surviving colonies (Ade+ colonies are white whereas Ade− colonies are red). Retention of the TRP1 marker located between the repeats was determined by replica plating to medium lacking tryptophan. A nCas9-induced deDSB could result in an Ade+ event through short tract gene conversion (STGC) with the ade2-n allele (intrachromatid or unequal sister chromatid interaction), whereas equal sister chromatid recombination would restore the ade2-I allele, potentiating a second round of nicking and DSB formation. Repair using the ade2-n allele would eliminate the gRNA binding site, preventing iterative nicking. A seDSB that invaded the ade2-n allele and initiated BIR would be predicted to copy the ade2-n allele generating Ade− recombinants, since the ade2-I mutation is close to the 5′ end of the ORF and the ade2-n mutation is near the 3′ end (Figure 4A). After expression of Cas9D10A or Cas9H840A and I-SceI gRNA we found that >90% of cells survived and most of the survivors were Ade+ Trp+, consistent with repair of a deDSB by short tract gene conversion (Figure 4B). Induction of a DSB with Cas9 resulted in a modest survival defect and most events were Ade+ Trp+ (Figure 4B), similarly to products formed by nCas9. Thus, nCas9 is proficient for induction of recombination between tandem repeats, and the predominance of STGC products is consistent with repair of a deDSB by conservative synthesis-dependent strand annealing.Since the survival assays indicate repair of a replication-dependent DSB using the sister chromatid, we anticipated that a nick would not induce recombination between non-sister chromatids. To test this idea, we used an inter-chromosomal assay that consists of the same ade2-I allele on Chr XV, but the ade2-n repair template is integrated at the LEU2 locus on Chr III (Figure 4C).41 In this scenario, the Cas9D10A-induced DSB has the potential to repair from the sister chromatid, resulting in an Ade− event. However, for the nick to result in an Ade+ event, the ectopic ade2-n donor must be used (Figure 4C). We found that expression of Cas9D10A and I-SceI gRNA failed to stimulate Ade+ recombinants (Figure 4D). By contrast, induction of Cas9 resulted in reduced cell survival (77%), but most of the surviving colonies were Ade+, similar to the outcome from an I-SceI-induced DSB.41 We presume that the strong preference for Ade− repair outcomes with Cas9 nickase is due to cohesion of sister chromatids, a process in which the MRX complex has been implicated.44–48 Therefore, eliminating Mre11 may release the constraint on sister-chromatid recombination and result in Ade+ events from ectopic recombination. Indeed, when we deleted MRE11, we found that most of the survivors after Cas9D10A induction were Ade+ (Figure 4D). There was a notable drop in survival in mre11∆ cells, emphasizing the importance of Mre11 for efficient repair of replication-associated DSBs (Figure 4D). Although survival of mre11∆ cells to the Cas9-induced DSB was lower than WT, survivors were mostly Ade+ (Figure 4D). These data indicate that replication-coupled DSBs are preferentially repaired by sister chromatid recombination and that Mre11 plays an important role in reinforcing this preference.Role of sister chromatid cohesion in repair of collapsed replication forksTo further address the role of sister chromatid cohesion in repair of collapsed replication forks, we analyzed two temperature-sensitive mutants of the cohesin complex, scc1-73 and smc3-42. At the semi-permissive temperature of 31 °C for scc1-73 or 33 °C for smc3-42, expression of Cas9D10A with gRNA1 or gRNA6 resulted in growth inhibition (Figure 4E), in line with a previous study showing that cohesin is partially required for HO nick-induced sister-chromatid recombination.16 We also tested the requirement for CTF4 and CTF8, two non-essential genes that reinforce cohesion establishment during S-phase.49,50 The ctf4∆ mutant showed high sensitivity to Cas9D10A expression, particularly with gRNA6, whereas ctf8∆ exhibited a modest but reproducible growth inhibition with both gRNAs (Figure S4E).A genome-wide screen identifies dependencies for replication-associated DSB repairAfter identifying several factors necessary for repair of nickase-induced DNA damage, we next sought to identify other factors/pathways in an unbiased manner by performing a genome-wide screen for mutants that are sensitive to induction of Cas9D10A. We used selective ploidy ablation (SPA) and plasmid transfer to introduce plasmids containing Cas9D10A and a gRNA into the yeast deletion strain collection (see Methods for details).51 Since, the deletion strain collection is a different genetic background to our standard laboratory strain (W303 background), we first verified that S288C derivatives deleted for MRE11 or RAD51 show sensitivity to nick induction (Figure S5A). For the screen, we used four plasmids: three expressed Cas9D10A with gRNA1, gRNA6, or a scrambled gRNA1, and one was an empty vector, the latter two serving as negative controls (Figure S5B). After carrying out the SPA procedure, colony size was assessed, and a reduction in colony size was indicative of a genetic dependency. Two repeats of the screen were performed. Overall, the strains exhibiting growth defects were consistent between the replicates for both gRNA1 and gRNA6 (Figure S5C).The strains that exhibited a significant growth reduction were overwhelmingly mutants of the RAD52 epistasis group (Figure 5A). Several other hits for both gRNAs, including SRS2, TOP3 and RMI1, may be involved in processing of the recombination intermediates that arise during repair. Srs2 is an anti-recombinase, whose function may be important for disrupting non-sister strand invasion events or invasion of ssDNA behind the migrating D-loop.52–55 The Top3-Rmi1 complex can disrupt D-loops, decatenate double HJ intermediates and resolve chromosome entanglements,56–58 which may arise during repair of replication-dependent DSBs. RAD6 was also a weak hit with both gRNAs but could not be verified on further testing.We did not recover mutants defective for the DNA damage checkpoint in the screen. Consistent with this finding, we detected only minimal phosphorylation of Rad53 after induction of Cas9D10A with gRNA1 or gRNA6 in asynchronous cultures (Figure S6). Elimination of Rad51 resulted in a strong checkpoint response, as expected when DNA repair is compromised. Cas9 nickase stimulates sister chromatid recombinationTo determine whether Cas9 nickases can induce HR, we employed an ade2 direct repeat assay to compare recombination frequencies in response to Cas9 or nCas9 expression. A gRNA was designed to target the I-SceI cut site within the ade2-I allele (Figure 4A). Log-phase cells were plated on medium with or without β-estradiol and the Ade+/− phenotype assessed by the colony color of surviving colonies (Ade+ colonies are white whereas Ade− colonies are red). Retention of the TRP1 marker located between the repeats was determined by replica plating to medium lacking tryptophan. A nCas9-induced deDSB could result in an Ade+ event through short tract gene conversion (STGC) with the ade2-n allele (intrachromatid or unequal sister chromatid interaction), whereas equal sister chromatid recombination would restore the ade2-I allele, potentiating a second round of nicking and DSB formation. Repair using the ade2-n allele would eliminate the gRNA binding site, preventing iterative nicking. A seDSB that invaded the ade2-n allele and initiated BIR would be predicted to copy the ade2-n allele generating Ade− recombinants, since the ade2-I mutation is close to the 5′ end of the ORF and the ade2-n mutation is near the 3′ end (Figure 4A). After expression of Cas9D10A or Cas9H840A and I-SceI gRNA we found that >90% of cells survived and most of the survivors were Ade+ Trp+, consistent with repair of a deDSB by short tract gene conversion (Figure 4B). Induction of a DSB with Cas9 resulted in a modest survival defect and most events were Ade+ Trp+ (Figure 4B), similarly to products formed by nCas9. Thus, nCas9 is proficient for induction of recombination between tandem repeats, and the predominance of STGC products is consistent with repair of a deDSB by conservative synthesis-dependent strand annealing.Since the survival assays indicate repair of a replication-dependent DSB using the sister chromatid, we anticipated that a nick would not induce recombination between non-sister chromatids. To test this idea, we used an inter-chromosomal assay that consists of the same ade2-I allele on Chr XV, but the ade2-n repair template is integrated at the LEU2 locus on Chr III (Figure 4C).41 In this scenario, the Cas9D10A-induced DSB has the potential to repair from the sister chromatid, resulting in an Ade− event. However, for the nick to result in an Ade+ event, the ectopic ade2-n donor must be used (Figure 4C). We found that expression of Cas9D10A and I-SceI gRNA failed to stimulate Ade+ recombinants (Figure 4D). By contrast, induction of Cas9 resulted in reduced cell survival (77%), but most of the surviving colonies were Ade+, similar to the outcome from an I-SceI-induced DSB.41 We presume that the strong preference for Ade− repair outcomes with Cas9 nickase is due to cohesion of sister chromatids, a process in which the MRX complex has been implicated.44–48 Therefore, eliminating Mre11 may release the constraint on sister-chromatid recombination and result in Ade+ events from ectopic recombination. Indeed, when we deleted MRE11, we found that most of the survivors after Cas9D10A induction were Ade+ (Figure 4D). There was a notable drop in survival in mre11∆ cells, emphasizing the importance of Mre11 for efficient repair of replication-associated DSBs (Figure 4D). Although survival of mre11∆ cells to the Cas9-induced DSB was lower than WT, survivors were mostly Ade+ (Figure 4D). Cas9 nickase stimulates sister chromatid recombination

To determine whether Cas9 nickases can induce HR, we employed an ade2 direct repeat assay to compare recombination frequencies in response to Cas9 or nCas9 expression. A gRNA was designed to target the I-SceI cut site within the ade2-I allele (Figure 4A). Log-phase cells were plated on medium with or without β-estradiol and the Ade+/− phenotype assessed by the colony color of surviving colonies (Ade+ colonies are white whereas Ade− colonies are red). Retention of the TRP1 marker located between the repeats was determined by replica plating to medium lacking tryptophan. A nCas9-induced deDSB could result in an Ade+ event through short tract gene conversion (STGC) with the ade2-n allele (intrachromatid or unequal sister chromatid interaction), whereas equal sister chromatid recombination would restore the ade2-I allele, potentiating a second round of nicking and DSB formation. Repair using the ade2-n allele would eliminate the gRNA binding site, preventing iterative nicking. A seDSB that invaded the ade2-n allele and initiated BIR would be predicted to copy the ade2-n allele generating Ade− recombinants, since the ade2-I mutation is close to the 5′ end of the ORF and the ade2-n mutation is near the 3′ end (Figure 4A). After expression of Cas9D10A or Cas9H840A and I-SceI gRNA we found that >90% of cells survived and most of the survivors were Ade+ Trp+, consistent with repair of a deDSB by short tract gene conversion (Figure 4B). Induction of a DSB with Cas9 resulted in a modest survival defect and most events were Ade+ Trp+ (Figure 4B), similarly to products formed by nCas9. Thus, nCas9 is proficient for induction of recombination between tandem repeats, and the predominance of STGC products is consistent with repair of a deDSB by conservative synthesis-dependent strand annealing.

Since the survival assays indicate repair of a replication-dependent DSB using the sister chromatid, we anticipated that a nick would not induce recombination between non-sister chromatids. To test this idea, we used an inter-chromosomal assay that consists of the same ade2-I allele on Chr XV, but the ade2-n repair template is integrated at the LEU2 locus on Chr III (Figure 4C).41 In this scenario, the Cas9D10A-induced DSB has the potential to repair from the sister chromatid, resulting in an Ade− event. However, for the nick to result in an Ade+ event, the ectopic ade2-n donor must be used (Figure 4C). We found that expression of Cas9D10A and I-SceI gRNA failed to stimulate Ade+ recombinants (Figure 4D). By contrast, induction of Cas9 resulted in reduced cell survival (77%), but most of the surviving colonies were Ade+, similar to the outcome from an I-SceI-induced DSB.41 We presume that the strong preference for Ade− repair outcomes with Cas9 nickase is due to cohesion of sister chromatids, a process in which the MRX complex has been implicated.44–48 Therefore, eliminating Mre11 may release the constraint on sister-chromatid recombination and result in Ade+ events from ectopic recombination. Indeed, when we deleted MRE11, we found that most of the survivors after Cas9D10A induction were Ade+ (Figure 4D). There was a notable drop in survival in mre11∆ cells, emphasizing the importance of Mre11 for efficient repair of replication-associated DSBs (Figure 4D). Although survival of mre11∆ cells to the Cas9-induced DSB was lower than WT, survivors were mostly Ade+ (Figure 4D). These data indicate that replication-coupled DSBs are preferentially repaired by sister chromatid recombination and that Mre11 plays an important role in reinforcing this preference.

DISCUSSIONHere we have established a system to induce nicks on either the leading or lagging strand template that are converted to DSBs during S-phase. We found that Rad51-dependent HR is essential for repair of these lesions and observed a strong bias for repair using a sister chromatid template. Although the core HR factors, HJ resolvases and cohesin components are required to repair nicks induced on both leading and lagging strand templates, we identified a unique requirement for the RCNA pathway to repair lesions induced on the leading strand template.By physical methods, we have shown that Cas9D10A induces S-phase dependent deDSBs at nicks induced on the lagging strand template. Since deletion of neighboring origins did not prevent deDSB formation, we favor the hypothesis that CMG continues unwinding on the leading strand template at a Cas9D10A-induced nick,71 displacing the lagging strand for initiation of an Okazaki fragment that would extend to the nick site (Figure 7). This finding agrees with recent observations in human cells using Cas9D10A and studies using a reconstituted Xenopus laevis egg extract system.23,72 The Cas9D10A post-cleavage complex might be conducive for CMG to continue translocation on the leading strand instead of translocating onto parental duplex DNA at the nick, as has been reported using the Cas9H840A nickase.5,23 Surprisingly, we observed deDSBs with two of the gRNAs that target the leading strand. We note that deDSBs are also detected using the HO-nick and Flp-nick systems in a plasmid context in budding yeast, and in the S. pombe genome using gpII and nCas9 nickases.15,73–75 Given the proximity of nearby inefficient origins that might be activated by local fork stalling or collapse,35,76 we suggest that deDSBs arise by fork convergence. dCas9 has been shown to stall replication forks raising the possibility that persistent Cas9D10A binding may provide more time for a fork initiated from a neighboring origin to converge with the nicked template.77,78 Fork convergence to generate a deDSB would require that the hybrid nick formed between the parental duplex and nascent strand at a seDSB remain unligated,79 or be rapidly re-nicked by Cas9D10A after gap filling and ligation. Thus, the balance between formation of single-ended and deDSBs from nicks on the leading strand is likely influenced by the proximity of nearby origins, duration of replisome stalling in response to the Cas9D10 post-cleavage complex and the efficiency of hybrid nick ligation.32,78,79 The yeast genome has a high density of active and dormant origins and this feature could ensure that most collapsed replication forks are rescued by fork convergence from a neighboring origin.76 Since extensive DNA synthesis from a seDSB is more mutagenic than normal S-phase synthesis,4,10 conversion to a deDSB could be a mechanism to limit genomic instability.Regardless of whether a seDSB or a deDSB is formed, we find that repair is highly dependent on HR factors and is independent of NHEJ. A replication-dependent deDSB formed by nCas9 may be inefficiently repaired by end joining because one end would have an overhang up to the size of an Okazaki fragment rendering it a poor substrate for canonical NHEJ. Yeast cells lack Pol θ-mediated end joining (TMEJ) and repair using microhomologies is inefficient.80 In agreement with our findings, nCas9 and Flp-nick-induced HR in murine and human cells is not suppressed by canonical NHEJ factors in contrast to I-SceI or Cas9-induced events.21,81 Surprisingly, we observe a requirement for HJ resolvases, but not for Sgs1-mediated dHJ dissolution, in tolerance of nicks induced by Cas9D10A. This finding could indicate that single HJs are generated during repair, either by D-loop processing or fork convergence.We found that nCas9-induced nicks at the ade2-I allele of an ade2 direct repeat reporter stimulate recombination with the non-allelic donor located 4.3 kb downstream of ade2-I. Most products resulted from STGC, regardless of whether the nick was on the leading or lagging strand template, consistent with repair of a deDSB. The recombination products generated by Flp-mediated nicking in mouse cells are also consistent with repair of a replication-dependent deDSB.81 Although we did not identify products consistent with LTGC, studies in mammalian cells have shown an increased LTGC/STGC ratio in response to nCas9 or Flp-nick expression.20,24,81 These findings could be explained by BIR initiating at a seDSB or to more extensive DNA synthesis associated with HR during S-phase in mammalian cells. Although we assume that recombination is initiated by a replication-dependent DSB in our assay, we cannot exclude the possibility of nick expansion to form a ssDNA gap for Rad51 binding to initiate HR in G2 cells.By contrast, very few recombinants were generated by Cas9D10A expression when the donor was located on a different chromosome, consistent with a strong preference to repair using a sister chromatid template. Cas9 did stimulate inter-chromosomal recombination indicating that repair can default to an ectopic donor in the absence of an intact sister chromatid. While deletion of MRE11 allowed for inter-chromosomal recombination with Cas9D10A, it also reduced the survival frequency. We assume that deletion of MRE11 allows for inter-chromosomal recombination due to a loss of sister chromatid tethering by the MRX complex, as has been suggested previously,44–47,82 or indirectly via diminished recruitment of cohesin.48,83,84 Notably, the scc1-73 and smc3-42 conditional alleles conferred high sensitivity to nCas9 expression, consistent with the need to tether sister chromatids for efficient repair. The observation that in WT cells, nearly all nick-induced repair events were Ade−, while nearly all Cas9-induced repair events were Ade+ highlights the propensity for repair of replication-associated DSBs by sister-chromatid recombination. Our findings are consistent with studies in human cells showing a single nick induced by nCas9 fails to induce inter-homolog recombination.85 However, Cas9 nickases can induce allelic and ectopic inter-chromosomal gene conversion in Drosophila somatic cells,86 and Flp-nick is reported to induce allelic recombination in yeast.87 More studies will be needed to determine how well nicks can induce allelic recombination in different systems and genetic control of these events.The genome-wide screen was carried out with the intent of identifying novel factors required for the repair of replication-dependent DSBs. The RAD52 epistasis group was overwhelmingly represented in the nCas9 screen, which is consistent with results from CPT sensitivity screens and Flp-nick sensitivity.4,13,26,27 One of the more interesting findings from the screen is the gRNA specific sensitivity of RCNA mutants, a phenotype not observed for other mutants tested, such as rad51∆. The RCNA pathway has long been implicated in resistance to replication stress and DNA damage-induced sister-chromatid recombination;26–28,59,88–92 however, the exact role of these proteins in the replication stress response is not completely understood. Our data indicate a specific requirement for the RCNA factors to repair a DSB generated on the leading strand, whereas a DSB formed on the lagging strand is tolerated. These requirements do not necessarily fit with the Rad51 loading role of Mms2293,94 because Rad51 loading should be important regardless of whether the replication-associated DSB is on the leading or lagging strand. Similarly, the sister-chromatid cohesion function of RCNA95 is unlikely to contribute to the gRNA specificity since conditional mutants of the cohesin complex showed equal sensitivity to gRNA1 and gRNA6 (Figure 4E).A replication-coupled DSB on the leading strand would require invasion of the lagging strand template to initiate replication restart (Figure 7). The lagging strand template could be less favorable for strand invasion if Okazaki fragments are not completely ligated or if there is an imbalance of nucleosomes. Okazaki fragment lengths are not noticeably different in RCNA mutants but changes in ligation efficiency have not been evaluated.96 There is a slight bias for incorporation of H3K56Ac-containing nucleosomes on the leading strand template, whereas parental nucleosomes are preferentially transferred to the lagging strand template.97 This bias, which might be accentuated in the RCNA mutants, could make the lagging strand less accessible for strand invasion. The H3K56Ac mark has been suggested to loosen the histone-DNA interaction at the entry and exit points of the nucleosome, which may allow for a more favorable chromatin environment for strand invasion on the leading strand.61Finally, the role of RCNA in sister chromatid recombination could be post strand invasion. Nucleosomes displaced by end resection would need to be replaced after repair synthesis and this would require incorporation of newly synthesized histones. A post-strand invasion role for RCNA in restoration of chromatin structure at DSBs has been proposed previously and linked to DNA damage checkpoint adaptation.98 Although restoration of nucleosomes post repair should be important for leading and lagging strand collapses, if the nucleosome composition of the leading and lagging strand templates is different, then chromatin restoration on the leading strand might be more challenging in the absence of the RCNA pathway.

This finding agrees with recent observations in human cells using Cas9D10A and studies using a reconstituted Xenopus laevis egg extract system.23,72 The Cas9D10A post-cleavage complex might be conducive for CMG to continue translocation on the leading strand instead of translocating onto parental duplex DNA at the nick, as has been reported using the Cas9H840A nickase.5,23 Surprisingly, we observed deDSBs with two of the gRNAs that target the leading strand. We note that deDSBs are also detected using the HO-nick and Flp-nick systems in a plasmid context in budding yeast, and in the S. pombe genome using gpII and nCas9 nickases.15,73–75 Given the proximity of nearby inefficient origins that might be activated by local fork stalling or collapse,35,76 we suggest that deDSBs arise by fork convergence. dCas9 has been shown to stall replication forks raising the possibility that persistent Cas9D10A binding may provide more time for a fork initiated from a neighboring origin to converge with the nicked template.77,78 Fork convergence to generate a deDSB would require that the hybrid nick formed between the parental duplex and nascent strand at a seDSB remain unligated,79 or be rapidly re-nicked by Cas9D10A after gap filling and ligation. Thus, the balance between formation of single-ended and deDSBs from nicks on the leading strand is likely influenced by the proximity of nearby origins, duration of replisome stalling in response to the Cas9D10 post-cleavage complex and the efficiency of hybrid nick ligation.32,78,79 The yeast genome has a high density of active and dormant origins and this feature could ensure that most collapsed replication forks are rescued by fork convergence from a neighboring origin.76 Since extensive DNA synthesis from a seDSB is more mutagenic than normal S-phase synthesis,4,10 conversion to a deDSB could be a mechanism to limit genomic instability.

Regardless of whether a seDSB or a deDSB is formed, we find that repair is highly dependent on HR factors and is independent of NHEJ. A replication-dependent deDSB formed by nCas9 may be inefficiently repaired by end joining because one end would have an overhang up to the size of an Okazaki fragment rendering it a poor substrate for canonical NHEJ. Yeast cells lack Pol θ-mediated end joining (TMEJ) and repair using microhomologies is inefficient.80 In agreement with our findings, nCas9 and Flp-nick-induced HR in murine and human cells is not suppressed by canonical NHEJ factors in contrast to I-SceI or Cas9-induced events.21,81 Surprisingly, we observe a requirement for HJ resolvases, but not for Sgs1-mediated dHJ dissolution, in tolerance of nicks induced by Cas9D10A. This finding could indicate that single HJs are generated during repair, either by D-loop processing or fork convergence.

We found that nCas9-induced nicks at the ade2-I allele of an ade2 direct repeat reporter stimulate recombination with the non-allelic donor located 4.3 kb downstream of ade2-I. Most products resulted from STGC, regardless of whether the nick was on the leading or lagging strand template, consistent with repair of a deDSB. The recombination products generated by Flp-mediated nicking in mouse cells are also consistent with repair of a replication-dependent deDSB.81 Although we did not identify products consistent with LTGC, studies in mammalian cells have shown an increased LTGC/STGC ratio in response to nCas9 or Flp-nick expression.20,24,81 These findings could be explained by BIR initiating at a seDSB or to more extensive DNA synthesis associated with HR during S-phase in mammalian cells. Although we assume that recombination is initiated by a replication-dependent DSB in our assay, we cannot exclude the possibility of nick expansion to form a ssDNA gap for Rad51 binding to initiate HR in G2 cells.

By contrast, very few recombinants were generated by Cas9D10A expression when the donor was located on a different chromosome, consistent with a strong preference to repair using a sister chromatid template. Cas9 did stimulate inter-chromosomal recombination indicating that repair can default to an ectopic donor in the absence of an intact sister chromatid. While deletion of MRE11 allowed for inter-chromosomal recombination with Cas9D10A, it also reduced the survival frequency. We assume that deletion of MRE11 allows for inter-chromosomal recombination due to a loss of sister chromatid tethering by the MRX complex, as has been suggested previously,44–47,82 or indirectly via diminished recruitment of cohesin.48,83,84 Notably, the scc1-73 and smc3-42 conditional alleles conferred high sensitivity to nCas9 expression, consistent with the need to tether sister chromatids for efficient repair. The observation that in WT cells, nearly all nick-induced repair events were Ade−, while nearly all Cas9-induced repair events were Ade+ highlights the propensity for repair of replication-associated DSBs by sister-chromatid recombination. Our findings are consistent with studies in human cells showing a single nick induced by nCas9 fails to induce inter-homolog recombination.85 However, Cas9 nickases can induce allelic and ectopic inter-chromosomal gene conversion in Drosophila somatic cells,86 and Flp-nick is reported to induce allelic recombination in yeast.87 More studies will be needed to determine how well nicks can induce allelic recombination in different systems and genetic control of these events.

The genome-wide screen was carried out with the intent of identifying novel factors required for the repair of replication-dependent DSBs. The RAD52 epistasis group was overwhelmingly represented in the nCas9 screen, which is consistent with results from CPT sensitivity screens and Flp-nick sensitivity.4,13,26,27 One of the more interesting findings from the screen is the gRNA specific sensitivity of RCNA mutants, a phenotype not observed for other mutants tested, such as rad51∆. The RCNA pathway has long been implicated in resistance to replication stress and DNA damage-induced sister-chromatid recombination;26–28,59,88–92 however, the exact role of these proteins in the replication stress response is not completely understood. Our data indicate a specific requirement for the RCNA factors to repair a DSB generated on the leading strand, whereas a DSB formed on the lagging strand is tolerated. These requirements do not necessarily fit with the Rad51 loading role of Mms2293,94 because Rad51 loading should be important regardless of whether the replication-associated DSB is on the leading or lagging strand. Similarly, the sister-chromatid cohesion function of RCNA95 is unlikely to contribute to the gRNA specificity since conditional mutants of the cohesin complex showed equal sensitivity to gRNA1 and gRNA6 (Figure 4E).

A replication-coupled DSB on the leading strand would require invasion of the lagging strand template to initiate replication restart (Figure 7). The lagging strand template could be less favorable for strand invasion if Okazaki fragments are not completely ligated or if there is an imbalance of nucleosomes. Okazaki fragment lengths are not noticeably different in RCNA mutants but changes in ligation efficiency have not been evaluated.96 There is a slight bias for incorporation of H3K56Ac-containing nucleosomes on the leading strand template, whereas parental nucleosomes are preferentially transferred to the lagging strand template.97 This bias, which might be accentuated in the RCNA mutants, could make the lagging strand less accessible for strand invasion. The H3K56Ac mark has been suggested to loosen the histone-DNA interaction at the entry and exit points of the nucleosome, which may allow for a more favorable chromatin environment for strand invasion on the leading strand.61

Finally, the role of RCNA in sister chromatid recombination could be post strand invasion. Nucleosomes displaced by end resection would need to be replaced after repair synthesis and this would require incorporation of newly synthesized histones. A post-strand invasion role for RCNA in restoration of chromatin structure at DSBs has been proposed previously and linked to DNA damage checkpoint adaptation.98 Although restoration of nucleosomes post repair should be important for leading and lagging strand collapses, if the nucleosome composition of the leading and lagging strand templates is different, then chromatin restoration on the leading strand might be more challenging in the absence of the RCNA pathway.

LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDYThe use of nCas9 to model replication fork collapse has many attractive features, including site and strand specificity, but may not fully mimic spontaneous lesions. Cas9 is known to remain associated with the target sequence after cleavage and this could impact how the replisome behaves when encountering a nick.78,99 Indeed, the deDSB observed using Cas9D10A to generate a nick on the lagging strand template could be specific to use of this nickase since Cas9H840A has been shown to generate seDSBs at both leading and lagging strand template nicks.5,23 Another limitation is that little is known about the dynamic interaction between nCas9 and the target sequence. In our studies, the nickase is continually expressed in cells after induction and may undergo multiple iterative cycles of binding, nicking and release. We imagine that many of the nicks are ligated prior to arrival of a replication fork. For nicks that result in fork collapse, nCas9 could also nick the homologous site in the sister chromatid that is used as a template for strand invasion, potentially influencing repair dynamics. Despite these limitations, the genetic requirements we observe for repair of nCas9-induced DNA damage closely match those reported with other nickases and CPT-induced lesions,4,13–15,26,27,92 suggesting that the repair mechanisms used are common to SSBs and are not specific to nCas9.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)-based resection assayResection was measured using a qPCR-based assay as previously described (Gnugge et al., 2018) with the following modifications. ~108 cycling cells were collected 0, 1, 2, and 4 hours after β-estradiol was added to a final concentration of 2 μM for Cas9D10A induction. Per sample and amplicon, reactions were performed in technical triplicates containing 4.4 μL digested or undigested genomic DNA, 300 nM of each forward and reverse primer (listed in Table S2), and 1x SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix in a total volume of 10 μL and analyzed on a CFX384 Real-Time System with 10 minutes initial denaturation at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 1 minute denaturation at 95°C and 1 minute annealing and extension at 58°C. The fraction of ssDNA, fssDNA, at the restriction enzyme recognition site was calculated as previously described (Pfaffl, 2001; Zierhut & Diffley, 2008) with the following formula:

fssDNA=2/(((ERSΔCq(digest-mock)/EADH1ΔCq(digest-mock))+1)*f)

where ERS and EADH1 are the primer efficiencies for the restriction site (RS) and ADH1 amplicons, ΔCqdigest-mock is the difference between quantification cycles for the digested minus the mock-digested sample, and f is the fraction of genomes where Cas9D10A cleaved the target locus. f for a locus cleaved by Cas9D10A can be calculated as:

f=(1-(EnCas9cs)ΔCq(t0−t)/(EADH1)ΔCq(t0−t))/0.75

where EnCas9cs and EADH1 are the primer efficiencies for the Cas9D10A-cut site and ADH1 amplicons and ΔCqt0-t is the difference between the quantification cycles at the first time point (before β-estradiol induction of Cas9D10A) and the evaluated time point.

Genome-wide screen using selective ploidy ablation and Cas9-gRNA plasmid transferSelective ploidy ablation (SPA) and plasmid transfer takes advantage of a Universal Donor Strain (UDS), in which all 16 chromosomes contain a galactose promoter oriented toward the centromere and also contain a counter-selectable URA3 marker 51. Briefly, a plasmid of interest can be transformed into the UDS, which can then be mated to a large number of target strains. After mating, galactose-induced transcription through the donor strain centromeres leads to their destabilization, leaving only the target strain chromosomes. Subsequent counter-selection of the remaining donor strain chromosomes with 5-fluoroorotic acid (5-FOA) ensures that surviving colonies only have the target strain chromosomes. This method has proven useful to introduce plasmids into yeast gene deletion libraries containing thousands of target strains when combined with automated pinning. For this screen, pRS415 (empty vector), pLS627 (Cas9D10A-gRNA1), pLS633 (Cas9D10A-gRNA6), and pLS632 (Cas9D10A-scr.gRNA) were transformed into the MATα UDS (W8164-2C/ LSY5158-2C) for mating to the yeast gene deletion library. Donors and library strains were allowed to mate for 6 hours on YPD medium and then transferred to synthetic medium lacking leucine and containing galactose. These plates also contained 2 μM β-estradiol to induce expression of Cas9D10A. After 2 days of incubation colonies were transferred to synthetic medium lacking leucine and containing galactose, 5-FOA and 2 μM β-estradiol. Plates were then incubated for 3 days. All incubations were carried out at 30 °C. After the final incubation, plates were imaged at 300 dpi using a Scanmaker 9800XL-plus flatbed scanner (MicroTek) fitted with a TMA1600 transparency adapter.
