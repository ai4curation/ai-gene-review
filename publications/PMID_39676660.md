The INO80 chromatin remodeler is a versatile enzyme capable of several 
functions, including spacing nucleosomes equal distances apart, precise 
positioning of nucleosomes based on DNA shape/sequence and exchanging histone 
dimers. Within INO80, the Arp5 subunit plays a central role in INO80 remodeling, 
evidenced by its interactions with the histone octamer, nucleosomal and 
extranucleosomal DNA, and its necessity in linking INO80's ATPase activity to 
nucleosome movement. We find two distinct regions of Arp5 binding near the 
acidic pocket of nucleosomes. One region has an arginine anchor that binds 
nucleosomes and is vital for INO80 mobilizing nucleosomes. The other region has 
a hydrophobic/acid patch of Leu and Asp that binds free histone H2A-H2B dimers. 
These two regions have different roles in remodeling nucleosomes as seen both in 
vitro and in vivo and the hydrophobic/acidic patch of Arp5 is likely needed for 
displacing DNA from the H2A-H2B surface and dimer exchange by INO80.

Introduction

The holocomplex referred to as INO80, with its catalytic subunit known as Ino80, plays a pivotal role in chromatin remodeling and is important in transcription regulation, DNA repair and replication, nuclear organization and cellular differentiation (1–7). INO80 remodels chromatin by repositioning nucleosomes in a DNA shape and sequence-dependent manner, placing nucleosomes equally separated by the same length of linker DNA and facilitating the exchange of histone dimers (8–13). Deletion of Ino80 hampers ventricular compaction during heart development and correlates with defective coronary vascularization (14). Mutations in YY1AP1, a subunit of INO80, disrupt smooth muscle differentiation and are associated with Grange syndrome that occasionally manifests as arterial disease (15). Elevated expression of Ino80 is crucial for the proliferation of melanoma (16), small cell lung cancer (SCLC) (17) and colon cancer (18). INO80 increases accessibility at enhancer regions in melanoma and SCLC, thereby facilitating Mediator complex binding (16,17). Additionally, at centromeres INO80 displaces canonical H3 to promote the incorporation of CENP-A and is required for the ectopic incorporation of CENP-A when overexpressed (19,20). In homologous recombination, INO80 facilitates Rad21 filament formation and DNA re-sectioning through the removal of H2A.Z (21–23). H2A.Z removal by INO80 also occurs at pericentric regions(24) and certain promoters(25,26), although at many promoters RNA polymerase II primarily displaces H2A.Z (27).

One of the distinctive architectural features of INO80 is the binding of the Arp8 module to linker DNA, which is tethered to the Ino80 catalytic subunit via the HSA (helicase–SANT-associated) domain of Ino80 (28–30). This binding is disrupted by either truncating the N-terminus of Arp8 or by mutating the DNA binding surface of the HSA domain (9,10,31,32). The nucleosome mobilizing activity of INO80 is significantly impaired when the Arp8 module fails to bind linker DNA, suggesting that this mechanism governs INO80’s nucleosome spacing and potentially enables the positioning of nucleosomes in a DNA shape-specific manner (8).

Another architectural element of INO80 is the Arp5 module that interacts with the histone octamer, nucleosomal DNA at SHL-1, -2 and -3 and extranucleosomal DNA where DNA enters nucleosomes(8,29,33). Arp5 forms a heterodimer with the Ies6 subunit in Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Sc) and contains a flexible grappler domain that exhibits two alternative conformations known as the ‘cross’ and ‘parallel’ conformations, as observed by cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) (29). Recently, an improved structure of Chaetomium thermophilum (Ct) INO80:NCP and AlphaFold2 modeling of the grappler domain has provided insights into the ‘cross’ conformation of the grappler domain. In this model, regions termed the ‘foot’ and ‘heel’ bind to the acidic pocket of nucleosomes using an arginine anchor (8,34). This model is supported by mutations in the arginine anchor and other regions of Arp5 predicted to interact with nucleosomal DNA effectively blocking nucleosome mobilization by Ct INO80 (34).

The acidic pocket represents a crucial binding site on the nucleosome, characterized by a negatively charged depression. It serves as a binding site for various chromatin-associated factors, including viral proteins (35), histone modifiers (36,37) and ATP-dependent chromatin remodelers (29,38–41). Interactions at this site play pivotal roles in regulating transcription, DNA repair and other genomic processes (39,42–44).

In this study, we confirm the presence of a weak arginine anchor in the grappler domain of Sc Arp5 which binds to the acidic pocket and is required for the nucleosome mobilizing activity of INO80. Somewhat surprisingly, we find there is another part of Arp5 that also binds to the lateral face of the nucleosome distinct from the arginine anchor based on different INO80 phenotypes and biochemical properties. This other region has a high affinity for free H2A–H2B dimers, does not appear to bind directly to the acidic pocket and based on structural models is spatially disparate from the arginine anchor. We provide evidence for the grappler domain dynamically switching contact with the nucleosome that is likely important for the dimer exchange activity of INO80.

ResultsAn arginine anchor in the grappler domain is required for INO80 mobilizing nucleosomes and for Arp5 contacting the acidic pocket of nucleosomesThe cryo-EM structures of Ct and Sc INO80 coupled with AlphaFold2 modeling have provided evidence for a putative arginine anchor in the grappler domain of Arp5 that interacts with the acidic pocket of nucleosomes (8,33). The functional role of this putative arginine anchor has unfortunately not been experimentally validated for Sc Arp5 and prompted us to mutate three conserved arginines in the ‘foot’ and ‘leg’ regions of Sc Arp5. Arg 482 is conserved in Sc and D. melanogaster but in S. pombe and T. thermophilia is replaced by Lys and Arg 488 and Arg 496 in Sc Arp5 are well conserved up to D. melanogaster (Figure 1A). Mutating these three arginines to alanine greatly reduced the nucleosome mobilizing activity of INO80 and its ATPase activity is reduced 4-fold without altering the complex integrity of INO80 (Supplementary Figures S1A–C and S7A and Table 1) (34). These arginines are shown to be important for Arp5 to contact the surface proximal to the nucleosome acidic pocket as seen when mutating them to alanine reduces Arp5 crosslinking to modified residue 89 of histone H2A to 23% of that observed with WT (Figure 1D). The in vivo role of the arginine anchor of Arp5 is tested using the AA method to determine if mutant versions of Arp5 can compensate for the acute removal of WT Arp5 and avoid cells adapting after prolonged exposure to mutant Arp5. WT Arp5 is shown to be vital for INO80’s role in response to DNA replication stress caused by hydroxyurea (HU), heat/cold stress and transcription of the INO1 gene, all of which is rescued by ectopic expression of Arp5 (Supplementary Figure S1B). The arginine anchor of Arp5 and its regulation of the nucleosome mobilizing activity of INO80 is however not the critical activity of INO80 required for alleviating DNA replication stress (HU) or facilitating in DNA repair (MMS) and potentially transcription elongation (6-azauracil; 6-AU) (Figure 1F).

To better understand the role of the arginine anchor for INO80 remodeling, we examine DNA movement on the two sides of the nucleosome and DNA displacement on the entry side by site-directed histone-DNA crosslinking (Figure 2A) (48,49). We focus on early DNA movement and the most prominent steps of 32 and 20 bp at the exit and entry sides of nucleosomes. DNA movement on the entry side of nucleosomes is observed in a limited number of nucleosomes (12%), stops after only 40 s and subsequently decreases (Figure 2B; Supplementary Figure S2). DNA movement of 32 bp on the exit side is more prominent than on the entry side as it occurs in nearly half of all nucleosomes in the time window of 160 s (Figure 2B). Mutation of the arginine anchor strongly blocks early DNA movement on both sides of the nucleosome (Figure 2C and D). In the same early time window, INO80 displaces DNA from the histone octamer as seen by a net loss of histone-DNA crosslinking on the entry side that is not dramatically changed by mutating the arginine anchor (Figure 2E). In summary, early DNA movement on both sides of the nucleosome is severely impact by mutating the arginine anchor but not early DNA displacement on the entry side.

The DBD domain binds DNA close to where the grappler domain binds and could tether the grappler domain to the nucleosome, which would be consistent with the earlier phenotype observed when mutating the DBD domain and either the arginine anchor or LLD region (Supplementary Figure S6F). The DNA binding activity of DBD alone does not dictate whether the grappler domain binds to the lateral face of nucleosomes as seen by no reduction in Arp5 crosslinking to residue 89 of H2A or 80 of H3 with the Arp5DBD compared with WT complex (Figure 5B). DBD binding DNA at SHL-2/-3 is required however to tether the grappler domain to the lateral surface of the nucleosome when the interactions of the LLD are disrupted as shown by histone crosslinking (Figure 5B; Supplementary Figure S8E). Mutation of LLD alone reduces the crosslinking of Arp5 at H2A89 to 23% of WT and to 46% of WT at H380, and when combined with mutation of DBD the crosslinking at H2A89 and H380 is reduced to only 7–8%. These data suggest  DBD could have an important tethering function for the grappler domain when one conformation of the grappler domain releases its contact and rotates to the other conformation. If the grappler domain rotates to the conformation and is unable to bind the nucleosome surface, then the grappler domain would be prone to dissociate and thereby lose its ability to rotate back to the starting conformation.

Biotinylated LLD and arginine anchor peptides are used in pulldown assays to determine whether these parts of Arp5 can independently bind to the acidic patch of nucleosomes or free histone H2A–H2B dimers. In comparison to the prototypical arginine anchor of LANA, the Arp5 arginine anchor binds weakly to nucleosomes but is nonetheless specific as seen by the loss of binding when the arginines corresponding to residue 482, 488 and 496 of Arp5 are mutated to alanine (Figure 5C). The preferred binding target for LLD is the free H2A–H2B dimer with a higher affinity for the dimer than either the LANA or R3 peptide and does not detectably bind nucleosomes and presumably the acidi patch (Figure 5C and D). These results indicate that the LLD region likely recognizes the part of the H2A–H2B dimer either bound to DNA or the H3–H4 tetramer due to its lack of interactions with nucleosomes. The LLD peptide was further confirmed to not bind nucleosomes at the acidic patch based on the LLD peptide not interfering with INO80 remodeling like the LANA peptide (Figure 5E). Binding of the LLD peptide to H2A–H2B is dependent on Leu 567, Leu 568 and Asp 571 as shown by reducing 4-fold the amount of dimer pulled down when changed to alanine (Figure 5D). The dimer specificity of LLD suggests this region could be involved in dimer exchange, which is consistent with Arp5LLD being defective for DNA replication where INO80 needs to replace H2A.Z with H2A (13,21–23,25).

The LLD residues are located at one end of an alpha-helix that associates by way of a leucine zipper with the ‘leg’ helix (Figure 6A–C and 3C; Supplementary Figure S4C). Disruption of the interactions between these two helices potentially could affect binding of the arginine anchor since the ‘leg’ helix is attached to where the anchor resides in the ‘foot’ region. Mutation of Leu 567 and Leu 568 is expected however to not disturb the leucine zipper holding these two alpha helixes together, based on its location at the extreme end of the zipper and the maintenance of five Leu–Leu or Leu–Ile pairs and two ionic pairs crucial in the helix–helix interactions (Figure 6B and C). Protein modeling with AlphaFold3 also confirms no change is predicted in the tertiary structure of the grappler domain when Leu 567, Leu 568 and Asp 571 are mutated to Ala (Figure 6D). Mutation of LLD is therefore expected to not impact binding of the arginine anchor to the acidic pocket.

The stage(s) of INO80 remodeling impacted by mutation of LLD is determined by mapping DNA movement and displacement as described earlier (48,49). The initial rate of DNA movement by Arp5LLD on the exit side of nucleosomes is reduced 4-fold compared with WT but is overall 6.5-fold more active than Arp5R3 with DNA moving 2.7-fold faster and occurring in 2.4-fold more nucleosomes (Figure 7A–B and Table 1). Like Arp5R3, the Arp5DBD complex is also more defective moving DNA on the exit side than Arp5LLD (Figure 7A and B and Table 1). In contrast, the Arp5LLD complex on the entry side moves DNA less than Arp5R3 and Arp5DBD as seen by the reduced rate in the loss of the starting DNA contact (Figure 7C). Furthermore, Arp5LLD displaces DNA from the histone octamer 2- to 4-fold less than Arp5R3, Arp5DBD and WT and after only 20 s stops displacing DNA (Figure 7D). The LLD region appears to have a more critical role for moving and displacing DNA from the histone octamer on the entry side than DNA movement on the exit side.

DiscussionWe have identified a region of the Arp5 subunit of INO80 required in vitro and in vivo for INO80 remodeling activity that binds free H2A–H2B dimers, associates with the lateral face of nucleosomes and is potentially involved in H2A/H2A.Z dimer exchange by INO80. A peptide spanning residues 557 to 581 of Arp5 binds free H2A–H2B dimers that is dependent on a short acidic hydrophobic patch consisting of Leu 567, Leu 568 and Asp 571 (LLD). The surface of the H2A–H2B dimer bound by this region does not correspond to the region normally exposed in nucleosomes as the peptide is unable to bind nucleosomes or inhibit INO80 remodeling as does an arginine anchor. Nonetheless, this region is bound in the vicinity of the acidic pocket of nucleosomes in an LLD dependent manner and is required for stable binding of the grappler domain. The LLD region could bind to the surface of dimers exposed either due to INO80 binding that lifts DNA off the H2A–H2B consistent with the cryo-EM structure of INO80 nucleosome (28,29) and/or DNA being lifted off the dimer by INO80 during nucleosome remodeling(13). Interestingly, we observe the LLD mutant has the most pronounced adverse impact on INO80 remodeling at the stage of DNA displacement. Nonetheless, this region is bound in the vicinity of the acidic pocket of nucleosomes in an LLD dependent manner and is required for stable binding of the grappler domain. The LLD region could bind to the surface of dimers exposed either due to INO80 binding that lifts DNA off the H2A–H2B consistent with the cryo-EM structure of INO80 nucleosome (28,29) and/or DNA being lifted off the dimer by INO80 during nucleosome remodeling(13). Interestingly, we observe the LLD mutant has the most pronounced adverse impact on INO80 remodeling at the stage of DNA displacement. H2A.Z is specifically replaced by H2A on the same entry side of nucleosomes where mutation of LLD interferes with DNA displacement as shown using 3-color single molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer, consistent with earlier biochemical data (13,23). These data point to the LLD region having the ability to promote DNA lifting  off the histone octamer in a manner tied to binding the normally excluded surface of H2A–H2B and potentially links the LLD region to dimer exchange.

The connection of the LLD region to INO80 replacing H2A.Z with H2A is further validated based on in vivo assays examining the response to DNA replication stress using HU. INO80 has also been shown in vivo to replace H2A.Z with H2A in homologous recombination, maintenance of genome integrity, normal DNA replication and recovery from replication stress (51–54). INO80 facilitates in the response to DNA replication stress by removing H2A.Z in order to resolve R-loops formed by replication forks colliding with transcription complexes (7,51,55). In this study, we find that mutation of LLD produces an HU or DNA replication stress-sensitive phenotype consistent with the LLD region being required for the dimer exchange activity of INO80. Although mutation of LLD also reduces the nucleosome mobilizing activity of INO80, it is not likely the reason for its HU-sensitivity since mutation of the arginine anchor of Arp5 is not HU-sensitive even though it severely inhibits INO80 nucleosome mobilizing activity more than mutation of LLD.

Structural modeling of the grappler domain bound to the lateral face of the histone octamer highlights that the LLD region and arginine anchor cannot simultaneously bind to the same region of the nucleosomes and are on opposite faces of the grappler domain. Site-specific histone-Arp5 crosslinking and peptide mapping reveals the grappler domain binds nucleosomes in two different conformations, one in which the arginine anchor is bound to the acidic pocket and the other with the LLD region associated nearby. We validate that both regions are bound to the lateral face of nucleosomes by mutation of the arginine anchor and the LLD residues equally diminishing Arp5 interactions near the acidic pocket. Mutation of the LLD region is not expected to perturb binding of the arginine anchor based on structural constraints and modeling and therefore the loss of grappler domain binding with mutant LLD is most likely a direct effect.

The two-conformation model of the grappler domain suggests that dimer exchange by INO80 may involve switching the grappler domain surface engaged with nucleosomes. Given the properties of the LLD region, LLD could bind a free H2A–H2B dimer and then rotate into the position where it could switch out the H2A.Z–H2B for H2A–H2B dimer. We find the grappler domain is likely prone to releasing its contact with the nucleosome face, based on the relatively weak binding of the arginine anchor of Arp5 to the acid pocket and the lack of the LLD region binding alone to nucleosomes. The transient binding of the grappler domain to nucleosomes would facilitate in the dynamic switching back and forth of its binding interface.

Other evidence consistent with the dynamic shifting of the grappler domain comes by investigating the potential role of the DBD domain binding DNA at SHL-2 and -3 coupled to shifting of the grappler domain. We observe that mutation of DBD alone does not reduce binding of the grappler domain either at the acidic pocket or near residue 80 of H3 proximal, but when combined with the LLD mutant there is a dramatic loss of grappler domain binding that is much greater than either mutation alone. These data suggest the DBD domain is necessary to tether the grappler domain when the binding of one conformation of the grappler is reduced. Although there may be several reasons for this observation, it seems plausible that the DBD domain could become critical for the grappler when in the process of switching between conformations one of them is unable to bind. In this situation the loss of DBD binding to DNA would cause a greater tendency for the grappler to dissociate from the nucleosome and thereby be unable to switch back. With this in mind, it is interesting to point out the loss of both LLD and arginine anchor binding to nucleosomes is phenotypically similar to the combined mutation of LDD and DBD consistent with the proposed model.
