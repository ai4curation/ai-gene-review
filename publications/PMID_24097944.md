Abstract

A variety of bacterial pathogenicity determinants, including the type VI secretion system and the virulence cassettes from Photorhabdus and Serratia , share an evolutionary origin with contractile-tailed myophages. The well-characterized Escherichia coli phage P2 provides an excellent system for studies related to these systems, as its protein composition appears to represent the “minimal” myophage tail. In this study, we used nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy to determine the solution structure of gpX, a 68-residue tail baseplate protein. Although the sequence and structure of gpX are similar to those of LysM domains, which are a large family associated with peptidoglycan binding, we did not detect a peptidoglycan-binding activity for gpX. However, bioinformatic analysis revealed that half of all myophages, including all that possess phage T4-like baseplates, encode a tail protein with a LysM-like domain, emphasizing a widespread role for this domain in baseplate function. While phage P2 gpX comprises only a single LysM domain, many myophages display LysM domain fusions with other tail proteins, such as the DNA circulation protein found in Mu-like phages and gp53 of T4-like phages. Electron microscopy of P2 phage particles with an incorporated gpX-maltose binding protein fusion revealed that gpX is located at the top of the baseplate, near the junction of the baseplate and tail tube. gpW, the orthologue of phage T4 gp25, was also found to localize to this region. A general colocalization of LysM-like domains and gpW homologues in diverse phages is supported by our bioinformatic analysis.

INTRODUCTION

Phages with contractile tails (myophages) infect diverse clades of bacteria and are the second-most prevalent class of phages ( 1 ). Interest in the functioning of myophage tails has been heightened in recent years by the discovery that the type VI secretion system, a widespread and clinically relevant system of Gram-negative bacteria, is evolutionarily related to myophage tails and shares several common components ( 2 , 3 ). In addition, bacteriocins such as the R-type pyocins of Pseudomonas aeruginosa ( 4 ), and virulence determinants of Photorhabdus and Serratia ( 5 , 6 ) are also closely related to myophage tails. Thus, structural and functional studies of myophage tail systems will advance our knowledge of these complex molecular machines and provide insight into mechanisms of bacterial pathogenesis.

The contractile phage tail consists of a tube assembled from the polymerization of a single protein. This tube is fully coated by a sheath, which is also a polymer of a single protein. A baseplate assembly and associated fibers positioned at the tip of the tail are responsible for binding to the cell surface and mediating injection of the phage genome into the host cell. Upon interaction with the host, large conformational changes in the baseplate are coupled with contraction of the tail sheath and lead to penetration of the cell envelope by the tail tube ( 7 ).

The structure and function of the Escherichia coli myophage T4 baseplate and tail have been characterized extensively and have served as the paradigm for myophage tails ( 7 ). The T4 baseplate is comprised of 12 different proteins. Seven of these proteins interact to form a wedge; six wedges are then assembled radially around a ring-shaped central hub ( 8 ). A tail spike responsible for membrane penetration is attached below the hub and fibers are positioned at the periphery of each wedge subunit. Although the phage T4 tail has been the most intensively studied, many myophages possess baseplates that are far simpler in structure. The myophage genomes that are found within bacterial genomes in the form of prophages invariably encode simpler types of baseplates distinct from the T4 family of phages. Since the type VI secretion system, R-pyocins, and Photorhabdus and Serratia virulence cassettes were likely derived from prophages, a deeper understanding of the less complex classes of myophage baseplates will provide important insight into these systems.

E. coli phage P2 is one of the best-characterized phages with a simple myophage tail. The P2 tail baseplate is likely made up of only six different proteins and appears to represent the “minimal” myophage baseplate ( 7 ). Among the P2 baseplate constituents, gpD, the baseplate hub protein, and gpV, the tail spike protein, are homologues of the T4 proteins performing the same functions, gp27 and gp5, respectively ( 7 ). In addition, the P2 baseplate protein gpW is a homologue of T4 gp25, the structure and function of which are not well characterized. The P2 proteins gpI and gpJ are believed to comprise the baseplate wedges ( 7 ), and gpJ has been detected at the periphery of the P2 baseplate ( 9 ). While gpJ has weak sequence similarity to gp6 of T4, no similarity of gpI to T4 baseplate proteins has been identified. A final 67-residue P2 protein, gpX, is required for tail formation ( 10 ). This protein may be located in the baseplate, but no studies have yet addressed this issue.

In this paper, we focus on phage P2 gpX, which presents a particularly interesting subject of study because its sequence resembles that of LysM domains. LysM domains (Pfam family PF01476) occur in more than 27,000 proteins in all kingdoms of life. They are found in innate immunity proteins in humans, defense proteins in plants, peptidoglycan-degrading enzymes in prokaryotes, and many other protein classes. A number of LysM domain proteins have been implicated in the binding of peptidoglycan or chitin, but few have been characterized in detail. Because of gpX's probable location in the P2 baseplate, we speculated that a potential peptidoglycan-binding activity of gpX could be involved in the injection of phage DNA into the host cell, which is mediated by the baseplate complex and must involve penetration of peptidoglycan present in the bacterial cell wall. To address this issue and more generally investigate the role of gpX in P2 virion function, we have performed structural studies utilizing nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and electron microscopy, in vivo functional assays, and bioinformatic analyses. Our studies show that gpX does adopt a LysM fold and is a component of the baseplate. Furthermore, we found that a large number of myophage baseplates contain proteins with LysM-like domains.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Expression plasmid construction.

Genes X and W were individually cloned into plasmid pMal.c4x (NEB) using the EcoRI and XbaI cloning sites such that the maltose binding protein (MBP) was fused to the N terminus of each protein. Gene X was cloned into a pET21d vector (Novagen) derivative with a C-terminal 6-His affinity tag for nuclear magnetic resonance, complementation, and peptidoglycan binding studies. Spr, a lipoprotein involved in cell wall metabolism with a C-terminal NLPC_P60 domain, was cloned into the NdeI site of a pET15b vector (Novagen) derivative modified such that a tobacco etch virus (TEV)-cleavable 6-His tag was fused to its N terminus using ligation-independent cloning.

gpX purification and NMR spectroscopy.

Recombinant gpX with a C-terminal 6-His affinity tag was expressed in E. coli ER2566 (New England BioLabs) cells in 1 liter of M9 minimal medium enriched with 1 g of 15 NH 4 Cl and 2 g of 13 C 6 H 12 O 6 . The cells were grown to an optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) of 0.8, protein expression was induced by the addition of 1 mM isopropyl-β- d -thiogalactopyranoside IPTG, and the culture was incubated for 3 h at 37°C. The cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in binding buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 500 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole), lysed by sonication, purified in the same buffer using nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) agarose resin (Qiagen), and eluted by the addition of buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, and 250 mM imidazole. The eluted protein was dialyzed overnight at 4°C in 25 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.8), 200 mM NaCl, and 10% (vol/vol) D 2 O (or 100% [vol/vol] D 2 O for 13 C nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy [NOESY]).

NMR spectra were collected with a 1 mM protein sample at 25°C on a Varian INOVA 500-MHz or Bruker 800-MHz spectrometer at the Québec/Eastern Canada High Field NMR Facility. NMR data were processed using NMRPipe software ( 11 ), and chemical shifts were assigned using Sparky 3.114 ( 12 ). Backbone and aliphatic resonance assignments were attained using a combination of standard triple resonance experiments ( 13 , 14 ). Structure calculations were carried out with CYANA 2.0 ( 15 ) using automatically assigned and manually verified distance restraints from 15 N and 13 C NOESY experiments and dihedral angle restraints derived from TALOS ( 16 ) ( Table 1 ). One hundred structures were calculated, from which the 10 lowest energy structures were chosen for the ensemble and submitted to ProCheck-NMR analysis via PSVS (Protein Structure Validation Suite) for quality validation ( 17 ).

Peptidoglycan binding assay.

E. coli 594 cells were grown in LB to an OD 600 of 0.6, at which point the outer membrane was permeabilized by incubation in chloroform-saturated 0.05 M Tris-Cl buffer (pH 8) for 45 min ( 18 ). The permeabilized cells were washed twice with 20 mM Tris-Cl (pH 8) and resuspended in the same buffer at an OD 600 of 1.0. One hundred microliters of crude peptidoglycan preparation was mixed with 2.5 μg of gpX or Spr in a total volume of 150 μl and incubated in ice for 30 min. The samples were centrifuged at 4°C (6,000 × g ) for 4 min, and the supernatant was removed. The peptidoglycan pellets were washed twice with 150 μl of 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8) and resuspended in 150 μl of the same buffer. All samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE followed by Western blotting using anti-6-His antibodies.

Preparation of phage extracts.

E. coli C1a ( 19 ) cells were grown in Luria broth at 37°C with shaking until an OD 600 of 0.1 was reached. Phage P2 (wild type, Xam , or Wam ) was added to the culture at a multiplicity of infection of 0.01, and the culture was incubated at 37°C until cell lysis was complete. The cell debris was collected by centrifugation, and the resulting phage lysates were stored at 4°C. The titers of the phage lysates were assessed by spotting 4-μl aliquots of 100-fold serial dilutions onto a lawn of E. coli C1a or C1792 ( 20 ) suspended in 0.7% top agar.

Complementation of the Xam and Wam P2 phages, which contain amber nonsense mutations in genes X and W , respectively, was assessed by spotting serial dilutions of high-titer phage stocks prepared in the suppressor strain, C1792, onto C1a cells containing plasmids expressing MBP-tagged gpX or gpW. The degree of complementation was scored by observing plaque formation after overnight incubation at 37°C. To prepare the phages with MBP-tagged proteins incorporated for electron microscopy, C1a cells expressing MBP-tagged gpX or gpW were infected with the relevant amber-containing phage at a multiplicity of infection of 0.01 and were incubated at 37°C until cell lysis occurred. The cell debris was cleared from the phage lysate by centrifugation.

EM.

Carbon-coated grids were glow discharged, and 5 μl of phage lysate was applied for 2 min, followed by three washes with water. Samples were stained by submerging the grid in a 15-ml drop of 2% (wt/vol) uranyl acetate. Excess stain was blotted off with filter paper, and the grid was air dried for 2 min. Electron microscopy (EM) images were acquired on an H-7000 Hitachi transmission electron microscope, and images were captured using a 100-kV electron beam and recorded with a 6.0-megapixel charge-coupled-device (CCD) camera. Images for particle averaging were collected on a Tecnai F20 electron microscope, recorded on photographic films, and subsequently digitized. The phage particles were iteratively aligned using SPIDER ( 21 ).

Accession numbers.

The chemical shift assignments have been deposited in the BioMagResBank under accession number 18475. The coordinates for gpX were deposited in the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB) under accession code 2LTF.

RESULTS

Phage P2 gpX is a baseplate protein.

Phage P2 gpX had previously been identified as a tail protein by in vitro complementation studies ( 10 ). To confirm this finding and define the morphogenetic intermediates produced by phages lacking gpX, we used electron microscopy (EM) to examine the lysate of nonsuppressor cells infected by a P2 phage carrying an amber nonsense mutation in gene X ( Xam phage). The phage titer present in these lysates is less than 100 PFU per ml, which is 10 7 -fold lower than the titer of wild-type lysates produced under the same conditions. An abundance of DNA-filled free heads were observed in these lysates, but no mature viral particles or tail-like particles were found (data not shown). These data imply that the activity of gpX in tail assembly takes place early in the process, before tail tube and tail sheath polymerization.

To ensure that the effects on tail assembly caused by the Xam mutation were caused by the absence of gpX and not a polar effect, we measured the plating efficiency of this mutant phage on cells bearing a plasmid expressing wild-type gpX with an N-terminal hexahistidine tag. This plasmid was able to complement the Xam phage to a titer of 2 × 10 8 PFU/ml, which is approximately 10-fold less than that produced by infection of an amber suppressor strain (3 × 10 9 PFU/ml). We also found that a plasmid expressing an N-terminal fusion of maltose binding protein (MBP; 367 residues) to gpX was also able to complement the Xam phage with a titer 10-fold less than that of the amber suppressor.

To determine whether gpX is a component of the phage particle, we infected a nonsuppressor strain bearing the plasmid expressing the MBP-gpX fusion with the Xam phage. Phage particles resulting from this infection, which incorporated the MBP-gpX protein, were purified by centrifugation through two CsCl gradients and were examined by EM. Particle averaging of the negatively stained images of these phages clearly displayed a region of extra density at the top of the baseplate compared to wild-type particles ( Fig. 1 ). The symmetrical appearance of the extra density around the top of the baseplate suggested that gpX is distributed evenly around the baseplate in a ring-like structure.

gpW is adjacent to gpX in the baseplate.

The position of gpX at the top of the baseplate was similar to that expected for gpW, the orthologue of phage T4 gp25. gp25 surrounds the hub and likely interacts with the tail sheath protein ( 22 ). To determine if gpW is also localized to this region, we complemented a phage P2 Wam mutant with a plasmid expressing an MBP-gpW fusion protein. The resulting phage particles were purified and examined by EM in the manner described above for particles containing the MBP-gpX fusion protein. Like the P2 particles that incorporated MBP-gpX fusion protein, particles bearing the MBP-gpW fusion also displayed a region of extra density near the baseplate-tail tube junction ( Fig. 1 ). These data imply that gpW and gpX are located near each other in the baseplate structure. Like the Xam phage, lysates of cells infected with a P2 Wam phage contained filled heads but no tail-related structures (data not shown).

The gpX structure reveals a LysM fold.

To gain further insight into the mechanism of action of gpX, we determined its structure by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. We used a combination of two- and three-dimensional experiments to assign greater than 98% of the 1 H, 13 C, and 15 N chemical shifts of the backbone and side chain atoms for residues 1 to 67. An ensemble of the 10 lowest energy structures is shown in Fig. 2A . The structured region of the protein (residues 1 to 56) is well defined, with a backbone root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 0.61 Å, while the 11 C-terminal residues lacked long-range NOEs and appeared to be disordered. The unstructured nature of the C-terminal residues was confirmed through assessment of the { 1 H}- 15 N heteronuclear-NOEs, which indicated dynamics on the ns-ps timescale within this region (data not shown). gpX displays a βααβ secondary structure, with the two α-helices packing onto the same side of an antiparallel β-sheet ( Fig. 2B ). This fold closely resembles that of LysM domains, as was predicted by sequence similarity. The similarity of the gpX structure to that of LysM domains was confirmed by searching the protein structure database using the program DALI ( 23 ). This revealed a number of LysM domains with significant structural similarity to gpX. For example, the structures of the LysM domains of Bacillus subtilis l , d -transpeptidase YkuD (PDB identification code [ID], 3ZQD) and the E. coli membrane-bound lytic murein transglycosylase D (MltD; PDB ID, 1E0G) could be overlaid onto gpX with RMSD values of 1.2 Å and 1.6 Å, respectively ( Fig. 2C ).

To correlate the gpX structure with conservation observed in the family of gpX homologues encoded in phage genomes, we aligned the sequences of 14 of these homologues ( Fig. 3A ). These sequences showed 33 to 75% sequence identity with gpX. Residues at 18 positions in the gpX structure were buried (<30% exposed to solvent), and 15 of these positions exhibited a high degree of conservation in the alignment ( Fig. 3A , yellow residues). In contrast, only 13 out of 32 exposed (non-Gly) residues in the structured part of gpX were highly conserved. Interestingly, most of these conserved exposed positions were found on one face of the gpX structure ( Fig. 3B , red residues), suggesting that this may be the interaction interface of gpX with other components of the baseplate, such as gpW. Supporting the identification of this functionally important surface of gpX, we found that the nine most variable residue positions in the structured portion of gpX are localized to the opposite side of the protein ( Fig. 3B , blue residues). As expected from its unstructured nature, a portion of the gpX C terminus (residues 57 to 62) displays low conservation. However, a very high degree of conservation is observed in four of the last five residues, suggesting that these residues may play an important functional role ( Fig. 3A ).

Purified monomeric gpX does not bind peptidoglycan.

As most bacterial LysM domains are found in bacterial peptidoglycan hydrolases and are necessary for peptidoglycan binding, we assayed the ability of gpX to bind to E. coli peptidoglycan. The membrane-anchored E. coli cell wall hydrolase Spr ( 24 , 25 ) was used as a positive control. Following incubation with peptidoglycan preparations from E. coli strain 594, the peptidoglycan and bound proteins were collected by centrifugation and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Spr localized to the pellet fraction, indicating binding to peptidoglycan, while gpX remained in the supernatant ( Fig. 4 ). These results indicate that purified monomeric gpX does not bind to peptidoglycan under these assay conditions.

LysM domains are a common component of myophage tails.

The P2 gpX sequence is a member of the Phage_Tail_X Pfam family (PF05489). A search of 210 myophage genomes present in the NCBI Refseq database with the hidden Markov model (HMM) corresponding to this family revealed highly significant hits ( P < 10 −7 ) to proteins encoded in 21 myophage genomes (see Table S1 in the supplemental material). These proteins were all less than 80 residues in length and were encoded within the morphogenetic regions of the phage genomes. The genomes of 15 of these phages were arranged like P2, with their X -like genes located between the head and tail genes ( Fig. 5A ). Four phages, exemplified by Vibrio phage VHML, displayed a tail gene arrangement very similar to that of P2 except that the X -like gene was located between genes encoding baseplate proteins related to gpU and gpD (baseplate hub) of phage P2. Two additional phages, exemplified by Burkholderia phage BcepMu, also possessed X -like genes located between genes encoding gpU and gpD homologues. However, the tail genes of these phages were arranged in a manner distinct from that of phage P2 but similar to the canonical arrangement found in many other myophages ( Fig. 5A ). The discovery of X -like genes located next to other genes encoding baseplate proteins supports our finding that P2 gpX is localized to the baseplate ( Fig. 1 ).

Fig 5.

The Phage_Tail_X Pfam family is included in the LysM clan (CL0187). Searches of myophage genomes with HMMs corresponding to the other two family members of this clan, LysM (PF01476) and OapA (PF04225), revealed a number of proteins containing domains with similarity to the LysM HMM. Many of these proteins were likely baseplate proteins because the genes encoding them were adjacent genes encoding known baseplate proteins. Strikingly, all of these LysM-containing putative baseplate proteins were much longer than gpX and appeared to possess other domains. Further PSI-BLAST searches and analysis of these proteins using HMM profile-profile comparisons as implemented in the HHpred program ( 26 ) revealed that 14 phages encode proteins consisting of an N-terminal LysM-like domain fused to a C-terminal domain with high similarity to phage P2 gpW, as illustrated by Bacillus phage SPO1 ( Fig. 5 ; see also Table S2 in the supplemental material). The identification of proteins comprised of a LysM-like domain fused to a gpW-like domain is consistent with our finding that gpX and gpW are located in the same region of the baseplate ( Fig. 1 ). We also discovered eight proteins similar to Clostridium phage phiC2 gp21, consisting of an N-terminal domain related to phage P2 gpU fused to a C-terminal LysM like domain ( Fig. 5 ). A homologous protein, called XkdP, is also found in the well-characterized defective B. subtilis prophage PBSX ( 27 ). XkdP was previously mistakenly identified as a lysin ( 28 ). Although the function of gpU has not been characterized, the location of the U gene between the genes encoding the tape measure and baseplate hub proteins suggests that this protein forms a ring between the bottom of the tail tube and the baseplate hub, similar to those that have been observed in the baseplate structures of phage T4 (gp48/gp54) and in siphophages infecting Gram-positive bacteria (i.e., the Dit proteins of Bacillus phage SPP1 [ 29 ] and Lactobacillus phage TP901-1 [ 30 ]). A relationship between gpU and the Dit proteins is supported by our HHpred results in which similarity was detected between the Pfam HMM corresponding to the Dit proteins (Sipho_tail), gpU, and phiC2 gp21 ( Fig. 5B ; see also Table S2). Since gpU is likely positioned at the tail tube-baseplate junction, the same position indicated for gpX by our EM data, the existence of fusions between gpU-like domains and LysM-like domains is also expected.

Two other families of phage baseplate proteins were predicted by HHpred to contain LysM-like domains. Homologues of the DNA circulation protein (product of gene N ) of E. coli phage Mu were predicted to possess a LysM-like domain at their C terminus. This protein is required for tail assembly of phage Mu ( 31 ) and also is responsible for circularizing the phage DNA after it has been injected into the host cell. Consistent with the positioning of the genes encoding DNA circulation proteins between genes encoding the tape measure and baseplate hub proteins, the N-terminal domain of these proteins is predicted by HHpred to be related to gpU and to the N-terminal domain of the Dit proteins ( Fig. 5B ; see also Table S2 in the supplemental material). A final protein family predicted to contain LysM-like domains is Pfam family Phage_gp53, which includes phage T4 gp53 ( Fig. 5B ). We identified 51 phages that possess T4-like baseplates (see Table S1 in the supplemental material), and all of these conserve this LysM-like domain in their gp53 homologue. Remarkably, structural analyses by cryo-electron microscopy have shown that gp53 is located adjacent to gp25 ( 22 ), the gpW homologue of T4, near the top of the T4 baseplate structure. Thus, the theme of a LysM-like domain being located near a gpW-like protein at the top of the baseplate is repeated in the baseplates of T4 and related phages. A complete list of myophage baseplate proteins containing LysM-like domains is provided in Table S1.

Although the families of LysM-like domains that we identified in diverse myophages are distinct from one another in sequence (<20% sequence identity), an alignment of representative LysM-like sequences from each family and the non-phage-derived LysM domain from MltD ( Fig. 3C ) displays many conserved positions ( Fig. 6 ). For example, predominantly hydrophobic residues are observed at eight of nine positions in the alignment that correspond to buried (<10% solvent exposed) hydrophobic residues in the structure of gpX. In addition, Gly residues are conserved at three different positions corresponding to turns in the gpX and MltD structures. An Asn residue (position 33 in gpX) is completely conserved in all of the sequences, and two surface-exposed hydrophobic positions (Leu11 and Leu30) are also highly conserved. These conserved hydrophobic positions lie in the center of the conserved surface patch on the gpX structure ( Fig. 3A ), suggesting that these diverse phage LysM domains may share an interaction interface. The conservation of residues at most of the key positions responsible for stabilizing the LysM structure implies that all of these sequences do adopt the LysM fold.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we have determined the NMR structure of phage P2 gpX, and as was predicted from its sequence, it adopts a fold very similar to that of typical LysM domains. Electron microscopy of P2 particles containing MBP-tagged gpX clearly showed that gpX is located at the top of the baseplate near the junction with the tail tube. The identity of gpX as a baseplate protein was also supported by the complete lack of tail-like particles that we observed in the lysate of a phage mutant lacking gpX. Since the tail tube of myophages is likely to be driven through the outer cell membrane during the DNA injection process, the positioning of gpX at the bottom of the tube, where it may remain attached after membrane penetration, could allow it to contact the peptidoglycan layer. In this way a peptidoglycan-binding function of gpX, which is the most common putative function for bacterial LysM domains, might facilitate DNA injection. Contradicting this idea, we were not able to detect peptidoglycan-binding activity for purified monomeric gpX. It is possible that conformational changes or oligomerization of gpX that occurs during virion assembly may confer a peptidoglycan-binding ability on gpX. Alternately, despite its LysM-like fold, the function of gpX may not involve interaction with peptidoglycan. Notwithstanding the uncertainty of its function, our work provides the first experimental evidence that a protein bearing a LysM fold is required for the morphogenesis of a myophage tail.

To determine whether LysM domains are commonly occurring constituents of myophage tails, we performed a bioinformatic analysis of the 210 sequenced myophage genomes available in the NCBI Refseq database ( 32 ). Remarkably, we found that approximately half of myophage genomes, including all myophages with T4-like baseplates, encode a likely tail baseplate protein containing a LysM-like domain. LysM domains are typically found as parts of larger proteins with a variety of domain architectures; Pfam currently lists 602 different architectures for the LysM domain-containing proteins. Consistent with this observation, most tail-associated LysM domains occur in proteins with other distinguishable domains, such as the phage P2 gpU-like domains or gpW-like domains. In contrast, the gpX homologues in phages with P2-like tails ( Fig. 5 ) provide an unusual example of proteins comprised of only a LysM domain. Our discovery of diverse phages encoding LysM domain-gpW fusions strongly validates our EM data showing that P2 gpX and gpW localize to the same region of the baseplate and implies that the colocalization of LysM domains and gpW homologues is an important functional feature of myophage baseplates. Our results are also consistent with cryo-EM studies on the phage T4 baseplate showing that the LysM-like domain containing gp53 is located adjacent to gp25, the homologue of gpW. Finally, our identification of diverse proteins composed of a phage P2 gpU-like N-terminal domain and a LysM-like C-terminal domain suggests that gpU is also located at the tail tube-baseplate junction in the region of gpX and gpW localization. This finding coupled with the HHpred-detected similarities between Dit proteins, gpU, and the phage Mu DNA circulation protein (Mup43) support previous speculation that these proteins play a role similar to that of the phage T4 gp48/gp54 tail tube initiator complex, forming a ring at the junction between the tail tube and baseplate ( 7 ).

Many tailed phages infecting both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria possess virion proteins that display peptidoglycan hydrolytic activities ( 33 ). It has been speculated that these activities are required for phage to penetrate the peptidoglycan cell wall during genome injection. Paradoxically, phage P2, phage Mu, and many related phages possess no proteins in their tails with recognizable domains associated with peptidoglycan hydrolysis. Perhaps their LysM-like domains interact with peptidoglycan to facilitate DNA injection without actually effecting hydrolysis. The LysM-like domain may have evolved to play a purely structural role in at least some phage tails and provides another example, similar to the Ig-like fold ( 34 , 35 ) and tail tube fold ( 2 , 36 ), of a commonly occurring fold that phages may have adopted for a variety of functions.
