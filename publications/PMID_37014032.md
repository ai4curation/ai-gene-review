Myoregulin (MLN) is a member of the regulin family, a group of homologous
membrane proteins that bind to and regulate the activity of the sarcoplasmic
reticulum Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA). MLN, which is expressed in skeletal muscle,
contains an acidic residue in its transmembrane domain. The location of this
residue, Asp35, is unusual because the relative occurrence of aspartate is very
rare (<0.2%) within the transmembrane helix regions. Therefore, we used
atomistic simulations and ATPase activity assays of protein co-reconstitutions
to probe the functional role of MLN residue Asp35. These structural and
functional studies showed Asp35 has no effects on SERCA's affinity for Ca2+ or
the structural integrity of MLN in the lipid bilayer. Instead, Asp35 controls
SERCA inhibition by populating a bound-like orientation of MLN. We propose Asp35
provides a functional advantage over other members of the regulin family by
populating preexisting MLN conformations required for MLN-specific regulation of
SERCA. Overall, this study provides new clues about the evolution and functional
divergence of the regulin family and offers novel insights into the functional
role of acidic residues in transmembrane protein domains.

Introduction

Myoregulin (MLN) is a member of the regulin proteins, a family of transmembrane proteins that regulate the activity of the calcium pump (sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase, SERCA). MLN is a 46-residue transmembrane protein encoded by a putative long non-coding RNA, and it is found primarily in adult mouse skeletal muscle.1–2 MLN shares functional and structural similarities with the regulin proteins sarcolipin (SLN) and phospholamban (PLN), which form heterodimeric complexes with the calcium pump3 and modulate SERCA-mediated Ca2+ transport in muscle cells.4 MLN knock-out mice show enhanced SERCA-dependent calcium handling and improved exercise performance.2 MLN also plays a role in modulating Ca2+ homeostasis in a murine model of Duchene myopathy.5 We have previously shown that MLN has similar functional effects on SERCA as those induced by SLN, i.e., partially inhibiting the ATPase activity of the pump. However, we also found that, unlike PLN and SLN, MLN does not influence SERCA’s Ca2+ affinity.4 Together, these studies indicate that MLN is a novel regulator SERCA with unique functional properties.

MLN binds to skeletal muscle SERCA (SERCA1a) in a 1:1 heterodimeric complex (Fig. 1A).3 This protein has a cytosolic domain (residues ~1–17) that is unstructured and oriented perpendicularly to the membrane normal, a transmembrane helix (residues 18–40), and a short luminal domain (residues 41–46) (Fig. 1A).4 This topology is consistent with that of PLN and SLN and shows that MLN shares a substantial structural similarity with other members of the regulin family.4 MLN differs from SLN and PLN because it has an acidic residue, Asp35, in its transmembrane domain (Fig. 1A).4 The position of Asp35 in the membrane is unusual because negatively charged residues are in the flanks of transmembrane helices6 and are often found in marginally hydrophobic transmembrane regions.7 Indeed, the occurrence of aspartate within the transmembrane helix regions has been estimated at 0.16%.8 Given the unusual location and low occurrence of aspartate in transmembrane helices, we hypothesize that this residue may serve a unique functional role in regulating SERCA. In this study, we used complementary atomistic simulations and ATPase activity assays of SERCA1a–MLN co-reconstitutions to understand the functional role of residue Asp35 in the transmembrane domain of MLN. In all cases, we used a two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test to determine differences between MLN peptides.

We first evaluated whether Asp35 is an anchor residue that confers stability to the SERCA–MLN interface in a lipid environment. We used molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to study the complexes of SERCA bound to wild-type MLN (MLNWT), and MLN mutations Asp35Leu (MLND35L) and Asp35Val (MLND35V). We chose these mutations because sequence alignment showed that structurally related positions of Asp35 in MLN are occupied by leucine in PLN (Leu42), and valine in SLN (Val19);4 see supplementary figure S1 (Supporting Information). We used the AMBER20 on Tesla V100 GPUs9 using the AMBER ff19SB force field10 to generate five independent 1-μs trajectories of each SERCA‒MLN complex; detailed computational protocols are in Supporting Information. The complexes were stable in the microsecond timescale as shown by the time-dependent evaluation of the backbone root-mean-square deviation (Figures S2-S3, Supporting Information).

Analysis of μs-long MD trajectories of the SERCA–MLNWT complex showed that the indole proton of SERCA residue Trp794 interacts stably with residue Asp35 of MLNWT in all MD trajectories of the complex (Fig. 1B-C). The formation of a stable interaction between Trp794 and Asp35 is consistent with root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis showing that the region of residue Asp35 has the lowest mobility averaged over all MD trajectories (RMSF<1.3 Å, Fig. 1D). Although polar residues are less abundant in transmembrane protein-protein contacts, the interaction between polar residues of opposite charges are predicted to be generally strong,11 thus suggesting that mutation of Asp35 to a non-polar residue should influence the SERCA-MLN interaction. We found that mutants MLND35L and MLND35V maintain their interaction with SERCA residue Trp794 (Fig. 1B-C) and further stabilize the SERCA–MLN complex around positions Leu35 or Val35 (RMSF≤1 Å, Fig. 1D).

Next, we determined if Asp35 is an element required to modulate SERCA through structural changes in the Ca2+-transport sites. We have previously shown that residue Lys27 of MLN occupies the position of residue Asn11 of SLN Asn34 of PLN.4 Asparagine in this position is essential for modulation of Ca2+ affinity by PLN and SLN, 12–13 by producing a geometry of the transport sites that is incompetent for Ca2+ binding to SERCA.14–15 Therefore, we tested if Lys27 of MLNWT mimics the effect of asparagine in SLN/PLN and whether this effect is altered by mutants MLND35L and MLND35V. In the SERCA–MLN complex, Lys27 either interacts directly with residue Asp800, sequestering this residue from the Ca2+ transport site (Fig. 2A) or faces away from the SERCA–MLN interface (Fig. 2B). We found that in the SERCA–MLNWT complex, this interaction (Lys27–Asp800 distance <6 Å) changes the geometry of the transport sites by increasing the distance between Glu771 and Asp800 of SERCA (Fig. 2C, red rectangle). However, the interaction between Lys27 and Asp800 in the SERCA-MLNWT complex exists only temporarily during the entire 5 μs of simulation time (Fig. 2C). Surprisingly, we found that the interaction landscape between residue Lys27 of mutants MLND35L and MLND35V and SERCA is similar to that induced by MLNWT (Fig. 2D-E).

The atomistic simulations showed that (i) MLN residue Asp35 is not necessary for the stability of the SERCA–MLN complex, (ii) MLNWT does not change the apparent Ca2+ affinity of SERCA and (iii) MLN residue Asp35 is not an element necessary to control Ca2+ affinity by SERCA. We tested these MD-based predictions using ATPase activity assays of skeletal muscle SERCA1a and MLNWT, MLND35L, or MLND35V co-reconstituted in proteoliposomes; detailed experimental protocols are in Supporting Information. Ca2+ concentration-dependent curves showed that compared to the SERCA alone (negative control), MLNWT decreases the maximal velocity (Vmax) of SERCA by 20% (Fig. 3A). MLN mutants MLND35L and MLND35V also decrease the Vmax of SERCA by 10% and 15%, respectively (Fig. 3B-C). Despite the decreased activity of the mutants vs the wild-type control, our experiments indicate that, upon mutation of residue Asp35, MLN can exert an inhibitory effect on SERCA. We also found that MLNWT inhibits SERCA without changing its apparent Ca2+ affinity (KCa, Table 1), and that mutation of Asp35 to leucine or valine does not induce significant effects on the KCa of SERCA (p>0.99; Table 1). The interaction landscape of MLN residue Lys27 and SERCA residue Asp800 (Fig. 2) explains the inability of MLNWT and mutants MLND35L and MLND35V to alter the apparent Ca2+ affinity of SERCA. Specifically, changes in KCa by regulators PLN and SLN involve substantial interdomain interactions within this region of the SERCA-regulin interface,16–18 but these changes are not induced by any of the MLN peptides tested here (Table 1).

There is an agreement between the atomistic simulations and ATPase experiments showing that mutations of residue Asp35 decrease but do not abolish the inhibitory activity of MLN. Yet, the significant inhibitory effect on SERCA induced by MLNWT (p<0.01) was observed for the mutant MLND35V (p<0.05) but not for MLND35L (p=0.0848) (Table 1). These findings are intriguing and show that MLN residue Asp35 is a functional element required for the regulation of SERCA. Our simulations showed that the mobility of the luminal tail of MLN changes upon the introduction of Asp35 mutations (Fig. 2). Therefore, the mobility of the luminal tail in MLNWT may play a functional role in the regulation of SERCA, as observed previously for the homologous regulator SLN.19 Asp35 then controls the functional dynamics of this region via partial solvation of the luminal interface of the SERCA–MLN complex (Fig. 1C, top panel). We found that the introduction of leucine of valine at position Asp35 of MLN decreases the mobility of its luminal tail (ΔRMSF: −0.4 to −0.7 Å), in agreement with a loss of inhibitory function of mutants MLND35L and MLND35V (Fig. 1D). However, we argue that control of the luminal tail dynamics is not the main mechanisms for loss of inhibitory function because our simulations predict that the mobility of the luminal tail is larger in the SERCA–MLND35L than in the SERCA–MLND35V complex, in contrast with the ATPase experiments showing that MLND35V is more inhibitory than MLND35L (Table 1).

Charged residues in transmembrane domains do not desolvate entirely upon insertion into the lipid bilayer. Instead, these residues keep hydration by “snorkeling”, a phenomenon wherein charged groups reach out and escape the hydrophobic core of the membrane.20 Snorkeling may induce a torque that tilts entire transmembrane helices,21 so residue Asp35 in the transmembrane domain of MLN may control the inhibitory activity of MLN by modulating the tilt angle of its transmembrane domain. We tested this mechanistic hypothesis by performing five independent 1-μs atomistic simulations of the unbound MLNWT and mutants MLND35L and MLND35V in a lipid-water environment; detailed computational protocols are in the Supporting Information.

Analysis of the trajectories showed that the transmembrane helix of MLNWT is stable in the μs timescale, and that mutation of Asp35 to leucine or valine does not alter the stability of this domain (RMSD<2 Å; Fig. S5 in the Supporting Information). These findings show Asp35 is not required for the structural stability of the transmembrane domain of MLN. The lipid headgroup-protein interaction patterns are similar for all three MLN peptides (Fig. 4A), with one exception: the carboxylic moiety of Asp35 snorkels toward the membrane-water interface, but this effect is eliminated upon mutation of Asp35 to leucine or valine (Fig. 4A-B). We also found that, unlike MLND35L and MLND35V, MLNWT bends at residues Val31-Asp35 with an angle of 12 ± 2.4º (Fig. 4B). Bending of MLNWT likely occurs to compensate for desolvation and avoid large deformation of either the lipid bilayer or the protein.22 We found MLNWT crosses the lipid bilayer with a tilt angle of 32.5 ± 3.2º with respect to the membrane normal; remarkably, this behavior is highly reproducible across independent MD trajectories of MLNWT (Fig. 4C). Mutation of Asp35 to leucine or valine decreases the average tilt angle of MLN from ~32 º to ~19º (Fig. 4C). These changes are significant in both MLND35L (p= 0.0424) and MLND35V (p=0.0372), showing that snorkeling of Asp35 toward the lipid-water interface tilts the transmembrane helix of MLN. Remarkably, we found the tilt angle of MLN in the SERCA–MLNWT complex (tilt=39.7±2.3º; Fig. 4C, dashed trace) does not deviate significantly from the average tilt angle of unbound MLNWT (p=0.4087). Conversely, the tilt angles of MLND35L and MLND35V differ significantly from that of MLN in the SERCA–MLNWT complex (p-values of 0.0017 and 0.0015, respectively). We also found that most tilt angle distributions of MLND35L and MLND35V fit to a two-Gaussian distribution with tilt angle means at 12–17º and 23–25º (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, the populations of MLND35L shift more toward a lower tilt angle compared to MLND35V. These differences explain the functional differences between the WT and mutant peptides because a shift toward a lower tilt angle of the unbound MLN results in reduced inhibition of SERCA activity.

We note that the N-terminus of MLN is buried deeper into the membrane in the wild-type protein, but less so upon mutation of Asp35 to Leu or Val (Fig. 4A-B). These differences are likely induced by the change in tilt angle upon mutation of Asp35 to a hydrophobic residue, thus pushing the N-terminus toward the lipid headgroups. This structural change is modest, so we hypothesize that it does not contribute to the loss of inhibitory activity observed for the MLN mutants. Nevertheless, the function of the N-terminal domain of MLN remains unknown, so we do not rule out the possibility that the differences in the position of the N-terminal may have other functional consequences, e.g., regulation via phosphorylation of this domain (See Figure S1, Supporting Information).

The complementary simulations and experiments show that Asp35 controls SERCA inhibition by populating a bound-like orientation of MLN. These findings raise an important question: what is the functional advantage of MLN populating a bound-like state in the lipid bilayer? To answer this question, we refer to the concept of conformational memory,23 in which a choice of substrate conditions promotes specific conformational states from which SERCA activity can be inhibited.24 Based on this concept, we propose that Asp35 provides a functional advantage over other members of the regulin family through populating preexisting MLN conformations required for MLN-specific regulation of SERCA. Accordingly, Asp35 provides a functional advantage over other members of the regulin family. This functional effect is supported by spectroscopy studies showing that the relative affinity of MLN for SERCA is similar to that of PLN but higher than that of SLN.3 In summary, the structural and functional evidence presented in this study offers this study provides new clues on the evolution and functional divergence of the regulin family and offers novel insights into the functional role of acidic residues in transmembrane protein domains.
