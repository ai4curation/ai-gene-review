Comment in
    Immunol Cell Biol. 2024 Oct;102(9):752-756. doi: 10.1111/imcb.12816.

Innate immune pattern recognition receptors, such as the Toll-like receptors
(TLRs), are key mediators of the immune response to infection and central to our
understanding of health and disease1. After microbial detection, these receptors
activate inflammatory signal transduction pathways that involve IκB kinases,
mitogen-activated protein kinases, ubiquitin ligases and other adaptor proteins.
The mechanisms that connect the proteins in the TLR pathways are poorly defined.
To delineate TLR pathway activities, we engineered macrophages to enable
microscopy and proteomic analysis of the endogenous myddosome constituent MyD88.
We found that myddosomes form transient contacts with activated TLRs and that
TLR-free myddosomes are dynamic in size, number and composition over the course
of 24 h. Analysis using super-resolution microscopy revealed that, within most
myddosomes, MyD88 forms barrel-like structures that function as scaffolds for
effector protein recruitment. Proteomic analysis demonstrated that myddosomes
contain proteins that act at all stages and regulate all effector responses of
the TLR pathways, and genetic analysis defined the epistatic relationship
between these effector modules. Myddosome assembly was evident in cells infected
with Listeria monocytogenes, but these bacteria evaded myddosome assembly and
TLR signalling during cell-to-cell spread. On the basis of these findings, we
propose that the entire TLR signalling pathway is executed from within the
myddosome.

## Full Text

After microbial detection by sensing of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)2,3, most TLRs seed the formation of a cytoplasmic protein complex called the myddosome4–8. Myddosome activities within cells activate biochemically distinct effector protein complexes that collectively mediate inflammatory responses. For example, genetic analysis demonstrated that MyD88 acts upstream of the IKKα–IKKβ–IKKγ complex, which activates the inflammatory transcription factor nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB)9,10, diverse complexes of ubiquitin ligases and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs)10–13, which are associated with gene expression and autophagy, and the metabolic and interferon (IFN)-inducing protein kinase TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1)14,15.

Most studies of TLR signalling have examined endogenous gene functions (using knockout approaches) but correlated this genetic analysis with the study of non-endogenous protein activities (using overexpressed transgenes)5,8,16,17. As such, although biological models of TLR signalling are commonly illustrated in the publication record18–21, the accuracy of these models is largely speculative. Here we studied the endogenous TLR pathways in human and mouse macrophages. We provide evidence that myddosomes are dynamic and long-lived entities, numbering in the dozens within individual cells. We demonstrate that the complex cellular responses of the entire TLR pathway are coordinated and executed from within these structures.

Generation of MyD88 knock-in macrophagesTo study the native TLR signalling pathway, we edited the genomes of human and mouse macrophages to append a C-terminal tag to the endogenous MyD88 protein. We used a multifunctional tag (hereafter AGF) comprising the enzyme APEX222, monomeric EGFP (mEGFP)23 and a 3×Flag-tag (Extended Data Fig. 1). Comparisons of mRNA abundance demonstrated no difference in MYD88 or Myd88 expression between wild-type (WT) and MyD88–AGF knock-in human THP-1 cells or mouse immortalized bone-marrow-derived macrophages (iBM-DMs) (Extended Data Fig. 2a). WT and knock-in cells behaved similarly after stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to activate TLR4 or Pam3CSK4 (P3C) to activate TLR2, in magnitude and kinetics of signalling pathway activation, TLR surface localization, expression of TLR-inducible genes and cytokine secretion (Extended Data Fig. 2b–e). These results indicated no interference of the AGF-tag with the functions of MyD88 after TLR activation.

Consistent with this idea, confocal live-cell imaging revealed the durable lifetime of myddosomes (Fig. 1a). After LPS treatments, we observed that MyD88–AGF shifts from a uniform cytosolic distribution to a granular appearance representing hundreds of proto-myddosomes per cell (Fig. 1a). Within 10–15 min, proto-myddosomes gave rise to larger clusters, hereafter referred to as myddosomes, which persisted for more than 12 h (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Videos 2 and 3). We observed simultaneous formation of 1–2 even larger clusters per cell (>2 μm2), which we refer to as major myddosomes (Fig. 1a). We investigated the dynamics of the complexes in PAMP-treated macrophages by quantifying our imaging data (Fig. 1b; further details are provided in the Methods and Supplementary Fig. 1). Overall, the dynamics were conserved between species and independent of the inducing TLR (Fig. 1b). Tracking of myddosomes combined with lifetime analysis revealed that individual MyD88 complexes existed for an average of around 25 min, before they either disappeared, merged with other myddosomes or split into remnants (Fig. 1b). Extracting merging and splitting events of myddosomes in LPS-treated iBMDMs enabled us to correlate their dynamics with size, appearance and disappearance (Fig. 1c). Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assays of myddosomes revealed these complexes to be dynamic up to 3 h after PAMP treatment, with rapid fluorescence recovery (indicating influx of unbleached MyD88 molecules). At later timepoints, this plasticity was lost (Fig. 1d). When we extended the time course, we observed that the number of myddosomes declined after 12 h, with the myddosomes disappearing after 24 h (Fig. 1e). The observed kinetics of LPS-induced MyD88 clusters correlated well with the biochemical detection of MyD88–AGF in complex with IRAK2 and IRAK4, and MyD88 oligomerization (Fig. 1f,g). Distinct phases of myddosome dynamics therefore exist (summarized in Fig. 1h).We reasoned that, if the observed MyD88 clusters were functional myddosomes, then myddosome formation should ultimately be associated with NF-κB activation. We therefore generated iBMDM MyD88–AGF cells that produce mPlum-tagged p65 NF-κB, under the control of a doxycycline-inducible promoter (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b and Supplementary Video 4). We quantified NF-κB activation (defined as active if the p65 nuclear:cytosolic ratio was >1.0) alongside myddosome formation and found that myddosome formation preceded p65 nuclear translocation after TLR stimulation (Extended Data Fig. 4c,d). Stratifying these data revealed that cells with major myddosomes activated NF-κB faster and more efficiently compared with cells without major myddosomes (Extended Data Fig. 4e). Only cells with myddosomes activated NF-κB and all cells with active NF-κB signalling also contained myddosomes (Extended Data Fig. 4f). This finding supports the concept that the visualized structures within cells are functional signalling complexes—myddosomes.

Myddosomes dissociate from the seeding TLRPlotting the localization of myddosomes in LPS-treated macrophages over time demonstrated that the complexes were initially proximal to the plasma membrane and then collectively moved towards the cell centre (Fig. 2a). To determine whether the initial sites of myddosome assembly correlated with the positioning of TLR ligands, we used LPS-coated beads (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Video 5). Most myddosomes initially appeared adjacent to beads, but the proximity to beads dissipated over time (Fig. 2b). This raised the possibility that TLRs may not be stable myddosome constituents. To test this idea, we generated a polyclonal antibody that detects endogenous mouse TLR4 by immunoblot analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In a Flag immunoprecipitation analysis, we observed a complex between TLR4 and MyD88–AGF after treatment with LPS, but not P3C (Fig. 2c). This complex was transient and was lost after 30 min. These results suggest that, while TLRs seed myddosome assembly, most of the hours-long lifespan of a myddosome occurs in a TLR-free state.

Mapping myddosome componentsWe assessed the proportion of MyD88 contained within oligomeric complexes using differential detergent extraction (Fig. 2d). Approximately 35% of the cellular MyD88 pool became mild-detergent insoluble after LPS stimulation, indicating that MyD88 was contained within difficult to solubilize aggregates—presumably myddosomes (Fig. 2d). To map proteins that assemble into myddosomes, we used the APEX2 peroxidase in the AGF-tag to perform proximity biotinylation of MyD88-associated proteins at 0.5, 1, 3 or 12 h after PAMP treatment, followed by affinity purification of the biotinylated proteins and protein identification using multiplexed mass spectrometry (MS) (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). Initial high-level analysis confirmed sufficient enrichment of MyD88 in all datasets (Extended Data Fig. 6c). Principal component analysis demonstrated high precision of the replicates and clustering of the treated versus untreated samples (Extended Data Fig. 6d). To extract myddosome-associated proteins, we filtered and normalized the proteomics data and performed enrichment analysis compared with untreated cells (Extended Data Fig. 6e). This analysis produced a list of 55 total proteins that were significantly enriched over the time course of 12 h (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data 2). We performed STRING network analysis and k-means clustering, which revealed four protein modules that we defined as (1) the myddosome core, (2) signalling effector proteins, (3) autophagy related proteins and (4) IFN-stimulated gene products (Fig. 2e).The proteins in the myddosome core were the kinases IRAK2 and IRAK4, which were detected across species, timepoints and treatment conditions, and TLR2 which was detected only at early timepoints after P3C-treatment (Fig. 2e). Peptides corresponding to known myddosome components, the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRAF6 and TLR4, were not detected. However, direct biochemical analysis (described in Fig. 2c and below) confirmed the presence of these proteins in myddosomes. At 12 h after treatment, we found enrichment for autophagy related proteins including ATG16L1, LC3B, LAMP1 and p62 (Fig. 2e). Notably, proteins that have been classically defined as components of the IKK and MAPK complexes, acting downstream of the myddosome, were associated with MyD88 at timepoints up to 3 h after PAMP treatment (Fig. 2e). Distal signalling effectors in the IKK complex (NF-κB p65) and the MAPK complexes (p38α and ERK2) were also in proximity of MyD88 (Fig. 2e). Biochemical analysis confirmed that MyD88 formed a complex with the distal effectors p65 and p38α and the upstream kinase TBK1 up to 3 h after TLR stimulation, whereas p62 and LC3B associated at 12 h after stimulation (Extended Data Fig. 7a). MyD88 formed a complex with IRAK2, IRAK4 and TRAF6 in LPS- or P3C-treated macrophages across all examined timepoints (Extended Data Fig. 7a).We reasoned that, if the myddosomes represents the principal subcellular site that executes the entire TLR signalling pathway, then immunoisolations of myddosomes should deplete cell lysates of the signalling proteins associated with TLR activities. Consistent with this idea, Flag-immunoprecipitation of MyD88–AGF depleted the pool of phosphorylated IKKα/β, p38α, p65 and TBK1 from cell lysates at early timepoints of TLR signalling (Extended Data Fig. 7b). Under no condition did we detect phosphorylated STAT1 in myddosomes (Extended Data Fig. 7b). As NF-κB p65 and p38α represent distal effectors of their respective signalling pathways, we imaged their recruitment into myddosomes and nuclear translocation (Extended Data Fig. 7c). We also examined the TLR-responsive transcription factor IRF528. All three effectors initially accumulated in myddosomes with subsequent release and translocation to the nucleus (Extended Data Fig. 7c). Collectively, these findings suggest that the myddosome consists of a wide spectrum of proteins, which range from the upstream receptors to the terminal effector proteins in the TLR pathways.

Myddosomes organize into nanoscale signalling assembliesTo determine the composition of myddosomes at the single-organelle level, we quantified association of the identified proteins and overlap with markers for cellular compartments by fluorescence microscopy examination (Extended Data Fig. 8a). An initial assessment of LPS-treated iBMDMs using colocalization analysis showed specific accumulation of MyD88-proximal proteins with myddosomes, as compared to proto-myddosomes present in the cytosolic background (Fig. 3a). Indepth analysis of more than 250,000 individual myddosomes in human or mouse macrophages and classification based on protein accumulation using a deep convolutional neural network (Supplementary Fig. 1) validated the identified myddosome associated proteins (Fig. 3b). We observed nearly all myddosomes to contain TRAF6, IRAK2 and IRAK4 at all timepoints (Fig. 3b). We observed a high degree of ubiquitination, including Lys63- and Met1-linked (linear) poly-ubiquitination (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, we found active (phosphorylated) species of IKKα/β, TBK1, p38α and p65 within myddosomes (Fig. 3b). Staining with Proteostat showed no dye accumulation, indicating that the myddosomes were not aggregates of misfolded proteins (Fig. 3b). The p62 cargo receptor and autophagy-related proteins appeared in myddosomes starting from 12 h after PAMP treatment (Fig. 3b). The pattern-recognition receptors cGAS and NLRP3 were not associated with myddosomes (Fig. 3b), which was consistent with our proteomics data (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 2). Finally, we did not observe myddosome association with mitochondria (MitoTracker), the Golgi apparatus and trans-Golgi network (GM130 and TGN38), peroxisomes (PEX14), the microtubule-organizing centre (γ-tubulin), recycling endosomes (Rab11a) or stress granules (G3BP1), together suggesting that myddosomes represented standalone cytosolic signalling organelles (Fig. 3b). DiscussionOn the basis of the data presented, we suggest that the adaptors within the myddosome represent the core of a dynamic multifunctional signalling organelle that may execute the entire TLR signal transduction pathway (Supplementary Fig. 5). Several lines of evidence support this conclusion: (1) proteomic analysis identified MyD88-proximal proteins acting at all stages of the TLR pathways—from the receptors to the terminal kinases and transcription factors that act in the IKK complex, MAPK complexes and autophagy complexes. (2) MyD88 immunoprecipitations as early as 30 min after TLR activation depleted cell lysates of the active kinases that operate in diverse signalling pathways. This finding suggests the myddosome is the dominant subcellular site of signalling enzyme activation. (3) High-resolution microscopy revealed diverse signalling proteins in an organized architecture within myddosomes, with MyD88 forming barrel-like scaffolds with the IRAKs in the topological core. (4) Microscopy and genetic analysis revealed that, despite being activated within the same physical multiprotein complexes, the TLR signalling modules were functionally distinct and recruited independently of one another. This idea may apply to a diverse set of physiological conditions, as the dynamics of myddosome formation were comparable irrespective of species (human versus mouse), seeding TLR or agonist (pure PAMP versus infectious bacterium).We suggest that the earliest stage of TLR signalling involves the assembly of proto-myddosomes, which dissociate from the plasma membrane and the seeding TLR, and subsequently operate as building blocks to form cytosolic myddosomes. In the case of TIRF imaging, single-molecule mEGFP calibration images were used to detect proto-myddosomes based on fluorescence intensities that were >3 larger than the single GFP molecules. The average myddosome position over time was extracted by determining the centroid of each myddosome label within single cells, and merging the cells over time, thereby generating the overall cell outline/canvas and a topographical map depicting the movement of myddosomes.

Other object detection was performed using the BactFluorOmni neural network, as implemented in the Omnipose (v.1.0.6)67 framework for detection of Listeria. Nuclei were detected using the ImageJ plugin for StarDist (v.0.7.0)68. Label images of nuclei and cells were used for tracking of objects using the ImageJ plugin Trackmate (v.7.12.1)69,70 and the simple LAP tracker (cells did not divide after PAMP stimulation). Tracking was performed using the following empirically determined parameters: max. linking distance: 8 μm; max. gap closing max distance: 10 μm; and max. frame gap: 2. As myddosomes were highly dynamic and were merging/splitting over time, we used the myddosome label images and tracked the complexes using the LAP tracker. Tracking was performed using the following empirically determined parameters: max. linking distance: 2 μm; max. gap closing max distance: 4 μm; max. frame gap: 3; max distance (split): 0.5 μm; and max distance (merge): 0.5 μm. Calculated tracks were filtered for a minimal length of 3 frames for the myddosomes and 50 frames for the cells and nuclei to remove artefacts. The obtained tracking schemes were further used to determine the average myddosome lifetime as well as merge and split rates of myddosomes per cell. To produce myddosome fate maps (plotting size versus IRAK4:MyD88 ratio), myddosomes were tracked as described above but filtered for a minimum track length of 175 frames. Tracked labels were then classified on the basis of whether or not they exceed 2 μm2 for more than 10 consecutive frames at any point during the live imaging (major myddosomes), and 50 randomly selected myddosomes or major myddosomes were analysed for their IRAK4:MyD88 ratio and sizes over time.

Detected nuclei and cell labels were used to analyse NF-κB activation in PAMP-treated macrophages. To do so, we tracked the labels over time, and used labelling arithmetic to match the cell labels with the corresponding nuclei labels. The labels were used as masks to calculate the total mPlum–p65 fluorescence in the respective areas and the ratio between nuclear and cytosolic fluorescence on a per cell basis. Cells with a nuclear:cytosolic ratio of >1.0 were considered to have active NF-κB signalling.

To classify myddosomes on the basis of recruitment of core and effector proteins, we first detected the cells and myddosomes and then used the myddosome labels as seeds to crop areas of interest surrounding the myddosomes from the immunofluorescence images. Cropped images were normalized and passed through an in-house-trained neural network classifier (MyddoNet) to sort myddosomes into three categories: positive, negative or artefacts. MyddoNet is derived from a previously trained deep convolutional neural network for classification of protein recruitment to subcellular structures71 and was retrained using a dataset of 2,000 manually annotated myddosome images. Cross-validation showed that MyddoNet classified myddosomes with an overall accuracy of 94.9%. As biological immunofluorescence imaging data are insensitive to orientation, myddosome images were passed through the classifier in five different orientations (rotation and flip) and the mode of classification was used as overall result to increase accuracy. After discarding the images classified as artefacts, the proportion of stain-positive myddosomes was calculated.

Pixel-based colocalization analysis was performed by calculating the Pearson’s correlation coefficient with the Coloc2 plugin (v.3.0.6) as implemented in ImageJ. The analysis was restricted to the cell cytosol or the myddosomes using previously determined cell and myddosome labels. Colocalization was calculated using z-stack images with 15 planes and 0.5 μm spacing and on a per-cell basis. Cells were stained with deep/far-red dyes and channels were imaged sequentially to minimize bleed-through to the GFP channel (MyD88–AGF). Moreover, calibration images were acquired using 0.5 μm TetraSpeck Microspheres (T7281, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and used to determine chromatic-shift between the channels. Shift was corrected with sub-pixel resolution using the TransformJ: Translate plugin in ImageJ prior to analysis.

To quantify myddosome association with LPS-coated beads, a circular region of interest was drawn around the detected bead labels extending outward by 0.5 μm in all directions. Myddosomes were classified as bead associated if >50% of their label overlapped with this region of interest.
