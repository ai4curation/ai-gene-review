HNRNPA2B1 contains two RRM domains and has been investigated for its possible
role as an m6A reader protein. The targetome of HNRNPA2B1 was shown to overlap
with the m6A methylation motif, but further investigations of its binding
affinity for m6A-containing RNAs have been less clear on the relative
selectivity of HNRNPA2B1 for methylated transcripts. Our computational and
experimental studies depict that when an m6A modification is positioned in
between the two RRM domains, and in the context of the GGACU motif that can be
written by methyltransferases, the binding affinity is nearly identical and
slightly less favourable to the unmodified sequence. Our study suggests that
HNRNPA2B1 is not a (selective) reader but has high affinity for m6A in a
sequence-dependent manner. This can be attributed to the strong interactions
conferred by AGG and UAG motifs rather than adenine or m6A in the GGACU motif
which are predicted to interact weakly, intercalating between the two RRMs or
positioned outwards. Overall, our findings highlight the higher complexity of
HNRNPA2B1 and RRM recognition properties compared to well-studied YTH domains in
the recognition of m6A, as well as modified and unmodified sequences.

Our study suggests that HNRNPA2B1 is not a (selective) reader but has high affinity for m6A in a sequence-dependent manner. Overall, our study highlights the higher complexity of HNRNPA2B1 and RRM recognition properties for modified and unmodified RNAs in comparison to other well-studied m6A reader proteins [32].

Introduction

The heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein (HNRNP) family consists of 33 proteins which contain one or more RNA-binding domains (RBDs) [1]. Of these RBDs, the RNA recognition motif (RRM) is the most common and is present in a majority of HNRNPs [1]. HNRNPs have been demonstrated to play critical roles in important cellular processes, including post-transcriptional modifications, transcription, and translation. Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A2B1 (HNRNPA2B1) is one such protein which contains two RRMs (RRM1 and RRM2) in the N-terminus and is associated with transcription, RNA transport, and alternative splicing of pre-mRNA [2–6]. Additionally, HNRNPA2B1 has been associated with neurological diseases like Alzheimer’s disease through its interaction with the oligomeric tau protein [7] HNRNPA2B1 mutations and decreased activity have been observed to play a role in the pathogenesis of Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) [8] and Frontotemporal Lobar Dementia (FTLD) [9]. Moreover, overexpression has also been deemed detrimental to neuron survival [10]. This protein has also been associated with N6-methyladenosine (m6A) containing RNAs in this disease context [7], as well as in m6A-dependent cancer progression [11]. This RNA modification is one of the
most abundant in eukaryotic mRNA [12], and the implication of HNRNPA2B1 and m6A in a number of disease phenotypes [11–21] led to the hypothesis that HNRNPA2B1 could interact with m6A bearing transcripts in vivo.

Analysis of the HNRNPA2B1 targetome through immunoprecipitation and high-throughput RNA sequencing studies in the nervous system indicated a preference for AGG and UAG motifs [22,23] by RRM1 and RRM2, respectively [5] as well as for UAGGG, UUAGG, GGUAGUAG, and AGGAUAGA RNA motifs [24,25]. Additionally, HNRNPA2B1 was shown to recognize a consensus motif comprising UAASUUAU, where S represents G or C [26]. In addition to these preferred HNRNPA2B1 RNA motifs, the targetome of this protein was also shown to overlap with the m6A methylation motif RGAC, where R represents a purine base [6]. Furthermore, in vitro binding studies identified a preference of HNRNPA2B1 for m6A bearing transcripts and suggested that the RRM1 domain could partially mediate this interaction [6]. Yet, the molecular mechanisms for RNA recognition by HNRNPA2B1, particularly in the context of unmodified and m6A-modified substrates, remained elusive.

To identify molecular mechanisms underlying HNRNPA2B1 recognition of its preferred binding motif, a subsequent study performed in vitro binding assays coupled with structure resolution via X-ray crystallography [5]. This work predominantly examined substitutions in a short, synthetic 10-mer sequence (5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’), which contains a combination of established HNRNPA2B1 preferred motifs, AGG and UAG, respectively, for RRM1 and RRM2. Their results suggested that both domains of the protein contribute to the variety of RNA sequence recognition. The RRM1 domain shows specificity for a ‘purine rich AG motif’, while the RRM2 domain shows a greater degree of flexibility for the sequences that it binds [5]. In addition to testing unmodified RNA sequence variation, HNRNPA2B1 binding was also investigated in the context of the m6A modification. Given previous work suggesting that this protein could recognize m6A bearing transcripts, in vitro binding assays were conducted with an m6A modified 10-mer (5’-AAGG-m6A-CUAGC-3’) [5], combining AGG and UAG, as well as m6A within the DRACH motif. Methyltransferases, known as writer proteins, catalyse m6A modification within the consensus sequence DRACH (D = A, G, or U; R = A or G; H = A, C, or U; A = m6A), in which GGACH is a favoured insertion context [27,28], while GGACU comprises a pronounced consensus motif according to higher precision tools [29]. Experiments showed a significant decrease in affinity for the m6A-modified RNA in comparison to unmodified RNA in the context of 5’-AAGG-m6A-CUAGC-3’ [5]. Interestingly, when binding assays were conducted with an RNA pentamer corresponding to the m6A methylation motif (5’-GGACU-3’), HNRNPA2B1 showed a lower affinity for unmodified RNA compared to m6A modified RNA, but at the same order of magnitude [5]. These results, in conjunction with previous research linking HNRNPA2B1 to m6A-dependent processes [30,31] raise significant interest in further understanding the mechanisms of protein-RNA binding, both in the context of the preferred binding motifs of this protein and m6A modification.

To investigate HNRNPA2B1–RNA binding in these contexts, we used a combination of state-of-the-art computational and experimental tools and additionally employed computational affinity screens to predict putative RNA modifications that may be accommodated by HNRNPA2B1. Our studies agree with previous experimental studies on the affinity of several RNAs with canonical substitutions [5] and we provide an atomistic explanation for these findings. Notably, our computational and experimental studies depict that when the m6A modification is positioned in between the two RRM domains, in the context of the GGACU methylation motif, the binding affinity is nearly identical and slightly less favourable to the unmodified sequence. Our study suggests that HNRNPA2B1 is not a (selective) reader but has high affinity for m6A in a sequence-dependent manner. This can be attributed to the strong interactions conferred by AGG and UAG motifs rather than the presence of adenine or m6A in the GGACU motif which are predicted to interact weakly, intercalating between the two RRMs or positioned outwards. Our computational studies demonstrate that canonical substitutions at the preferred motifs AGG and UAG lower binding affinity to different degrees, in line with previous experiments [5]; yet, our computations suggest that the introduction of methylation-based modifications in place of adenine at AGG or the introduction of uridine-modifications in the context of uridine at the UAG motif can lead to binding affinities on par or slightly weakened, underlining the potential promiscuity of HNRNPAB1 for RNA modifications in the context of the naturally preferred motif. Our experiments validate the slight decrease in affinity of m6A modification at adenine of AGG. Overall, our study highlights the higher complexity of HNRNPA2B1 and RRM recognition properties for modified and unmodified RNAs in comparison to other well-studied m6A reader proteins [32].

Computational methodsStructure selection, modelling, and parametrization for the protein-RNA complexThe structure of HNRNPA2B1 in complex with 10-mer RNA 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ (PDB: 5HO4) [5] was used as a starting conformation to model a series of HNRNPA2B1-RNA complexes (Figure 1A). In these complexes, canonical substitutions and modifications were introduced to the RNA strand using CHARMM [33] in conjunction with CHARMM-GUI [33–35]. Correspondence between literature naming, symbolic abbreviation and CHARMM abbreviation of RNA modifications is presented in Table S1. According to crystallographic symmetry, the first four nucleotides of the RNA strand interact primarily with RRM1 (residues 21 to 104) and in part with RRM2 (residues 112 to 191) of the first protein (which we define as the first homodimer), while the last 4 nucleotides primarily interact with RRM2 (residues 112 to 191) and in part with RRM1 (residues 21 to 104) of the second protein (which we define as the second homodimer) [5]. In general, HNRNPA2B1 is used, in what follows, to describe the two protein homodimers within the computational studies. The CHARMM36m force field [36] was used for the parametrization of the protein and RNA, in conjunction with additional parameters for modified RNAs provided by Nilsson and Mackerell [37]. Additionally, the parameters of 8-OxoG and 8-OxoA were re-parameterized by our lab using CGenFF [38] compared to the original parameters used in our previous study [39].

Simulations were performed using OpenMM [40] with CHARMM36m force field [36]. Simulation files for all HNRNPA2B1-RNA complexes were initially prepared using CHARMM-GUI’s Solution Builder [33–35]. Additional changes were made to improve the equilibration and production runs, considering system specific properties. Each complex under investigation was solvated in a water box with an inner edge distance of 12 Å; a concentration of 0.15 M KCl ions was added, and additional ions were introduced to neutralize the system. Histidine residues were considered uncharged, and no
protonation or deprotonation was applied for amino acids at pH 7.4; this is in line with the protonation suggested by CHARMM-GUI [33–35] for the starting conformation of 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ in complex with HNRNPA2B1 (PDB:5HO4) [5]. A multi-stage equilibration was implemented to smoothly equilibrate the complexes before production runs. An in-house FORTRAN program was used to assign constraints in three consecutive equilibration stages. In these stages, three different zones of the protein were defined. Zone A was defined as the set of protein residues with any atom (backbone or sidechain) within 6 Å of the RNA strand. Zone B was defined as the set of protein residues with any atom more than 6 Å from the RNA stand. Similarly, Zone C was defined as the set of protein residues with any atom more than 56 Å from the RNA strand. Importantly, zones were prioritized over one another. In other words, protein residues in Zone A were not considered in Zone B or C, and protein residues in Zone B were not considered in Zone A or C. The first stage of equilibration comprised a short NVT simulation of 0.5 ns. Heavy constraints with a force constant of 400 kJ/(mol*nm2) were imposed on Zone A and all atoms in the RNA strand. Light constraints with a force constant of 40 kJ/(mol*nm2) were imposed on Zone B (and implicitly Zone C), as well as X-ray resolved water molecules. In the second stage, an NPT simulation of 4 ns was performed. Constraints imposed on Zone A were decreased to 40 kJ/(mol*nm2), and water molecules were released fully. Zone B (and implicitly Zone C), as well as the entire RNA strand, remained constrained with force constants of 40 kJ/(mol*nm2) and 400 kJ/(mol*nm2), respectively. In the third and final stage of equilibration, an NPT simulation of 4 ns was performed. Constraints on Zones A and B were released fully. Zone C, as well as the first two and last two residues of the RNA strand, were constrained with a force constant of 40 kJ/(mol*nm2). During the production run, constraints were maintained on Zone C, and these constraints preserved the structural integrity of the two domains in the absence of the entire protein sequence; this was done analogously in [41–43] aiming at preserving the integrity of truncated protein domains within simulations. The RNA strand was released fully during the production run. Overall, each complex was simulated in triplicate with equilibration and production times of 32 and 200 ns, respectively. This provided the complex ample time to equilibrate and form potentially favourable interactions with the RNA strand. In all simulations, the temperature was maintained at 300 K using a Langevin thermostat. The Lennard-Jones (LJ) 6–12 (i.e. van der Waals) interactions were smoothed over the range of 10 to 12 Å using the force-based switching function, and the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method was used for the long-range electrostatic interactions [35]. Snapshots were extracted from production simulations every 1 ns; thus, 200 snapshots were analysed per run, and 600 snapshots were analysed in total per complex. Hydrogen bond occupancies were calculated using VMD [44], according to the following criterion: A hydrogen bond was present if the donor (D) – acceptor (A) distance was less than 3.5 Å and the corresponding angle (D-H … A) was larger than 90°.Energetic analysisWe assessed the binding free energies of RNA in complex with HNRNPA2B1 using the produced simulation snapshots of the HNRNPA2B1-RNA complexes under investigation. Upon completion of the simulations, VINARDO [45] was used to calculate the binding free energy of RNA in complex with HNRNPA2B1, with files prepared using OpenBabel [46]. This served as our primary method to determine binding free energies. According to González-Alemán et al. [47], VINARDO scoring performed better compared to other binding free energy calculation methods, including the MM-GBSA approximation [43], [48–51] using the GBSW [52] or the GBMV [53,54] implicit solvent models. Additionally, our findings suggested that results from VINARDO agree with previous and current experiments (see Results below), and are in slightly better agreement compared to the MM-GBSA approximation.Furthermore, for particular simulated complexes, we calculated interaction free energies between pairs of protein residues and RNA nucleotides, referred to as residue-nucleotide pairwise interaction free energy calculations. In these calculations, snapshots were extracted from production simulations every 20 ns; thus, 10 snapshots were analysed per run, and 30 snapshots were analysed in total per complex. The ΔG interaction free energy for each residue-nucleotide pair was averaged over extracted snapshots and decomposed into polar and non-polar components to shed light on which interactions contribute to the stabilization of the bound complexes. The methodology for calculating residue-nucleotide pairwise interaction free energies is detailed in [55], and is based on previous studies involving residue-residue pairwise
interaction free energy calculations [50,56,57]. In summary, polar components comprise electrostatic and polar solvation interaction free energies, while non-polar components comprise van der Waals and non-polar solvation interaction free energies within the MM-GBSA approximation. Polar and non-polar solvation free energies were calculated using GBMV II [58,59] where the Generalized Born setup was provided by CHARMM-GUI [33–35,60]. In particular cases, residue-nucleotide ΔΔG pairwise interaction free energy calculations were performed to compare the average interaction free energy across two systems and elucidate which residue-nucleotide pairs are associated with improved or weakened polar and non-polar interactions.Moreover, free energy perturbation with Replica Exchange Molecular Dynamics (FEP/λ-REMD) [61] was employed to evaluate the binding free energy of adenine and m6A nucleobases to a particular binding pocket of HNRNPA2B1. Input files for absolute ligand binding FEP/λ-REMD were obtained from CHARMM-GUI [34,62], and performed in NAMD [63]. As a control, we additionally investigated the binding of T4 Lysozyme L99A (PDB ID: 4W53) [64] to n-propylbenzene, for which published experimental and computational results are available [62]. For the same T4 Lysozyme L99A-propylbenzene system, 32 windows were linearly employed, and 6 ns FEP/λ-REMD simulations were performed. The simulation duration enabled convergence in our results and nearly excellent agreement of our calculations with experimental results (−6.50 kcal/mol compared to −6.55 kcal/mol according to experiments) [62]; this served as a validation for our setup. Following this set up, we performed 6 ns FEP/λ-REMD simulations with 32 linearly employed windows for HNRNPA2B1 in complex with the nucleobase of adenine and m6A, at a particular binding pocket, guided by our simulations and energetic calculations described above.Screening-based search of modified RNAs using short implicit solvent MD simulationsMotivated by our previous studies [39], we employed an in-house computational protocol to efficiently investigate which modified RNAs at different positions in the context of 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ are prone to interact favourably with HNRNPA2B1. Given the structure of a protein-RNA complex, the position of RNA under investigation, and a set of possible RNA modifications and their corresponding parameters, this protocol evaluates RNA modifications at the position of interest using short implicit solvent MD simulations and energy calculations. For computational efficiency, the protein-RNA complex under investigation, corresponding to HNRNPA2B1 in complex with 10-mer RNA 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ (PDB: 5HO4) [5] was truncated such that all protein residues with any atom (backbone or sidechain) within 15 Å of the RNA strand were considered, and small gaps were automatically filled to create a cohesive binding pocket. The balance between stability and flexibility of the truncated system was maintained using force constraints on defined zones of the protein. It is worth noting that the zones defined below in the screening-based search are different from Zones A, B, and C defined above in the explicit-solvent MD simulations. Thus, for clarity, these zones are mentioned in Roman numerals. Zone (i) was defined as any protein residue with any atom (backbone or sidechain) within 6 Å of the RNA strand, and during simulations, the backbone and sidechain of residues within this zone were constrained using CHARMM harmonic force constants of 0.4 and 0.2 kcal/(mol*Å2), respectively. Zone (ii) was defined as any protein residue with any atom between 6 to 11 Å of the RNA strand, and during simulations, the backbone and sidechain of residues within this zone were constrained using CHARMM harmonic force constants of 0.8 and 0.4 kcal/(mol*Å2), respectively. Zone (iii) was defined as any protein residue with any atom within 11 to 15 Å of the RNA strand, and during simulations, the backbone and sidechain of residues within this zone were constrained using CHARMM harmonic force constants of 1.6 and 0.8 kcal/(mol*Å2), respectively. These zones were prioritized such that any protein residue in Zone (i) was not considered in Zone (ii) or (iii), and any protein residue in Zone (ii) was not considered in Zone (iii). Lastly, during simulations, the RNA strand was constrained using CHARMM harmonic force constants of 0.4 kcal/(mol*Å2) on its backbone and 0.2 kcal/(mol*Å2) on all side chains, except the one incorporating the RNA modification. This truncated system was simulated and proceeded through modified RNAs organized in different generations (in tree diagrams) starting from parental, followed by 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th up to 6th generation modifications (Figure S1). A database of 113 RNA modifications was investigated, comprising 111 modifications that were parameterized by MacKerell et al. [37] and 8-oxoG and 8-oxoA which were re-parameterized by us using CGenFF [38] in this study, following the first parametrization used in our previous study [39].When canonical nucleotides adenine, cytosine, guanine, and uridine were initially introduced at the position of investigation, the simulation durations were 4.5 ns, and 50 frames from the last 1.5 ns of the simulation were considered for energetic analysis. For energetic analysis, the full RNA strand was scored as a rigid body using VINARDO [45] to account for constraints imposed on the truncated system to maintain its stability. The frame of the trajectory with the lowest energy was then used as the initial structure for the next generation RNA modifications in its corresponding tree. Subsequently, when RNA modifications were introduced, gradually following their positions in clades of trees, the simulation durations were 3 ns, and similarly, 50 frames from the last 1.5 ns were considered for energetic analysis. In this way, the lowest energy complex was also always considered, and every new simulation of the complex within a tree would begin from the lowest free energy snapshot of the preceding modification, allowing additional potential favourable interactions to form, following a criterion. Given the approximations and relatively short simulation time, a modification was selected under the criterion that its binding free energy was more favourable or no more than 2% less favourable compared to the parent nucleotide. This aided in screening and selecting which modified RNAs are likely to be recognized by HNRNPA2B1 for further investigation using multiple and longer all-atom explicit solvent simulations. During this screening stage, all simulations were performed in implicit solvent represented by GBMVII [65], and the simulation setup was provided by CHARMM-GUI [33–35]. The temperature was maintained at 300 K using a Langevin thermostat.

Set up of explicit solvent MD simulations and force fieldsTriplicate all-atom explicit solvent MD simulations were used to investigate the binding of a series of RNA sequences to HNRNPA2B1. Simulations were performed using OpenMM [40] with CHARMM36m force field [36]. Simulation files for all HNRNPA2B1-RNA complexes were initially prepared using CHARMM-GUI’s Solution Builder [33–35]. Additional changes were made to improve the equilibration and production runs, considering system specific properties. Each complex under investigation was solvated in a water box with an inner edge distance of 12 Å; a concentration of 0.15 M KCl ions was added, and additional ions were introduced to neutralize the system. Histidine residues were considered uncharged, and no
protonation or deprotonation was applied for amino acids at pH 7.4; this is in line with the protonation suggested by CHARMM-GUI [33–35] for the starting conformation of 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ in complex with HNRNPA2B1 (PDB:5HO4) [5]. A multi-stage equilibration was implemented to smoothly equilibrate the complexes before production runs. An in-house FORTRAN program was used to assign constraints in three consecutive equilibration stages. In these stages, three different zones of the protein were defined. Zone A was defined as the set of protein residues with any atom (backbone or sidechain) within 6 Å of the RNA strand. Zone B was defined as the set of protein residues with any atom more than 6 Å from the RNA stand. Similarly, Zone C was defined as the set of protein residues with any atom more than 56 Å from the RNA strand. Importantly, zones were prioritized over one another. In other words, protein residues in Zone A were not considered in Zone B or C, and protein residues in Zone B were not considered in Zone A or C. The first stage of equilibration comprised a short NVT simulation of 0.5 ns. Heavy constraints with a force constant of 400 kJ/(mol*nm2) were imposed on Zone A and all atoms in the RNA strand. Light constraints with a force constant of 40 kJ/(mol*nm2) were imposed on Zone B (and implicitly Zone C), as well as X-ray resolved water molecules. In the second stage, an NPT simulation of 4 ns was performed. Constraints imposed on Zone A were decreased to 40 kJ/(mol*nm2), and water molecules were released fully. Zone B (and implicitly Zone C), as well as the entire RNA strand, remained constrained with force constants of 40 kJ/(mol*nm2) and 400 kJ/(mol*nm2), respectively. In the third and final stage of equilibration, an NPT simulation of 4 ns was performed. Constraints on Zones A and B were released fully. Zone C, as well as the first two and last two residues of the RNA strand, were constrained with a force constant of 40 kJ/(mol*nm2). During the production run, constraints were maintained on Zone C, and these constraints preserved the structural integrity of the two domains in the absence of the entire protein sequence; this was done analogously in [41–43] aiming at preserving the integrity of truncated protein domains within simulations. The RNA strand was released fully during the production run. Overall, each complex was simulated in triplicate with equilibration and production times of 32 and 200 ns, respectively. This provided the complex ample time to equilibrate and form potentially favourable interactions with the RNA strand. In all simulations, the temperature was maintained at 300 K using a Langevin thermostat. The Lennard-Jones (LJ) 6–12 (i.e. van der Waals) interactions were smoothed over the range of 10 to 12 Å using the force-based switching function, and the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method was used for the long-range electrostatic interactions [35]. Snapshots were extracted from production simulations every 1 ns; thus, 200 snapshots were analysed per run, and 600 snapshots were analysed in total per complex. Hydrogen bond occupancies were calculated using VMD [44], according to the following criterion: A hydrogen bond was present if the donor (D) – acceptor (A) distance was less than 3.5 Å and the corresponding angle (D-H … A) was larger than 90°.

Energetic analysisWe assessed the binding free energies of RNA in complex with HNRNPA2B1 using the produced simulation snapshots of the HNRNPA2B1-RNA complexes under investigation. Upon completion of the simulations, VINARDO [45] was used to calculate the binding free energy of RNA in complex with HNRNPA2B1, with files prepared using OpenBabel [46]. This served as our primary method to determine binding free energies. According to González-Alemán et al. [47], VINARDO scoring performed better compared to other binding free energy calculation methods, including the MM-GBSA approximation [43], [48–51] using the GBSW [52] or the GBMV [53,54] implicit solvent models. Additionally, our findings suggested that results from VINARDO agree with previous and current experiments (see Results below), and are in slightly better agreement compared to the MM-GBSA approximation.Furthermore, for particular simulated complexes, we calculated interaction free energies between pairs of protein residues and RNA nucleotides, referred to as residue-nucleotide pairwise interaction free energy calculations. In these calculations, snapshots were extracted from production simulations every 20 ns; thus, 10 snapshots were analysed per run, and 30 snapshots were analysed in total per complex. The ΔG interaction free energy for each residue-nucleotide pair was averaged over extracted snapshots and decomposed into polar and non-polar components to shed light on which interactions contribute to the stabilization of the bound complexes. The methodology for calculating residue-nucleotide pairwise interaction free energies is detailed in [55], and is based on previous studies involving residue-residue pairwise
interaction free energy calculations [50,56,57]. In summary, polar components comprise electrostatic and polar solvation interaction free energies, while non-polar components comprise van der Waals and non-polar solvation interaction free energies within the MM-GBSA approximation. Polar and non-polar solvation free energies were calculated using GBMV II [58,59] where the Generalized Born setup was provided by CHARMM-GUI [33–35,60]. In particular cases, residue-nucleotide ΔΔG pairwise interaction free energy calculations were performed to compare the average interaction free energy across two systems and elucidate which residue-nucleotide pairs are associated with improved or weakened polar and non-polar interactions.Moreover, free energy perturbation with Replica Exchange Molecular Dynamics (FEP/λ-REMD) [61] was employed to evaluate the binding free energy of adenine and m6A nucleobases to a particular binding pocket of HNRNPA2B1. Input files for absolute ligand binding FEP/λ-REMD were obtained from CHARMM-GUI [34,62], and performed in NAMD [63]. As a control, we additionally investigated the binding of T4 Lysozyme L99A (PDB ID: 4W53) [64] to n-propylbenzene, for which published experimental and computational results are available [62]. For the same T4 Lysozyme L99A-propylbenzene system, 32 windows were linearly employed, and 6 ns FEP/λ-REMD simulations were performed. The simulation duration enabled convergence in our results and nearly excellent agreement of our calculations with experimental results (−6.50 kcal/mol compared to −6.55 kcal/mol according to experiments) [62]; this served as a validation for our setup. Following this set up, we performed 6 ns FEP/λ-REMD simulations with 32 linearly employed windows for HNRNPA2B1 in complex with the nucleobase of adenine and m6A, at a particular binding pocket, guided by our simulations and energetic calculations described above.

Screening-based search of modified RNAs using short implicit solvent MD simulationsMotivated by our previous studies [39], we employed an in-house computational protocol to efficiently investigate which modified RNAs at different positions in the context of 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ are prone to interact favourably with HNRNPA2B1. Given the structure of a protein-RNA complex, the position of RNA under investigation, and a set of possible RNA modifications and their corresponding parameters, this protocol evaluates RNA modifications at the position of interest using short implicit solvent MD simulations and energy calculations. For computational efficiency, the protein-RNA complex under investigation, corresponding to HNRNPA2B1 in complex with 10-mer RNA 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ (PDB: 5HO4) [5] was truncated such that all protein residues with any atom (backbone or sidechain) within 15 Å of the RNA strand were considered, and small gaps were automatically filled to create a cohesive binding pocket. The balance between stability and flexibility of the truncated system was maintained using force constraints on defined zones of the protein. It is worth noting that the zones defined below in the screening-based search are different from Zones A, B, and C defined above in the explicit-solvent MD simulations. Thus, for clarity, these zones are mentioned in Roman numerals. Zone (i) was defined as any protein residue with any atom (backbone or sidechain) within 6 Å of the RNA strand, and during simulations, the backbone and sidechain of residues within this zone were constrained using CHARMM harmonic force constants of 0.4 and 0.2 kcal/(mol*Å2), respectively. Zone (ii) was defined as any protein residue with any atom between 6 to 11 Å of the RNA strand, and during simulations, the backbone and sidechain of residues within this zone were constrained using CHARMM harmonic force constants of 0.8 and 0.4 kcal/(mol*Å2), respectively. Zone (iii) was defined as any protein residue with any atom within 11 to 15 Å of the RNA strand, and during simulations, the backbone and sidechain of residues within this zone were constrained using CHARMM harmonic force constants of 1.6 and 0.8 kcal/(mol*Å2), respectively. These zones were prioritized such that any protein residue in Zone (i) was not considered in Zone (ii) or (iii), and any protein residue in Zone (ii) was not considered in Zone (iii). Lastly, during simulations, the RNA strand was constrained using CHARMM harmonic force constants of 0.4 kcal/(mol*Å2) on its backbone and 0.2 kcal/(mol*Å2) on all side chains, except the one incorporating the RNA modification. This truncated system was simulated and proceeded through modified RNAs organized in different generations (in tree diagrams) starting from parental, followed by 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th up to 6th generation modifications (Figure S1). A database of 113 RNA modifications was investigated, comprising 111 modifications that were parameterized by MacKerell et al. [37] and 8-oxoG and 8-oxoA which were re-parameterized by us using CGenFF [38] in this study, following the first parametrization used in our previous study [39].When canonical nucleotides adenine, cytosine, guanine, and uridine were initially introduced at the position of investigation, the simulation durations were 4.5 ns, and 50 frames from the last 1.5 ns of the simulation were considered for energetic analysis. For energetic analysis, the full RNA strand was scored as a rigid body using VINARDO [45] to account for constraints imposed on the truncated system to maintain its stability. The frame of the trajectory with the lowest energy was then used as the initial structure for the next generation RNA modifications in its corresponding tree. Subsequently, when RNA modifications were introduced, gradually following their positions in clades of trees, the simulation durations were 3 ns, and similarly, 50 frames from the last 1.5 ns were considered for energetic analysis. In this way, the lowest energy complex was also always considered, and every new simulation of the complex within a tree would begin from the lowest free energy snapshot of the preceding modification, allowing additional potential favourable interactions to form, following a criterion. Given the approximations and relatively short simulation time, a modification was selected under the criterion that its binding free energy was more favourable or no more than 2% less favourable compared to the parent nucleotide. This aided in screening and selecting which modified RNAs are likely to be recognized by HNRNPA2B1 for further investigation using multiple and longer all-atom explicit solvent simulations. During this screening stage, all simulations were performed in implicit solvent represented by GBMVII [65], and the simulation setup was provided by CHARMM-GUI [33–35]. The temperature was maintained at 300 K using a Langevin thermostat.

The overall similarities between the two complexes were also reflected by the calculated residue-nucleotide pairwise interaction free energies of the two individual complexes (Figure S4-S5, Figure S26-S27), as well as comparison of the two complexes (Figure S28-S29).We aimed to comprehend any differences between the two systems by comparing the residue-nucleotide ΔΔG interaction free energies (Figure S28-S29) and hydrogen bond occupancies (Table 1). The introduction of the additional methyl group in m6A compared to adenine in 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ and 5’-A-m6A-GGACUAGC-3’ resulted in weakening of particular interactions associated with the loss of the hydrogen bond formed between m6A and R95, lower occupancy hydrogen bonds formed between m6A with V97 on one side, as well as guanine at position 3 with S102, which could be attributed to steric stress due to the methyl group presence in the binding pocket. Of note, the interactions formed by these two modifications were similar to those formed by m6A and adenine at position 2 (Figure 4(A,B)). This was reflected by the average ΔG and corresponding ΔΔG residue-nucleotide interaction free energies calculated with reference to 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ (Figure S4-S5, Figure S30-S37). This similarity suggests that unmodified adenine and other adenine-based methylations could potentially be accommodated by the particular RRM1 binding pocket of HNRNPA2B1, presumably in accordance with the importance of adenine in the AGG motif. Thus, apart from adenine, our simulations predict that methylations to adenine can also potentially be favoured in this particular sequence context.Overall, we identified that in addition to m6A, HNRNPA2B1 may bind both i6A and m62A with comparable-affinity to 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ when they are present at the second position of the RNA strand. This confirmed the importance of binding pocket 2 in RRM1 of HNRNPA2B1 and highlights its ability to accommodate certain methylation-based RNA modifications. In addition to the position 2 modified RNAs, two RNA modifications at position 7, 5’-AAGGAC-Ψ-AGC-3’ and 5’-AAGGAC-m5U-AGC-3’, exhibited computationally predicted binding affinities of 134.3 nM and 203.8 nM, respectively, while a modification at position 8, 5’-AAGGACU-Am-GC-3’, exhibited a computationally predicted binding affinity of 139.3 nM, which was on par with 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ (Table S3A, Table S3C). Simulations provide additional mechanistic insight to previous observation that the AGG and UAG motifs are essential for RRM1 and RRM2 recognition, respectively

The structure of HNRNPA2B1 in complex with 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ was experimentally resolved by Wu et al. (Figure 1(A), PDB:5HO4) [5]. This structure served as the basis for our study investigating canonical substitutions and modifications. Initially, we used explicit-solvent MD simulations to investigate HNRNPA2B1 in complex with 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’, referred to by us as sequence (i) (Figure 1A). Throughout our simulations, the conformation of the 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ RNA strand was relatively well preserved with reference to the initial starting conformation present in the experimentally resolved structure [5] (Figure 1(B)). This was reflected by the low average RMSD of the RNA backbone with respect to the initial conformation of bound RNA in the experimentally resolved structure (Table S3A). Overall, the protein sidechain orientations and positions within the simulations were in line with the experimentally resolved structure [5] (Figure 1(B), Table S3A). This served as an indication that the RNA binding conformation in the simulations is preserved compared to X-ray [5]. RMSD calculations were performed by superimposing the protein and not the RNA strand, thus accounting for deviations of the RNA backbone with respect to its initial position and shape.

Additionally, we used explicit-solvent MD simulations to investigate HNRNPA2B1 in complex with additional sequences that were experimentally studied by Wu et al. [5] (Figure 2(A)). Sequence (i) served as the reference, while the sequences (ii)-(v) span a range of substitutions with experimentally reported affinities [5]. Within 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ (i), as well as in systems (ii)-(v), the nucleotide at position 5 intercalated between the two homodimers of HNRNPA2B1 while the nucleotide at position 6 was oriented away from the two proteins (Figure 1(B)). The nucleotides at positions 2 - 4 and 7 - 9 of the RNA strands were oriented towards the protein domains of HNRNPA2B1 and had well-defined binding pockets, while nucleotides at terminals, positions 1 and 10, remained flexible throughout the simulations. Notably, VINARDO performed slightly better than MM-GBSA in comparison to experimentally determined binding free energies [5] (Figure 2(B); Figure S3A). VINARDO was subsequently used as the metric for computationally predicted binding free energies within the simulations. Prompted by the high-agreement depicted by the linear correlation between computationally calculated binding free energies and experimentally determined binding free energies [5], this correlation was subsequently used in the study to relate the computationally calculated binding free energies (kcal/mol) to predicted affinities (nM) based on experimental data for (i) - (v) (Table S3A). To investigate the key differences in protein-RNA interactions between oligos (i) – (v), we used residue-nucleotide pairwise interaction free energy analysis on our simulation trajectories (Figure S4-S21). This analysis elucidated which interactions were weakened or strengthened and contributed to the oligo-protein affinities; emphasis was given on the key weakened interactions aiming to understand the role of perturbing the preferred binding motifs AGG (RRM1) and UAG (RRM2). Our simulations demonstrated that canonical substitution of guanine at position 7 (ii) in the context of 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’, resulting in a GAG motif instead of UAG, led to less favourable binding (Table S3A) in line with the lower affinity seen in previous experiments [5]. According to our simulations, the larger nucleobase size of guanine at position 7 facilitated the formation of an additional hydrogen bond between guanine 7 and N181 (RRM2), causing guanine to shift direction slightly, while forming hydrogen bonds with lower occupancies with residues R99
(RRM1), G118, E183, and R185 (RRM2), and overall weakened polar interactions with F115, G117, E183 and R185 (RRM2) compared to the unmodified sequence (i) (Table 1; Figure S6-S9, Table S3A). Similar observations on the weakening of interactions with R99 and R185 were depicted by Wu et al. [5] (see comparison of Figures 3, 4 in ref. [5]), based on the crystallographic structure of the particular oligo (ii) in complex with HNRNPA2B1 (PDB: 5WWF) [5] in comparison to 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ (PDB: 5HO4) [5]. Our simulations depict that mutations of the AGG motif to GGG (iii), ACG (iv), and AGC (v) have more drastic effect in binding favourability, in increasing order, and provide mechanistic insights delineating the impact of substitutions within the AGG motif, in line with a higher decrease in affinity according to previous experiments [5]. Canonical substitution of guanine at position 2 (iii) in the context of 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’, resulting in a GGG motif instead of AGG, notably weakened its interaction with H108, disrupting the π-π interactions between H108, guanine, and F24, leading to lower tendency to be enclosed within the aromatic and imidazole rings of F24 and H108 (Table 1; Figure S10-S13). According to Wu et al. [5], the particular substitution resulted in a ‘shift’ of the RNA motif, and a shifting of the interactions that nucleotides 1–4 form based on the crystallographic structure of the particular oligo (iii) in complex with HNRNPA2B1 (PDB: 5WWE) [5], which is discussed below.

Canonical substitution of guanine at position 3 (iv) in the context of 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’, resulting in an ACG motif instead of AGG, led to disturbance of interactions formed within the binding pocket due to cytosine’s smaller size. The hydrogen bond occupancy with V97 decreased and hydrogen bonding with K22 was completely lost (Figures 2(C,E), Table 1; Figure S14-S17). Additionally, salt bridges between the negatively charged phosphate group of cytosine 3 and the positively charged R62 and R99 were weakened (Figures 2(C,E)), which was reflected by less favourable polar interaction energies compared to AGG (Figure S14-S17). Moreover, canonical substitution of guanine at position 4 (v) in the context of 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’, resulting in an AGC motif instead of AGG, led to complete loss of its capacity to form hydrogen bonds with K22 and D49 and weaker non-polar contact with V51 (Figures 2(C,F); Figure S18-S21). Additionally, interactions formed by guanine 3 were also affected; reduced hydrogen bond occupancy between guanine 3 and K22 (Figures 2(C,F)) was observed, which was reflected by weakened polar interactions (Figure S18-S21).

The introduction of the additional methyl group in m6A compared to adenine in 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ and 5’-A-m6A-GGACUAGC-3’ resulted in weakening of particular interactions associated with the loss of the hydrogen bond formed between m6A and R95, lower occupancy hydrogen bonds formed between m6A with V97 on one side, as well as guanine at position 3 with S102, which could be attributed to steric stress due to the methyl group presence in the binding pocket. These weakened interactions were partly counterbalanced by improved interactions formed between m6A and the surface comprising residues F24, R62, F64, F66, and K94 on the other side, including the strengthening of a salt bridge between the backbone of guanine at position 3 and the R62 positively charged group in the first homodimer (RRM1), and higher occupancy hydrogen bonds between guanine at position 3 with Q19 (RRM1), and guanine at position 4 with K22, D49 and R99 (RRM1), resulting in stronger polar interactions. Additionally, the m6A modification in place of adenine at position 2 seemed to lead to a balance of improved and weakened interactions with of nucleotides 7–10 with residues of RRM2 which may be attributed to allosteric effects (Figure S26-S27).

To investigate if HNRNPA2B1 can interact with other modified nucleobases outside of the m6A context, we performed a screening-based search of modified RNAs at different binding pockets of HNRNPA2B1 with reference to 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’. Beyond position 2 that has already been investigated using screening above, we performed screening of RNA modifications at positions 4 and 6–9 in the RNA strand, exploring trees of modified RNAs stemming from the natural nucleotide at each position (Table S4); position 5 was also omitted as it primarily intercalated between the two RRM domains.

We investigated twenty 10-mer sequences with RNA modifications at different positions using explicit solvent MD simulations (Table S3C). These twenty sequences were derived from the list of modifications we
screened, including: Im, m62A, I, m6A, m2A, m1A, i6A, Am, 8-oxoG, m1G, Gm, m22G, Ψ, m5U, Um, m1Ψ,
and Cm. The twenty sequences did not include 5’-A-m6A-GGACUAGC-3’, 5’-AAGG-m6A-CUAGC-3’, or 5’-AAGGACU-m6A-GC-3’, as they were examined above. Upon completion of the simulations, we calculated the binding free energy of each complex and additionally used the correlation in Figure 2(B) to predict their binding affinities. Of the examined twenty sequences, predicted affinities ranged widely from ~100 nM to ~6000 nM (Table S3C); for comparison the unmodified (5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’) oligo showed predicted Kd values of 101.3 (Table S3A). While most modifications stemming from corresponding natural nucleotides in the original sequence showed significantly reduced affinity, RNA including a few different modifications can bind comparably to the unmodified sequence. Of these cases, two RNAs with modifications at position 2 (5’-A-i6A-GGACUAGC-3’, 5’-A-m62A-GGACUAGC-3’) showed high computationally predicted binding affinities within the range of 100–200 nM (Table S3C). These modified sequences exhibited low predicted binding affinities that are on par with the unmodified (5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’) oligo. Of note, the interactions formed by these two modifications were similar to those formed by m6A and adenine at position 2 (Figure 4(A,B)). This was reflected by the average ΔG and corresponding ΔΔG residue-nucleotide interaction free energies calculated with reference to 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ (Figure S4-S5, Figure S30-S37). This similarity suggests that unmodified adenine and other adenine-based methylations could potentially be accommodated by the particular RRM1 binding pocket of HNRNPA2B1, presumably in accordance with the importance of adenine in the AGG motif. Thus, apart from adenine, our simulations predict that methylations to adenine can also potentially be favoured in this particular sequence context.

Overall, we identified that in addition to m6A, HNRNPA2B1 may bind both i6A and m62A with comparable-affinity to 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ when they are present at the second position of the RNA strand. This confirmed the importance of binding pocket 2 in RRM1 of HNRNPA2B1 and highlights its ability to accommodate certain methylation-based RNA modifications. In addition to the position 2 modified RNAs, two RNA modifications at position 7, 5’-AAGGAC-Ψ-AGC-3’ and 5’-AAGGAC-m5U-AGC-3’, exhibited computationally predicted binding affinities of 134.3 nM and 203.8 nM, respectively, while a modification at position 8, 5’-AAGGACU-Am-GC-3’, exhibited a computationally predicted binding affinity of 139.3 nM, which was on par with 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ (Table S3A, Table S3C). Particularly, Ψ seems to be equally favourable to uridine at position 7 in the RNA strand, which could indicate that the modification introduced by Ψ does not alter binding affinity and can be well-tolerated at this position. Additional details into the interactions formed by 5’-AAGGAC-m5U-AGC-3’ (Figure S38A), and 5’-AAGGACU-Am-GC-3’ (Figure S38B) in complex with HNRNPA2B1 are provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S39-S46). Our results also depict several RNA modifications with potential low binding affinity to HNRNPA2B1 in the range of (~200–600 nM) in the context of the 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’ sequence (Table S3C), one of which was comprised of 8-oxoG at position 4, with affinity ~550 nM. Notably, this may not be surprising given that other HNRNP family proteins were reported to bind 8oxoG-modified RNA [75–78]. However, further experimental investigation is needed to validate our computational predictions.

DiscussionOf the over 150 RNA modifications identified to date, m6A is the most prevalent modified base in eukaryotic mRNA [70]. This modification is catalysed by the METTL3/METTL14 methyltransferase complex, which recognizes the GGACH sequence motif [27,28]. Of the proteins implicated in m6A-dependent regulation, HNRNPA2B1 represents a complex case. Some previous work has shown direct binding to m6A-bearing transcripts and an overlap of HNRNPA2B1 binding sites with the RGAC motif [6]. Other work, however, has shown that HNRNPA2B1 binds to methylated RNA at lower affinities in the context of an HNRNPA2B1 preferred binding motif, which varies depending on whether the entire protein was included versus RRM domains only [5]. In another study, HNRNPA2B1 was identified in proteomics results to interact with both an unmodified and an m6A-modified RNA ‘bait’ [71], suggesting that the protein can recognize both canonical and m6A-containing GGACU motifs, with slight favourability for the former compared to the latter. Although other m6A binding proteins, such as the YTH family of proteins [32,72–74], have shown clear mechanisms of recognition for this modification, the mechanism underlining HNRNPA2B1 binding properties for m6A has remained elusive. Given the two-domain binding mechanism of HNRNPA2B1, this system is significantly more complex than the YTH domains. This complexity is especially relevant for understanding the m6A binding mechanism, particularly within the context of an RNA sequence encompassing a GGACH motif. Prompted by the availability of
HNRNPA2B1 protein structure and affinity data for a variety of RNA substrates, our study has investigated

This enabled us to investigate how the substitution of other nucleosides affects HNRNPA2B1’s ability to bind RNA. Importantly, our results are energetically in agreement with previous experimental studies on RNA sequences (i) - (v) starting from the RNA-HNRNPA2B1 conformation in PDB:5HO4 [5], and provide a detailed analysis of the interactions weakened and that could be critical for the high affinity of the AGG and uridine of UAG motif. Thus, HNRNPA2B1 binding favourability to GGACU or GG-m6A-CU motifs could be attributed to their positioning in between RRM1 and RRM2, and the favourable interactions formed by GG and U as part of the AGG and UAG motifs, recognized by RRM1 and RRM2, respectively. Within the simulations, constraints were imposed to maintain the relative position of RRMs interacting with RNA; this could presumably highlight that tight packing of RRMs could enhance m6A modification and needs further investigation.

Considering these findings, it is possible that HNRNPA2B1 might interact with other RNA modifications. Thus, in contrast to the well-defined binding pocket for well-studied m6A readers (i.e. the YTH domains), our results suggest it could be possible that HNRNPA2B1 can recognize different RNA modifications at different pockets. Thus, our findings can pave the way for future studies investigating the complexity of RNA modifications recognition by HNRNPA2B1 and other HNRNPs.Our findings highlight the delicate balance between sequence context, RNA modifications, and RRM recognition, and they provide a framework to speculate in disease context on how amyloidogenic proteins, like Tau, may further influence this regulatory layer [80]. Tau binds RNA through low-complexity domains and has been shown to interact with HNRNP proteins, including HNRNPA2B1, within stress granules [7]. Importantly, previous studies showed that oligomeric Tau and HNRNPA2B1 to both co-localize with cytoplasmic m6A granules and that HNRNPA2B1 is required for co-localization of oligomeric Tau with m6A, further suggesting that HNRNPA2B1 tethers oligomeric Tau to m6A and enables
a translational stress response mediated by oligomeric Tau [7]. While m6A involvement in oncology is
well studied, its association to tauopathy and AD was more recently uncovered [7]. Under basal conditions, m6A is confined mostly to the nucleus, and with stress, m6A can translocate into the cytoplasm [81,82]. Similar cytoplasmic accumulation of m6A was evident in vivo for P301S tau mice as well as in AD-affected human brain tissue [7]. A recent study showed that m6A abundance is elevated in the hippocampus in 6-month-old APP/PS1 mice, an AD mouse model, and comparative analysis of mRNA m6A modification profiles revealed substantial variation in m6A modifications between APP/PS1 mouse model and control mice [83]. While m6A modification of RNA is found to be relatively high in neurons, it is noticeably low in glial cells, and as ageing occurs, the general level of m6A modification tends to decrease in the brain from 5xFAD and control mice [83]. Given the importance of dynamics of m6A during AD development, and the potential role for m6A modification in the onset of the disease [84], high levels of m6A within GGACU-containing transcripts can lead to high-affinity interactions with HNRNPA2B1. An analogous HNRNP, i.e. HNRNPDL2, was proposed to possess RNA recognition properties along with its fibrillar forming properties in its C-terminal domain, and its functionality to bind RNA was suggested to be attributed to its RRM domains [85]. We speculate that high levels of m6A in neurons can potentially lead to high-affinity interactions with the RRM domains of HNRNPA2B1, while HNRNPA2B1 C-terminal domain fibrils can cross-interact with Tau fibrils. Thus, a potential mechanism could comprise m6A transcripts binding HNRNPA2B1 in the nucleus and cytoplasm, and HNRNPA2B1 co-localization and co-aggregation with tau in stress granules. Previous studies suggested HNRNPA2B1 to act as a reader of m6A in the nucleus, and also as an m6A reader in the cytoplasm, allowing oligomeric Tau to interact with m6A transcripts (reviewed in [7]); we suggest that this could be most likely due to its high-affinity to GG-m6A-CU transcripts, and not necessarily due to specificity over unmodified transcripts. It is also important to note that our study does not necessarily preclude the possibility of additional indirect binding, potentially via a mechanism shown for HNRNPC [31]. However, given the fact that HNRNPC contains a single RRM domain which recognizes poly-(U) sequences [86], the mechanism of recognition for m6A-containing transcripts by the two RRM domains of HNRNPA2B1 could be more complex. Furthermore, HNRNPA2B1 has been found to denature stem loop structures, such as the one present in the CDK6 3’UTR [87]. This further obfuscates the hypothesis that HNRNPA2B1 binds with a similar mechanism to HNRNPC, as the latter relies on an increase in accessibility of a single-stranded region of the MALAT1 transcript upon m6A methylation [30]. Notably, on top of the structural and functional differences between HNRNPA2B1 and other HNRNPs, the in vitro affinity of RNA binding proteins for their substrates does not always correlate to their in vivo affinity or functionality. Binding of these proteins can be influenced by the relative abundance of certain RNA transcripts, as well as the presence of other accessory proteins [88]. Given these points, further study is required to clarify the predominant mechanism for m6A recognition within RNA transcripts by HNRNPA2B1, as well as the specific contexts in which this modification is recognized by the protein, in relevance to biology and diseases.

Although not examined here, the arrangement of RRMs could play a key role in m6A recognition, and slight differences in the environment could potentially lead to variations in affinity. Within the simulations, constraints were imposed to maintain the relative position of RRMs interacting with RNA; this could presumably highlight that tight packing of RRMs could enhance m6A modification and needs further investigation.

Within our work, we see a slight, but statistically significant, weakening of RNA binding affinity when m6A was placed at position 2 (5’-A-m6A-GGACUAGC-3’), which represents interactions between the m6A and the RRM1 domain of the protein (Figure 6). Our work therefore supports the idea that HNRNPA2B1 might accommodate m6A-containing transcripts within its RRM domains to a variable degree through a direct binding mechanism, yet in the sequence above, m6A is outside the context of the GGACU motif written by methyltransferases. As m6A modification at position 2 has the capacity to form interactions similar to those of the unmodified adenine, the modification does not disrupt the binding as severely as other canonical substitutions within the AGG (RRM1) motif. The extra methyl group introduces subtle differences, allowing the RNA to maintain most of its native interactions, unlike other canonical substitutions. This also emphasizes the importance of the AGG motif, as its ‘preservation’ with m6A was able to keep high binding affinity.

It is known that other HNRNPs besides HNRNPA2B1 can bind other RNA modifications. HNRNPD, for example, has been shown to interact with 8-oxoG [75–78], and different HNRNP binding sites overlap with intronic pseudouridines [79]. Considering these findings, it is possible that HNRNPA2B1 might interact with other RNA modifications. To investigate if HNRNPA2B1 can interact with other modified nucleobases outside of the m6A context, we performed a screening-based search of modified RNAs at different binding pockets of HNRNPA2B1 with reference to 5’-AAGGACUAGC-3’. We find that i6A, m6A, m62A modifications can be accommodated at positions 2, pseudouridine and m5U modifications at position 7, and Am modification at position 8, and represent possible novel modified targets for HNRNPA2B1 recognition. Thus, in contrast to the well-defined binding pocket for well-studied m6A readers (i.e. the YTH domains), our results suggest it could be possible that HNRNPA2B1 can recognize different RNA modifications at different pockets. Additionally, the length of the RNA motif recognized by this protein suggests a more complex relationship of protein-RNA interactions than for the YTH domains. Thus, our findings can pave the way for future studies investigating the complexity of RNA modifications recognition by HNRNPA2B1 and other HNRNPs.

Our findings highlight the delicate balance between sequence context, RNA modifications, and RRM recognition, and they provide a framework to speculate in disease context on how amyloidogenic proteins, like Tau, may further influence this regulatory layer [80]. Tau binds RNA through low-complexity domains and has been shown to interact with HNRNP proteins, including HNRNPA2B1, within stress granules [7]. Importantly, previous studies showed that oligomeric Tau and HNRNPA2B1 to both co-localize with cytoplasmic m6A granules and that HNRNPA2B1 is required for co-localization of oligomeric Tau with m6A, further suggesting that HNRNPA2B1 tethers oligomeric Tau to m6A and enables
a translational stress response mediated by oligomeric Tau [7]. While m6A involvement in oncology is
well studied, its association to tauopathy and AD was more recently uncovered [7]. Under basal conditions, m6A is confined mostly to the nucleus, and with stress, m6A can translocate into the cytoplasm [81,82]. Similar cytoplasmic accumulation of m6A was evident in vivo for P301S tau mice as well as in AD-affected human brain tissue [7]. A recent study showed that m6A abundance is elevated in the hippocampus in 6-month-old APP/PS1 mice, an AD mouse model, and comparative analysis of mRNA m6A modification profiles revealed substantial variation in m6A modifications between APP/PS1 mouse model and control mice [83]. While m6A modification of RNA is found to be relatively high in neurons, it is noticeably low in glial cells, and as ageing occurs, the general level of m6A modification tends to decrease in the brain from 5xFAD and control mice [83]. Given the importance of dynamics of m6A during AD development, and the potential role for m6A modification in the onset of the disease [84], high levels of m6A within GGACU-containing transcripts can lead to high-affinity interactions with HNRNPA2B1. An analogous HNRNP, i.e. HNRNPDL2, was proposed to possess RNA recognition properties along with its fibrillar forming properties in its C-terminal domain, and its functionality to bind RNA was suggested to be attributed to its RRM domains [85]. We speculate that high levels of m6A in neurons can potentially lead to high-affinity interactions with the RRM domains of HNRNPA2B1, while HNRNPA2B1 C-terminal domain fibrils can cross-interact with Tau fibrils. Thus, a potential mechanism could comprise m6A transcripts binding HNRNPA2B1 in the nucleus and cytoplasm, and HNRNPA2B1 co-localization and co-aggregation with tau in stress granules. Previous studies suggested HNRNPA2B1 to act as a reader of m6A in the nucleus, and also as an m6A reader in the cytoplasm, allowing oligomeric Tau to interact with m6A transcripts (reviewed in [7]); we suggest that this could be most likely due to its high-affinity to GG-m6A-CU transcripts, and not necessarily due to specificity over unmodified transcripts. Thus, the involvement of HNRNPA2B1 could be simply associated with high-affinity interactions between the two, while HNRNPA2B1 does not need to be a (selective) reader for its involvement in such pathological pathways.

Our work highlights that the m6A recognition properties by the RRM domains of HNRNPA2B1 are of higher complexity than its recognition by well-studied YTH domains [32]. It is also important to note that our study does not necessarily preclude the possibility of additional indirect binding, potentially via a mechanism shown for HNRNPC [31]. However, given the fact that HNRNPC contains a single RRM domain which recognizes poly-(U) sequences [86], the mechanism of recognition for m6A-containing transcripts by the two RRM domains of HNRNPA2B1 could be more complex. Furthermore, HNRNPA2B1 has been found to denature stem loop structures, such as the one present in the CDK6 3’UTR [87]. This further obfuscates the hypothesis that HNRNPA2B1 binds with a similar mechanism to HNRNPC, as the latter relies on an increase in accessibility of a single-stranded region of the MALAT1 transcript upon m6A methylation [30]. Notably, on top of the structural and functional differences between HNRNPA2B1 and other HNRNPs, the in vitro affinity of RNA binding proteins for their substrates does not always correlate to their in vivo affinity or functionality. Binding of these proteins can be influenced by the relative abundance of certain RNA transcripts, as well as the presence of other accessory proteins [88]. Given these points, further study is required to clarify the predominant mechanism for m6A recognition within RNA transcripts by HNRNPA2B1, as well as the specific contexts in which this modification is recognized by the protein, in relevance to biology and diseases.
