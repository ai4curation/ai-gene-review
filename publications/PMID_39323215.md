INTRODUCTION: Porokeratosis (PK) is an autoinflammatory keratinization disease
(AIKD) characterized by circular or annular skin lesions with a hyperkeratotic
rim, pathologically shown as the cornoid lamella. Four genes that cause PK are
associated with the mevalonate (MV) pathway. In Chinese PK patients, mevalonate
diphosphate decarboxylase (MVD) is the most common causative gene. The lack of
an animal model has greatly limited research on PK pathogenesis.
MATERIALS AND METHODS: In this research, we constructed K14-CreERT2-Mvdfl/fl
mice using the Cre-LoxP system to create a mouse model for in-depth studies of
PK. The Epidermal Mvd gene was knocked out by intraperitoneal injection of
Tamoxifen (TAM). Pathology, immunohistochemistry, RNA-seq, and Western Blot
analysis were performed.
RESULTS: Skin lesions appeared following Mvd deficiency, and pathological
examination revealed the characteristic cornoid lamella, as well as cutaneous
inflammation. Furthermore, we observed elevated levels of IL-17A and IL-1β, and
a decreased Loricrin level in epidermal Mvd-deficient mice. Compared with the
wild-type (WT) group, Mvd deficiency activated the expression of lipid
metabolism-related proteins.
CONCLUSION: We developed the first mouse model for PK research, enabling further
studies on disease development and treatment approaches.

Introduction

Porokeratosis (PK, MIM 175 800) is a group of autosomal dominant inherited diseases [1]. In 2020, PK was classified as an autoinflammatory keratinization disease (AIKD), a hyperkeratotic skin disease caused by inflammation and hyper‐activated innate immunity [2, 3, 4]. The characteristic manifestation of PK is a circular or annular skin lesion with a distinct hyperkeratotic rim, pathologically shown as the cornoid lamella [5]. PK can transform into skin malignancies such as squamous and basal cell carcinomas [6]. PK significantly impacts aesthetics and poses a potential risk to mental well‐being, thus warranting further attention. However, the exact pathogenesis and effective therapy for PK remain obscure, and there are no animal models available for PK investigations.

Mevalonate diphosphate decarboxylase (MVD) is a critical rate‐limiting enzyme in the mevalonate (MV) pathway [1, 7]. Mutations in the MVD gene are the most prevalent cause of PK in Chinese patients, with the highest detection rate for MVD. cDNA 746T>C (p. F249S) mutation [1]. It has been proven that the inhibition of MVD triggers DNA damage, cell cycle arrest, and ATP concentration reduction [8, 9]. Additionally, mvda silencing in zebrafish exhibits abnormal epidermal morphology and impaired angiogenesis [10].

Previously, our group established a mouse model (Mvd
F250S/+) with a heterozygous point mutation Mvd. cDNA 749T>C (p.F250S) using CRISPR/Cas9. However, no spontaneous and persistent PK‐like skin lesions were observed in Mvd
F250S/+ mice from birth to 60 weeks of age [9]. In addition, we did not obtain any homozygous Mvd point mutation mice, suggesting that a homozygous mutation of Mvd in all organs and cells may cause embryonic lethality in mice.

In this study, we proceeded with constructing a PK mouse model. Considering the potential fatality linked to systemic Mvd silencing, we opted to knock Mvd out specifically in the epidermis. Keratin 14 (K14) is expressed in basal keratinocytes of the stratum basale, including a cluster of progenitor cells [11, 12]. Thus, we generated K14‐CreERT2‐Mvd
fl/fl mice using the Cre‐LoxP system. The transgenic mice allowed for the induction of Mvd deficiency exclusively in keratinocytes through the administration of Tamoxifen (TAM). Skin lesions appeared on the ears, paws, and tail of the mice in which the epidermal Mvd gene was knocked out (Mvd
KO/KO). Pathologic examination revealed classic cornoid lamella. Activated cutaneous inflammation and epidermal barrier defects were also discovered, potentially related to lipid metabolism activation.

In summary, we preliminarily proved that Mvd deficiency in mice could lead to PK pathological symptoms. We speculated that lipid metabolism activation plays a role in PK pathogenesis. Our study lays a foundation for further investigation of the mechanism and therapy of PK.

The expression of Mvd decreased by around 80% in Mvd
KO/KO mice, whereas in MvdF250S/+
 mice, the decline was only around 40% [9]. It is hypothesized that the extent of Mvd reduction plays a critical role in triggering PK‐like skin lesions.

The majority of PK patients with MVD mutations show discrete red‐brown annular keratotic papules or maculopapules [1]. A study on disseminated superficial actinic porokeratosis revealed that the epidermis of PK annular rings had a combination of mutant cells and wild‐type keratinocytes [5]. In this study, the homozygous knockout of the Mvd gene led to the complete deactivation of the epidermal Mvd gene, thus amplifying the severity of the lesions in Mvd
KO/KO mice. This might explain why the lesions were distinct from those observed in clinical PK patients. However, the Mvd
KO/KO model remains the most effective one available.

Abnormalities in lipid metabolism were noted in Mvd
F250S/+ mice. To investigate the potential causes of PK, we conducted a transcriptome analysis of the related genes. Lipid metabolism regulates several physiological processes, including cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and immunological response [17]. PLA2 facilitates the breakdown of phospholipids in cell membranes, resulting in the production of arachidonic acid (AA). AA is then metabolized into cyclooxygenase and prostaglandins, which contribute to the initiation of inflammatory reactions [18]. FABP5 was reported to efficiently promote cell proliferation and activate inflammation [19, 20, 21]. Lipid metabolism is essential for maintaining the structural integrity and balance of the epidermal barrier [17]. Activated lipid metabolism corresponds to our discovery of epidermal barrier damage and elevated inflammation in Mvd
KO/KO mice.

As one of the AIKDs, PK is caused by both inflammation and hyper‐activated innate immunity [2, 3, 4]. Similar to PK patients, our model mice exhibited a chronic inflammatory state for a 3‐month observation period. Recently, Li et al. [22] documented a case of a PK patient with a mutation in the PMVK gene. This patient exhibited increased activation of IL‐17 signaling, and treatment targeting IL‐17 was shown to be effective. In this study, we found that Mvd deficiency also led to IL‐17 overexpression. However, whether anti‐IL‐17 therapy is effective requires further investigation. γδ T cells migrate to the periphery to become tissue‐resident memory T (T
RM) cells [23]. Vγ9Vδ2 T cells are considered to represent both innate and adaptive immunity [24]. Our previous study discovered decreased frequencies of circulating CD8+ and Vγ9Vδ2 T cells in PK patients with MVD variants [25]. We speculated that the reduction in circulating Vγ9Vδ2 T cells might potentially lead to an increase in Vγ9Vδ2 T cells in the skin, hence triggering the activation of innate immunity. In the mouse model, the expression of IL‐1β was upregulated, suggesting that innate immunity had been activated, as is the case with PK patients.

It is well‐established that four causative genes of PK are linked to the MV pathway. The pathogenesis of PK may involve both the accumulation of toxic mevalonate metabolites and potential cholesterol insufficiency. In recent years, topical statin/cholesterol has emerged as a targeted therapeutic approach for PK [6, 26, 27]. Additionally, the MV pathway is responsible for protein prenylation, which modifies the post‐translation of signaling proteins and serves as a critical mechanism in several cellular processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, cytoskeletal function, and vesicle trafficking [28, 29]. We speculate that Mvd deficiency may cause PK by affecting protein prenylation. Further research based on this mouse model will be conducted to explore this hypothesis.

Furthermore, we investigated the impact of various TAM dosages on the dorsal skin. We discovered a positive correlation between the dose and the severity of the skin lesions. Although modest and moderate doses of TAM elicited dorsal lesions, these lesions resolved spontaneously, and histological examinations showed no presence of the characteristic PK signs. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the reduction in Mvd expression was significantly lower in the back compared to the ears. Similar to Mvd
F250S/+ mice, the inadequate knockout efficiency led to the absence of skin lesions. Increasing the TAM concentration to 100 mg/kg resulted in lesions on the back that resembled PK in appearance. However, histological evidence only revealed the presence of inflammation and necrosis, without any PK symptoms. In addition to the mortality resulting from severe skin lesions in mice, the potential adverse effects of TAM cannot be ignored. While TAM is generally regarded as a safe approach for inducing gene knockout, it does have certain harmful consequences, with lethality directly linked to the dose supplied [30]. An alternative, 4‐hydroxytamoxifen (4‐OHT), an active metabolite of TAM, could effectively induce Mvd knockout at the targeted location and enhance the ablation effect [31]. However, it is important to note that this alternative option is more expensive. Given the possibility of future drug experiments on the back to achieve more effective knockout outcomes and optimize the model, we may administer 4‐OHT topically to induce more stable PK lesions on the back.

5ConclusionIn summary, we have established the first mouse model for PK research by inducing Mvd deficiency in the epidermis. This model exhibits key features of PK, including characteristic cornoid lamella, chronic inflammation, and epidermal barrier defects. We hypothesized that activation of lipid metabolism plays a role in the pathogenesis of PK. Based on this model, we have the potential to uncover the fundamental pathogenic mechanisms underlying PK and consequently investigate effective treatments.
