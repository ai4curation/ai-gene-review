The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane protein Rft1 is a scramblase for the
anionic glycolipid Man5GlcNAc2-PP-dolichol (M5-DLO), a key intermediate in the
pathway of protein N-glycosylation. As a member of the
multidrug/oligosaccharidyl-lipid/polysaccharide (MOP) transporter superfamily
Rft1 resembles the bacterial MurJ Lipid II flippase which has an analogous
substrate, but the mechanism by which it translocates M5-DLO is not known. Here
we used AlphaFold3 and Chai1 to develop conformational models of yeast
Rft1-M5-DLO complexes. The models suggest an alternating access transport
mechanism with inward-open, occluded, and outward-facing states. A central
cavity accommodates the hydrophilic M5-DLO head group, while the dolichol lipid
tail is guided through a portal formed between transmembrane helices 1 and 8 to
a hydrophobic groove outside the cavity. Comparative mutational analysis of
cavity residues revealed a significant mechanistic divergence from MurJ which
strictly relies on a conserved charged triad to anchor Lipid II via its
pyrophosphate neck. Whereas mutations to these residues in MurJ result in total
loss of function, Rft1 showed high tolerance to most charge inversion mutations
in the central cavity when tested for function using a yeast reporter strain.
This tolerance implies greater flexibility in Rft1's electrostatic requirements
for engaging M5-DLO. Our findings suggest that Rft1 functions as a specialized,
alternating access transporter for M5-DLO and identify key molecular
determinants that are essential for function. The alternating access mechanism
distinguishes Rft1 from all currently known scramblases which make use of a
hydrophilic transmembrane groove to provide a pathway for lipid transit.

Introduction

Rft1 is an endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane protein1, essential for the viability of yeast and human cells2,3. Its deficiency underlies a congenital disorder of glycosylation, RFT1-CDG2,4,5, associated with defects in protein N-glycosylation in the ER. In Rft1-deficient cells proteins are under-glycosylated and the glycolipid Man5GlcNAc2-PP-dolichol (termed M5-dolichol-linked oligosaccharide, or M5-DLO) accumulates. M5-DLO is a key intermediate in the dolichol pathway that generates Glc3Man9GlcNAc2-PP -dolichol (G3M9-DLO), the oligosaccharide donor for N-glycosylation in the ER lumen (Fig. 1A)6–10. Paradoxically, M5-DLO is synthesized on the cytoplasmic face of the ER and must be flipped across the membrane to the luminal side to be converted to G3M9-DLO (Fig. 1A). Translocation of M5-DLO across the ER membrane is essential for N-glycosylation, and it was proposed, based on yeast genetics experiments, that this key step is facilitated by Rft13,11. In support of this proposal a recent report indicated that purified yeast and human Rft1 proteins are indeed capable of translocating M5-DLO across the membrane when reconstituted into synthetic lipid vesicles12. As translocation occurred in the absence of metabolic energy, Rft1 is considered to be a scramblase-class lipid transporter13,14.

In contrast to these results, other biochemical studies using microsomes and reconstituted systems suggest that Rft1 is not necessary for M5-DLO translocation in complex membrane protein mixtures, and that redundant transport mechanisms exist. Thus, intact microsomes prepared from Rft1-deficient yeast cells were shown to convert newly synthesized GlcNAc2-PP-dolichol to Man9GlcNAc2-PP-dolichol and import GlcNAc2-PP-dolichol15, a water-soluble M5-DLO analog15. These results indicate that the microsomes have M5-DLO translocation activity despite Rft1 deficiency. In complementary experiments, ER membrane proteins were extracted with detergent from yeast or rat liver microsomes and reconstituted into large unilamellar vesicles16. The resulting proteoliposomes were shown to translocate M5-DLO in an ATP-independent manner, indicative of the activity of a scramblase(s)1,16–18. Scramblase activity in the reconstituted system was highly specific such that higher order DLOs (Glc1–2Man6–9GlcNAc2-PP-dolichol) and a triantennary structural isomer of M5-DLO were scrambled much more slowly than the natural lipid19. However, elimination of Rft1 from the protein mixture prior to reconstitution did not have a detectable effect on activity1,16,17. These results indicate that the contribution of Rft1’s demonstrated scramblase activity to the overall M5-DLO scramblase activity of the ER protein mixture is minor and that other M5-DLO scramblases are present in the ER extract.

The combined genetic and biochemical evidence suggests that Rft1 is a moonlighting protein20 with dual (or multiple) functions. It’s essential role in yeast and human cells is inextricably tied to the dolichol pathway of protein N-glycosylation yet appears to be something other than its M5-DLO scramblase activity as redundant scramblases exist. Nevertheless, we considered that an analysis of Rft1’s scrambling mechanism would yield novel insights into how a lipid with the structural complexity of M5-DLO is translocated across a membrane bilayer and also potentially guide methods for the identification of additional M5-DLO scramblases. We note that the only precedents for scramblases transporting lipids with large headgroups are members of the TMEM16 and GPCR families and bacterial MprF which are capable of scrambling PEGylated phospholipids (headgroup size up to 5 kDa)21,22 and, in the case of the GPCR opsin, also an anionic glycosylphosphatidylinositol glycolipid23. Interestingly, opsin cannot scramble M5-DLO23.

Sequence analyses place Rft1 in the multidrug/oligosaccharidyl-lipid/polysaccharide (MOP) exporter superfamily of transporters24 which includes the bacterial MurJ flippases25. MurJ transports Lipid II (glycopeptide -PP-undecaprenol), a large anionic lipid that is structurally analogous to M5-DLO and essential for peptidoglycan synthesis in bacteria26. Structurally, MurJ is built on the canonical 12-transmembrane helix (TM) MOP core (TM1–12) but features two additional helices (TM13 and TM14) that form a deep, hydrophobic groove on the protein’s surface, positioned to accommodate the long (C55) undecaprenyl tail of Lipid II during flipping27,28. MurJ transports Lipid II across the bacterial inner membrane using an alternating access mechanism involving transitions between inward-open and outward-open conformations. This transport cycle is powered by membrane potential (ΔΨ) and coupled to Na+ antiport which is required to reset the transporter from the outward-open to the inward-open state29. Transport of Lipid II strictly depends on a charged-residue triad that coordinates the anionic pyrophosphate of the substrate within the central cavity of the protein30. The energy requirement makes MurJ a flippase-type transporter, distinguishing it from Rft1.

In the absence of an experimentally determined atomic structure of Rft1, we used state-of-the-art structure prediction tools, Alphafold331 and Chai132, to model the conformational landscape of the protein and its interaction with M5-DLO. Although the results from the two algorithms were complementary, Chai1 additionally predicted apo- and M5-DLO-bound Rft1 structures consistent with an alternating access translocation mechanism. Using sequence conservation and residue interaction analysis of the M5-DLO bound models we predicted putative M5-DLO interacting residues to guide the design of loss of function point mutants. To test the functionality of these mutants experimentally, we used Tet-off yeast cells in which transcription of the RFT1 gene is controlled by doxycycline (Dox). These cells fail to grow on Dox unless Rft1 is supplied via ectopic expression from a plasmid. Unexpectedly, the majority of the mutants, singly or in combination, supported growth of the cells on Dox. However, mutation at a single site which is predicted to engage the diphosphate neck of the M5-DLO structure had a deleterious effect on function that could be partially rescued by over-expression of the mutant protein. These results and the difference in energy requirement, suggest significant mechanistic divergence of Rft1 from MurJ, arguably its closest MOP family relative of known mechanism.

To investigate Rft1’s scramblase mechanism, we modeled the interaction between Rft1 and M5-DLO using AlphaFold3 and Chai1. AlphaFold3 outperformed Chai1 in generating realistic models of M5-DLO, when the ligand was defined using the CCD format linked via the BAP syntax35, successfully enforcing the correct topology and stereochemistry of the ligand specifically of the N-acetyl-α-D-glucosamine residues which had longer than ideal bond lengths in the Chai1 models (Fig. S3). Crucially, the Chai1 algorithm also predicted inward-open and partially occluded conformations for both the apo, and M5-DLO-bound states whereas AlphaFold3 only modeled the inward facing open conformation based on the experimentally determined topology of the N- and C- termini1. Thus, the outputs of the two algorithms are complementary (Fig. 2A– B).

In the docked models, the M5-DLO glycan headgroup is sequestered within the central cavity, stabilized by a network of hydrogen bonds with residues in TMs 2, 7, and 8, while the diphosphate neck electrostatically engages R334 (contact frequency = 5/5) and K34 (contact frequency = 3/5) (Fig. 2C, see also Fig. 5A). The lipid tail traverses the lateral portal formed between TM1 (F38) and TM8 (Y328) to nestle within the hydrophobic groove (Figs. 2B, see also Fig. 6D). We identified, in total, 64 putative interacting residues within 5 Å of the docked M5-DLO using the Chai1 models; notably, five correspond to positions mutated in RFT1-CDG, underscoring their functional importance (Table S2). Based on conservation (ConSurf grade ≥ 8) and contact frequency in the 5 predicted conformational poses, we selected 21 of the 64 residues for mutagenesis screening (Table 1). For 13 of the 21 residues, we assessed whether charge inversion, e.g., K34D, resulted in a functional protein. For the others, we made polar to hydrophobic mutations, choosing residues not found at the equivalent site in other Rft1 orthologs.

A reporter strain to test functionality of Rft1 mutants.We considered several cell-based methods to test the functionality of Rft1 mutants and opted for a promoter replacement system in which transcription of the RFT1 gene in yeast is controlled by Dox36,37. In these haploid Tet-off cells the addition of Dox is expected to cause rapid cessation of transcription and turnover of mRNA, followed by loss of Rft1 protein through degradation and dilution due to cell division38. As Rft1 is essential, cell growth should slow on Dox addition as Rft1 levels fall. To test the Tet-off strain, we transformed the cells with an empty vector (EV) or a vector encoding wild-type (WT) Rft1 under control of the relatively weak ADH promoter39. Both transformants grew well in the absence of Dox, although the doubling time of the EV cells (4.9 h) was 1.8x greater than that of Rft1-expressing cells (2.7 h), indicating that endogenous Rft1 (expressed via the Tet promoter system) is limiting in the EV cells (Fig. 3B). Indeed, the level of Rft1 expressed under the control of the ADH promoter is >10-fold higher than that of Rft1 expressed from its own promoter (not shown). DiscussionThe alternating access mechanism suggested by the Chai1 models (Fig. 2A) fundamentally distinguishes Rft1 from known scramblases which operate via a hydrophilic groove employing a “credit card mechanism” to facilitate transbilayer lipid transport27,40–42. The predicted Rft1 structure mirrors the non-canonical MOP fold seen in MurJ in which a central cavity accommodates the polar/anionic head group of the lipid substrate facilitating its translocation across the hydrophobic bilayer while the tail of the lipid emerges through a portal to associate with an external hydrophobic groove27,43 (Fig. 2A).A key feature of MurJ is the electrostatic environment of the central cavity, which is lined with conserved basic residues, including a charged triad (R24 and D25 on TM1 and R255 on TM8 in T. africanus MurJ (in E. coli MurJ the third residue of the triad is R270)) which stabilizes the anionic diphosphate neck of Lipid II27. Charge inversion or alanine substitution of these basic residues and other key cavity residues results in a total loss of function, indicating a strict requirement for precise electrostatic coordination of the substrate (Fig. S1C) 30. Our Rft1-M5-DLO model diverges from MurJ in that it has only two of the three residues of the charged triad, R34 (TM1) and R334 (TM8) (a structural equivalent to the essential D25 residue does not exist), which we nevertheless predict to form electrostatic interactions with the anionic diphosphate moiety of M5-DLO (Fig. 2B). Indeed, our tests showed that the charge-inversion mutation R334D rendered Rft1 poorly functional (Fig. 5): when expressed under the ADH promoter in the Tet-off reporter strain, R334D-Rft1 barely supported growth in Dox-containing media but showed increased functionality when expression was driven up 4–5-fold using a stronger promoter (Fig. 5B,D). This rescue by mass action suggests that the R334D mutation likely compromises the catalytic efficiency of the transporter rather than causing total loss of function, a finding that stands in contrast to the stringent electrostatic requirements observed in E. coli MurJ, where – as noted above – charge reversal of multiple key cavity residues (including R18, R24 and R52) results in total loss of function, not merely a reduction in catalytic efficiency or expression levels30.We were surprised that charge inversion and other mutations at most of the sites that we predicted would be relevant in contacting M5-DLO had relatively little effect on the functionality of Rft1 in our yeast reporter assay (Fig. 4). A possible explanation is that the large headgroup of M5-DLO is stabilized via multiple contacts and that single mutations would not necessarily have a striking effect (except in the case of R334D, as noted above, which involves a highly specific, targeted interaction between the cationic residue and the diphosphate of the lipid). We also considered the possibility that the robust functionality of most of the mutants may be a result of Rft1 being expressed in excess of cellular requirements, thereby masking partial defects in specific activity. Indeed, the functionality of the T303L mutant, which supports normal growth despite accumulating to only ~7% of WT levels (Fig. 4), implies a significant ‘functional reserve’ in the system. However, two lines of evidence suggest that the charge tolerance we observed is a genuine mechanistic feature. First, our liquid culture assays proved sensitive enough to detect partial defects, such as those seen in the F38D/Y328K portal mutant (Fig. 6), yet no such defects were observed for the cavity charge mutants K34D, E60K, R71D, R179D, E183K, K299D and E342K. Second, and most importantly, analogous mutations in the bacterial homolog MurJ result in complete lethality, a phenotype that would not be masked by expression levels. Thus, the contrast between the lethality of charge inversions in MurJ and the full functionality of those in Rft1 reflects a fundamental divergence in the electrostatic mechanisms of substrate coordination between these two proteins.Despite this general tolerance to charge inversion, the functional analysis demonstrated the importance of specific charges, particularly evinced by the impaired function of R334D (discussed above) and to a lesser extent, D72K, D306K, and K307D. The functionality of the polar-to-hydrophobic triple mutant (T303L/S326L/N327L) further suggests a high degree of plasticity in substrate engagement, characteristic of MOP superfamily members such as the Multidrug and Toxin Extrusion family, which accommodate chemically dissimilar substrates44. Alternatively, the transport mechanism may rely heavily on shape complementarity rather than precise electrostatic steering. This proposed recognition mechanism diverges from structural models of MurJ, wherein substrate binding is predicted to be driven primarily by electrostatic interactions with the diphosphate moiety, involving fewer contacts with the relatively smaller disaccharide-pentapeptide of lipid II (Fig. S1C). Conversely, the Rft1–M5-DLO interaction appears driven by an extensive hydrogen bond network with the large glycan head group and a rigorous steric fit, necessitating a relatively minor contribution from diphosphate coordination (Fig. 2C). This hypothesis is supported by the deleterious effect of mutating the uncharged G424 to a bulky glutamine (G424Q) (Fig. 5) and is consistent with prior observations that the structural isomer iM5-DLO is poorly translocated in reconstituted systems19.Our prediction of Rft1’s structure and potential conformational transitions, leaves open the question of the energetic driving force for M5-DLO translocation. Biochemical studies indicate that Rft1 activity is ATP-independent12, ruling out primary active transport. In bacteria, MurJ and other MOP superfamily members often function as secondary active transporters coupled to a proton motive force or sodium gradients25,29. However, given that M5-DLO is continuously synthesized on the cytoplasmic leaflet and rapidly consumed in the lumen for G3M9-DLO synthesis, a steep chemical gradient exists across the ER membrane. Thus, Rft1 likely acts as a scramblase to lower the energetic barrier for translocation, with transport directionality imposed by the irreversible consumption of the substrate in the lumen. Nevertheless, further biophysical studies will be necessary to determine potential ion-dependence of transport and whether Rft1 retains any of the cation-coupling characteristics of its bacterial ancestors or has evolved into a pure facilitator.

Yeast growth assays.Plasmids were introduced into the Tet-off-RFT1 strain (YAKM147: URA3::CMV-tTA, his3–1 leu2–0 met15–0 RFT1::kanR-TetO7-CYC1TATA-RFT1). Transformants were selected on an SD(-Leu) plate. For growth assays on plates (spotting assays), colonies were resuspended in sterile water and adjusted to OD600 = 1.0. Ten-fold serial dilutions were prepared and 10 μL of each dilution was spotted onto SD(-Leu) plates with or without 10 μg/mL Dox. Plates were incubated at 30°C for 2–3 days before being photographed. For analysis of the growth of cells in suspension, growth curves were generated essentially as described previously1. Briefly, transformed cells were pre-cultured overnight in SD(-Leu) medium, diluted into fresh SD(-Leu) medium, and grown to mid-log phase (OD600 = 0.4–0.8). Cells were then diluted into YPD + Dox (0.5 μg/mL), OD600 = 0.01, and dispensed into a 96-well plate (200 μL per well). The plate was sealed with a Breathe-Easy polyurethane membrane (Sigma-Aldrich, Z380059) and incubated at 30°C in a SpectraMax i3x plate reader (Molecular Devices). OD600 was recorded every 15 min for 40 h with 5 s of orbital shaking prior to each measurement. Doubling time was calculated as previously described1, where C1 and C2 were chosen within the linear portion of the growth curve, usually OD600 = 0.5–0.65.

Protein extraction and immunoblotting.This was done as described previously1. Briefly, cells were cultured to mid-log phase, and 1 OD600 unit of cells was collected. Cells were lysed in 0.28 mL of 0.2 M NaOH containing 0.5% β-mercaptoethanol and incubated on ice for 5 min. Proteins were precipitated by adding 0.5 mL of 30% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and incubating on ice for 10 min. Precipitated proteins were collected by centrifugation (14,000 rpm, 5 min, 4°C), washed twice with 0.2 mL of ice-cold acetone, air-dried, and solubilized overnight in 50 μL of 2% SDS, 5 mM NaOH, and 2% β-mercaptoethanol. The samples were mixed with 50 μL of 2× SDS-PAGE loading buffer and subjected to SDS-PAGE/immunoblotting. CPY, GAPDH, and Rft1–3xFLAG variants were detected with anti-CPY (1:5000; Invitrogen 10A5B5), anti-GAPDH (1:100,000; Sigma G9545), and anti-FLAG (1:5000; Sigma-Aldrich F1804) antibodies and visualized using horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000; Promega W402B) and anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000; Promega W401B). Signals were visualized using an Odyssey XF imaging system (LI-COR), and band intensities were quantified with Image Studio software (LI-COR).
