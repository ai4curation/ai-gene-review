GCN5-related N-acetyltransferases (GNATs) are essential for regulating bacterial
metabolism by acetylating specific target proteins. Despite their importance in
bacterial physiology, the mechanisms behind their enzymatic and regulatory
functions remain poorly understood. In this study, we investigated the
structures of Escherichia coli protein acetyltransferase Z (PatZ), a Type I
GNAT, and examined its ligand interactions, catalytic mechanism, and allosteric
regulation. PatZ functions as a homotetramer, with each subunit comprising a
catalytic and a regulatory domain. Our results demonstrate that the regulatory
domain is vital for acetyltransferase activity, as it triggers cooperative
conformational changes in the catalytic domain and directly aids in the
formation of substrate-binding pockets. Additionally, a protein structure-based
evolutionary analysis of bacterial GNAT types revealed a distinct regulatory
domain pattern across phyla, highlighting its crucial role in responding to
cellular energy levels.

## Full Text

Posttranslational modifications (PTMs) are essential in altering protein structure, stability, activity, and cellular localization (1–3). Acetylation, a prominent PTM, involves the attachment of an acetyl group to a lysine residue or the N terminus of a protein (4). This modification occurs across all life forms, mediated enzymatically by acetyltransferases or nonenzymatically via high-energy compounds such as acetyl phosphate (AcP) or acetyl coenzyme A (AcCoA) (5–8). In eukaryotes, acetylation affects both histones and nonhistone proteins (9, 10), thereby influencing a wide range of cellular and physiological processes, including transcription, phase separation, autophagy, mitosis, cellular differentiation, and neuronal activities (11–16). Disruptions in acetylation regulation are linked to cancer and aging (17, 18). In prokaryotes, acetylation regulates metabolism, cell growth, and survival, modifying metabolic fluxes (19–21), and influences gene expression by altering transcription factor activity in response to environmental shifts (22, 23). Although the physiological importance of Nε-lysine acetylation is well established in eukaryotes, its biological significance in bacteria is still emerging.

Enzymatic acetylation involves transfer of an acetyl group to a lysine residues by lysine acetyltransferases (KATs) (24). Based on sequence homology and biochemical characteristics, KATs are classified into families, including the p300/cAMP response element binding protein (CBP) family; Moz, Ybf2/Sas3, Sas2, Tip60 (MYST) family; and GCN5-related N-acetyltransferase (GNAT) family (11, 25, 26). The p300/CBP and MYST families are found exclusively in eukaryotic cells, whereas the GNAT family encompasses homologous proteins in bacteria, eukaryotes, and archaea (24). Approximately 65% of the GNAT domain superfamily is found in bacteria (27). Recent studies show that acetylation significantly affects bacterial physiology, impacting translation in Escherichia coli (28), metabolic flux in Salmonella enterica (29), and virulence in Francisella novicida (30). These findings highlight the broad and critical impact of acetylation on bacterial processes, from fundamental cellular functions to complex pathogenic mechanisms.

The catalytic GNAT domain is highly conserved across species, but bacterial GNATs possess unique regulatory domains that vary among different bacterial species (8). These GNATs are classified into five types based on the structure and arrangement of the catalytic GNAT and regulatory domains (Fig. 1A). Types I and II have a homologous nucleoside diphosphate–forming AcCoA synthetase (ACD) structure in the regulatory domain, whereas Type III features a cAMP-binding protein; aspartate kinase, chorismate mutase, TyrA (ACT); or an NADP+-binding protein (8). Types IV and V lack a regulatory domain. Truncations or mutations in these regulatory domains significantly reduce GNAT catalytic activity (8, 31), implying that the regulatory and GNAT domains are functionally interdependent. Notably, protein acetyltransferase Z (PatZ), also known as YfiQ, is a well-characterized Type I GNAT, with approximately 80% of its amino acids in the regulatory domain and 20% in the GNAT domain. PatZ alters its oligomeric state and activity depending on AcCoA presence, exhibiting distinct patterns in E. coli and S. enterica (32, 33). Recent studies of PatZ show that the regulatory domain enhances acetyltransferase activity, and the ligand binding to the regulatory domain increases AcCoA affinity for the GNAT domain (32). However, the precise mechanisms remain unclear.

The regulatory domain of PatZ shares structural homology with ACDs, which are key energy-conserving enzymes in the sugar and peptide fermentation pathways of hyperthermophilic archaea (34). In Candidatus Korarchaeum cryptofilum ACD1 (ckcACD1), the enzyme adopts an α2β2-heterotetrameric organization, where the α- and β-subunits first form a heterodimer, which subsequently assembles into the functional quaternary structure. In contrast, PatZ consists of a single polypeptide chain. Sequence analysis reveals that the catalytic H254 of ACD1’s α-subunit is replaced by L245 in PatZ. This substitution eliminates the activity of PatZ to catalyze ACD-like reactions, fundamentally altering its enzymatic function.

In this study, we elucidated the homotetrameric structure of functional PatZ using cryoelectron microscopy (cryoEM). Each subunit of PatZ consists of four structural domains, binding two AcCoA molecules, one phosphate and one ATP. The apo-PatZ adopts an inactive conformation, with a closed substrate-binding pocket. Our findings demonstrate that the regulatory domains undergo conformational changes upon ligand binding, altering their interactions with the GNAT domain. Specifically, AcCoA binding to the regulatory domain triggers a cooperative structural rearrangement in the GNAT domain, facilitating AcCoA binding and organizing the substrate for catalysis. Additionally, our analysis suggests that the PatZ regulatory domain plays a pivotal role in substrate interaction. We also performed a phylogenetic analysis of bacterial GNAT-mediated acetyltransferase families, providing insights into the regulatory domain’s role in activating distinct acetyl-PTM responses to various metabolic signals.

ResultsOverall Architecture of E. coli PatZ.To investigate the biochemical and structural properties of PatZ, we purified the E. coli PatZ and assessed its acetyltransferase activity on purified E. coli AcCoA synthetase (Acs) using western blotting with an acetyl-lysine-specific antibody (Fig. 1B). When supplied with AcCoA, PatZ efficiently catalyzed the acetylation of Acs, in agreement with previous studies (35, 36). This PatZ-mediated enzymatic reaction occurred at a significantly faster rate than the AcP-mediated nonenzymatic reaction (Fig. 1B) (37), confirming the activity and functionality of the purified PatZ.We then elucidated the three-dimensional structure of the apo-PatZ to better understand its molecular architecture and assembly. The protein, with a protomer mass of approximately 100 kDa, predominantly forms a homotetramer in solution, as indicated by size exclusion chromatography (Fig. 1C). Consistent with this, the cryoEM structure of PatZ revealed a tetramer with a four-fold symmetric architecture. We obtained a resolution of 2.52 Å for a consensus map, which revealed the composition of four distinct subunits. These subunits exhibited varying resolution around the structure’s periphery, which was enhanced through multiple rounds of three-dimensional (3D) classification and variability analysis for each domain (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–E and Table S1).A molecular model was built based on the cryoEM map and validated by high-quality statistical analysis (Q-score = 0.67, SI Appendix, Fig. S1F). The tetramer adopts a dimer-of-dimers organization, with each dimer rotated 180° relative to its adjacent dimers (Fig. 1D and Movie S1). The tetramer dimensions are approximately 190 Å along the long axis and 130 Å along the short axis. Each subunit consists of four distinct structural domains. Based on structural similarities with ACDs (34) and proteins containing GNAT motifs (38), we identified these regions as the coenzyme A (CoA)-binding domain (CBD), ligase-CoA domain (LCD), ATP grasp domain (AGD), and GNAT domain (GNATD), arranged sequentially from the N- to the C-terminus (Fig. 1E and Movie S1).PatZ exhibits a strong tendency to form a stable homotetramer, with significant interactions among the four protomers. The interactions exhibited significant contact areas with considerable stabilization energies (Fig. 1 F and G). The lateral interfaces between protomers 1 and 2, and 3 and 4, feature contact areas of approximately 3,000 and 3,025 Å2, with corresponding ΔiG values of −32.2 kcal/mol and −36.4 kcal/mol, respectively. Additionally, the axial interfaces between protomers 1 and 3, and 2 and 4, exhibit smaller contact areas of 482 and 484 Å2, with ΔiG values of −8.8 kcal/mol and −8.9 kcal/mol, respectively. The lateral interface is established through an interaction between the CBD and LCD, with dominant polar interactions between the CBD and LCD (Fig. 1F). Conversely, the axial interface is stabilized by CBD–CBD interactions, with the domains twisted at a 60-degree angle relative to each other (Fig. 1G), which stabilizes the structure through symmetric hydrophobic contacts. However, in the cryoEM map of wild-type PatZ in the presence of ATP, no density corresponding to ATP was observed. To further investigate potential ATP binding to wild-type PatZ, we performed differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assays. The DSF analysis revealed no change in the inflection temperature (Ti) values in the presence or absence of ATP (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C), and while the ITC assay detected heat signals, it did not yield a measurable Kd (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). A buffer control titration produced no significant heat changes (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A) supporting the interpretation that wild-type PatZ exhibits highly low ATP affinity under the tested conditions. The binding of AcCoA to GNATD induces significant structural changes, particularly in the substrate gating motif (V746-T763, hereafter referred to as the gating motif), which is located near the substrate access channel and undergoes a notable transition in secondary structure (Fig. 3A and Movie S3). To investigate how AcCoA binding to the RD affects PatZ activity, we analyzed the AcCoA binding affinity of PatZ using ITC. In the absence of AcCoA, the gating motif sterically blocks the substrate-binding sites and occludes the passage, indicating an inhibited conformation (Fig. 4 H–J). Upon AcCoA binding, the gating motif interacts with AcCoA to expose the substrate-binding sites and align the AcCoA with the protein substrates, indicating an activated conformation (Fig. 4 H–J). Similar AcCoA-induced structural changes have been observed in other GNATs, such as RimL from Salmonella typhimurium (44), Rv0819 from Mycobacterium tuberculosis (45), and ssPat from Sulfolobus solfataricus (46). RimL and ssPat are GNATD-only enzymes without the RD (Type IV GNAT), whereas Rv0819 is a tandem-repeated GNAT enzyme (Type V GNAT). In these studies, the homologous region to the gating motif is referred to as the α1–α2 loop, mobile loop, or bent helix, which remains unresolved in the absence of AcCoA owing to their inherent dynamics. AcCoA binding rigidifies this loop, forming a substrate-binding pocket similar to the gating motif of PatZ. Notably, a mutation study of ssPat demonstrated that the bent helix is essential for enzyme activity.Importantly, the gating motif in GNATs is conserved not only in prokaryotes but also in eukaryotes, including humans (SI Appendix, Fig. S12A), suggesting a common autoregulation mechanism across species. Human Naa40p [PDB: 4U9V (47)] and Naa50p [PDB: 3TFY (48)] are N-terminal acetyltransferases that are structurally similar to GNATD of PatZ (SI Appendix, Fig. S12B). Both enzymes have an “α1–α2 loop” that is similar in sequence and structure to the gating motif in PatZ’s GNATD (SI Appendix, Fig. S12B). Studies (47, 48) have shown that the α1–α2 loop is flexible and plays an important role in how substrates access and interact with the enzymes. Additionally, research by Abboud et al. (49) using molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that the movements of the α1–α2 subdomain control ligand uptake and substrate interactions. These findings suggest that the α1–α2 loop in Naa40p and Naa50p works in a similar way to the gating motif in PatZ, pointing to a common mechanism in acetyltransferases with different substrate preferences. Based on the clear visualization of both the inhibitory and active conformations of GNATD in PatZ, we propose a gating motif–mediated autoregulatory mechanism for GNATs. Interestingly, despite the high structural similarity among GNATs, the gating motif shows relatively low sequence conservation (50) (SI Appendix, Fig. S12A). Given its role in forming the substrate binding pocket, it is plausible that the gating motif also contributes to substrate specificity, which warrants further investigation.In this study, we demonstrate that PatZ undergoes allosteric regulation, where AcCoA binding to CBD not only enhances GNATD’s affinity for AcCoA but also plays a crucial role in the proper allosteric control of PatZ’s enzymatic activity. Based on our structure of liganded-PatZ, we generated the RR mutant, in which R26 and R81 residues were substituted with glutamate to abolish the AcCoA-binding pocket in CBD. As expected, ITC analysis of this RR mutant revealed one-site binding and a loss of positive cooperativity. However, the RR mutant still exhibited acetyltransferase activity (Fig. 4 C and D). To investigate whether the RR mutant adopts an activated or inactivated conformation, we performed 3D reconstruction of PatZ RR mutant using cryoEM after mixing it with AcCoA. The results revealed that the RR mutant adopts the activated conformation with AcCoA bound to CBD (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). This suggests that the RR mutant may not accurately represent the fully AcCoA-unbound state of the CBD but instead reflects a state with reduced AcCoA binding affinity. In the enzymatic activity assay, significant activity was detected only under conditions with sufficiently high AcCoA concentrations, indicating that reduced affinity can be overcome at elevated substrate levels. Similarly, during structural analysis, an excess amount of AcCoA was used, likely facilitating the stabilization of the active conformation of the CBD. These data support the assumption that the RR mutant has a decreased AcCoA binding affinity.We demonstrated that ligand binding to the RD significantly stabilizes the intersubunit interface and rigidifies PatZ’s homotetrameric architecture (Fig. 4G). This conformational stabilization of the RD correlates with the activation of the gating motif induced by AcCoA binding to GNATD (Fig. 4I). These cooperative structural events result in distinct domain-wise conformations of GNATD in both active and inactive positions. The terms “active”- and “inactive”-positioning are used based on the state of the substrate access channel regulated by GNATD (Fig. 4 H–J). In the absence of AcCoA, the gating motif of GNATD interacts with CBD, leading to the inactive-positioning of GNATD (Fig. 4 I, Left panel). In this conformation, the tethered gating motif restricts the substrate access channel, indicating an inhibitory state (Fig. 4 J, Left panel). Upon AcCoA binding, the gating motif loses its interaction with CBD, allowing GNATD to interact with AGD and adopt an active position (Fig. 4 I, Right panel). This positioning creates a wide opening between GNATD and CBD, enabling access to the substrate binding site and indicating an activated conformation (Fig. 4 J, Right panel). Structural analyses suggest that binding of AcCoA to both CBD and GNATD contributes to opening the substrate access channel (Fig. 4 J, Right panel). However, our data do not conclusively demonstrate that simultaneous binding at both sites is strictly required for gate opening. Further biochemical and structural experiments will be necessary to determine whether single-site binding alone is sufficient to induce an activated conformation.Interestingly, the structure of PatZ with bound phosphate also shows GNATD in an inactive position, similar to the inactive gating motif conformation. The RD in this structure is identical to that of the AcCoA-bound form, suggesting that the stability of the RD alone does not activate GNATD. Because GNATD alone reduces enzymatic activity, these findings indicate that AcCoA binding to both CBD and GNATD is likely required for optimal PatZ activation. Our results (Fig. 4 C and D) and previous studies demonstrate the cooperative binding of AcCoA to GNATD and CBD. AcCoA binding to wild-type GNATD is approximately twice as strong as that to GNATD alone and six times stronger to the RD than to GNATD (Fig. 4C). Collectively, these findings suggest that AcCoA binding to CBD reduces the dynamics of the RD, enhances AcCoA binding to GNATD, promotes gating motif rearrangement, and activates the enzyme.Thus, our study elucidates the structural basis of the active and inactive conformations in the interplay between GNATD and the RD, providing key insights into the regulatory mechanism of PatZ. This mechanism operates through a dual strategy: dynamic transitions between active and inactive states and allosteric modulation of enzyme activity via cooperative AcCoA binding.Previous studies have highlighted PatZ’s unique specificity for Acs (33, 51), and it has been observed that strains lacking PatZ do not acetylate Acs (52). Our results identified a structural opening event in GNATD, in cooperation with CBD, which forms an Acs-specific binding pocket (Fig. 5). These findings reveal additional functional layers of the RD. Moreover, GNATD alone in PatZ reduces enzymatic activity toward Acs (41) (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S8), and point mutations in the RD that disrupt Acs interaction significantly reduce Acs acetylation (Fig. 5 C and D). Our results further demonstrate that Acs binds complementary to the substrate binding site formed by both GNATD and the RD (Fig. 5 A and B). The residues involved in Acs–PatZ interaction are highly conserved (SI Appendix, Fig. S10E). The gating motif (V746-T763), located near the substrate-binding site in GNATD, undergoes significant structural changes, governing the opening and closing of a passage that connects the donor AcCoA and the acceptor protein substrate (Figs. 4 H–J and 5). In the absence of AcCoA, the gating motif sterically blocks the substrate-binding sites and occludes the passage, indicating an inhibited conformation (Fig. 4 H–J). Upon AcCoA binding, the gating motif interacts with AcCoA to expose the substrate-binding sites and align the AcCoA with the protein substrates, indicating an activated conformation (Fig. 4 H–J). Similar AcCoA-induced structural changes have been observed in other GNATs, such as RimL from Salmonella typhimurium (44), Rv0819 from Mycobacterium tuberculosis (45), and ssPat from Sulfolobus solfataricus (46). RimL and ssPat are GNATD-only enzymes without the RD (Type IV GNAT), whereas Rv0819 is a tandem-repeated GNAT enzyme (Type V GNAT). In these studies, the homologous region to the gating motif is referred to as the α1–α2 loop, mobile loop, or bent helix, which remains unresolved in the absence of AcCoA owing to their inherent dynamics. AcCoA binding rigidifies this loop, forming a substrate-binding pocket similar to the gating motif of PatZ. Notably, a mutation study of ssPat demonstrated that the bent helix is essential for enzyme activity.

Importantly, the gating motif in GNATs is conserved not only in prokaryotes but also in eukaryotes, including humans (SI Appendix, Fig. S12A), suggesting a common autoregulation mechanism across species. Human Naa40p [PDB: 4U9V (47)] and Naa50p [PDB: 3TFY (48)] are N-terminal acetyltransferases that are structurally similar to GNATD of PatZ (SI Appendix, Fig. S12B). Both enzymes have an “α1–α2 loop” that is similar in sequence and structure to the gating motif in PatZ’s GNATD (SI Appendix, Fig. S12B). Studies (47, 48) have shown that the α1–α2 loop is flexible and plays an important role in how substrates access and interact with the enzymes. Additionally, research by Abboud et al. (49) using molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that the movements of the α1–α2 subdomain control ligand uptake and substrate interactions. These findings suggest that the α1–α2 loop in Naa40p and Naa50p works in a similar way to the gating motif in PatZ, pointing to a common mechanism in acetyltransferases with different substrate preferences. Based on the clear visualization of both the inhibitory and active conformations of GNATD in PatZ, we propose a gating motif–mediated autoregulatory mechanism for GNATs. Interestingly, despite the high structural similarity among GNATs, the gating motif shows relatively low sequence conservation (50) (SI Appendix, Fig. S12A). Given its role in forming the substrate binding pocket, it is plausible that the gating motif also contributes to substrate specificity, which warrants further investigation.

In this study, we demonstrate that PatZ undergoes allosteric regulation, where AcCoA binding to CBD not only enhances GNATD’s affinity for AcCoA but also plays a crucial role in the proper allosteric control of PatZ’s enzymatic activity. Based on our structure of liganded-PatZ, we generated the RR mutant, in which R26 and R81 residues were substituted with glutamate to abolish the AcCoA-binding pocket in CBD. As expected, ITC analysis of this RR mutant revealed one-site binding and a loss of positive cooperativity. However, the RR mutant still exhibited acetyltransferase activity (Fig. 4 C and D). To investigate whether the RR mutant adopts an activated or inactivated conformation, we performed 3D reconstruction of PatZ RR mutant using cryoEM after mixing it with AcCoA. The results revealed that the RR mutant adopts the activated conformation with AcCoA bound to CBD (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). This suggests that the RR mutant may not accurately represent the fully AcCoA-unbound state of the CBD but instead reflects a state with reduced AcCoA binding affinity. In the enzymatic activity assay, significant activity was detected only under conditions with sufficiently high AcCoA concentrations, indicating that reduced affinity can be overcome at elevated substrate levels. Similarly, during structural analysis, an excess amount of AcCoA was used, likely facilitating the stabilization of the active conformation of the CBD. These data support the assumption that the RR mutant has a decreased AcCoA binding affinity.

We demonstrated that ligand binding to the RD significantly stabilizes the intersubunit interface and rigidifies PatZ’s homotetrameric architecture (Fig. 4G). This conformational stabilization of the RD correlates with the activation of the gating motif induced by AcCoA binding to GNATD (Fig. 4I). These cooperative structural events result in distinct domain-wise conformations of GNATD in both active and inactive positions. The terms “active”- and “inactive”-positioning are used based on the state of the substrate access channel regulated by GNATD (Fig. 4 H–J). In the absence of AcCoA, the gating motif of GNATD interacts with CBD, leading to the inactive-positioning of GNATD (Fig. 4 I, Left panel). In this conformation, the tethered gating motif restricts the substrate access channel, indicating an inhibitory state (Fig. 4 J, Left panel). Upon AcCoA binding, the gating motif loses its interaction with CBD, allowing GNATD to interact with AGD and adopt an active position (Fig. 4 I, Right panel). This positioning creates a wide opening between GNATD and CBD, enabling access to the substrate binding site and indicating an activated conformation (Fig. 4 J, Right panel). Structural analyses suggest that binding of AcCoA to both CBD and GNATD contributes to opening the substrate access channel (Fig. 4 J, Right panel). However, our data do not conclusively demonstrate that simultaneous binding at both sites is strictly required for gate opening. Further biochemical and structural experiments will be necessary to determine whether single-site binding alone is sufficient to induce an activated conformation.

Interestingly, the structure of PatZ with bound phosphate also shows GNATD in an inactive position, similar to the inactive gating motif conformation. The RD in this structure is identical to that of the AcCoA-bound form, suggesting that the stability of the RD alone does not activate GNATD. Because GNATD alone reduces enzymatic activity, these findings indicate that AcCoA binding to both CBD and GNATD is likely required for optimal PatZ activation. Our results (Fig. 4 C and D) and previous studies demonstrate the cooperative binding of AcCoA to GNATD and CBD. AcCoA binding to wild-type GNATD is approximately twice as strong as that to GNATD alone and six times stronger to the RD than to GNATD (Fig. 4C). Collectively, these findings suggest that AcCoA binding to CBD reduces the dynamics of the RD, enhances AcCoA binding to GNATD, promotes gating motif rearrangement, and activates the enzyme.

Thus, our study elucidates the structural basis of the active and inactive conformations in the interplay between GNATD and the RD, providing key insights into the regulatory mechanism of PatZ. This mechanism operates through a dual strategy: dynamic transitions between active and inactive states and allosteric modulation of enzyme activity via cooperative AcCoA binding.

Previous studies have highlighted PatZ’s unique specificity for Acs (33, 51), and it has been observed that strains lacking PatZ do not acetylate Acs (52). Our results identified a structural opening event in GNATD, in cooperation with CBD, which forms an Acs-specific binding pocket (Fig. 5). These findings reveal additional functional layers of the RD. Moreover, GNATD alone in PatZ reduces enzymatic activity toward Acs (41) (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S8), and point mutations in the RD that disrupt Acs interaction significantly reduce Acs acetylation (Fig. 5 C and D). Our results further demonstrate that Acs binds complementary to the substrate binding site formed by both GNATD and the RD (Fig. 5 A and B). The residues involved in Acs–PatZ interaction are highly conserved (SI Appendix, Fig. S10E). Therefore, our study implies that PatZ employs a specific mechanism through cooperative substrate recognition involving both GNATD and the RD (Fig. 6A). Although we have elucidated substrate-specific mechanisms, it is worth noting that PatZ is known for its ability to acetylate a wide variety of protein substrates within the bacterial proteome, with around 1,000 substrates compared with 20 to 600 for GNATD-only enzymes like YjaB, YiaC, RimI, and PhnO. The molecular mechanisms underlying PatZ’s ability to acetylate such a diverse array of substrates remain unclear and necessitate further investigation using a broader spectrum of substrates.

Protein acetylation plays a critical role in cellular physiology, influencing various processes and regulatory mechanisms (53). Present across all domains (54) of life—Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya—protein acetyltransferases, including GNATs, suggest that protein acetylation has been a fundamental process since the earliest stages of life, predating the Last Universal Common Ancestor (LUCA) more than 4 billion years ago (55). Despite significant evolutionary divergence from LUCA, essential cellular functions such as genetic information processing and core metabolic pathways have remained conserved across phylogeny (56).

Our study highlights that the mechanistic coupling of AcCoA binding to the RD modulates the activity of Type I GNATs like PatZ. Given that different types of RDs in other GNATs can bind to a variety of ligands, this variation suggests that bacteria regulate GNAT activity in response to changing metabolic conditions (Fig. 6B). We performed a protein structure–based phylogenetic analysis using the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (AFDB) (57) to explore the distribution of GNAT types in bacteria (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). Our analysis revealed that, although bacteria can harbor multiple GNAT types, Type IV GNATs, which consist solely of GNATD, are widely distributed across the bacterial domain (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 E and F). In contrast, GNATs with unique RDs follow distinct distribution patterns, with these types predominantly found in specific bacterial phyla (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 E and F).

Type IV GNATs, which contain only the catalytic GNATD, are ubiquitous in bacteria (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 E and F) and are involved in regulating key processes such as nucleoid organization (58), transcription (23, 59), and central metabolism (60). This widespread presence suggests that GNATs are crucial for regulating essential cellular functions. Contrastingly, Type I, II, and III GNATs, which feature additional RDs (Fig. 6B and SI Appendix, Fig. S14 B–D), likely have the ability to sense various environmental signals and tightly regulate genetic information flow and metabolic pathways in response to nutrient availability and cellular energy status.

For example, in E. coli, the Type I GNAT PatZ uses AcCoA not only as an acetyl-donating substrate but also as a ligand that binds to its RD, thus allosterically regulating its catalytic activity (Figs. 2 and 4). Among the well-studied Type II GNATs, PatA from S. lividans (61) has been predicted through AlphaFold (43) and AlphaFill (62) to bind AcCoA and ATP to its RD (Fig. 6B). In Type III GNATs, activity is regulated by the binding of amino acid, cAMP, and NADP+ to their RDs (63–65). Although each GNAT type uses different ligands, these ligands generally correspond to indicators of nutritional and cellular energy states (66).

Notably, in most, if not all, bacterial species studied, the activity of Acs is regulated by acetylation through GNATs (4, 67).
