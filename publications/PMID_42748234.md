Adenosine 5'-triphosphate-dependent switch/sucrose nonfermentable
(SWI/SNF)-family chromatin remodelers regulate genome accessibility by
repositioning nucleosomes, yet how these complexes diversified across evolution
while preserving a common remodeling mechanism has remained unresolved. Here, we
determine cryo-electron microscopy structures of the SWI/SNF and RSC (remodels
the structure of chromatin) complexes from the thermophilic fungus Chaetomium
thermophilum in apo and nucleosome-bound states, providing an evolutionarily
informative structural bridge between budding yeast and metazoan remodelers. We
show that SWI/SNF-family complexes are built around a conserved mechanochemical
core, whereas architectural and functional diversification is achieved through
modular rewiring of peripheral regulatory elements. Despite extensive sequence
divergence, several regulatory subunits preserve conserved structural motifs and
occupy equivalent positions within the remodeler scaffold, revealing previously
unrecognized structural relationships, including the ARP (actin-related
protein)-associated SMARCJ and QLQ-binding SMARCL proteins. Our findings
establish a unifying framework for the evolution of SWI/SNF-family remodelers
and illustrate how large chromatin remodeling assemblies preserve catalytic
cores while diversifying regulatory architecture across eukaryotes.

INTRODUCTION

Chromatin remodeling complexes of the switch/sucrose nonfermentable (SWI/SNF) family play central roles in regulating genome accessibility by repositioning nucleosomes in an adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP)–dependent manner (1–3). By altering nucleosome positioning and the chromatin structure, these remodelers influence a wide range of nuclear processes, including transcription, DNA repair, and DNA replication (1, 4). Consistent with their fundamental role in chromatin regulation, dysfunction of SWI/SNF complexes is strongly associated with human disease. Mutations in SWI/SNF subunits occur in more than 20% of human cancers and are frequently linked to developmental disorders and tumorigenesis (5, 6). These observations highlight the importance of understanding the molecular mechanisms that govern the assembly and regulation of SWI/SNF-family remodelers.

SWI/SNF-family remodelers are large multisubunit assemblies built around a catalytic adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) of the Snf2 family (7). Over the past decades, genetic, biochemical, and structural studies have extensively characterized SWI/SNF complexes in budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae and mammalian cells (8–12). Recent cryo–electron microscopy (cryo-EM) studies have begun to illuminate the structural organization of SWI/SNF-family complexes. Structures of yeast SWI/SNF and RSC (remodels the structure of chromatin) as well as human BAF and PBAF complexes reveal a modular architecture consisting of a catalytic ATPase Motor module, an actin-related protein (ARP) module, and a large Base module that serves as the structural scaffold of the complex (13–27). These studies provide important insights into how the ATPase engages nucleosomal DNA and couples ATP hydrolysis to nucleosome remodeling (7).

Despite these advances, several fundamental questions remain unresolved. SWI/SNF-family remodelers exhibit substantial diversity across eukaryotes, forming related but distinct assemblies such as SWI/SNF (BAF) and RSC (PBAF), each containing both conserved and lineage-specific subunits. In metazoans, additional specialized assemblies such as the noncanonical BAF (ncBAF or GBAF) complex have been identified (28), further expanding the functional repertoire of SWI/SNF-family remodelers. How these large multisubunit assemblies are organized and how alternative subunits generate complex-specific architectures remain incompletely understood. In addition, many regulatory components show little detectable sequence conservation across species, making it difficult to establish their evolutionary relationships or infer their structural roles within the complex. Last, the mechanisms by which auxiliary subunits regulate ATPase activity, coordinate nucleosome engagement, and control genome targeting remain poorly defined (29).

Comparative structural analysis across species offers an effective approach to address these questions. The thermophilic fungus Chaetomium thermophilum provides an attractive system for structural studies because its proteins exhibit enhanced stability and its SWI/SNF-family complexes retain an intermediate level of compositional complexity between yeast and metazoan systems. Deciphering the structures of C. thermophilum complexes will help elucidate the core architectural principles and the substantial diversity of SWI/SNF-family complexes across eukaryotes.

Here, we determine cryo-EM structures of SWI/SNF and RSC chromatin remodeling complexes from C. thermophilum in both apo and nucleosome-bound states. Integrating structural and biochemical analyses, we delineate the conserved architectural framework of SWI/SNF-family remodelers and identify regulatory modules that modulate nucleosome engagement and remodeling activity. Our structures reveal how alternative subunits generate RSC-specific architecture and clarify the structural principles that govern the organization and diversification of SWI/SNF-family chromatin remodelers.

RESULTSIdentification of SWI/SNF-family complexes in C. thermophilumTo identify SWI/SNF-family chromatin remodeling complexes in C. thermophilum, we searched its genome for homologs of previously characterized SWI/SNF subunits from S. cerevisiae, Schizosaccharomyces pombe, and humans (fig. S1). This analysis yielded 16 candidate proteins, most of which showed the highest sequence similarity to their S. pombe counterparts. Accordingly, we adopted the S. pombe nomenclature for the C. thermophilum proteins.To experimentally define the composition of SWI/SNF-family complexes, we generated transgenic C. thermophilum strains expressing candidate subunits fused to a 2xProteinA-3xFlag tag and performed affinity purification followed by mass spectrometry analysis (AP-MS) (30). Using the SWI/SNF–specific subunits CtSnf5 and CtSol1 as baits, AP-MS consistently identified 12 shared proteins among the top interactors, defining the putative C. thermophilum SWI/SNF complex (CtSWI/SNF) (fig. S1, A and B). While 10 of these components are evolutionarily conserved, two uncharacterized subunits, XP_006691747.1 and XP_006694278.1, lack detectable homologs in yeast or animals and were therefore named CtSnf70 and CtSnf80 on the basis of their molecular weights. Reciprocal purification using CtSnf70 as bait recovered the same 12-subunit complex, confirming that CtSnf70 and CtSnf80 are bona fide components of CtSWI/SNF (fig. S1C).Using a similar strategy, we next determined the composition of the C. thermophilum RSC complex (CtRSC). AP-MS with the RSC-specific subunit Rsc7 as bait identified 12 associated proteins (fig. S1D). Comparison of the two complexes revealed six shared core subunits, including the Snf2 ATPase, two ARPs (Arp4 and Arp9), and three conserved core components (Ssr1, Ssr3, and Ssr4) (Fig. 1A). Complex identity is specified by alternative paralogs: CtSWI/SNF contains Snf5, Sol1, and Snf59, whereas CtRSC incorporates their corresponding paralogs Sfh1, Rsc9, and Rsc7. In addition, CtSWI/SNF includes three complex-specific components (Snf70, Snf80, and Taf14), while CtRSC contains three RSC-specific subunits (Rsc1, Rsc4, and Rsc58). Notably, the SWI/SNF-family complexes in C. thermophilum have compositional complexity that is intermediate between those of budding yeast and mammals. A conserved mechanochemical core underlies SWI/SNF-family remodelers

SWI/SNF and RSC are organized around a conserved mechanochemical core embedded within the tripartite architecture. This core consists of the Snf2 ATPase motor and a structured Base scaffold, which together provide the catalytic and architectural foundation for nucleosome remodeling. The Motor module, comprising the Snf2 ATPase domain, serves as the primary engine for nucleosome engagement and DNA translocation. The Snf2 ATPase domain binds nucleosomal DNA at superhelical location 2, adopting a catalytic configuration highly similar to those observed in previously reported yeast and mammalian SWI/SNF-family structures (Fig. 2A and fig. S12, A to D). A defining feature of Snf2-family ATPases is the presence of two helical insertions, protrusion 1 (P1) and protrusion 2 (P2) (fig. S12A), which are conserved in the Snf2 subfamily but diverge in INO80, ISWI, and CHD remodelers (fig. S13). P1 packs against the brace, post–helicase-SANT–associated (HSA), and HSA helices to form a regulatory hub that couples the Motor module to the ARP module (Fig. 2B), whereas P2 contacts nucleosomal DNA and the SnAC region (Fig. 2C). The SnAC domain can be further delineated into a P2-binding motif and an arginine anchor (fig. S12E). The P2-binding motif engages P2 through conserved hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions (Fig. 2C), while an arginine-rich motif binds the H2A-H2B acidic patch (fig. S12, F to H). Together, the Motor module of Snf2-family remodelers engages the nucleosome through a conserved set of anchoring and coupling elements.

CtSnf5 and CtSfh1 (SMARCB1 homolog) serve as central organizers of the nucleosome-binding lobe, sharing conserved RPT1/2 domains and a nucleosome-binding finger helix, whereas their N- and C-terminal regions diverge, consistent with their distinct structural roles in SWI/SNF and RSC (fig. The N-terminal region of CtSnf5 extends toward the scaffold body lobe through a long CC helix that interacts with Sol1, Ssr1, and Snf2, thereby stabilizing communication between the scaffold body and nucleosome-binding lobes (fig. S19, C and D). In contrast, Sfh1 remains confined within the nucleosome-binding lobe, with its N-terminal segment folding back to RPT1/2 to form a compact structure (fig. S19E). The C-terminal regions of Snf5 and Sfh1 further distinguish the two proteins. CtSfh1 has a C4 zinc-finger domain that packs against the RPT1/2 domains (fig. S19, B and C). Structural superposition shows that this zinc-finger domain of CtSfh1 occupies the Snf70-binding site present in CtSnf5 (Fig. 4C). This steric incompatibility explains why Snf70 associates with CtSWI/SNF but is absent from CtRSC.

Large portions of Snf59 and Rsc7 were not visible in the cryo-EM reconstruction, likely reflecting intrinsic flexibility. The modeled regions of both Snf59 and Rsc7 contain a conserved Req helix that stacks with the RPT1/2 domain of Snf5 and the SWIRM domain of Ssr1 (Fig. 4, A and B), resembling those found in ScSwp82, ScRsc7, HsDPF1/2/3, and HsPHF10, which we collectively classify as members of the SMARCG family (fig. S20, A and B). In addition, the RSC-specific paralog CtRsc7 has an extended region C-terminal to the Req helix that creates additional contacts with Ssr1 to stabilize the nucleosome-binding lobe. This eReq (extended Req) motif is also observed in RSC components ScRsc7 and HsPHF10 (Fig. 4B and fig. S20A), explaining why ScRsc7 is essential for RSC assembly, whereas its SWI/SNF counterpart ScSwp82 is dispensable for SWI/SNF assembly (36–38). Outside the Req or eReq motif, these structural-equivalent proteins display highly divergent domain organizations (fig. S20, B and C). The N-terminal CRC domains from CtSnf59 and CtRsc7 as well as their homologs in budding yeast form an atypical winged-helix (WH) domain with additional N- and C-terminal extensions, producing an extended WH (eWH) fold, while the SAY domain from HsPHF10 is a WH domain without N- or C-terminal extension (fig. S20, D to H).

The primary nucleosome-binding element of this lobe is the conserved finger helix of Snf5 and Sfh1 (Fig. 4E and fig. S19F). This helix contains multiple arginine residues that engage the nucleosomal acidic patch and appear to be stabilized upon nucleosome binding (fig. S19, F and G). Additional elements likely contribute to nucleosome interaction, including the N-terminal WH-like domain in CtSnf59/Rsc7, the N-terminal region in CtSsr1, and the N- or C-terminal domain in CtSnf5/Sfh1, which all lie adjacent to the nucleosome (Fig. 4E). Notably, these auxiliary domains in CtSnf5/Sfh1, CtSnf59/Rsc7, and CtSsr1 are highly divergent across species (figs. S14E, S19B, and S20C), and some of these domains could directly bind 0N0 nucleosomes (fig. S21). These observations suggest that the nucleosome-binding lobe integrates multiple conserved and lineage-specific elements to anchor SWI/SNF-family remodelers onto the nucleosome substrate, supporting customized nucleosome engagement in different species.

DISCUSSIONAlthough structures of yeast and mammalian remodelers have provided important insights into SWI/SNF-family architecture (13–24), how these large multisubunit assemblies are diversified across eukaryotes has remained incompletely understood. Here, we present structures of SWI/SNF and RSC complexes from C. thermophilum, which has an intermediate level of compositional complexity between budding yeast and metazoan systems, thus serving as an intriguing paradigm to examine the conservation and diversity of SWI/SNF during evolution. Systemic structural comparison of SWI/SNF remodelers from C. thermophilum, S. cerevisiae, and Homo sapiens establishes a general principle for SWI/SNF evolution, in which a conserved mechanochemical core is combined with modular, exchangeable regulatory elements that enable functional specialization.The Motor and scaffold body lobes are well conserved across species, sharing a common nucleosome engagement strategy. These observations indicate that the mechanochemical core of SWI/SNF-family remodelers is strongly constrained during evolution. Surrounding this conserved core are regulatory modules that modulate nucleosome binding and remodeling activity. Consistent with this idea, CtRSC and CtSWI/SNF exhibited distinct remodeling trajectories in nucleosome sliding assays (fig. S2, E and F), suggesting that diversification of peripheral regulatory modules may influence nucleosome remodeling dynamics while preserving a conserved ATPase-driven translocation mechanism.Within the ARP module, we identify a structurally conserved SMARCJ-family protein that stabilizes the ARP heterodimer and enhances nucleosome remodeling efficiency. Structurally, SMARCJ binding may reorient the ARP-associated HSA helix, promoting tighter packing between the HSA helix, post-HSA, and P1-α1/2 to stabilize the regulatory hub (fig. S25). Although structures of yeast and mammalian remodelers have provided important insights into SWI/SNF-family architecture (13–24), how these large multisubunit assemblies are diversified across eukaryotes has remained incompletely understood. Here, we present structures of SWI/SNF and RSC complexes from C. thermophilum, which has an intermediate level of compositional complexity between budding yeast and metazoan systems, thus serving as an intriguing paradigm to examine the conservation and diversity of SWI/SNF during evolution. Systemic structural comparison of SWI/SNF remodelers from C. thermophilum, S. cerevisiae, and Homo sapiens establishes a general principle for SWI/SNF evolution, in which a conserved mechanochemical core is combined with modular, exchangeable regulatory elements that enable functional specialization.

The Motor and scaffold body lobes are well conserved across species, sharing a common nucleosome engagement strategy. These observations indicate that the mechanochemical core of SWI/SNF-family remodelers is strongly constrained during evolution. Surrounding this conserved core are regulatory modules that modulate nucleosome binding and remodeling activity. Consistent with this idea, CtRSC and CtSWI/SNF exhibited distinct remodeling trajectories in nucleosome sliding assays (fig. S2, E and F), suggesting that diversification of peripheral regulatory modules may influence nucleosome remodeling dynamics while preserving a conserved ATPase-driven translocation mechanism.

Within the ARP module, we identify a structurally conserved SMARCJ-family protein that stabilizes the ARP heterodimer and enhances nucleosome remodeling efficiency. Structurally, SMARCJ binding may reorient the ARP-associated HSA helix, promoting tighter packing between the HSA helix, post-HSA, and P1-α1/2 to stabilize the regulatory hub (fig. S25). This arrangement may facilitate coupling between ATPase motor movement and the ARP module during chromatin remodeling. In the nucleosome-binding lobe, besides the well-characterized finger helix of Snf5/SMARCB1, several additional elements likely contribute to nucleosome engagement, including the N- or C-terminal regions of SMARCB, SMARCC, and SMARCG subunits. Our preliminary data show that N-terminal domains of human SMARCB1 and SMARCC1 could directly bind nucleosomes (fig. S21). In addition, the CtRSC-specific lobe contains three subunits (Rsc1, Rsc4, and Rsc58) harboring five bromodomains, most of which were not resolved in current cryo-EM reconstruction, except for the Rsc58 bromodomain. Although these bromodomains are not required for complex assembly, they may contribute to genome targeting through their histone “reader” activities (16, 19). These varied DNA-binding and histone-binding modules could enable remodelers to adapt different nucleosome engagement strategies to distinct chromatin and epigenetic landscapes.

One notable feature of SWI/SNF-family remodelers is the extensive sequence divergence among many peripheral regulatory subunits. Despite this divergence, these proteins often preserve similar structural motifs and occupy equivalent positions within the complex. This pattern likely reflects the evolutionary constraints imposed by their structural roles in the complex assembly. For example, the ARP-binding interface formed by CtSnf70, ScRtt102, and HsBCL7 is defined by a short β-hairpin motif that stabilizes the Arp9-Arp42 heterodimer (27, 44), suggesting that preservation of this local geometry is sufficient to maintain function even as the overall sequence similarity erodes. Moreover, many SWI/SNF subunits contain extended intrinsically disordered regions and modular interaction motifs (45), which are known to evolve rapidly while retaining structural compatibility with their binding partners. Thus, conservation of structural topology rather than primary sequence might represent a common evolutionary strategy for maintaining the functional integrity of SWI/SNF-family complexes.

The comparative structural analyses presented here provide an opportunity to establish a unified nomenclature for SWI/SNF-family subunits, given the highly conserved architecture across eukaryotes. Historically, subunits were named independently in different organisms, often obscuring their evolutionary relationships. Previous efforts to harmonize naming using the HUGO SMARC nomenclature relied primarily on sequence homology (41). Our comparative structural analyses suggest that several regulatory proteins are more accurately defined by their structural positions and interaction interfaces rather than by sequence similarity alone. We therefore propose a unified nomenclature in which subunits are grouped into SMARC families on the basis of both sequence homology and structural equivalence (Fig. 7A). In this system, the homologous or structural-equivalent proteins in different species are assigned the same SMARC designation with a species prefix (e.g., Hs for H. sapiens and Sc for S. cerevisiae), whereas paralogs within the same organism are distinguished numerically (e.g., SMARCC1 and SMARCC2) (Fig. 7A). This framework provides a consistent vocabulary for describing SWI/SNF-family subunits and clarifies the relationships among structurally conserved regulatory subunits.

Cryo-EM image processing

All movie stacks were subjected to beam-induced motion correction and dose weighting using MotionCor2 (58). Contrast transfer function parameters were estimated with CTFFIND4 (59). Particle picking and extraction were performed on binned datasets in RELION-3.1 (60). Subsequent two-dimensional (2D) classification and heterogeneous refinement were carried out in cryoSPARC (61).

For the CtSWI/SNF and CtRSC datasets, multiple rounds of 2D classification yielded 1,167,261 particles for CtSWI/SNF and 1,619,398 particles for CtRSC. These particles were used to generate initial three-dimensional (3D) models and subjected to heterogeneous refinement. For CtSWI/SNF, a subset of 158,634 particles was refined by nonuniform refinement, yielding a final map with an overall resolution of 3.01 Å on the basis of the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation 0.143 criterion. For CtRSC, a subset of 250,420 particles was refined by nonuniform refinement, yielding a 2.96-Å map. However, the RSC-specific lobe showed poor alignment because of conformational flexibility. These 250,420 particles were therefore subjected to a second round of heterogeneous refinement, yielding a subclass of 30,165 particles that enabled reconstruction of the RSC-specific lobe. Because of the marked reduction in particle number, the global resolution of the resulting RSC map was limited to 3.48 Å. To further improve the interpretability of the RSC-specific lobe, these 30,165 particles were subjected to signal subtraction and focused refinement.

For the CtSWI/SNF-NCP dataset, iterative rounds of 2D classification yielded 761,943 selected particles. These particles were used to generate an initial 3D model and were subjected to heterogeneous refinement, resulting in a major class consistent with CtSWI/SNF bound to the nucleosome. A subset of 108,901 particles from this class was refined by nonuniform refinement, producing a map with a global resolution of 3.56 Å. Given the intrinsic flexibility between different regions, these 108,901 particles were further subjected to signal subtraction and focused refinement using soft masks targeting the NCP, ARP-ATPase module, and core module separately. This strategy yielded three final maps at 3.09-, 4.84-, and 3.40-Å resolutions, respectively.

For CtRSC-NCP, seven datasets were collected and processed individually by 2D classification, heterogeneous refinement, and nonuniform refinement. The core module appeared blurred owing to structural heterogeneity. Refined particles from all datasets were then combined, yielding a total of 754,609 particles for map generation. To obtain a more homogeneous particle population, 3D focused classification was performed after subtraction of the NCP and ARP-ATPase module densities, together with a local soft mask focused on the core module. This procedure identified a class containing 117,380 particles with well-resolved features in the core module. These particles were subjected to nonuniform refinement, yielding a final map at a 4.45-Å resolution. To further improve the interpretability of the RSC-NCP structure, focused refinements with soft masks were performed separately for the core module and the NCP-ARP-ATPase module.
