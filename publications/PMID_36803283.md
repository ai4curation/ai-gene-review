ABSTRACT

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is prone to the accumulation of mutations. To prevent harmful mtDNA mutations from being passed on to the next generation, the female germline, through which mtDNA is exclusively inherited, has evolved extensive mtDNA quality control. To dissect the molecular underpinnings of this process, we recently performed a large RNAi screen in Drosophila and uncovered a programmed germline mitophagy (PGM) that is essential for mtDNA quality control. We found that PGM begins as germ cells enter meiosis, induced, at least in part, by the inhibition of the mTor (mechanistic Target of rapamycin) complex 1 (mTorC1). Interestingly, PGM requires the general macroautophagy/autophagy machinery and the mitophagy adaptor BNIP3, but not the canonical mitophagy genes Pink1 and park (parkin), even though they are critical for germline mtDNA quality control. We also identified the RNA-binding protein Atx2 as a major regulator of PGM. This work is the first to identify and implicate a programmed mitophagy event in germline mtDNA quality control, and it highlights the utility of the Drosophila ovary for studying developmentally regulated mitophagy and autophagy in vivo .

KEYWORDS: Autophagy, Drosophila, germline, mitochondria, mitochondrial DNA, mitophagy, mtDNA, purifying selection

Mitochondria contain their own genomes that encode essential genes required for oxidative phosphorylation. Yet despite its importance, mtDNA is subject to a high mutation rate. With limited error repair mechanisms and lacking appreciable levels of genomic recombination, mutations in mtDNA would accumulate across generations, as is predicted by Muller’s ratchet. This mutational accumulation would eventually lead to a decline in organismal fitness. To prevent this decay, the female germline has evolved quality control mechanisms to ensure the transmission of healthy mtDNA across generations. Despite being conserved in yeast, worms, fruit flies, mice and humans, how this mtDNA quality control works remains poorly understood.

To uncover new genes and pathways essential for germline mtDNA quality control, we performed a large tissue-specific knockdown screen in the Drosophila female germline of over 700 genes [ 1 ]. We found numerous autophagy and mitophagy factors to be required for germline mtDNA quality control. These include the canonical mitophagy factors Pink1 and park, the mitophagy adaptor BNIP3, the Drosophila orthologs of the autophagy initiation complex members ULK1/ULK2 (Atg1) and RB1CC1/FIP200 (Atg17), and the ULK1-interactor TBK1 (IKKε). We also identified less well-characterized autophagy players, including Wdr81 and Rack1. Together, the results from our screen imply an important role for autophagy and mitophagy in mtDNA quality control in the female germline. Excitingly, recent work suggests this may also be conserved in germline mtDNA quality control in mammals.

We assessed mitophagy in vivo to determine when and where it occurs in the germline and to explore its function in germline mtDNA quality control. Using the mitophagy reporters mito-QC and mito-Keima, we observe a striking mitophagy event occurring in the early stages of oogenesis, as germ cells enter meiosis ( Figure 1 ). This mitophagy event is coincident with the onset of germline mtDNA quality control, consistent with it playing an important role in this process. Through further imaging, we observed mitochondrial proteins accumulating in large (~1 µm) acidified puncta at these stages, approximately one per meiotic germ cell, which colocalize with autophagic markers, including Atg8a and Rab7. Interestingly, these unique mito-autophagic vacuoles associate with the ends of the fusome, a germline-specific structure rich in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and cytoskeletal proteins. Precisely what role the fusome plays in this mitophagy remains to be determined; however, as it is rich in ER it may serve as a membrane/lipid source for mito-autophagic vacuole biogenesis.

We next asked if this germline mitophagy event was triggered by mitochondrial dysfunction caused by mutant mtDNA. Unexpectedly, we found it was not, as it also occurred in the absence of mutant mtDNA, suggesting it must be developmentally programmed. We thus named it programmed germline mitophagy (PGM). We next wondered if PGM might instead be induced by developmental cues similar to meiosis, given that they both begin at the same time. Entry into meiosis in flies is triggered by the inhibition of mTorC1 activity. We therefore asked if mTorC1 inhibition is also necessary to induce PGM. Indeed, among the strongest suppressors we identified in our screen for mtDNA quality control factors is the RNA binding protein and mTorC1 inhibitor Atx2. Knockdown of Atx2 strongly prevents PGM, as does knockdown of key components of other mTorC1 negative regulators, the Tsc1-gig/Tsc2 and GATOR1 complexes. Precisely how Atx2 triggers PGM remains to be determined; however, structure/function analysis indicate that it requires its poly(A)-binding protein interacting motif 2, suggesting that it might regulate the mRNA stability of, and/or the translation of, mTorC1 pathway components. Together, our data demonstrate that PGM is genetically and developmentally coupled with meiosis, indicating that the processes that maintain the nuclear genome’s and mitochondrial genome’s fitness over generations are co-regulated.

Last, we delved further into the molecular mechanism of PGM. We first asked if all the mitophagy and autophagy factors we identified in our mtDNA quality control screen are necessary for PGM. As expected, the core autophagy initiation complex members, Atg1 and Atg17, and the mitophagy adaptor BNIP3, are required. Surprisingly, however, Pink1 and park are not, consistent with a previously described non-mitophagy role for Pink1 in germline mtDNA quality control. We next asked if any of the remaining genes we identified in our mtDNA quality control screen, which had not previously been implicated in mitophagy or autophagy, are required for PGM. We found that several factors involved in the expression of mtDNA-encoded proteins are critical, although their precise functions in PGM remain to be determined. We also found that the mitochondrial fission factors Drp1 and Tango11/MFF were essential, suggesting that PGM occurs in a piecemeal fashion to remove mitochondrial sub-domains rather than in a wholesale manner to remove whole mitochondria.

In sum, through RNAi screening we have identified a new developmentally programmed mitophagy that is essential for mtDNA quality control in the Drosophila germline. Remaining to be determined is which mitochondrial components PGM degrades, and how PGM drives mtDNA quality control. Going forward, the Drosophila ovary will prove to be a powerful system in which to study mitophagy in vivo , with a plethora of genetic tools and without a need for harsh mitochondrial uncouplers. Furthermore, this work represents a significant advance in understanding the mechanisms of mtDNA quality control.
