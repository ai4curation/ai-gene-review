Summary

Plants often encounter simultaneous imbalances in multiple nutrients, but the regulatory logic coordinating their responses remains poorly understood. We aimed to uncover shared transcriptional programs and regulatory nodes underpinning multinutrient adaptation in Arabidopsis thaliana roots. We analyzed publicly available RNA‐seq datasets spanning 15 nutrient and beneficial element conditions using differential expression, co‐expression network (WGCNA), and gene regulatory network analysis. Selected transcription factors (TFs) were validated via root phenotyping, suberin staining, and ionomic profiling under two‐nutrient stress conditions. We identified a core set of 2050 genes responsive to multiple nutrient treatments, enriched for suberin biosynthesis, and structured into modular co‐expression clusters. Eight prioritized candidate TFs (ARR10, GBF3, HHO5, NAC32, NF‐YA3, NF‐YB2, SARD1, and WRKY33) were shown to modulate root system architecture under specific nutrient combinations. WRKY33 and NF‐YB2, in particular, regulated nutrient‐responsive suberin deposition and ionomic plasticity. These findings reveal suberin remodeling as a shared downstream process in multinutrient responses and suggest that plasticity is not a fixed trait but a modular, polygenic, and context‐dependent outcome. Repurposed TFs with pleiotropic functions coordinate structural and physiological traits, providing regulatory entry points for improving nutrient resilience.

Keywords: Arabidopsis thaliana , combinatorial nutrient stress, context‐dependent response, gene regulatory network, plasticity, root system architecture, suberin

I. Introduction

As sessile organisms, plants must continuously adapt to fluctuating environmental conditions, with nutrient availability being a major constraint on growth and productivity. Essential macronutrients (N, P, K, S, Mg, and Ca) and micronutrients (B, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, Ni, Mo, and Cl), along with beneficial elements such as Al, Ce, Co, I, La, Na, Se, Si, Ti, and V, influence growth and development as well as biotic and abiotic stress responses (Gómez‐Merino & Trejo‐Téllez, 2018 ). Their phytoavailability is shaped by soil chemistry, plant demand, and environmental cues (Marschner & Rengel, 2012 ), and the uptake of one element often impacts the availability of others through complex interactions (Fan et al ., 2021 ). Mineral nutrient interplay begins in the soil, in which nutrient bioavailability can be reduced by element interactions or the formation of insoluble complexes that plants cannot uptake (Rouached et al ., 2010 ).

To cope with these challenges, plants rely on dynamic root barrier systems to regulate nutrient uptake under these conditions. Nutrient movement into the stele is regulated by the endodermis, in which the Casparian strip, a lignin‐based paracellular barrier unique to land plants, and the suberin lamellae, a hydrophobic barrier characteristic of seed plants, form selective barriers for radial transport and elemental flow (Barberon et al ., 2016 ; Doblas et al ., 2017 ; Kreszies et al ., 2018 ). Suberin, in particular, plays a crucial role in restricting apoplastic flow and modulating nutrient transport. Its deposition is responsive to nutrient status in a nonuniform manner: Fe, Mn, and Zn deficiencies can delay suberization, while S and K insufficiencies enhance it (Barberon et al ., 2016 ). While much of plant nutrition research has focused on individual nutrient effects, real‐world nutrient availability is often imbalanced and dynamic. Classical tools such as Mulder's chart (a diagram illustrating complex relationships between nutrients in soil) have long illustrated how nutrients interact synergistically or antagonistically at the physiological level (Mulder, 1953 ). However, the molecular basis for these interactions – how nutrient combinations shape gene expression programs – remains largely unexplored.

At the gene regulatory level, nutrient homeostasis involves an interconnected transcriptional network, including well‐characterized interactions among P, Zn, and Fe (Xie et al ., 2019 ; Kumar et al ., 2021 ). These interactions are coordinated by a MYB‐like transcription factor PHOSPHATE STARVATION RESPONSE 1 (PSR1) (Briat et al ., 2015 ). To investigate higher order nutrient crosstalk, Brumbarova & Ivanov ( 2019 ) constructed a co‐expression network from Arabidopsis thaliana microarray data available in ATTED‐II, using 408 marker genes responsive to 12 nutrient conditions. While their findings are illuminating and provide proof of concept for transcriptional coordination, the results are constrained by reliance on marker selection and lack functional validation. By contrast, we employed an unsupervised, data‐driven approach based on 27 public RNA‐seq datasets spanning 15 nutrient and beneficial element conditions. This enabled a broader, system‐level view of transcriptional regulation in multinutrient responses.

Lateral roots (LRs) play a crucial role in nutrient foraging by expanding the surface area of the root system architecture (RSA). Nutrient deficiencies lead to distinct and nutrient‐specific RSA responses: low levels of P, Ca, K, Mn, and B typically reduce primary root length, while N and Fe deficiencies tend to promote elongation. The type and concentration of nutrients determine the extent and direction of these changes (Gruber et al ., 2013 ). Interactions among nutrients – whether antagonistic or synergistic – can influence gene regulatory network (GRN) and cellular behaviors, ultimately shaping root morphology and, in some cases, affecting shoot biomass (Kellermeier et al ., 2014 ; Shahzad & Amtmann, 2017 ; Fan et al ., 2021 ). These cumulative effects underscore the complex and integrated nature of nutrient responses at both molecular and morphological levels.

To elucidate multinutrient interactions in roots at transcriptomic, gene regulatory, and phenotypic levels, we analyzed public RNA‐seq datasets capturing responses to 15 different nutrients and beneficial element conditions in A. thaliana (Fig. 1 ). This unsupervised analysis revealed a core set of nutrient gene modules and candidate master transcription factors (TFs). The roles of eight regulatory TFs were functionally validated through RSA assays under selected nutrient deficiency combinations. In particular, mutants of the WRKY family ( wrky33 ), the Nuclear Factor Y (NF‐Y) family ( nf‐yb2 ), and the bZIP G‐box binding factor ( gbf3 ) exhibited altered suberization and ionomic profiles, implicating these TFs in coordinating barrier remodeling and nutrient homeostasis under complex conditions.

Fig. 1.

Our findings highlight the modular and context‐specific regulatory system that allows plants to translate complex nutrient cues to mediate plasticity at both structural and physiological levels. Beyond advancing our understanding of how plants interpret combinatorial nutrient cues, this framework also shows that TFs primarily associated with other functions act as key integrators. This underscores how transcriptional modularity enables roots to adapt to their environment. By identifying how these regulators integrate multiple environmental inputs, our work provides a foundation for exploring nutrient resilience strategies in more complex or applied settings.

II. Materials and Methods

1. RNA‐seq data collection and processing

We curated 318 RNA‐seq datasets derived from root samples encompassing 13 nutrient and beneficial elements. Two of these (Fe and Mg) included both deficient (low) and excessive (high) treatments, leading to a total of 15 unique nutrient stress conditions. After quality filtering and principal component analysis‐based outlier removal (Supporting Information Methods S1 ; Fig. S1 ), 309 samples were retained. Reads were trimmed by the BBDuk (v.38.96) from the BBMap suite (RRID:SCR_016965) (Bushnell, 2014 ), aligned to the TAIR10 (Lamesch et al ., 2012 ) using BBMap (v.38.96), and quantified using featureCounts (v2.0.3) (Liao et al ., 2014 ) based on Araport11 (Cheng et al ., 2017 ) and a published lincRNA annotation (Palos et al ., 2022 ). Detailed RNA‐seq dataset information and metadata are summarized in Table S1 . Parameters used in the computation pipeline are detailed in Methods S1–S4 .

2. DEG analysis

A total of 307 samples and 23 167 genes were included for differential expression analysis. After normalization and batch correction, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified for each nutrient condition using edgeR (v.3.36.0) (Robinson et al ., 2010 ), with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 0.01, with the exception of high Al, low Cu, and low Mo (FDR < 0.05) due to less consistent biological replicates and the availability of only one study (Fig. S2 ). These DEGs served as the basis for identifying shared and condition‐specific transcriptional responses across the 15 nutrient and beneficial element treatments. For additional DEG analysis of selected mutants, genes with FDR < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed. For more details, see Methods S4 and Figs S2 , S3 , and for scripts used for DEG analysis, see the Data availability section.

3. Co‐expression network construction

In parallel, gene‐level counts from quality‐filtered samples were used to construct a weighted gene co‐expression network analysis using WGCNA (v.1.7.1) (Langfelder & Horvath, 2008 ). After filtering for low expression and variance (Fig. S1 ), 15 452 genes were retained. Modules of co‐expressed genes were identified based on topological overlap and merged based on eigengene similarity, resulting in 26 distinct modules. For more details related to WGCNA processing, see Methods S2 . Comparative co‐expression analysis was done using MEGENA (v1.3.7) (Song & Zhang, 2015 ) with the same quality‐filtered samples used for WGCNA. For more details related to MEGENA processing, see Methods S3 . For scripts used for WGCNA and MEGENA, see the Data availability section.

4. Identification of core modules and candidate TFs

The DEGs from each of the 15 nutrient conditions were mapped onto co‐expression modules. Based on the breadth of nutrient conditions showing significant overlap, modules were classified as core (responsive across many conditions), specific (condition‐restricted), or base (largely unresponsive). Overlap significance was assessed using hypergeometric tests. Modules associated with the largest number of nutrient conditions (the core nutrient cluster) were subsequently subjected to Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analyses using ShinyGO (v.0.77) (Ge et al ., 2020 ). Candidate TFs were prioritized by integrating regulatory evidence from ConnecTF (Brooks et al ., 2021 ) and ShinyGO (v0.77) (Ge et al ., 2020 ), with additional consideration given to the availability of mutant lines for experimental validation. Detailed implementation is described in Methods S5 , and all scripts used for DEG–module intersection are provided in the Data availability section.

5. GRN inference

We inferred GRN for the 2050 genes in the core nutrient cluster using GENIE3 (Huynh‐Thu et al ., 2010 ), treating all expressed TFs as potential regulators. The resulting network was pruned using ConnecTF (Brooks et al ., 2021 ) to retain high‐confidence regulatory interactions, resulting in a precision threshold of 0.45. The final GRN connected 263 TFs to 1405 target genes, forming functional modules associated with suberin biosynthesis, defense response, and cell wall remodeling. More details can be found in Methods S6 .

6. Plant material and growth conditions

Detailed information about the T‐DNA mutant lines used in this study, obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center, is provided in Fig. S4 . All lines were confirmed for homozygosity and validated for transcript‐level alterations (Fig. S4 ; Table S2 ; Methods S7 ). Seedlings were germinated on 0.5× Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium (MSP09‐50LT, Plant Cell Technology, Smithfield, UT, USA) or modified MS media, supplemented with 1% phytoagar (P1003.1000; Duchefa Biochemie, Haarlem, The Netherlands) under continuous light. The composition of modified MS media to induce deficiency of selected nutrients is detailed in Table S3 .

7. RSA assay

Three‐day‐old seedlings germinated on modified MS medium (control, high‐high for both nutrients tested) were transferred to new treatment plates representing four nutrient combinations (low‐low, low‐high, high‐low, and high‐high) for N and Zn, K and Fe, Mg and Fe, or K and Zn, depending on the experiment. A ‘High’ label corresponds to the control levels of nutrients sufficient for plant optimal growth, while a ‘low’ label corresponds to the levels of nutrients that would exhibit deficiency symptoms within a set time frame (N – 500 μM; K – 250 μM; Mg – 10 μM; Fe – 0 μM; Zn – 0 μM). The composition of control medium (high‐high) and corresponding deficient media for each nutrient combination is detailed in Table S3 . The experiment was repeated to have at least two independent plates for each condition. Root systems were imaged 3–4 d after transfer and analyzed using the EZ‐Rhizo software (Armengaud et al ., 2009 ). RSA traits corresponding to primary, lateral, and total root lengths, number of LR, and LR density were collected. Shoot fresh weight was measured for 10‐d‐old seedlings. Root and shoot data were collected from the same plants. Data distribution was assessed by inspecting quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots of model residuals using the qnorm function in R. Nutrient responses were analyzed within each genotype using two‐way ANOVA to assess the effects of individual nutrient and their interaction. Differences between wild‐type (WT) and mutant lines were interpreted based on these response patterns and interaction plots (Figs S5–S8 ).

8. Suberin staining

Seedlings were fixed and stained with Fluorol Yellow 088 following established protocols (Fujita et al ., 2020 ; Emonet et al ., 2021 ; Ursache et al ., 2021 ). Five‐to‐six‐day‐old seedlings grown on modified MS medium (Table S3 ) were fixed in methanol for 0.5 to 5 d at room temperature in the dark with constant shaking (55 rpm) (DSR‐D‐N1; DIGISYSTEM, New Taipei City, Taiwan). Cleared seedlings were transferred to a 0.2% (w/v) Fluorol Yellow 088 (sc‐215052; Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., Dallas, TX, USA) solution (prepared in methanol) and incubated for 1 h in the dark at room temperature with constant shaking (55 rpm). After staining, seedlings were rinsed with methanol and counterstained in 0.5% (w/v) aniline blue (415 049‐25 g; Sigma‐Aldrich) solution (in methanol) and incubated for 1 h in the dark at room temperature. Counterstained seedlings were washed in water before mounting. Suberin patterns were imaged with a Zeiss Axio Imager Z2 fluorescence microscope (GFP filter). The length of the fully suberized, patchy, and nonsuberized zone was measured using the I mage J software (Fiji) (Schindelin et al ., 2012 ). Within each genotype, we used two‐way aligned rank transform (ART) ANOVA to evaluate the effects of nutrient treatments and their interaction on suberin patterning. Differences between WT and mutants were interpreted by comparing their suberin response profiles across conditions, with ART‐C tests applied where appropriate and adjusted for multiple testing using FDR correction.

9. Ionome profiling by ICP‐MS

The elemental composition of 7‐d‐old seedlings grown on modified MS media was analyzed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS) based on a modified protocol (Lyčka et al ., 2023 ). Fresh tissue was briefly predried (20 min at 60°C), frozen at −80°C, then fully dried (2 h at 60°C) before acid digestion. Samples were mineralized in 500 μl of concentrated HNO 3 (trace metal grade, A509‐P500; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 3 d at 60°C and 4 h at 80°C, with daily venting. Digests were diluted to 5% (v/v) HNO₃ and analyzed on a 7900 ICP‐MS system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) in helium collision cell mode. Quantification of selected isotopes ( 39 K, 44 Ca, 24 Mg, 55 Mn, 56 Fe, 63 Cu, 66 Zn, and 95 Mo) was based on the standard curves from the analysis of the 26‐element certified reference materials standard (AN9090MN‐100; ANALYTIKA, Praha, Czech Republic). The signal was normalized using internal standards. Agilent MassHunter Software (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) in full quantitative data analysis mode was used in processing the data. Sample preparation was performed in Taiwan, and ICP‐MS measurements were conducted at the Institute of Biophysics, Czech Academy of Sciences. The ICP‐MS analysis in Figs S9 and S10 was analyzed by three‐way ART ANOVA post hoc test using ART‐C (FDR‐corrected).

III. Results

1. Molecular version of Mulder's chart reveals transcriptionally distinct and connected nutrient interactions

To investigate how plants transcriptionally respond to multiple nutrient conditions, we first performed DEG analysis across 15 treatments (Table S4 ). Each condition triggered a distinct transcriptional response – the majority of the DEGs are nutrient‐specific and responsive under three or fewer conditions, with the most widely shared gene appearing for 11 responsive conditions (Fig. 2a ). Interestingly, nutrient conditions differed not only in the strength of the response (total number of DEGs) but also in the breadth (overlap of DEGs among conditions). For example, Fe and N triggered large numbers of DEGs but had minimal overlap with other conditions, indicating a strong, condition‐specific response. By contrast, conditions such as low Mg, low K, and low S produced fewer DEGs, yet a higher proportion was shared across treatments. This was reflected in a quantified ‘social level’ metric, which summed the weighted overlap of shared DEGs across all condition pairs, showing that treatments with fewer DEGs tended to have a higher proportion of common DEGs (Fig. 2b ).

Fig. 2.

To further explore these interactions, we intersected the up‐ and downregulated DEGs across all condition pairs and identified the top three overlapping partners for each treatment. This analysis revealed both synergistic and antagonistic transcriptional relationships, reminiscent of classical nutrient interactions depicted in Mulder's chart. For instance, N and Mn were broadly antagonistic, whereas K, low Mg, and Zn showed strong synergistic relationships (Fig. 2c ). Notably, Mn and Zn conditions showed significant antagonistic interactions displayed by the thicker line corresponding to a high Jaccard index (Fig. 2c ). Transcriptionally weaker conditions often aligned with stronger ones, suggesting regulatory convergence (Fig. 2 ).

While this ‘molecular Mulder's chart’ highlighted interaction patterns at the nutrient condition level, it did not resolve broader transcriptional programs shared across treatments. To uncover such coordinated gene sets, we turned to co‐expression network analysis.

2. Co‐expression analysis reveals core gene modules enriched in suberin biosynthesis

Because only a small number of genes were shared across many nutrient conditions, we used WGCNA to capture broader patterns of transcriptional coordination. A total of 26 co‐expression modules were constructed and intersected with nutrient‐responsive DEGs to classify genes into three categories: core, specific, and base (Fig. 3a ; Table S5 ). The core cluster included six modules (2050 genes) enriched in DEGs across most nutrient treatments, capturing a shared transcriptional program underlying multinutrient responses. The specific cluster comprised 12 modules with condition‐specific enrichment, while the remaining eight modules, largely unresponsive, were assigned to the base cluster. This classification enabled the identification of regulatory modules beyond single‐condition overlaps.

Fig. 3.

Pathway enrichment analysis of the core cluster revealed a strong overrepresentation of cutin, suberin, and wax biosynthesis, followed by pathways related to amino acid, lipid, and energy metabolism (Fig. S11a ). GO enrichment further supported the dominance of suberin biosynthesis, along with processes such as phenylpropanoid metabolism, water transport, and response to hypoxia. These findings suggest that barrier remodeling, particularly suberin deposition, is a central and conserved component of the plant's multinutrient response. By contrast, the specific nutrient cluster was enriched for pathways involved in carbon metabolism and photosynthesis (Fig. S11b ), highlighting distinct metabolic adjustments under individual nutrient stresses. The base cluster was enriched for ribosome‐related pathways and other core cellular functions (Fig. S11c ), likely representing background transcriptional activity unaffected by nutrient changes.

Together, these module clusters define a structured transcriptional landscape in which suberin biosynthesis emerges as a hallmark of shared nutrient stress, setting the stage for identifying upstream regulators and functional outputs. Consistent with this framework, a comparative co‐expression network analysis using MEGENA independently supported suberin biosynthesis and related phenylpropanoid pathways as shared downstream processes in multinutrient responses (Fig. S12 ).

3. Candidate TFs of core multinutrient responses converge on suberin and cell wall pathways

To identify TFs coordinating the core nutrient response, we combined two complementary strategies. First, we curated top‐ranked TFs from public TF‐target databases, ConnecTF (Brooks et al ., 2021 ) and ShinyGO (v.0.77) (Ge et al ., 2020 ), based on their known or predicted regulatory relationships with genes in the core nutrient cluster. From these, we selected eight candidates ( ARR10 , GBF3 , HHO5 , NAC32 , NF‐YA3 , NF‐YB2 , SARD1 , and WRKY33 ) prioritized by enrichment and the availability of mutant lines (Fig. 3b ).

To contextualize these TFs within a broader regulatory framework, we constructed a GRN using GENIE3 (Huynh‐Thu et al ., 2010 ) with all expressed TFs as potential regulators of the 2050 core genes. The network was pruned using TF‐binding and perturbation data from ConnecTF to retain high‐confidence interactions (precision = 0.45), resulting in a GRN of 263 TFs and 1405 target genes (Table S6 ). Independent validation using publicly available RNA‐seq datasets for eight selected TF mutants showed that, on average, 39.41% of GRN‐predicted targets were differentially expressed upon TF perturbation (Table S7 ), providing transcriptome‐wide support for the inferred regulatory relationships. Analysis of the relative expression levels of target genes in roots of selected TF mutants ( wrky33 , hho5 , and nac32 ) under control (high–high) and double‐deficiency (low–low) conditions provided an additional layer of validation: seven out of 15 tested target genes (47%) showed altered expression in the mutant background, either at the endogenous level or in their response to nutrient deficiency (Figs S13–S15 ).

Functional annotation of densely connected clusters revealed modules associated with suberin and cutin biosynthesis, cell wall remodeling, detoxification, lipid metabolism, and defense responses (Fig. 4 ). The eight candidate TFs converged on key modules of the nutrient response network, shaping distinct yet interconnected aspects of root adaptation. WRKY33 , NAC32 , and NF‐YA3 were embedded within transcriptional modules enriched for immune signaling, cell wall remodeling, and suberin biosynthesis, respectively. These modules also featured TFs previously implicated in relevant processes: NAC42 , upregulated during potassium deprivation (Hetherington et al ., 2021 ), co‐occurred with WRKY33 in a cluster enriched for indole and phytoalexin biosynthesis; ATAF1 , a mobile abscisic acid (ABA)‐responsive NAC gene (Lu et al ., 2006 ), defined a detoxification module alongside NAC32 ; and NF‐YA10 , known to regulate root architecture via LAZY gene targets (Barrios et al ., 2025 ), reinforced the suberin‐associated module containing NF‐YA3 .

Fig. 4.

While not all candidates were central hubs, most exhibited broad connectivity or strategic positioning. NF‐YB2 connected to all four major network clusters – linking barrier formation, stress responses, and metabolic regulation – highlighting its potential as a cross‐module integrator. HHO5 and ARR10 bridged modules associated with hormone signaling and cell wall dynamics. GBF3 occupied an integrative position linking NAC32 , ATAF1 , and GT2 , the TF hubs spanning two major network clusters. Even SARD1 , the sparsest in direct targets, aligned with regulatory junctions between defense and detoxification pathways.

Together, these configurations underscore a decentralized and layered architecture of transcriptional control underlying the multinutrient response. Rather than being governed by a single master regulator, plasticity emerges from the interplay of repurposed TFs – some broadly connected across modules, others tuned to specific neighborhoods. This modular logic enables plants to flexibly integrate diverse nutrient signals and coordinate structural and physiological adjustments accordingly. Central among these outputs is barrier remodeling, notably suberin biosynthesis, which recurrently surfaced as a transcriptional and phenotypic hallmark of nutrient stress adaptation. By embedding generalist TFs within nutrient‐responsive modules, the network positions them to fine‐tune gene expression in a context‐specific manner – offering a versatile strategy for navigating the complexity of real‐world nutrient environments.

4. Key TFs coordinate root architecture under nutrient‐specific contexts

To assess whether the selected candidate TFs contribute functionally to multinutrient responses, we examined phenotypes of corresponding T‐DNA insertion mutants under prioritized nutrient combinations. Rather than testing nutrients individually or selecting combinations arbitrarily, we developed a prioritization strategy based on the overlap between predicted TF targets and transcriptional responses across conditions (Fig. 5a ). This approach ranked nutrient conditions by the P ‐value of the intersection and highlighted zinc deficiency as a highly significant condition, consistent with the most connected nutrient in the ‘molecular Mulder's chart’ (Fig. 2c ). Accordingly, we focused on two‐nutrient combinations – such as N | Zn and K | Zn – associated with strong regulatory links for WRKY33 , NF‐YA3 , SARD1 , and NAC32 , Mg | Fe for GBF3 and NAC32 , as well as K | Fe and K | Zn for NF‐YB2 and HHO5 , respectively.

Fig. 5.

To evaluate phenotypic responses, we employed a comprehensive trait‐based approach, measuring five RSA parameters, including LR number, LR density, LR length, primary root length, and total root length, alongside shoot fresh weight (Figs S16–S19 ; Table S8 ).

Across these six traits, we found no single phenotype that consistently captured genotype × treatment interactions. This highlights the importance of tracking multiple traits in assay design as different traits responded independently across genotypes and nutrient contexts, reflecting the complex nature of root plasticity.

We next assessed nutrient responses within each genotype using two‐way ANOVA for element 1 (N, K, or Mg), element 2 (Zn or Fe), and their interaction (element 1 × element 2), depending on the specific nutrient combination tested. In wild‐type seedlings, K : Zn, N : Zn, and Mg : Fe combinations significantly influenced multiple RSA traits. However, these treatment responses were reduced or absent in mutants, including nf‐ya3 , hho5 , arr10 , sard1 , cbp60g , wrky33, gbf3 , and nac32 mutants (Fig. 5b,d,e ), indicating impaired nutrient‐induced plasticity. By contrast, K : Fe affected only LR density in the WT but influenced additional traits – such as primary root length, shoot fresh weight, and total LR length – in nf‐yb2 and nac32 mutants (Fig. 5c ). Notably, arr10 mutants showed Zn‐dependent reductions in LR density that were not observed in the WT, while nf‐ya3 displayed broad responses to N | Zn across all measured traits (Figs 5b–e , S5–S8 ).

Together, these results support a model in which nutrient‐induced RSA plasticity is coordinated by transcriptional regulators that modulate specific root traits in response to distinct nutrient combinations rather than functioning as general stress factors. This provides a clear molecular basis for how plants achieve context‐dependent developmental plasticity.

5. WRKY33 and NF‐YB2 link multinutrient signaling to root suberization and ionomic balance

Suberin biosynthesis emerged as one of the most strongly enriched pathways in the core nutrient gene cluster, implicating root barrier remodeling as a common transcriptional response to diverse nutrient stresses (Fig. 2b ). Several candidate TFs – including WRKY33, NF‐YB2, and GBF3 – were predicted to regulate known suberin‐associated genes such as MYB54 , NAC075 , KCS1 , and ABCG2 , suggesting they may influence root barrier dynamics in a multinutrient context. To investigate this, we examined suberin deposition and nutrient accumulation in their respective mutant lines under selected nutrient combinations.

In WT roots, suberin deposition patterns responded dynamically to nutrient availability (Figs 6a–b , S20 ). Specifically, under varying N and Zn combinations, as well as K and Fe combinations, significant shifts in suberin deposition were observed across root zones. By contrast, wrky33 mutants under N | Zn perturbations and nfyb2 mutants under K | Fe perturbations exhibited markedly reduced responsiveness: suberin profiles remained largely unchanged across treatments, indicating a loss of nutrient‐induced suberization plasticity. Similarly, gbf3 displayed altered suberin patterning under Mg | Fe conditions, with attenuated zone‐specific responses compared to the WT (Fig. S21 ). Together, these phenotypes suggest that all three TFs are required not simply for suberin deposition per se , but for modulating suberin in response to environmental cues.

Fig. 6.

To evaluate how nutrient responses at the physiological level reflect TF‐dependent regulation, we profiled elemental accumulation in wrky33 and nfyb2 mutants under matched nutrient conditions (Figs 6c,d , S9 , S10 ; Table S9 ). Overall, wrky33 showed increased fold changes in nutrient levels compared to the WT, suggesting heightened responsiveness or altered nutrient retention. By contrast, nfyb2 exhibited consistently reduced fold changes relative to the WT, indicating dampened uptake or buffering capacity. These trends were especially pronounced in roots and aligned with suberin phenotypes: while wrky33 retained some dynamic suberization, nfyb2 showed both impaired barrier plasticity and reduced ionomic responsiveness. Together, these results point to distinct regulatory roles – WRKY33 as a modulator of nutrient sensitivity, and NF‐YB2 as a gatekeeper of nutrient homeostasis – highlighting the layered control of root plasticity under multi‐nutrient conditions.

IV. Discussion

Plants rarely encounter singular nutrient deficiencies in nature. Instead, they must continuously interpret and respond to combinations of nutrient availabilities that shift over time and space. While the physiological interplay among mineral nutrients has been long appreciated – most famously illustrated in Mulder's chart – the transcriptional mechanisms that enable such integrative responses remain poorly defined. Here, we present a molecular version of Mulder's chart: a system‐level dissection of how Arabidopsis coordinates root‐level responses to diverse nutrient conditions through a shared gene network, regulatory modules, and trait plasticity (Fig. 2c ).

1. Dynamic barrier remodeling under multi‐nutrient conditions

By analyzing transcriptomic responses to 15 nutrient and beneficial element treatments, we identified a core cluster of coregulated genes enriched in suberin biosynthesis and stress‐related metabolic pathways (Figs 2a , S11a ). Instead of reacting to individual conditions, these genes formed a shared transcriptional signature across diverse nutrient stresses, reflecting a coordinated program for managing nutrient availability, barrier remodeling, and resource uptake under complex environments. The inferred GRN from this core cluster revealed several candidate TFs (Fig. 4 ), with WRKY33 and NF‐YB2 emerging as central hubs linking nutrient perception to downstream structural and physiological adaptation. Functional analysis confirmed their distinct contributions: WT roots dynamically adjusted root architecture and suberin deposition in response to nutrient combinations, while wrky33 and nfyb2 mutants showed markedly reduced plasticity across traits and barriers (Figs 5 , 6 ). This loss of context‐specific responsiveness underscores the importance of transcriptionally regulated barrier remodeling in nutrient adaptation going beyond simple constitutive suberin deposition.

This distinction between presence and responsiveness is central. Suberin, primarily understood as a structural barrier, has more recently been recognized as an adaptive interface (Barberon et al ., 2016 ; Doblas et al ., 2017 ). It functions as a bidirectional gate, regulating both solute influx and nutrient leakage, and in Arabidopsis , this role is especially critical due to the absence of an exodermis (Barberon, 2017 ). Previous studies have shown that suberin responds nonuniformly to single‐nutrient deficiencies (Barberon et al ., 2016 ; Namyslov et al ., 2020 ). Our results expand this view by demonstrating that suberin plasticity is modular, transcriptionally governed, and tightly coupled to nutrient status. Dynamic suberization thus emerges as a key component of root plasticity, fine‐tuning solute flux and resource uptake under fluctuating multinutrient environments.

These regulatory effects align with a broader architecture linking nutrient sensing to structural adaptation. Core nutrient cluster regulators identified in our GRN, including MYB54 and NAC075, have been previously associated with suberin biosynthesis and secondary wall remodeling (Shukla et al ., 2021 ; Xu et al ., 2022 ). The interaction between WRKY33 and NF‐YB2 with these targets reinforces their roles as key integrators of nutrient signaling and root barrier modulation (Fig. 4 ).

2. Repurposed TFs orchestrate multinutrient adaptation

The eight TFs selected for functional validation – ARR10 , GBF3, HHO5 , NAC32 , NF‐YA3 , NF‐YB2 , SARD1 , and WRKY33 – represent a diverse set of broadly acting regulators, many of which had not previously been linked to nutrient signaling. Their strong connectivity within the GRN of the core nutrient cluster suggests that multinutrient responses are modular and polygenic, coordinated not by a single master switch but by the flexible repurposing of TFs across modules.

Several candidates illustrate this pattern of transcriptional redeployment. HHO5 , known for maintaining floral meristem identity through regulating WUSCHEL , is also responsive to nitrogen status and influences phosphate uptake (Varala et al ., 2018 ; Li et al ., 2021 ). NAC32 , classically involved in senescence and shoot development, has been recently implicated in Mg and Ni toxicity responses (Sun et al ., 2023 , 2024 ). NF‐YB2 was previously predicted to act as a nutrient hub based on co‐expression network modeling (Brumbarova & Ivanov, 2019 ), whereas ARR10 and SARD1 , typically associated with cytokinin signaling and immune defense (Yokoyama et al ., 2007 ; Sun et al ., 2015 , 2018 ; Zubo et al ., 2017 ), emerged from our unsupervised GRN approach as regulators of nutrient‐responsive modules. Supporting the idea that cytokinin cues contribute to nutrient integration, we observed a significant overlap between the core nutrient genes ( n = 2050) and cytokinin‐responsive genes ( n = 226) (Bhargava et al ., 2013 ) (hypergeometric P < 10 −12 ) (Fig. 3b ), further underscoring the role of cytokinin as a key coordinator in the multinutrient response. Together, these findings highlight how plants rewire existing transcriptional circuits by repurposing general TFs into context‐specific coordinators to meet the demands of a fluctuating nutrient environment.

Phenotypic assays supported the modular and context‐specific roles of these repurposed TFs. The mutants validated in this work showed altered RSA traits under two‐nutrient stresses (Fig. 5 ), consistent with nutrient‐driven reprogramming of development. ARR10 was previously implicated to have a role in RSA regulation under other osmotic, salt, and heat stresses (Justamante et al ., 2025 ). Here, we showed that this role extends to abiotic stress caused by nutrient deficiency. WRKY33 and NF‐YB2 stood out for their strong integration within the core GRN and their direct association with suberin biosynthesis modules. While both interacted with suberin‐related TFs such as MYB54 and NAC075 , their phenotypes diverged. Although NF‐YB2 likely shares redundancy with other NF‐YB family members (Ballif et al ., 2011 ; Swain et al ., 2017 ), this compensation appears insufficient in the context of root barrier modulation – highlighting its nonsubstitutable role in coordinating transcriptional and physiological responses to complex nutrient inputs. By comparison, WRKY33 has previously been implicated in diverse stress‐related processes, including iron‐regulated transporter 1 degradation under Cd stress (Zhang et al ., 2023 ), as well as potential binding to nitrate and zinc transporter genes (Liu et al ., 2015 ; Birkenbihl et al ., 2017 ). Likewise, GBF3 has been linked to abiotic and biotic stress regulation through ABA‐mediated signaling (Ramegowda et al ., 2017 ; Dixit et al ., 2019 ).

Taken together, our findings outline a conceptual and experimental framework for understanding how plants translate complex nutrient environments into coordinated molecular and developmental responses. By revealing shared and context‐specific regulatory elements, we advance a model in which plasticity is not a trait, but a regulated outcome orchestrated by a network of TFs and gene modules that tune the root's architecture and barrier properties in response to nutrient context. Furthermore, our molecular iteration of Mulder's chart captures regulatory crosstalk between nutrients that may not yet be apparent at the physiological level. These insights offer a framework for crop improvement by enabling targeted modulation of nutrient‐response expression, for example, through promoter engineering of core or context‐specific regulatory genes to fine‐tune responses to particular nutrient combinations, or by using defined gene modules as biomarkers for precision breeding programs.
