Levels of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), an essential glycolipid on the surface of
most gram-negative bacteria, are tightly controlled-making LPS synthesis a
promising target for developing new antibiotics. Escherichia coli adaptor
protein LapB (YciM) plays an important role in regulating LPS synthesis by
promoting degradation of LpxC, a deacetylase that catalyzes the first committed
step in LPS synthesis. Under conditions where LPS is abundant, LapB recruits
LpxC to the AAA+ protease FtsH for degradation. LapB achieves this by
simultaneously interacting with FtsH through its transmembrane helix and LpxC
through its cytoplasmic domain. Here, we describe a cryo-EM structure of the
complex formed between LpxC and the cytoplasmic domain of LapB (LapBcyto). The
structure reveals how LapB exploits both its tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR)
motifs and rubredoxin domain to interact with LpxC. Through both in vitro and in
vivo analysis, we show that mutations at the LapBcyto/LpxC interface prevent
LpxC degradation. Unexpectedly, binding to LapBcyto also inhibits the enzymatic
activity of LpxC through allosteric effects reminiscent of LpxC activation by
MurA in Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Our findings argue that LapB regulates LPS
synthesis in two steps: In the first step, LapB inhibits the activity of LpxC,
and in the second step, it commits LpxC to degradation by FtsH.

## Full Text

The outer membrane (OM) of gram-negative bacteria features an asymmetric lipid arrangement, with phospholipids in the periplasmic leaflet and lipopolysaccharides (LPS) on the cell surface (1). The densely packed LPS molecules form an exceptional permeability barrier that protects gram-negative bacteria from various chemical insults (2) while also imparting the stiffness and strength to maintain cell shape (3) and endure osmotic pressure (4). As an essential glycolipid for nearly all gram-negative bacteria, the synthesis of LPS is subject to tight regulation (5–7). Insufficient LPS undermines the integrity of the OM, leading to cell death. Conversely, an excess of LPS results in the lethal accumulation of LPS within the inner membrane (IM) (8, 9). LPS synthesis begins in the cytoplasm with two building blocks, UDP-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc) and β-hydroxylmyristoyl-ACP. Whereas the first enzyme in the LPS synthesis pathway (LpxA) catalyzes a reversible reaction (10), the reaction catalyzed by the second enzyme (LpxC) represents the committed step of LPS synthesis (11). LpxC is a zinc-dependent deacetylase that cleaves the acetyl group from UDP-3-O-acyl-N-acetylglucosamine. Structures of LpxC reveal that its substrate-binding site features a hydrophobic tunnel that accommodates the acyl chain of the substrate (12, 13). Levels of Escherichia coli LpxC protein are increased 10-fold when early steps of LPS synthesis are inhibited (14), suggesting a control mechanism that involves modulation of LpxC synthesis/degradation.

The level of LpxC in E. coli is primarily determined by the rate of its degradation, mediated by FtsH, a plasma membrane-bound AAA+ ATPase (ATPase associated with diverse cellular activities) (15). FtsH consists of an N-terminal transmembrane domain, a central cytosolic ATPase domain, and a C-terminal proteolytic domain (16, 17). As a hexamer, the ATPase domain of FtsH forms a ring-like structure with a central channel and the protease domain shapes a sealed compartment that houses an enclosed catalytic site (18). FtsH employs ATP hydrolysis to unfold and translocate its substrates through an axial channel to the interior catalytic sites of the protease domain, wherein the substrate is processively broken down into short peptides (19, 20). FtsH degrades LpxC from its C-terminal flexible tail, which harbors a conserved LAXXXXXAVLA motif that functions as a degron (21, 22). LpxC degradation by FtsH is specifically regulated by two other essential inner membrane proteins, LapB and YejM, in a manner that depends on the LPS concentration in the IM. LapB functions as an adaptor protein, binding to FtsH and recruiting LpxC to the protease to target it for degradation (23, 24). YejM, by contrast, acts as an anti-adaptor protein, sensing LPS levels in the IM and sequestering LapB at low LPS levels to slow down LpxC degradation (9, 24–27).

LapB forms a homodimer, with each subunit consisting of an N-terminal transmembrane (TM) helix, nine cytoplasmic tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) motifs, and a C-terminal rubredoxin domain (28, 29). TPR motifs are conserved 34-residue-long sequences that fold into two antiparallel α-helices connected by a loop and are usually involved in protein–protein interactions (30). LapB’s rubredoxin domain has two CXXC motifs that can bind to either iron or zinc (28), but its exact function, and whether it is involved in electron transfer or redox reactions like other rubredoxin proteins (31), remains unclear. Protein engineering and biochemical analysis suggest that LapB interacts with FtsH mainly through the TM helices (24). This leaves the cytoplasmic domain of LapB free to recruit LpxC (24), but how it does so is unclear in the absence of structural information.

Here, we describe a cryoEM structure of the cytoplasmic domain of LapB (LapBcyto) in complex with LpxC, elucidating the structural basis for how LapB acts as an adaptor protein to specifically recruit LpxC for degradation. Through further biochemical analysis, we also unexpectedly found that binding of LapBcyto to LpxC inhibits the deacetylase activity of LpxC, indicating that LapB turns off the activity of LpxC before it is degraded. This finding introduces an additional layer of intricacy in the regulation of LPS synthesis, suggesting that LapB plays multiple roles in this key process.

DiscussionBy determining the cryoEM structure of the LapBcyto/LpxC complex, we elucidated how LapB specifically recruits LpxC for FtsH-mediated degradation. LapB exploits both its TPR motifs and rubredoxin domains to interact with LpxC. In contrast to the most common mode in which TPR motifs bind small peptides, namely through the concave surface formed by the helical solenoid, the LapB TPR motifs interact with LpxC through the loops between the α helices. The rubredoxin domain in LapB interacts with LpxC through its signature CXXC motifs. Strikingly, two LpxC molecules bind to the LapB homodimer, with their degrons positioned close to the inner membrane. Similar to the mode in which the adaptor protein SspB binds to ssrA-tagged proteins for ClpXP degradation (38, 39), the adaptor LapB binds the N-terminal and central part of the degron, leaving the C-terminal residues exposed. Given that the entrance of the central channel in the FtsH ATPase domain for substrate degradation is also close to the membrane (40), our structure suggests that LapB positions the LpxC degron close to the substrate entrance of FtsH for efficient degradation. Whether the two LpxC molecules are degraded sequentially or simultaneously by FtsH needs further investigation.An intriguing finding is that LapB binding inhibits the deacetylase activity of LpxC. Our mutagenesis analysis identifies the rubredoxin domain in LapB as playing a crucial role in this inhibitory effect. By determining the cryoEM structure of the LapBcyto/LpxC complex, we elucidated how LapB specifically recruits LpxC for FtsH-mediated degradation. LapB exploits both its TPR motifs and rubredoxin domains to interact with LpxC. In contrast to the most common mode in which TPR motifs bind small peptides, namely through the concave surface formed by the helical solenoid, the LapB TPR motifs interact with LpxC through the loops between the α helices. The rubredoxin domain in LapB interacts with LpxC through its signature CXXC motifs. Strikingly, two LpxC molecules bind to the LapB homodimer, with their degrons positioned close to the inner membrane. Similar to the mode in which the adaptor protein SspB binds to ssrA-tagged proteins for ClpXP degradation (38, 39), the adaptor LapB binds the N-terminal and central part of the degron, leaving the C-terminal residues exposed. Given that the entrance of the central channel in the FtsH ATPase domain for substrate degradation is also close to the membrane (40), our structure suggests that LapB positions the LpxC degron close to the substrate entrance of FtsH for efficient degradation. Whether the two LpxC molecules are degraded sequentially or simultaneously by FtsH needs further investigation.

An intriguing finding is that LapB binding inhibits the deacetylase activity of LpxC. Our mutagenesis analysis identifies the rubredoxin domain in LapB as playing a crucial role in this inhibitory effect. Residues W377 and H181 mediate interactions between the rubredoxin domain and the TPR motifs through π stacking, positioning the CXXC motifs of LapB right outside the substrate-binding tunnel of LpxC. Mutating these two residues restrained LapB’s ability to inhibit LpxC enzymatic activity, suggesting that positioning the CXXC motifs outside LpxC’s hydrophobic tunnel is key for LapB’s ability to inhibit LpxC deacetylase activity.

Given that binding of LpxC to LapB ultimately leads to its degradation by FtsH, the question arises as to why LapB also functions as an inhibitor of LpxC. We propose that—in contrast to the irreversible and relatively slow effect of LpxC degradation—inhibition of LpxC activity offers a reversible means to promptly halt LPS synthesis, allowing cells to respond quickly to dynamic shifts and environmental challenges. Beyond its implication in the regulation of LPS synthesis, this finding establishes that an adaptor protein can have the dual function of suppressing substrate activity before initiating its degradation, an insight poised to reshape our view of the role of adaptor proteins in protein degradation by AAA+ proteases.

How does the inhibitory function of LapB relate to its role as an adaptor protein to degrade LpxC? Under physiological conditions, LapB has different partners depending on the cellular LPS level. When E. coli cells have too much LPS, LapB binds to FtsH and recruits LpxC for degradation, which slows down LPS synthesis. In this case, LapB binding to LpxC and inhibiting its enzymatic activity aligns well with the purpose of halting LPS synthesis. However, when E. coli cells grow fast and require large amounts of LPS, LapB is titrated away from FtsH by YejM. Comparing the LapBcyto/LpxC structure with our previously reported YejM/LapB structure provides a possible explanation. In contrast to the planar LapB dimer in the LapBcyto/LpxC complex, the LapB dimer in the YejM/LapB complex has a tilted arrangement. This tilting leads to steric clashes with LpxC in the LapBcyto/LpxC structure (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C), effectively preventing LapB from interacting with LpxC when LapB forms a complex with YejM and thus abolishing LapB’s ability to inhibit LpxC. Although all mutations we tested so far affect both LapB’s inhibitory effect on LpxC enzymatic activity and its function in stimulating LpxC degradation simultaneously, we cannot exclude the possibility that these two processes can occur independently. For example, even when LapB does not interact with FtsH, as long as it forms a planar dimer it should still be able to inhibit LpxC activity without targeting LpxC for degradation.

Another puzzling question concerns the large disparity in copy numbers between LapB and LpxC. An empty pCA24N vector (pCA24N-Vec) was made by deleting the LapB CDS of pCA24N-LapB. pCA24N-LapB mutants were generated by site-directed mutation and verified by DNA sequencing. pCA24N-Vec, pCA24N-LapB WT or mutants were transformed to MG1655/pAra::lapB strain by electroporation and grown on LB plates supplemented with 10 mM arabinose and 25 μg/mL Chl. Single colonies were picked and suspended in 50 μL LB medium and 10-fold serially diluted. The diluted cells were spotted on LB+ Chl plates with arabinose, or without arabinose but supplemented with either 5 μM or 100 μM IPTG, or 5 μM IPTG plus Vancomycin (Van) or 100 μM IPTG plus Van. The plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight.

Western Blot.MG1655/pAra::lapB strain harboring pCA24N-Vec, or pCA24N-LapB WT or mutants were spread on LB plates with 25 μg/mL Chl and 10 mM arabinose. After incubating at 37 °C overnight, single colonies were picked and resuspended in 200 μL LB medium. The OD600 was measured, and cells were diluted to an initial OD600 of 0.01 in LB medium supplemented with 25 μg/mL Chl and 5 μM IPTG. Cells were grown in an incubator shaker at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 5 h. Cells were collected, resuspended in lysis buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.8, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, and 10% (v/v) glycerol] to a final OD600 of 10 and broken using a sonicator (BRANSON Sonifier 250). Cell lysates were mixed with SDS loading buffer and boiled for 6 min. Samples were run in 4 to 20% polyacrylamide gels and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane using a transfer system (Bio-Rad Trans-Blot Turbo). The membranes were blocked in 5% milk in TBST [20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20] for 30 min at room temperature and incubated with 1% BSA -TBST containing rabbit anti-LpxC or anti-LapB sera (Pocono Rabbit Farm) at 1:1,000 dilution, or mouse anti-LPS core antibody (Hycult Biotech HM6011) at 1:5,000 dilution or rabbit anti-GroEL antibody (Sigma G6532) at 1:50,000 dilution. After incubation at 4 °C overnight, the membranes were washed three times with TBST at room temperature. For LpxC, LapB, and GroEL blots, membranes were incubated with (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Sigma 12-348, 1:10,000 dilution) in 1% BSA-TBST. For the LPS blot, the membrane was incubated with (HRP)-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Sigma A4416, 1:5,000 dilution) in 1% BSA-TBST. After 45 min of incubation at room temperature, the membranes were washed three times with TBST at room temperature. Membranes were developed using the Clarity Western ECL substrate (Bio-Rad #170-5061) and imaged using the LI-COR Odyssey XF.
