The Notch signalling pathway is a conserved regulator of cellular processes,
including differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis, across Metazoa. While
its roles in bilaterians are well-characterized, the evolutionary history and
functional diversification of Notch signalling in early branching metazoans,
including cnidarians and parasitic cnidarians, myxozoans, remain underexplored.
This study examines the presence and structural diversity of Notch pathway
components across early diverging metazoan lineages, with a particular focus on
Myxozoa-parasitic cnidarians characterized by extreme morphological and genomic
reduction. Comparative analyses of 58 metazoan species revealed broad
conservation of core Notch components, alongside losses in ctenophores,
placozoans, poriferans, and cnidarians. Myxozoa retain only 14 of the 28
canonical pathway components, lacking key elements, such as MAML, Hes/Hey, and
DVL. Phylogenetic analyses of Notch receptors highlight structural divergence,
particularly in extracellular domains, reflecting lineage-specific variations.
The phylogeny of Delta and Jagged ligands reveals an evolutionary trajectory,
with Delta ligands showing early diversification within metazoans, and their
structural variability has been explored, though their functional roles remain
unknown. The Notch receptor was detected at the subcellular level in
proliferative stages of Sphaerospora molnari through immunolocalization studies,
suggesting it is active in these cells. Our findings contribute to understanding
the evolution of the Notch signalling pathway, highlighting its conserved role
in developmental regulation across early branching Metazoa.

Introduction

Notch signalling is one of the oldest known signalling pathways in metazoans. Its evolution can be traced back to early eukaryotes, with some components emerging in unicellular organisms [1]. Despite incorporating some genes from early eukaryotes, the fully functional canonical Notch pathway likely evolved later during the transition to multicellular animals [1–3]. In particular, the possible origin is in the common ancestor of cnidarians and bilaterians [4].

Distinctly, Notch signalling relies on juxtacrine communication, necessitating direct cell–cell contact between the receptor and ligand for activation. Stable cell–cell contacts are not always required, as transient interactions, such as those mediated by filopodia, can also trigger Notch activity and induce specific cell fates [5]. Upon interaction of Notch receptor and ligand (Delta or Serrate/Jagged type), a series of proteolytic events, including S2 cleavage by metalloproteases ADAM10 and TACE, and S3 cleavage by the gamma-secretase (γ-secretase) complex, releases the Notch intracellular domain (NICD) from the cell surface. The NICD then translocates to the nucleus, where it functions as a co-transcriptional activator by binding to CSL (RBPJ/CBF1/Su(H)/LAG-1), an essential transcriptional factor of the Notch pathway [6]. In the absence of NICD, CSL recruits corepressors (e.g., Hairless, CtBP, Groucho, SMRT/NCoR, CIR, and HDAC) to repress the transcription whereas in the presence of NICD it recruits coactivators (MAML/Mastermind, SKIP, and HAT) to activate transcription of Hes/Hey target genes [7]. By regulating target genes, the Notch pathway affects a variety of cellular processes, including cell fate determination, proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [8]. While some components (e.g., the Notch receptors, ligands, and CSL) are hallmark features specific to the Notch pathway, others function in alternative cellular pathways or have multiple roles (e.g., DVL mediates Wnt-Notch crosstalk, and the γ-secretase complex cleaves other transmembrane proteins, such as amyloid precursor protein [6, 9].

The pathway operates via either a canonical or non-canonical mechanism, adapting to specific cellular contexts. Canonical signalling involves direct receptor–ligand interactions, leading to the release of the NICD and subsequent transcriptional regulation mediated by the DNA-binding protein CSL. In contrast, non-canonical signalling operates independently of CSL and can be either ligand-dependent or ligand-independent, often involving alternative mechanisms or crosstalk with other signalling pathways [10].

The Notch receptor and its ligands exhibit a conserved modular structure that has diversified across metazoans through domain shuffling [3]. The Notch receptor is composed of an extracellular domain (NECD) containing epidermal growth factor-like (EGF) repeats, calcium-binding EGF (EGF Ca) repeats, LIN-12/Notch (LN) repeats, and NOD/NODP domains; a transmembrane domain (TM); and an intracellular domain (NICD) that includes an RBP-J-associated molecule (RAM) domain, ankyrin (ANK) repeats, and the DUF3435 domain. Notch ligands, also single-pass transmembrane proteins, possess an N-terminal MNNL domain, a conserved DSL domain required for receptor binding, and multiple EGF repeats (some EGF-Ca). Ligands are grouped into Delta and Jagged families, with the latter distinguished by the presence of a Von Willebrand factor type C (VWC) domain. Comparative analyses have shown that both receptors and ligands have undergone multiple duplication events across metazoans, as demonstrated by ligand duplications in Amphimedon queenslandica (Porifera) and receptor duplications in vertebrates [11, 12]. These duplications likely contributed to the functional diversification and lineage-specific adaptations of the pathway.

Studies of Notch signalling in early branching metazoans, particularly cnidarians, have provided insights into its potential ancestral role in metazoan evolution. Notably, different cnidarian model organisms appear to utilize distinct modes of Notch signalling: Nematostella vectensis predominantly follows a non-canonical, Hes-independent route [4], while Hydra vulgaris relies on the canonical, CSL-dependent pathway [13, 14]. Experimental inhibition of Notch signalling in both species using the γ-secretase inhibitor DAPT revealed its conserved regulatory role in balancing cell proliferation and differentiation [15], similar to its function in bilaterians. However, these two cnidarian model organisms exhibited contrasting outcomes following γ-secretase inhibitor treatment, particularly in neuronal development, nematocyst maturation, and tentacle formation [16, 17].

In Ctenophora (e.g., Beroe ovata and Mnemiopsis leidyi), the absence of Notch ligands (Delta and Jagged) raises questions about the functionality of the Notch pathway in these early branching metazoans [18]. In contrast, the sponge A. queenslandica possesses gene duplications resulting in five Delta ligands, with a regulatory role of Notch pathway in diverse cell types during development [12]. Although early branching metazoans represent some of the earliest diverging extant animal lineages, the function of Notch signalling in these groups remains unclear due to their diverse body plans and developmental pathways. Here, we explore the evolution of the Notch signalling pathway in metazoans, including ctenophores, poriferans, placozoans, cnidarians, and bilaterians with a focus on the extreme body plan and genetic reduction, in the parasitic cnidarian group, Myxozoa. This group is characterized by their microscopic size and parasitic lifestyle [19], and lacks the typical cnidarian body plan—composed of ectoderm and endoderm. Instead, they do not form distinct tissues and maintain only a few cells throughout most of their life cycle, which are involved in the functions, such as proliferation and spore formation. Their unique cell-in-cell developmental arrangement, where primary cells enclose secondary, and occasionally tertiary cells through phagocytosis [20], further underscores their structural reduction. This reduction extends to their genome, with sizes ranging from 22,5 to 206 Mb and a limited gene repertoire [21, 22]. Additionally, while most myxozoans maintain mitochondrial genomes, one species has lost its mitochondrial genome and the ability to perform aerobic respiration, while others retain a functional, though highly divergent, mitochondrial genome [23, 24]. Despite these reductions, genes associated with major developmental signalling pathways—including Hedgehog, TGF-β, Notch, and Wnt/β-catenin—have been identified in Myxozoa [21, 22, 25, 26]. However, their activity and functional roles remain unexplored, leaving significant gaps in our understanding of these pathways.

This study provides an in-depth overview of evolutionary trends in Notch signalling components in Metazoa, with a particular focus on Cnidaria. It examines the evolution and structure of Notch receptors and ligands, exploring the potential evolutionary processes that shaped the diversification of the Notch pathway in early branching Metazoa. To explore an example of pathway simplicity, we investigated the localization of the Notch receptor in the proliferative stage of the parasitic cnidarian Sphaerospora molnari (Myxozoa), focusing on its distribution within this specific stage. This organism offers a valuable framework for investigating Notch signalling in early metazoans, supported by its availability in both in vitro cultivation and established in vivo systems [27, 28]. Thus, this work contributes to our understanding of Notch signalling in early branching metazoans, offering new insights into fundamental biological processes.

Evidence of Notch pathway componentsWe found a conserved pattern within Cnidaria group except for the apparent loss of several Notch components in medusozoan subgroup (Fig. 1). Medusozoans lack the co-repressor ATXN1L and Fringe, a glycosyltransferase that modifies Notch receptor (EGF repeats), with hydrozoans being the only medusozoan group showing an apparent loss of the MAML coactivator. DiscussionNotch signalling components in early branching metazoansOur expanded data set refines the presence or absence of specific Notch signalling components—coactivators (SKIP/HAT), corepressors (CIR/ATXN1L/GROUCHO/HDAC1), and DVL—across early branching metazoans [1, 3]. Cnidarians, especially Anthozoa, show strong conservation of the Notch pathway, with nearly all components present (Fig. 1), while Hydrozoa lack MAML and Medusozoa lack ATXN1L/Fringe. Myxozoans retain only half of the canonical components, as supported by earlier genomic findings [21]. Notch signalling components in early branching metazoans

Our expanded data set refines the presence or absence of specific Notch signalling components—coactivators (SKIP/HAT), corepressors (CIR/ATXN1L/GROUCHO/HDAC1), and DVL—across early branching metazoans [1, 3]. While these findings indicate lineage-specific reductions of Notch components in several early branching metazoans—such as the widespread absence of MAML, SMRT, ATXN1L, O-Fut, Fringe, and Numb—similar patterns of gene loss are also observed in some bilaterians (Fig. 2) [3]. Notably, P. dumerilii lacks five Notch components, and the loss of MAML, TACE, and SMRT is shared between certain bilaterians and myxozoans. However, these observations should be interpreted with caution, as our data set includes broader taxon sampling for early branching metazoans than for bilaterians, which may influence the apparent frequency and distribution of component losses across the Metazoa (Fig. 1).

The Delta ligand was identified in B. forskalii but remains uncharacterized in other ctenophores, leaving its role in this lineage uncertain (this study). Among myxozoans, Delta ligand is absent in most species except for C. shasta and T. bryosalmonae. It was not identified in P. hydriforme or in some species of cnidarian classes, such as Hydrozoa, Cubozoa, Octocorallia, and Ceriantharia. These absences likely to reflect either genuine gene loss or limitations in available data and the challenges posed by highly divergent sequences that complicate orthology searches across species.

Altogether, our findings suggest that while key Notch components—including Delta—are broadly present across Cnidaria, Placozoa, and Bilateria, their presence is markedly reduced in several early branching lineages. The strong conservation of the pathway in anthozoans supports the hypothesis that the last common ancestor of cnidarians and bilaterians, possessed a complete Notch signalling toolkit [4]. In contrast, the recurrent absence of specific components in hydrozoans, medusozoans, and myxozoans points to lineage-specific secondary reductions, particularly in simplified or parasitic forms. Although the Notch pathway is generally well-conserved in bilaterians, a few taxa, such as P. dumerilii, also lack multiple components, highlighting that such reductions are not restricted to early branching metazoans. More extensive absences in poriferans and ctenophores further suggest that the full components of Notch signalling components may have evolved after their divergence [1, 4, 32, 33]. In our data set, the core component ADAM10 is absent in ctenophores, supporting the idea that a fully functional Notch pathway emerged after the split of ctenophores, consistent with Babonis et al. [1].

The presence of the transcription factor CSL in yeast suggests that some components of the Notch signalling pathway predate metazoan evolution within opisthokonts [34], potentially arising in unicellular ancestors like choanoflagellates. However, a fully functional Notch pathway, characterized by direct receptor–ligand interactions, proteolytic cleavage of the receptor, and NICD-mediated transcriptional regulation via CSL, likely emerged alongside the advent of multicellularity in the metazoan lineage [3, 35]. Evidence from non-metazoan opisthokonts, such as the filasterian Capsaspora owczarzaki, which contains several Notch components, and the choanozoans Ministeria vibrans and Mylnosiga fluctuans, which possesses domains related to Notch receptors and ligands, suggest that the individual components of the pathway assembled early in the evolutionary history of multicellular organisms [36, 37].

In Monosiga brevicollis, a choanoflagellate, earlier reports identified a Notch-like receptor [3]. However, our genomic analysis found no evidence of a true Notch receptor in this species, suggesting that while some domains and components existed in unicellular relatives, the fully integrated pathway—with sequential activation steps leading to transcriptional regulation—emerged later in the last common ancestor of Metazoa. This framework highlights the gradual assembly of Notch pathway components and their functional integration, enabling the pathway's role in cellular communication and developmental regulation at the onset of animal evolution.

These findings suggest that some components of the Notch pathway, particularly the γ-secretase complex and Notchless, were present in early eukaryotes and are not exclusive to Notch signalling. The γ-secretase complex, primarily responsible for the intramembrane proteolysis of type I membrane proteins, including the Notch receptor, regulates gene expression beyond traditional Notch targets, linking its downstream effects to other membrane proteins as well [9]. Components of γ-secretase are therefore found across various eukaryotic lineages, including Amoebozoa, Plantae, Heterokonta, Excavata, and Euglenozoa [1]. In early branching metazoans, the four components of the γ-secretase complex are generally present, with a few exceptions, such as the absence of APH1 in three out of four analysed ctenophores (Fig. 1). Notchless, which is involved in 60S ribosome biogenesis [38], is widely present in most eukaryotes [3]. Its absence in some early branching metazoans appears sporadic, with no clear lineage-specific trend.

Evolution of the Notch receptor

Molecular phylogenetic analysis of the Notch receptor highlights its evolutionary conservation across Metazoa, while also emphasizing diversification events. Initially, the Notch receptor likely evolved as a single gene, but over time, it underwent duplications, leading to the emergence of multiple paralogs. Interestingly, species such as C. sowerbii and S. kowalevskii each possess two Notch orthologues, while H. echinata exhibits two isoforms [39], likely generated by alternative splicing [40]. This diversification is particularly evident in vertebrates, where the presence of Notch1, Notch2, Notch3, and Notch4 corresponds with two whole-genome duplication events during the evolution of tetrapods [41]. In Danio rerio, teleost-specific genome duplications resulted in the emergence of two distinct Notch1 genes, Notch1a and Notch1b, which can be explained by a fish-specific genome duplication marking the transition from the 2R to 3R event [42].

The origin of the Notch receptor outside Metazoa remains uncertain. Current evidence suggests it likely originated in the last common ancestor of Metazoa [1, 3], though further studies are required to clarify its potential non-metazoan origin. The Notch receptor exhibits considerable structural variation across Metazoa, with early branching metazoans showing particularly notable differences. These variations are most evident in the receptor's length and the number of EGF repeats, with myxozoans possessing a particularly short NECD (Suppl. Fig. 2), likely due to the length of the ligands. Furthermore, repeat expansion in some families may be explained by exon shuffling [43] or domain/genomic rearrangement, which might reduce the level of tandem repeats in coding regions. High indel rates are often observed in protein families with these types of structural variations, particularly those involved in host–parasite co-evolution [44]. Such high rates of insertions and deletions can contribute to the variability in the Notch receptor's NECD, as well as its functional evolution. Within the NECD region, only two EGF repeats are known to bind or interact with the ligand [6].

The NICD structure shows structural differences across Metazoa. The essential peptide sequence within the RAM region—the WXP motif, which is critical for Notch binding to the nuclear CSL—is highly conserved across many taxa [45]. However, in H. vulgaris, this motif is altered, with the conserved WXP sequence replaced by WXF. Despite this alteration, the modified motif may still facilitate binding to CSL in the CSL-dependent Notch pathway of H. vulgaris [13, 14].

These structural and sequence-level divergences have implications for phylogenetic analyses. Myxozoans, in particular, frequently exhibit long branches in phylogenetic analyses (e.g., 46), a pattern that is also evident in our phylogenetic tree of the Notch receptor. Such long branches can result in long-branch attraction (LBA). In our analyses, the phylogenetic proximity of myxozoans and P. hydriforme to long-branching hydrozoan taxa may reflect LBA, potentially leading to artificial paraphyly of Hydrozoa (Fig. 3).

Ligand evolution

Our study provides evolutionary insights into the Notch ligands Delta and Jagged across metazoan lineages. Delta ligands in anthozoan species (Fig. 5; clade I) form a sister group to all Delta ligands across metazoans, including vertebrate Jagged ligands. This suggests that Delta ligands evolved in the common cnidarian ancestor and were subsequently lost in medusozoans [3].

While the Notch receptor has remained relatively conserved throughout metazoan evolution, its ligands have diversified considerably through duplication and domain rearrangements. This diversification gave rise to the functionally distinct Delta and Jagged ligands. These ligands enhanced the versatility and complexity of the Notch signalling pathway across metazoan lineages. Our findings suggest that the ancestral metazoan possessed a Delta orthologue, consistent with previous reports that Delta ligands emerged earlier in metazoans than Jagged ligands, which appeared later [18]. Delta and Jagged ligands share conserved domain architectures (MNLL, DSL, and EGF domains), emphasizing their functional divergence within the DSL family.

Interestingly, the Jagged-type ligand forms a distinct clade and is absent in early branching metazoans, except in Cnidaria, where it appears in both Anthozoa and Medusozoa. This supports the hypothesis of an ancestral origin for Jagged within this group [31]. In Myxozoa, we identified two ligands in species clustering with the putative Jagged-type ligand of H. vulgaris [51]. However, these myxozoan ligands lack the additional cysteine region (VWC domain) characteristic of Jagged, suggesting a Delta-like structure influenced by rapid evolutionary rates in Myxozoa, which drive divergent ligand architectures [46]. ConclusionThe absence of key Notch components, such as MAML (absent in myxozoans, hydrozoans, placozoans, ctenophores, and poriferans), TACE (missing in myxozoans and one poriferan), Hes/Hey (absent in myxozoans), and DVL (absent in myxozoans, placozoans, and one poriferan), in myxozoans and several early branching metazoans suggests the existence of alternative mechanisms for Notch pathway activation and receptor cleavage. These deviations from the classical pathway, including potential CSL-independent activation or non-canonical signalling routes. While such reductions align with the extreme morphological simplification and parasitic lifestyles of myxozoans similar patterns are also observed in some free-living early branching metazoans. Only certain protostomian species among bilaterians show a loss of MAML, while other key components remain largely conserved. This suggests that Notch pathway architecture is shaped by lineage-specific evolutionary trajectories that are not always associated with parasitism or body plan simplification. Further comparative and functional studies are needed to determine whether these losses reflect ancestral traits or independent reductions. Although our findings reveal considerable variation in Notch signalling components across Metazoa, experimental data are required to assess whether these changes reflect functional adaptations.

Slides were then labelled with goat anti-rabbit IgG-Alexa Fluor Plus 647 (ThermoFisher, USA) secondary antibody (1:500 dilution). Slides were counterstained with DAPI nuclear stain (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, 1:10,000 dilution) and mounted in Fluoroshield Mounting Medium (Searle Diagnostic Gurr Products, United Kingdom). For negative controls, we used host red blood cells (RBCs), white blood cells (WBCs), and parasite stages omitting exposure to either primary or secondary antibodies (Suppl. Fig. 4). Images were captured using Olympus FluoView FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with FluoView FV31S-SW software (v. 2.6).
