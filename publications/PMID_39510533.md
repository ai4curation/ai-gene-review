BACKGROUND: The mitochondrial contact site and cristae organising system (MICOS)
complex is required for cristae formation and is composed of seven proteins.
Among the genes of MICOS complex, variants of MICOS13, IMMT and APOO have been
reported to cause diseases.
METHODS AND RESULTS: We report a case in which whole genome sequencing
identified a variant of the MICOS10 gene associated with mitochondrial
hepatopathy along with mitochondrial DNA depletion. We identified the deletion
g.19596826_19601303del and the single nucleotide variant c.173G>C (p.Cys58Ser).
The deletion including exon 1 might have caused complete loss of gene
expression, indicating monoallelic expression from RNA sequencing. MIC10 was
lost at the protein level in the patient's fibroblasts, and mitochondrial oxygen
consumption was impaired. These were restored by overexpression of MICOS10 in
the patient's fibroblasts.
CONCLUSION: Taken together, these findings indicate that MICOS10 is a causative
gene for hepatopathy and neuropathy, a disease very similar to that associated
with MICOS13.

Introduction

Mitochondrial diseases are the most frequent diseases involving inborn errors of metabolism that cause multiple organ failure. Over 400 disease‐causing genes have been reported [1]. Among these genes, several variants of genes encoding mitochondrial contact site and cristae organising system (MICOS) complex components have been identified [2]. The MICOS complex involves seven components (IMMT/MIC60, CHCHD3/MIC19, CHCHD6/MIC25, MICOS10/MIC10, MICOS13/MIC13, APOO/MIC26 and APOOL/MIC27), of which MICOS13 [3, 4, 5, 6, 7], APOO [8, 9] and IMMT [10] are known to be associated with diseases. In particular, diseases associated with MICOS13 (MIM: 618329) are the most commonly reported, and MICOS13 deficiency is a symptomatic disease of the liver and central nervous system. Moreover, it has been reported that APOO (MIM: 300753) and IMMT (MIM: 600378) deficiencies are characterised by a neurological symptom‐driven phenotype.

We identified MICOS10 deficiency in a patient who had liver and neurological symptoms. A single nucleotide variant (SNV) with amino acid substitution and deletion was identified by whole genome sequencing (WGS). As the allele of deletion completely lost gene expression, monoallelic gene expression was revealed by RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq). We also identified abnormal oxygen consumption in the patient's cells, which was restored by overexpression of MICOS10.

RNA SequencingRNA‐seq was performed using RNA extracted from the patient's fibroblasts and was verified in terms of gene expression. RNA‐seq methods were as described previously [11]. Although c.173G>C in MICOS10 was called by WGS as a heterozygous variant, we confirmed that only the c.173G>C variant was expressed in RNA‐seq (Figure 2B). MICOS Complex and Mitochondrial FunctionSince RNA‐seq confirmed the decreased mRNA expression of MICOS10, protein expression was analysed by SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis/western blotting as described previously [7]. The results showed that the expression of MIC10 was significantly decreased compared to the finding in normal cells (Figure 2C). MIC13 was also analysed, as previous studies have shown that the binding of MIC10 and MIC13 results in a concomitant decrease in protein expression [14], and significant downregulation of MIC13 was confirmed.We also observed a decreased OCR in the fibroblasts of this patient and tested whether restoring MICOS10 overexpression would improve this phenotype. Rescue experiments and an OCR assay were performed as described previously [7]. When MICOS10 expression was restored by lentiviral vectors, the OCR was improved by MICOS10 expression compared with GFP, a control vector (Figure 2D). MICOS10 overexpression also confirmed the recovery of MIC13 expression levels (Figure 2C). On the other hand, the expression of TOM20 and TIM23 proteins was not affected in patient cells. This means that mitochondrial dysfunction is not due to quantitative changes in mitochondrial mass but depends on abnormalities in the MICOS complex. Ultrastructural analysis using transmission electron microscope further confirmed that restoration of MIC10 rescued abnormalities of the cristae structures in patient's fibroblasts (Figure 2E). Based on these results, we conclude that MICOS10 was associated with the disease in this patient.

Next, WGS was performed using DNA extracted from a liver biopsy specimen. WGS methods were as described previously [11]. We here found bi‐allelic variants in the MICOS10 gene. First, germline SNV or short insertion/deletion (indel) calls based on GATK best practices from WGS data identified variant c.173G>C (p.Cys58Ser) (Figure 1D). In fact, c.173G>C was also called by WES, but was overlooked because it was excluded as a heterozygous variant of unknown clinical significance. The minor allele frequencies of the c.173G>C variant were 0.00000796 and 0.00000657 in the gnomAD exomes v2 and genomes v3, respectively. The CADD score was 27.5, and the MetaRNN score was 0.9652 (pathogenic strong), suggesting a high possibility of a pathogenic variant. This variant was also confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and Sanger sequencing, which revealed that c.173G>C was inherited from the mother (Figure 1E). In parallel with conventional variant calling, we used Manta [12] and LUMPY [13] to detect copy number variation or structural variation and identified a deletion (NC_000001.11: g.19596826_19601303del) involving exon 1 of MICOS10 (Figure 2A). Genomic fragments of the patient were amplified by PCR around the deletion region, and the deletion sites were sequenced by Sanger sequencing (Figure 2A). The origin of the deletion could not be determined because the father's specimen could not be obtained. The mother did not have this deletion, suggesting that the deletion was paternally derived or de novo. The non‐affected brother had a wild‐type genotype (Figure 1E). As a result, c.173G>C and g.19596826_19601303del in a recessive manner were considered to be responsible for the loss of MIC10 function.

2.4
MICOS Complex and Mitochondrial FunctionSince RNA‐seq confirmed the decreased mRNA expression of MICOS10, protein expression was analysed by SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis/western blotting as described previously [7]. The results showed that the expression of MIC10 was significantly decreased compared to the finding in normal cells (Figure 2C). MIC13 was also analysed, as previous studies have shown that the binding of MIC10 and MIC13 results in a concomitant decrease in protein expression [14], and significant downregulation of MIC13 was confirmed.We also observed a decreased OCR in the fibroblasts of this patient and tested whether restoring MICOS10 overexpression would improve this phenotype. Rescue experiments and an OCR assay were performed as described previously [7]. When MICOS10 expression was restored by lentiviral vectors, the OCR was improved by MICOS10 expression compared with GFP, a control vector (Figure 2D). MICOS10 overexpression also confirmed the recovery of MIC13 expression levels (Figure 2C). On the other hand, the expression of TOM20 and TIM23 proteins was not affected in patient cells. This means that mitochondrial dysfunction is not due to quantitative changes in mitochondrial mass but depends on abnormalities in the MICOS complex. Ultrastructural analysis using transmission electron microscope further confirmed that restoration of MIC10 rescued abnormalities of the cristae structures in patient's fibroblasts (Figure 2E). Based on these results, we conclude that MICOS10 was associated with the disease in this patient.

3DiscussionWe performed WGS and RNA‐seq in our patient with liver and neurological abnormalities and found variants in MICOS10 that caused downregulation of its expression. The deletion in MICOS10 was thought to have resulted in complete loss of gene expression because the loss of exon 1 resulted in the loss of the transcription start site. c.173G>C (p.Cys58Ser) resulted in decreased protein expression and dysfunction of MIC10. According to the structure of AlphaFold DB (ID:AF‐Q5TGZ0‐F1), the amino acid residue p.Cys58 forms a disulphide bond with p.Cys13 between posing alpha helices. Therefore, the conversion of Cys to Ser at position 58 would have a significant impact on structure maintenance since the disulphide bond would be defective. Finally, lentiviral rescue of MICOS10 successfully ameliorated the loss of mitochondrial dysfunction by restoring gene function. Based on these findings, we conclude that this syndrome presents with hepatic and neurological manifestations owing to MICOS10 deficiency.
MICOS13, IMMT, and APOO were shown to be disease‐causing genes that encode components of the MICOS complex. Several previous studies have shown that variants in MICOS13 result in liver and neurological abnormalities [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. The phenotypic similarities between MICOS10 and MICOS13 deficiencies are expected to be related to gene function, as previous studies have also shown that MIC13 functions in concert with MIC10 [14, 15, 16, 17]. MIC13 has been shown to affect the MIC10/MIC26/MIC27 complex [3, 14, 15]. In knockout cells and patient cells with MICOS13 deficiency, loss of MIC13 results in protein expression of MIC10. Loss of MIC13 has been shown in MICOS10 knockout cells [18]. Abnormalities in the MICOS complex also have a significant effect on cristae morphology. In a study comparing cristae formation abnormalities in HeLa cells with MICOS complex defects, cristae morphology abnormalities in MIC10 and MIC13 defects showed very similar features, suggesting that these proteins are closely functionally related [17].It has also been shown that MICOS10 and MICOS13 deficiencies share a common denominator that results in mtDNA depletion. In previous studies, MICOS13 was shown to exhibit mtDNA depletion in the liver [6, 7]. Basic studies in model organisms have also shown that loss of the MICOS complex is associated with mtDNA maintenance [19, 20], and this was confirmed in MICOS10 as a consistent symptom. The formation of the MICOS complex is thought to be important for the proper positioning, distribution and replication of mitochondrial DNA. Indeed, abnormalities in cristae morphology have been observed in patient's cells, which may have a significant impact on the arrangement of mitochondrial DNA. Patient's fibroblasts exhibit mildly reduced respiratory chain complex activity (II and IV), which may lead to reduced oxygen consumption. Abnormal cristae morphology may also reduce the efficiency of ATP synthesis by preventing the formation of supercomplex.In conclusion, we reported a case in which WGS identified a variant of the MICOS10 gene associated with mitochondrial hepatopathy along with mitochondrial DNA depletion.
