Mitochondrial complex I powers ATP synthesis by oxidative phosphorylation,
exploiting the energy from ubiquinone reduction by NADH to drive protons across
the energy-transducing inner membrane. Recent cryo-EM analyses of mammalian and
yeast complex I have revolutionized structural and mechanistic knowledge and
defined structures in different functional states. Here, we describe a
2.7-Å-resolution structure of the 42-subunit complex I from the yeast Yarrowia
lipolytica containing 275 structured water molecules. We identify a proton-relay
pathway for ubiquinone reduction and water molecules that connect
mechanistically crucial elements and constitute proton-translocation pathways
through the membrane. By comparison with known structures, we deconvolute
structural changes governing the mammalian 'deactive transition' (relevant to
ischemia-reperfusion injury) and their effects on the ubiquinone-binding site
and a connected cavity in ND1. Our structure thus provides important insights
into catalysis by this enigmatic respiratory machine.

## Full Text

Mitochondrial complex I (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase)1–3 is a large, ~1 MDa membrane-bound enzyme through which electrons from the oxidation of carbohydrates, fats and proteins enter the electron transport chain to drive oxidative phosphorylation. Specifically, complex I couples oxidation of NADH and reduction of ubiquinone to proton transfer across the mitochondrial inner membrane, supporting the proton-motive force that powers ATP synthesis. As an essential metabolic enzyme, mutations in complex I-associated proteins are particularly detrimental to cells with high-energy demands and cause inherited neuromuscular and metabolic diseases4,5. Complex I is also an important contributor to cellular reactive oxygen species production6 and regulatory changes in its activity may minimise ischemia–reperfusion injury7,8.

Complex I, in all species that have it, contains 14 conserved, catalytic ‘core’ subunits, plus a varying number of ‘supernumerary’ subunits, for example 31 in mammals, that contribute to assembly, stability or regulation, or fulfil independent metabolic roles9,10. Complex I from the yeast Yarrowia lipolytica contains 29 supernumerary subunits11,12, and its genetic tractability, except for the seven mitochondria-encoded ND subunits, has led to its extensive application in both functional and structural studies13–17. The complex was first resolved by X-ray crystallography to 3.6–3.9 Å18 then, recently, by single-particle electron cryomicroscopy (cryo-EM) to 3.2 Å19, providing a near-complete model. Structural work on Y. lipolytica complements detailed cryo-EM analyses of mammalian complex I, which has been defined in two different, known functional states10,20,21, and at 3.3 Å-resolution for the enzyme from mouse (Mus musculus) heart mitochondria20.

In complex I, NADH is oxidised at the top of the hydrophilic domain by a flavin mononucleotide, which then passes the electrons down a chain of iron–sulfur clusters to the terminal 4Fe–4S cluster N2, and on to ubiquinone. The highly hydrophobic ubiquinone is thought to worm its way up a channel at the hydrophilic/hydrophobic domain interface of the NDUFS2, NDUFS7, ND1 and ND3 subunits, to position its redox-active headgroup in an active site above the membrane plane, ligated by hydrogen bonds to a conserved tyrosine and histidine pair. How the ~0.8 eV of energy liberated by NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreduction is captured and exploited to drive proton translocation is the major unanswered question in complex I catalysis1–3. Proton pumping may be initiated by a conserved aspartate linked by hydrogen bonding to the ubiquinone redox centre22, or the ubiquinone may switch between two subsites in a two-state stabilisation change mechanism23. Alternatively, the proposed ubiquinone-binding route passes through a network of charged residues where energy transfer, along a chain of glutamates to the membrane domain, may be initiated18,20,24–27. A ‘central axis’ of charged residues then runs along the middle of the membrane domain18,20,26,28, appearing to connect four proton-pumping units; four protons are pumped for each NADH oxidised29,30. The four units each comprise a proton-uptake and a proton-exit half-channel and they are located in ND1–ND6–ND3–ND4L, ND2, ND4 and ND526,28. Within the units, key polar residues and transmembrane helices (TMHs), interrupted by intrahelical loops to introduce charge to the centre of the membrane or distorted by π-helical stretches, are likely crucial for energy and proton transfer31,32. Intriguingly, one such TMH in ND4 has been observed in different states in two different structures20. Furthermore, in mammalian complex I, ND6-TMH3 is fully α-helical in the active state, but contains a π bulge in the ‘deactive’ state20. Deactive states20,21,33 are pronounced resting states, characterised by disorder in structural elements that form the ubiquinone-binding site, and unable to catalyse until the site is restructured upon reactivation20,21.

Here, we present a 2.7 Å-resolution structure for 42 subunits of Y. lipolytica complex I, plus a lower-resolution structure that includes the sub-stoichiometric sulfur-transferase subunit, ST1. Crucially, our 2.7 Å-resolution map, currently the highest resolution map of complex I available, reveals features from structured water molecules that are vital for the enzyme’s proton relays, energy transfer mechanisms, and proton-pumping pathways. Our model defines interactions between structural elements, functionally important residues and water molecules within the membrane domain, and unexpectedly reveals the links between seemingly disparate structural changes that occur during the active–deactive transition in the mammalian enzyme. Therefore, our structure considerably progresses understanding of the mechanisms of catalysis and energy transduction in this most complicated of respiratory enzymes.

Focused refinement maps assisted in modelling this 43rd subunit, which is resolved to 3.5–5.7 Å, revealing its weak interactions with the hydrophilic domain through NDUFS3, NDUFA7, NDUFA12 and NDUFA13 (Extended Data Figures 2 and 3). As no differences in any of these contact subunits could be identified between the ST1-containing and ST1-free complexes, both sets of particles were included in the final reconstruction of the 2.7 Å resolution map.

Our 2.7 Å-resolution map enabled identification of ordered water molecules throughout the structure, especially in the ~2.5 Å-resolution core subunits (Figures 2a and 2b). Waters were added to the model using Coot then inspected individually; 275 were retained (from 1674) based on their clear, distinct features (Figure 2c) and meaningful interactions with the nearby model. While the majority are located in local hydrogen-bonding networks in hydrophilic regions of the protein or on the solvent-accessible surface of the membrane domain, 26 are buried in the core of the membrane domain, on the central hydrophilic axis (Figures 2b and 2d). The eight waters modelled previously in the crystal structure of part of the membrane domain of E. coli complex I28 are all conserved here.

The ND1 cavity is contiguous with the ubiquinone channel

We utilised the CASTp cavity-searching tool47 to investigate the extent and connectivity of the ND1 and ubiquinone-binding cavities, which contain both resolved, stably-bound water molecules as well as unresolved map features that we attribute to dynamic solvent waters.

The ubiquinone-binding channel in our Y. lipolytica structure is both connected to the ND1 cavity and open to the matrix (Figure 7a). The same is observed in deactive mouse complex I (Figure 7c), whereas in active mouse (Figure 7d) the two cavities remain connected but are closed to the matrix. The inability of the deactive state to catalyse (except by reactivation) may thus be partly due to the extensive hydration that results from the unfolding of local structural elements.

A clear map feature in the central section of the ubiquinone-binding channel, overlapping the proposed location of isoprenoids 4–8, nicely accommodates a molecule of n-dodecyl β-D-maltoside (DDM), the detergent used for purification (Figure 6c). Three loops, ND1-TMH5–6, ND3-TMH1–2, and NDUFS2-β1–β2, that constitute the ubiquinone-binding site are well established to undergo substantial structural changes when mammalian complex I deactivates10,20,21. Here, we reveal how their movements are connected, and linked to the formation of ND6-π120, opening of a cavity in ND1, and changes to nearby loops in NDUFS717.

The deactive states of complex I are pronounced resting states, produced when unstable components of the catalytically active structure relax following turnover. The relaxation occurs at different rates and to different depths in different species (Figure 7). It occurs more slowly and to greater depth in the mammalian enzyme than in Y. lipolytica, perhaps due to the extra NDUFA10–NDUFA5 interface that must adjust in the mammalian enzyme20. NDUFA10 is absent from Y. lipolytica, and the NDUFA10–NDUFA5 interaction was identified in simulations to have a major impact on the global enzyme dynamics50. Although structural comparisons may be compromised because (unlike the mammalian forms) the deactive Y. lipolytica enzymes studied were not produced by incubation at 37 °C, and because of detergent/ubiquinone bound in some cases, the following structures may be ordered by decreasing activation level (see also Extended Data Table 4): i) active mouse20: ND6-π1 absent, ND1-TMH4 bent and ND1 cavity closed, ND1-TMH5–6 loop ordered, ND3-Cys39(Yl40) bound between ND1-Tyr127(Yl131) and NDUFS2-His55(Yl91); ii) ubiquinone-bound Y. lipolytica
19: ND6-π1 present, ND1-TMH4 straightened encouraging ND1 cavity opening, ND1-TMH5–6 loop shifted slightly, ND3-Cys40(Mm39)-binding pocket destabilised; iii) DDM-bound Y. lipolytica: ND6-π1 present, ND1-TMH4 straightened and ND1 cavity open, ND1-TMH5–6 loop partially disordered, ND3-Cys40(Mm39)-binding pocket lost; iv) deactive mouse20: ND6-π1 present, ND1-TMH4 straightened and ND1 cavity open, ND1-TMH5–6, ND3-TMH1–2 and NDUFS2-β1–β2 loops disordered. Assuming that the active mouse structure exhibits the elements required for catalysis, the series suggests that the α-helical form of ND6 may be the least stable and/or first to be overcome upon the onset of deactivation, accompanied by the start of changes to ND1-TMH4, and followed by increasing conformational changes to the loops of ND1, ND3, NDUFS2 and NDUFS7.

It has been proposed that the conformational changes of the active–deactive transition are also crucial steps in catalysis, and that the deactive state is ‘tightly-linked’ with on-pathway intermediates18,19,36. Movement of the glutamate-rich ND1-TMH5-6 loop during catalysis was proposed first18, based on its location where the E-channel meets the ubiquinone-binding channel. The idea was then supported when both it and the ND3, NDUFS2 and NDUFS7 loops were found in different conformations/disordered in different structures (although, in retrospect, this may result from their different deactivation levels), and when ND6-π1 was detected in the ubiquinone-bound Y. lipolytica enzyme19. Furthermore, the ‘closed’ and ‘open’ states of ovine complex I36, which match our biochemically characterised active and deactive states, were ascribed to on-pathway intermediates. Importantly, the proposed movement of the ND3-TMH1–2 loop has also been probed in functional work using the NDUFS7-Q133C variant in Y. lipolytica, where Cys133 is capable of forming a reversible disulfide bond with ND3-Cys40 (ND3-TMH1–2 loop)16. The disulfide-bonded enzyme exhibited ubiquinone-reduction but not proton-pumping activity, implying that when loop movement is prevented electron transfer cannot be coupled to proton translocation. However, the disulfide bond locks the loop in its deactive conformation (its structure16 matches our deactive structure), not the conformation in active mouse20 and ubiquinone-bound Y. lipolytica
19. Therefore, this result does not demonstrate that the loop moves during coupled catalysis, only that it must be able to escape its deactive conformation. We propose our deactive-like structure represents an off-pathway state because the disordering of key structural elements and substantial hydration we observe is unlikely during turnover of such an efficient and highly coupled enzyme.

The relay pathway identified in NDUFS2 (Figure 3) likely provides protons for ubiquinone reduction without requiring additional water molecules. However, there is increasing evidence that the ubiquinone-binding site is more extensively hydrated than initially expected for such a hydrophobic substrate. Water molecules may be intrinsic to hydrogen-bonding networks in the central, polar region of the site and simulations have begun to reveal how ubiquinone disrupts these networks27 (in particular, interactions between arginines in NDUFS7 and glutamates on the ND1-TMH5–6 loop17). Simulations have also predicted hydration of the core of ND131,42, now supported by our structural data, and the S1QEL family of inhibitors have been suggested to bind in the same cavity51. Notably, however, simulations have focused only on the T. thermophilus model26, in which the arginine-rich NDUFS7 loop is missing and which occupies an intermediate state with ND6-π1 present and ND1-TMH4 straight (deactive-like) but the ND1, ND3 and NDUFS2 loops ordered (activelike); the ND1 cavity is expanded, relative to active mouse, but closed. Simulations to probe hydration of the E-channel residues, ND6-π1 and the ND1 and ubiquinone-binding cavities in active mouse are required. Water molecules in the ND1 cavity may enable proton transfer directly to Glu196(Mm192) and Glu147(Mm143) at the bottom of the E-channel, or protons may traverse the whole E-channel from the ubiquinone-binding site42, and/or NDUFS2-Asp196(Tt139/Mm160) may operate as an electrostatic switch, linking ubiquinone reduction to protonation changes in E-channel residues22. We note that the simulations that support these mechanisms rely on side chain movements, not on substantial movements of the loops themselves.

Once energy transduction reaches the central axis, the ‘wave’ mechanism3 suggests how energy flows along the membrane domain priming each proton-pumping unit, then returns in a ‘backwave’ triggering both proton release and re-uptake. According to this scheme, our Y. lipolytica structure has ND2 primed, but ND4 and ND5 already triggered; ND2 is more highly hydrated and its putative leucine gate is open. The same is true for previous Y. lipolytica structures18,19,35, whereas in T. thermophilus
26 and both the active and deactive mammalian structures20 all three gates are closed. As discussed above for the unique position of ND4-TMH8-Lys235(Yl252) in T. thermophilus, this may be a species-specific perturbation. We suggest these variations result from how the different membrane domains relax when catalysis comes to a halt or during purification, providing important clues about on-pathway states.

In summary, our 2.7 Å-resolution cryo-EM map for Y. lipolytica complex I has enabled us to identify ordered water molecules that connect key residues and structural elements; we propose pathways for proton transfer and provide experimental data against which simulations of the catalytic mechanism27,31,32,42 should be measured. Our work has also provided new perspectives on the active–deactive status of existing structures, and hence on the implications of structural variations in the loops around the ubiquinone-binding site. Although these aspects, plus detergent molecules intercalated into some structures and the absence of lateral membrane pressure in all, complicate detailed interpretations, our ever-increasing wealth of structural data is now forging the way to working out the intricate mechanisms that complex I uses to exploit an energy-rich redox reaction to drive energydemanding proton translocation events.

FPLC was performed using an ÄKTAMicro. The column was washed until a steady baseline was reached, then complex I eluted with the same buffer but also containing 140 mM imidazole. The highest concentration peak fractions were used for cryo-EM grid preparation. Data processingData were processed using the Relion-3.153,54 pipeline as shown in Supplementary Figure 1. Briefly, beam-induced motion correction was performed with MotionCor255 followed by CTF estimation using GCTF56. Manual curation of automated particle picking yielded ~120K particles for 2D and subsequent 3D classification on ~106K particles. The resulting ~50K best particle class was used for 3D auto-refinements, CTF refinements and Bayesian polishing and provided a 2.7 Å map. For the sub-stoichiometric sulfur transferase ST1 subunit, the final ~50K particles were subjected to 3D classification without alignments with a mask over the ST1 region. This resulted in ~25.5K (51%) particles in a class containing ST1 (Supplementary Figure 1). Rounds of focused refinement, CTF refinement and Bayesian polishing were used to generate a 3.7 Å map of the ST1 region of complex I (Supplementary Figure 2). Output maps were subjected to local sharpening using MonoRes57 and LocalDeblur58 in the Scipion package59.

Model building

The model was built by rigid-body fitting homologous subunits from the active mouse complex I structure (PDB_6G2J)20 into the map obtained here, using UCSF Chimera 1.13.160, then mutating them to match the sequence of Y. lipolytica using Coot61, followed by subsequent remodelling. Bulky residues (tryptophan, tyrosine and phenylalanine) were fitted to their corresponding map features to enable the register of the subunit sequence to be identified. At the time of model building, the identity of NIGM/NDUFB2 was unknown. It was determined independently of the work of Parey and coworkers19 by introducing a poly-Ala helix into the unmodelled helical map feature using Coot 0.8.9.261. Then, characteristic residue-specific map features were used to guide construction of a partial helical sequence and a local alignment search was carried out using NCBI protein BLAST, restricting the organism to Y. lipolytica. The search returned a single hit (accession number SEI35205) that was searched against peptide fragments obtained from mass spectrometry, confirming the presence of the BLAST result sequence. MitoFates64, an online tool for estimating mitochondrial targeting sequences, predicted the sequence to have an MTS and proposed the cleavage position. As an independent control, a poly-Ala helix was again placed in the unmodelled helical feature but this time the sequence_from_map option in PHENIX 1.1665 was used to generate a sequence. Again, this was used as a search template in NCBI protein BLAST66 and, again, only sequence homology to the same sequence (SEI35205) was identified. The map of the modelled NIGM subunit accommodates the sequence of SEI35205 well and does not include the predicted MTS.

Water molecules were introduced using the Find Waters feature in Coot61, and then manually inspected to retain molecules that have clear map features and reasonable hydrogen-bonding interactions in well-resolved regions of the map. Lipid and detergent molecules were added using Coot where map features indicated they are present, and had their acyl tails clipped where the definition was lost, due to their flexible nature. Bulky aromatic residues at the headgroup of the lipid favoured the placement of phosphatidylcholine over phosphatidylethanolamine.

Model statistics were improved with iterations of real-space refinement using PHENIX 1.1665 and local remodelling with Coot 0.8.9.2 and 0.961, as well as with ISOLDE67, which includes molecular dynamics simulations during the remodelling to improve local regions of the model without creating strain elsewhere. Figures and Extended Data movies were generated using UCSF ChimeraX68.

The ST1 subunit was built independently from the rest of the complex using its focused refinement map. The rest of this map (for the main complex) was not used for building because the flexible interface between ST1 and the rest of the complex results in poor alignment outside of the ST1 mask, and therefore it is of low resolution. The initial model was obtained using SWISS–MODEL69 to generate a homologous model with the ST1 sequence threaded onto the homologous 3D structure (PDB_1RHD)70. Local remodelling using Coot and ISOLDE was performed in conjunction with iterations of real-space refinement with PHENIX 1.16. The ST1 model was docked onto the rest of the complex I model into the 3.7 Å map (masked around ST1) for real-space refinement in PHENIX 1.16. Models were inspected for overfitting (Extended Data Figure 2 and 3)71,72.

Classification and refinement of the cryo-EM maps for Y. lipolytica complex I.

The workflow, implemented in Relion 3.11,2, was used to obtain the map of the whole complex at 2.7 Å resolution, as well as a map from focused refinement of the sub-stoichiometric ST1 subunit (observed in 51% of the refined particles).

Resolution estimates of the cryo-EM maps for Y. lipolytica complex I.

a) the global map of Y. lipolytica complex I; b) the map from focused refinement of the sub-stoichiometric ST1 subunit. The masks used are indicated by outlines and local resolutions were estimated using Relion. The resolution estimates from the masked Fourier shell correlation curves, FSCfinal, are 2.7 and 3.5 Å for the global and ST1 maps at FSC = 0.143 (dotted lines) and 3.2 and 4.3 Å at FSC = 0.5 (solid lines). Model–map FSC curves (FSCmodel) are in magenta. Overfitting analyses3,4 were performed by refining the model with the atoms displaced by 0.5 Å against one half map, followed by FSC map–model validation (FSCwork). The output was used with the second half map for FSC map–model validation (FSCtest). For the global model the curves match well beyond the 2.7 Å data used for refinement. The ST1 model, which was generated from a homology model, shows evidence of overfitting. Residue side chains were removed in low resolution regions to reduce the overfitting, while the overall secondary structures and architecture of the homology model were retained.
