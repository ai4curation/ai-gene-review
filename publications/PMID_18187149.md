Mitochondria cannot be made de novo. Mitochondrial biogenesis requires that up
to 1000 proteins are imported into mitochondria, and the protein import pathway
relies on hetero-oligomeric translocase complexes in both the inner and outer
mitochondrial membranes. The translocase in the outer membrane, the TOM complex,
is composed of a core complex formed from the beta-barrel channel Tom40 and
additional subunits each with single, alpha-helical transmembrane segments. How
alpha-helical transmembrane segments might be assembled onto a transmembrane
beta-barrel in the context of a membrane environment is a question of
fundamental importance. The master receptor subunit of the TOM complex, Tom20,
recognizes the targeting sequence on incoming mitochondrial precursor proteins,
binds these protein ligands, and then transfers them to the core complex for
translocation across the outer membrane. Here we show that the transmembrane
segment of Tom20 contains critical residues essential for docking the Tom20
receptor into its correct environment within the TOM complex. This crucial
docking reaction is catalyzed by the unique assembly factor Mim1/Tom13.
Mutations in the transmembrane segment that destabilize Tom20, or deletion of
Mim1, prevent Tom20 from functioning as a receptor for protein import into
mitochondria.

Holland Keywords: mitochondria; protein import; protein targeting sequences; import receptors; transmembrane segment Introduction Mitochondria are essential organelles characteristic of eukaryotic cells. Derived from intracellular bacteria, mitochondria retain many bacterial characteristics including the presence of β-barrel proteins in the outer membrane. Since most of the mitochondrial proteins are now encoded on genes in the cell nucleus, mitochondrial proteins have to be imported from the cytosol across the mitochondrial membranes. Multisubunit protein translocases situated in the inner and outer mitochondrial membranes have evolved to perform this critical operation.
1–4 The translocase in the outer membrane of mitochondria, the TOM complex, is composed of a core of five subunits. The channel subunit, Tom40, has a β-barrel topology, while each of its partners has a single α-helical transmembrane segment. This combination is unique, a structure not yet observed for any membrane protein complex. While β-barrel proteins are common in the outer membranes of bacteria, α-helical transmembrane proteins are restricted instead to the bacterial inner membrane.
How an α-helical transmembrane segment can be packed against a β-barrel in the context of a lipid bilayer remains unknown and cannot be interpreted from the 100 or so high-resolution structures of membrane proteins currently known, all of which have either β-barrel or α-helical transmembrane domains.

Treatment of the TOM complex with urea has shown the core complex to be extremely stable: even 5 M urea is insufficient to disrupt the interaction between Tom5, Tom6, Tom7, Tom22 and the Tom40 β-barrel.
12 Tom5, Tom6, Tom7 and Tom22 each have a conserved proline residue in their transmembrane segments, as well as a preponderance of glycine and hydroxylated residues: these residues are uncommon in transmembrane segments and their conservation in these small TOM proteins suggests they mediate protein–protein interactions.
13,14 The bends and curves in transmembrane helices that can be facilitated by proline and glycine residues are becoming increasingly common as means to add functional complexity to membrane protein structures, 5 and a complicated architecture of protein – protein interactions is anticipated for the core TOM complex.
Tom20 is found in all species of animal and fungi.
15 It is removed from the core TOM complex more readily than Tom22 and the small TOM proteins, 12 and this and other evidence16–18 has been interpreted to suggest Tom20 docks only transiently and reversibly to the TOM complex, only to deliver substrate proteins. An alternative is that Tom20 is a constant component of the TOM complex, but that its interaction is more tenuous. The transmembrane segment of Tom20 contains a motif of residues, 15 and the conserved series of aromatic and small side-chain residues suggests a classic knobs-and-holes 19,20 method of packing the α-helical transmembrane segment of Tom20 against the surface of the TOM complex.
Here we have established assays to measure docking of Tom20 to the TOM complex to address three questions: Is the transmembrane segment of Tom20 critical for its function as a protein import receptor? Is the Tom20 subunit in a restrained location within the TOM complex, with the conserved residues packed in the vicinity of defined residues on other proteins? Is the SAM complex, or some other component of the outer membrane, responsible for catalyzing docking of Tom20 into the mature TOM complex? We suggest that Tom20 is a stable component of the TOM complex and that the transmembrane domain of Tom20 is specifically packed in an environment where it contacts other components of the TOM complex.

Discussion TOM20 and MIM1 are not essential for viability In a genome-wide survey of yeast strains derived from the type strain S288c two of the genes that were classified as essential for cell viability were YGR082W and YOL026C, and these genes encode Tom20 and Mim1, respectively.
38,39 Strains derived from the S. cerevisiae type strain S288c are known to have weak mitochondrial function 40,41 and only in these S288c-derived strains do the TOM20 gene and MIM1 gene appear essential; in most yeast strains, Tom20 loss-of-function mutants have defects in mitochondrial protein import and slow growth on some carbon sources, but remain viable, 29,42 and we find this correlation to hold true also of Δmim1 cells.
In another genome-wide study, Mnaimneh et al .35 integrated TetR-repressible promoters in front of 602 essential genes in S288c-derived strains and identified MIM1 as being required for mitochondrial import, because down regulation of MIM1 expression caused lethality and an accumulation of the precursor form of the mitochondrial protein F 1β.35 We suggest the protein import defects seen in the mim1 mutant yeast are a secondary consequence of defective assembly of the Tom20 receptor.

By starting with a yeast strain not subject to intragenic suppression, we have shown here that the transmembrane segment of Tom20 is required for receptor function and that conserved residues in the transmembrane segment are required for appropriate docking of Tom20 into the TOM complex. This primary structure motif is conserved, in reverse, in the transmembrane segment of the Tom20 receptor found in plants, 45 suggesting that the motif mirrors a common feature on an outer aspect of the core TOM complex that is conserved in fungi, animals and plants.
Tom20 and Tom22 function together to bind substrate preproteins Several independent observations suggest the function of Tom20 is intimately linked to that of Tom22: (i) precursor proteins experimentally stalled on the mitochondrial surface can be chemically cross-linked to both Tom20 and Tom22, (ii) antibodies against either Tom20 or Tom22 effectively block precursor protein binding to the mitochondrial surface, (iii) a case of “domain stealing ” between Tom20 and Tom22 has been demonstrated in Saccharomyces castellii and (iv) deletion of the gene encoding Tom20 causes a concomitant decrease in the stability of Tom22.
43,46–49 These various observations are consistent with the two proteins acting together as a single, composite receptor. However, biochemical characterization of the Tom20 –Tom22 partnership has not been possible, given the difficulties in stabilizing the putative receptor after solubilization of mitochondrial membranes. Our results show that Tom20 sits in a defined environment within the TOM complex; simply being present in the outer membrane is not enough for Tom20 to function, since transmembrane segment mutants, the Tom70Tom20 construct and even Tom20 itself in mim1 mutant cells demonstrate poor, if any, Tom20 function. In support of previous work on the co-dependence of Tom22 and Tom20 function, our data suggest assembly of Tom20 depends on a specific docking of its transmembrane segment in order for Tom20 to contact Tom22.
Mim1 catalyzes an interaction between the transmembrane domain of Tom20 and the core TOM complex Both mim1 mutants and mdm10 mutants have been reported to show defects in the assembly of the TOM complex, 36,37,50,51 but their precise function in the assembly process has not been clear. Here we define the specific function of Mim1: a unique protein, catalyzing a docking reaction for an α-helical transmembrane segment onto a membrane protein complex formed around a β-barrel. Mim1 is the first factor ever described with such an activity. During the review of this manuscript, an additional paper appeared detailing the role of Mim1 in the assembly of α-helical proteins into the TOM complex.
52 How does Mim1 catalyze a docking reaction of Tom20 onto preformed core TOM complexes? The transmembrane domain of Mim1 is highly conserved in primary structure in diverse groups of fungi and has overall analogy with the transmembrane segment of Tom22. We propose a mechanism for the Mim1-mediated docking of α-helical subunits into the TOM complex, based on the pseudosubstrate concept that explained the selective, reversible binding of substrates to protein kinases.
53 Many protein kinases initially bind a pseudosubstrate, which can subsequently be displaced by a genuine substrate. This prevents promiscuous interactions and effectively catalyzes productive interactions. In the case of Tom20, the transmembrane segment might be initially held and shaped by the unusual transmembrane segment of the pseudosubstrate Mim1. The surface of the TOM complex, displaying the transmembrane segment of Tom22, provides a site to which Tom20 binds with higher affinity, and the Mim1 transmembrane segment is thereby displaced. That Tom20 docks to a specific site in the TOM complex explains the cross-linking pattern seen both in situ and in vitro , and the dependency on Mim1 for docking Tom20 into the TOM complex, to potentiate its function as a receptor for protein import into mitochondria.
Mim1 might also assist binding of Tom5, Tom6, Tom7 and Tom70 by similar mechanism, as these subunits also depend on Tom22 for stable interaction in the mature TOM complex.

Plasmid construction Point mutations in Tom20 were created by overlap PCR as described by Ho et al.56 Oligonucleotides containing the desired base modifications to encode a different amino acid were constructed in the sense and antisense direction to produce two fragments of the TOM20 open reading frame that overlapped. The two overlapping fragments containing the base modification were used as the template for amplification of the full-length modified open reading frame. An Xba1 site was incorporated into the 5′ oligonucleotide and a HindIII site was incorporated into the 3 ′ oligonucleotide for cloning between either the AvrII and HindIII or the XbaI and HindIII sites of pYX243 and pSP65 plasmids, respectively. All plasmids were sequenced to confirm the fidelity of the PCR and the identity of the mutant constructs.
Isolation of mitochondria and measurement of Tom20 association Mitochondria were isolated from yeast as described by Daum et al.
57 Plasmid (pSP65) constructs were used for in vitro transcription using SP6 RNA polymerase (Promega).
RNA was translated in vitro using nuclease-treated rabbit reticulocyte lysate (Promega) in the presence of [ 35S]labeled methionine (MP Biomedicals). Translated [ 35S]labeled protein was incubated with isolated mitochondria for 30 min at 25 °C in import buffer [0.6 M sorbitol, 50 mM K+-Hepes (pH 7.4), 2 mM KH 2PO4 pH 7.4, 25 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl 2, 0.5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1 mM dithiothreitol]. Chemical cross-linking Cross-linking experiments were conducted using mitochondria isolated from yeast strains expressing Tom20 mutants in vivo and using in vitro translated [ 35S]-labeled Tom20 that had been imported into mitochondria isolated from wild-type (W303) yeast. Mitochondria were washed in cross-linking buffer (0.6 M sorbitol, 50 mM Hepes, pH 7.2, 2 mM KH 2PO4, pH 7.4, 25 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl 2, 0.5 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) and equilibrated in 100 μL fresh cross-linking buffer at 25 °C for 5 min. BMH (Pierce) freshly dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide was added to a final concentration of 1 mM. In control reactions the equivalent amount of dimethyl sulfoxide was added minus BMH. Samples were kept at 25 °C for 30 min, then on ice for a further 90 min. Mitochondria were collected by centrifugation and boiled in sample loading buffer containing dithiothreitol then subjected to SDS- PAGE. Tom20 and Tom20 conjugates were visualized by Western blotting using an antiserum raised against Tom20 or by phosphorimage analysis of the [ 35S]-labeled proteins. In all cases the experiments were run on a single gel; slight differences in mobility are seen for some of the Tom20 mutant forms.
