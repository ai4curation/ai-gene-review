## Full Text

Abstract

Centromeric chromatin plays a crucial role in kinetochore assembly and chromosome segregation. Centromeres are specified through the loading of the histone H3 variant CENP-A by the conserved chaperone Scm3/HJURP. The N-terminus of Scm3/HJURP interacts with CENP-A, while the C-terminus facilitates centromere localization by interacting with the Mis18 holocomplex via a small domain, called the Mis16-binding domain (Mis16-BD) in fission yeast. Fungal Scm3 proteins contain an additional conserved cysteine-rich domain (CYS) of unknown function. Here, we find that CYS binds zinc in vitro and is essential for the localization and function of fission yeast Scm3. Disrupting CYS by deletion or introduction of point mutations within its zinc-binding motif prevents Scm3 centromere localization and compromises kinetochore integrity. Interestingly, CYS alone can localize to the centromere, albeit weakly, but its targeting is greatly enhanced when combined with Mis16-BD. Expressing a truncated protein containing both Mis16-BD and CYS, but lacking the CENP-A binding domain, causes toxicity and is accompanied by considerable chromosome missegregation and kinetochore loss. These effects can be mitigated by mutating the CYS zinc-binding motif. Collectively, our findings establish the essential role of the cysteine-rich domain in fungal Scm3 proteins and provide valuable insights into the mechanism of Scm3 centromere targeting.

Introduction

Centromeres are the regions of chromosomes that direct and ensure proper segregation of the genome during cell division. Defective centromere function can lead to aneuploidy, defined by the loss or gain of chromosomes. Aneuploidy is the most common form of chromosome abnormality in humans linked to cancer and birth defects. Point centromeres, found in budding yeasts, consist of specific ∼125-bp centromeric DNA. Regional centromeres are found in organisms ranging from the fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe to humans and are composed of several kilobases to megabases of repetitive arrays ( 1 , 2 ). Paradoxically, rather than strictly relying on DNA sequence, the identity of regional centromeres is epigenetically defined by a unique histone H3 variant CENP-A (also known as CenH3). Their epigenetic nature allows centromeres to be activated in noncentromeric regions (i.e. neocentromeres), inactivated on dicentric chromosomes (e.g. when two chromosomes are fused) or repositioned to other locations on the chromosome ( 3–5 ).

Schizosaccharomyces pombe is a genetically amenable model system widely used to study regional centromeres and chromosome segregation ( 6 , 7 ). Fission yeast centromeres consist of two distinct domains: (i) a central core domain where CENP-A replaces the canonical histone H3, enabling kinetochore assembly, and (ii) heterochromatic outer repeats containing hypoacetylated nucleosomes methylated on lysine 9 of histone H3 ( 8 ). Similar to canonical histones, the deposition of CENP-A into chromatin requires a dedicated chaperone. In vertebrates, this function is performed by HJURP (Holliday junction recognition protein) ( 9 ), which associates with CENP-A in a prenucleosomal form via a conserved domain at the N-terminal region ( 10–12 ). Similarly, the HJURP orthologs in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and S. pombe , named Scm3, also play an essential role in centromere loading of CENP-A via this conserved N-terminal domain ( 13–18 ). In budding yeast, a unique hexameric nucleosome Scm3/CENP-A Cse4 /H4, which lacks H2A/H2B, has been proposed to exist ( 14 ). In fission yeast, Scm3 mediates the assembly of CENP-A chromatin without being a nucleosome component, although it dissociates from centromeres only during a small fraction of the cell cycle (from mitotic onset until late anaphase) ( 16 , 18 ). In any case, loss of Scm3 is deleterious as it disrupts CENP-A chromatin, leading to defective kinetochore assembly, aberrant chromosome segregation and ultimately loss of cell viability.

In both vertebrates and fission yeast, the centromere targeting of Scm3/HJURP depends on the Mis18 holocomplex, but not vice versa ( 16 , 18–20 ). The recruitment of HJURP to human centromeres requires a direct interaction between the Mis18α and Mis18β subunits of the Mis18 holocomplex and the HJURP centromere targeting domain ( 21–23 ). The S. pombe Mis18 holocomplex consists of Mis18, which shares high sequence similarity with human Mis18α/β, as well as the subunits Mis16—a homolog of the WD40-repeat proteins RbAp46/RBBP7 and RbAp48/RBBP4—and Mis19/Eic1 ( 24–26 ). In this case, targeting Scm3 to centromeres requires an interaction between Mis16 and the C-terminus of Scm3 ( 18 , 27 , 28 ). Specifically, residues ∼270–305 of Scm3 were identified as the minimum region required for Mis16 binding in vitro ( 28 ). The importance of this Mis16-binding domain (hereafter referred to as Mis16-BD) is consistent with the finding that the S281L mutation ( sim1-15 ) impairs Scm3 function ( 16 ).

Although conserved, fungal Scm3 proteins vary greatly in size, ranging from ∼200 aa in S. cerevisiae to ∼1300 aa in Neurospora crassa . The conserved N-terminal region of fungal Scm3 orthologs contains a single globular domain that is responsible for the specific interaction with CENP-A ( 14 , 29 ). The C-terminal region of Scm3 proteins harbors various types of motifs, including DNA-binding MYB and C2H2 zinc-finger domains, AT-hooks and cysteine-rich regions predicted to have metal-chelating properties with the sequence signature [CH]x4–6Cx4Cx2C. Remarkably, these motifs are not found in vertebrate HJURP proteins and are specific to certain lineages. For instance, they are absent from the smaller Scm3 proteins found in yeasts with point centromeres, which possess the distinctive centromere-specific DNA-binding protein Ndc10 ( 29 ). It is possible that the motifs present in larger Scm3 proteins have coevolved in response to the diversity of centromeric DNA and may therefore be required for centromere targeting. However, the role of these domains in Scm3 localization and function has not yet been explored.

In this study, we investigated the role of a small cysteine-rich domain, referred to as CYS, located in the C-terminus of the Scm3 protein in S. pombe . Through in silico , in vitro , genetic and genomic approaches, we identified a requirement for CYS in Scm3 centromere targeting, alongside the previously described Mis16-BD. We also find that CYS is capable of binding zinc. Specifically, we show that deleting CYS or mutating the zinc-binding motif prevents Scm3 from localizing to the centromere and compromises kinetochore integrity. Overall, our findings highlight the essential nature of the cysteine-rich domain in fungal Scm3 proteins and contribute to our understanding of the molecular mechanisms involved in the localization of Scm3/HJURP to centromeres.

Materials and methods

Fission yeast strains and methods

Standard procedures were used for fission yeast cell culture and genetic manipulations ( 30 ). Schizosaccharomyces pombe strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1 . The following mutant alleles and reporters were previously described: tetO at cen2 and tetR-tdTomato adjacent to zfs1 ( 31 ), scm3-19 ( 18 ), scm3-139 ( 16 ), mis16-53 ( 32 ), cnp3-tdTomato and sim4-tdTomato ( 33 ), mEOS2-cnp1 ( 34 ) and minichromosome pNBg ( 35 ). To create an scm3Δ strain, a standard auxotrophic strain SP44 was transformed with a ura4 + - marked plasmid pREP82- scm3 + resulting in SPDF1978. Subsequently, SPDF1978 was transformed with a NAT cassette replacing the entire scm3 ORF to generate ura + NAT R strains (e.g. SPDF2014) that were employed in plasmid-shuffling assays.

Plasmids

The following plasmids were previously described: pREP81 and pREP82X ( 36 ), pDUAL ( 37 ), pREP41-HA-N and pREP41-GFP-N ( 38 ). Plasmids carrying scm3 + gene and derivatives were generated as follows: constructs were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and subcloned into pREP41-HA-N and pREP41-GFP-N, accordingly. All DNA sequences were confirmed by Sanger sequencing at the NCI CCR Genomics Core Facility. Then, fragments containing the nmt41 promoter plus 3HA or GFP followed by scm3 constructs were subsequently cloned into pDUAL vector devoid of nmt1 promoter. The DNA sequence encoding Scm3-CYS (34 aa of the Scm3 C-terminus) was fused to GST in the Escherichia coli expression vector pGEX-4T1. A 9878-bp SphI fragment from the central core domain of centromere 1 (chromosome I: 3766198–3776075) was cloned into vector pBR322 to generate pBR322- cen1 .

Affinity chromatography to identify centromere DNA sequences that bind to Scm3-CYS

pBR322- cen1 plasmid DNA was digested with the restriction enzyme ApoI, which generates centromeric fragments ranging from 6 to 710 bp. The digested DNA was then incubated with purified recombinant GST or GST-Scm3-CYS fusion proteins that were bound to glutathione Sepharose beads. The ApoI fragments retained on the beads after incubation were analyzed using native agarose gels stained with SYBR Green I (Invitrogen). Subsequently, these ApoI fragments were subcloned into the EcoRI site of plasmid vector pBluescript and confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Nuclear magnetic resonance

The nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) samples were prepared in a 10 mM MES and 50 mM NaCl (pH 6.3) buffer. All 1 H– 15 N heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) spectra were acquired on a Bruker 700 MHz NMR spectrometer equipped with cryoprobe and processed with NMRPipe ( 39 ).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments were performed as previously described ( 40 ). Cells were grown overnight at 30°C until OD 600 0.5 in media containing thiamine [PMG 5S for ChIP-qPCR (ChIP coupled with quantitative PCR) and YEA for ChIP-seq (ChIP followed by sequencing)] and cross-linked using 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min. Cells were spun at 2000 × g for 10 min and pellets were lysed using glass beads and lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES/KOH, 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% DOC plus protease inhibitors). Cell lysates were sheared using a Bioruptor-300 (Diagenode) to an approximate size of 300–600 bp. Immunoprecipitation of the protein of interest was accomplished using 5 μg of anti-GFP antibody (Abcam, ab290). Protein A magnetic beads (NEB) were used to capture the GFP protein–antibody complexes. Beads were washed twice with lysis buffer, twice with lysis buffer containing 0.5 M NaCl and once in TE buffer (pH 8). Chromatin–antibody complex was eluted using TES buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS) and along with whole-cell extract input was de-cross-linked by heating to 65°C overnight and purified using Wizard SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up columns (Promega). Oligonucleotides used in ChIP-qPCR for central core ( cc1&3 , cc2:ura4 + ), heterochromatic dg and euchromatin controls fbp1 and vps33 were previously listed ( 41 ). For ChIP-seq, immunoprecipitated and input DNA were assessed using iTaqPCR SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina according to the manufacturer’s protocol and analyzed using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation system (Agilent). Sequencing was performed on the NextSeq500 platform (Illumina). Data processing was performed on the NIH HPC Biowulf Cluster ( http://hpc.nih.gov ). Single-ended fastq format sequences were quality-trimmed using fastp ( 42 ) and aligned to the S. pombe reference ( 43 ) using the Star aligner ( 44 ). Bedgraphs normalized to reads per million mapped reads (RPM) were generated using the Star ‘–outWigNorm’ option. RPM normalized bedgraphs for Ips were converted to wigs, further normalized by subtraction of the input DNA signal using Deeptools ( 45 ) and saved in bedgraph format. Signal values that were negative after input subtraction were set to zero in the final bedgraph.

Western blotting

Strains carrying plasmids expressing 3HA- or GFP-tagged Scm3 FL and Scm3 ΔCYS were grown on PMG −LEU media. The sample preparation for western blotting was performed using an alkaline protein extraction method. Anti-GFP (Roche) and anti-HA (16B12, BioLegend) antibodies were used.

Live-cell imaging

Cells were grown overnight at 30°C, unless otherwise indicated, until reaching logarithmic phase in minimal medium PMG 5S with or without thiamine. They were then mounted in 2% agarose as described ( 46 ). Images were acquired on a DeltaVision Elite microscope (Leica) with a 100×/1.35 NA oil lens (Olympus). For mEOS2 fluorescence, fifteen 0.40-μm z -sections were acquired, while twenty 0.35-μm z -sections were acquired for GFP and tdTomato fluorescence. Maximum intensity projections were generated in all cases. For mEOS2 photoconversion, two 2-s pulses of ultraviolet light were used (peak 390 nm; bandwidth 18 nm; ND filter 50%). Image processing, including background-subtracted centromere foci intensity measurements, was performed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health). Unless indicated, images were not subject to deconvolution.

Multiple sequence alignments and pattern searches

Multiple sequence alignments were constructed using MACAW, the Multiple Alignment Construction and Analysis Workbench ( 47 ). FASTA-formatted protein sequences from Protein Data Bank ( 48 ) structures as provided by National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) ( 49 ) and maintained on the NIH HPC Biowulf Cluster ( http://hpc.nih.gov ) were downloaded during the week of 2/6/2023 and subsequently scanned on the Biowulf Cluster for Mis16-BD and CYS motifs using PatScan ( 50 ).

Results

Scm3 harbors a cysteine-rich domain capable of binding zinc

Alignments of Scm3 proteins within the Schizosaccharomycetes class show conservation within all three previously described regions: SCM3, Mis16-BD and CYS domains. These domains are located at residues 38–88, 266–287 and 316–330 of the S. pombe protein, respectively ( Supplementary Figures S1 and S2A ). While the functions of SCM3 and Mis16-BD have been explored, the role of CYS has yet to be investigated.

Within the Schizosaccharomycetes class, CYS has a sequence signature of Hx4Cx3FCFxC. To gain more insight into the function of this domain, we searched for structurally characterized motifs in sequences from the Protein Data Bank ( 48 ) that matched a more generalized form of this signature, [HC]x4–6[HC]x3[YF][HC][YF]x[HC]. Using the generalized signature, a PatScan ( 50 ) search detected two such motifs in the conserved human protein PHF6 (PHD finger protein 6) and one in the human DNMT3L (DNA methyltransferase 3 like) ( 51 , 52 ) ( Supplementary Figure S2B ). Notably, crystal structure analyses of PHF6 indicate that each of the motifs (residues 94–108 and 292–306) is associated with a pair of Zn 2+ ions, which stabilize the two plant homeodomain folds of the protein ( 51 ). Similar structural analyses for the DNMT3L protein indicate that the identified motif (residues 108–122) is associated with Zn 2+ , this time within a FYVE domain ( 52 ). In both cases, however, the amino acids of CYS adopt a similar conformation, consisting of a central four amino acid extended structure connecting two loop-like folds in contact with separate Zn 2+ ions. These results strongly suggest that the fission yeast CYS is capable of binding zinc.

To test the binding of CYS to Zn 2+ , we performed NMR analysis on a 34-aa peptide (Scm3 residues 303–336) that contains CYS but lacks Mis16-BD. The 1 H– 15 N HSQC spectrum of the free Scm3 303–336 peptide showed ∼30 cross-peaks, indicating detectable signals from most of the residues ( Supplementary Figure S2C , left). Remarkably, upon the addition of Zn 2+ to the sample, the dynamics of the 1 H– 15 N HSQC spectrum changed dramatically ( Supplementary Figure S2C , center). The resonance of most of the residues disappeared or shifted. However, upon the removal of Zn 2+ using EDTA, the spectrum reverted to that of the free peptide ( Supplementary Figure S2C , right) indicating that, as predicted, CYS can bind Zn 2+ . Supporting this, nine residues located N-terminally to the His/Cys residues (Gly 309 and adjacent residues) showed minimal or no chemical shift changes upon Zn 2+ addition. Similarly, the group of Gln and Asn residues, identified by their amide side chain, exhibited only one chemical shift change, likely caused by Gln 328 ( Supplementary Figure S2C , compare left and center panels). Both in silico and in vitro approaches indicate that CYS is capable of binding zinc.

CYS is essential for the viability of fission yeast

To gain insight into the role of CYS in vivo , we tested whether disrupting CYS (Scm3 ΔCYS ; Figure 1A ) would affect its ability to rescue the inviability of scm3Δ cells. We conducted plasmid-shuffling assays, initially keeping scm3Δ cells viable by providing a copy of scm3 + on a plasmid with the ura4 + selection marker. We then transformed the cells with a plasmid carrying scm3 ΔCYS and the LEU2 selection marker, followed by growth on medium containing 5-fluoroorotic acid (FOA) to select for loss of the ura4 + -marked scm3 + plasmid. The ability of the scm3 ΔCYS construct to support viability is indicated by the growth of cells on medium containing FOA (Figure 1B ). Both scm3 ΔCYS and the full-length control scm3 FL constructs contained the coding sequence for GFP or 3HA inserted after the start codon and were expressed from the nmt41 thiamine-repressible promoter. The strains expressing Scm3 ΔCYS were unable to grow on FOA medium, indicating that CYS is required for the full function of the Scm3 protein (Figure 1C ). Importantly, the failure of the Scm3 ΔCYS protein to support viability was not due to low expression, as western blot analysis showed similar levels of expression for wild-type and mutant proteins ( Supplementary Figure S3A ). Consistent with these findings, similar results were obtained when we tested the ability of single-copy integrations of the constructs to support growth on FOA ( Supplementary Figure S3B ). To rule out any interference from the tag, we also expressed untagged scm3 ΔCYS and the control scm3 FL genes using an inducible nmt81 promoter. Unlike scm3 FL , scm3 ΔCYS was unable to confer viability to scm3Δ cells (Figure 1D ).

We then asked whether scm3 ΔCYS could restore normal growth in cells carrying the temperature-sensitive scm3-139 mutant allele ( 16 ). This mutant allele has a mutation in the CENP-A binding region. Whereas scm3 FL successfully rescued the growth defect, scm3 ΔCYS did not show any noticeable rescue of the temperature-sensitive growth phenotype (Figure 1E ). Therefore, CYS is essential for the viability of S. pombe .

CYS is required for Scm3 centromere localization

The observed phenotype of Scm3 mutants may be caused by defective localization to centromeres. To test this, we generated scm3 + strains that expressed GFP-tagged scm3 transgenes from the thiamine-repressible nmt41 promoter (Figure 1A ). To achieve a level of transgene expression comparable to the endogenous level, we used live-cell imaging to approximate the expression levels under our experimental conditions. We compared the fluorescence signal from GFP-tagged endogenous scm3 + and scm3 FL transgenes, which were expressed from a single-copy integration or from multicopy episomal plasmids, in cells grown in medium with thiamine (i.e. promoter repressed) or without thiamine (i.e. promoter induced) ( Supplementary Figure S4A ). Significantly, the single-copy transgenes in cells grown under the thiamine-repressible condition closely resembled the level of endogenous Scm3 expression ( Supplementary Figure S4B ), and we used this condition for all subsequent experiments.

We then assessed the impact on the chromatin association of the Scm3 ΔCYS mutant protein. ChIP-seq analysis revealed considerable enrichment of GFP-Scm3 FL at the central core domain of centromeres, a well-established region where CENP-A and kinetochore proteins bind (Figure 2A ), consistent with previous findings ( 53 ). No Scm3 enrichment was observed at the heterochromatic repeat elements surrounding the central core domains or other genome regions ( Supplementary Figure S5 ). In contrast, GFP-Scm3 ΔCYS showed no detectable enrichment at centromeres (Figure 2A ). To further confirm this result, we performed ChIP-qPCR analysis, which also demonstrated the absence of GFP-Scm3 ΔCYS at the centromere region, while GFP-Scm3 FL specifically localized to the central core domain ( cc1&3 , cc2:ura4 + ) but not to heterochromatic outer repeats ( dg ) of centromeres or euchromatic locations ( vps33 ) (Figure 2B ).

Figure 2.

To further investigate the role of CYS in Scm3 localization, we performed live-cell imaging of strains harboring a tetO array inserted at the central core domain of centromere 2 ( Note : A TetR-tdTomato fusion is expressed to decorate the tetO array, and this locus is referred to as cen2-tetO-tdTomato ). We observed a single GFP-Scm3 FL focus per cell ( 16 , 18 ) that colocalized with cen2-tetO-tdTomato . However, no detectable signal was observed for GFP-Scm3 ΔCYS (Figure 2C ).

To rule out the possibility that defective centromere localization of GFP-Scm3 ΔCYS was due to low expression, we overexpressed GFP-Scm3 ΔCYS (i.e. no thiamine). We observed nuclear localization; however, no GFP-Scm3 ΔCYS signal could be observed at cen2 . In contrast, overexpression of GFP-Scm3 FL resulted in a discernible focus that colocalized with cen2-tetO-tdTomato and showed nuclear localization ( Supplementary Figure S6A ). ChIP analyses confirmed the inability of GFP-Scm3 ΔCYS to localize to centromeres ( Supplementary Figure S6B ). Together, these results demonstrate that CYS is essential for Scm3 localization to the centromere, which could explain the requirement for this domain to support cell viability.

Mutations in the CYS zinc-binding motif impair Scm3 centromere localization

Having established the essential requirement for CYS, we wondered whether the zinc-binding motif found in our in silico and in vitro analyses is necessary for the localization and function of Scm3 ( Supplementary Figure S2 ). To address this, we substituted four conserved residues (three cysteines and one histidine), which are predicted to bind zinc in full-length Scm3, with alanine (Scm3 4A ; Figure 1A ). Live-cell imaging of GFP-Scm3 4A revealed the absence of a detectable centromere focus (Figure 2C ). Consistent with this finding, ChIP-qPCR analysis showed no enrichment of GFP-Scm3 4A at the central core of centromeres (Figure 3A ). Similar to Scm3 ΔCYS , Scm3 4A was unable to rescue the temperature sensitivity of the scm3-19 mutant ( 18 ) when expressed at levels comparable to endogenous Scm3 (Figure 3B ). However, overexpression of Scm3 4A resulted in a noticeable rescue of scm3-19 and scm3Δ cells (Figure 3B and C). Overexpression of Scm3 4A also produced detectable single centromeric foci that colocalized with cen2-tetO-tdTomato , although they were significantly dimmer than those observed in control cells expressing Scm3 FL (Figure 3D and Supplementary Figure S7A ). Thus, unlike the result from deleting CYS, mutating the zinc-binding motif decreased, but did not completely abolish centromere localization. Moreover, although Scm3 4A overexpression rescued the growth defect of scm3Δ or scm3-19 cells, it failed to confer cell viability under endogenous expression levels (+Thiamine) (Figure 3E and Supplementary Figure S7B ). Thus, mutating the zinc-binding motif impairs the ability of Scm3 to localize to centromeres. However, the expression of the mutant protein at higher levels can compensate for the partial loss of function of the Scm3 4A protein. Together, these results underscore a requirement for the zinc-binding motif of CYS for efficient Scm3 localization to centromeres.

Mutations in the CYS zinc-binding motif impair centromere/kinetochore function

Next, we performed a phenotypic analysis of viable scm3Δ cells that were rescued by Scm3 4A overexpression. Serial dilution assays showed no detectable growth defect at either 30 or 36°C. However, we observed a synthetic interaction at 36°C between scm3 4A and the tdTomato-tagged cnp3 gene, which is the ortholog of CENP-C. This interaction suggests a kinetochore malfunction (Figure 4A ). Indeed, live-cell imaging of these cells revealed a significant decrease in the intensity of CENP-C centromere foci in scm3 4A compared to control scm3 FL and wild-type cells (Figure 4B and C). Similar results were obtained when assessing the intensity of CENP-A centromere foci ( Supplementary Figure S7C and D ), confirming the defective kinetochore integrity in cells expressing scm3 4A as the sole source of Scm3.

Figure 4.

We then analyzed the fidelity of chromosome segregation in the scm3 4A strain by performing the minichromosome loss assay using pNBg ( 35 ), a ∼27-kb nonessential minichromosome that harbors a region of cen3 (Figure 4D ). pNBg carries an opal suppressor tRNA that complements a chromosomal ade6-704 mutation, resulting in white colonies. Loss of the minichromosome results in red or sectored colonies instead. Unlike the controls that displayed only white colonies, the scm3 4A strain formed mostly red colonies (Figure 4D ). Notably, the minichromosome loss in scm3 4A mutant cells occurred at levels comparable to those observed in cells lacking the histone H3 lysine 9 methyltransferase Clr4, which is required for heterochromatin assembly at centromeres and proper segregation of chromosomes ( 54 ). To confirm the impaired chromosome segregation in scm3 4A mutant cells through live-cell imaging, we monitored the segregation of endogenous chromosome II by observing septated and/or binucleated cells harboring cen2-tetO-tdTomato . As expected, we observed increased missegregation in s cm3 4A mutant cells (Figure 4E ). These results highlight the requirement for the CYS zinc-binding motif for proper centromere/kinetochore function.

CYS binds centromeric DNA in vitro and autonomously localizes to the centromere

The C-terminal region of fungal Scm3 proteins contains various types of predicted DNA-binding motifs ( 29 ). This led us to investigate whether the role of CYS is associated with DNA-binding activity. To test this possibility, we fused GST to Scm3-CYS (residues 303–336), which lacks Mis16-BD (Figure 5A ). We expressed and purified the GST fusion protein from E. coli . We also constructed a plasmid called pBR322- cen1 , which contains an ∼10-kb central core fragment of centromere 1 ( Supplementary Figure S8A ). We digested pBR322- cen1 with ApoI restriction enzyme, a frequent cutter on the AT-rich centromere DNA, and incubated the DNA fragments with purified GST proteins bound to glutathione Sepharose beads. GST-Scm3-CYS bound beads selectively pulled down several DNA fragments, while GST alone did not ( Supplementary Figure S8B ). The GST-Scm3-CYS pulldown showed an enrichment of certain centromeric fragments, but not the plasmid backbone, when compared to the input DNA, indicating a preference for those locations ( Supplementary Figure S8B and C ).

Figure 5.

The above findings indicate that CYS possesses DNA-binding activity in vitro and raise the possibility that CYS may also target centromeres autonomously in vivo . To address this possibility, we generated a fusion construct of GFP with Scm3-CYS (Figure 5A ). Remarkably, Scm3-CYS exhibited a discrete centromeric focus as well as diffuse localization (Figure 5B ) to the nucleus and cytoplasm. The intensity of the foci was more than half of those formed with Scm3 FL (Figure 5C ), indicating that CYS alone is capable of localizing to centromeres, although weakly. Additional features, such as Mis16-BD, are likely required for proper and efficient centromere localization of Scm3.

Mis16-BD and CYS together promote efficient Scm3 centromere targeting

To determine the effect of adding other domains to CYS, we designed a construct expressing Scm3-CT (residues 238–336) that also contains Mis16-BD (Figure 5A ). Live-cell imaging revealed that GFP-Scm3-CT showed a single focus that colocalized with cen2-tetO-tdTomato (Figure 5B ), whose intensity was nearly the same as the control GFP-Scm3 FL (Figure 5C ). Consistent with this result, ChIP-qPCR analysis revealed that GFP-Scm3-CT specifically localized to the central core domain of centromeres, but not to heterochromatic or euchromatic locations (Figure 6A ). These results underscore the combined contribution of both CYS and Mis16-BD to centromere targeting.

Figure 6.

To further analyze the individual contribution of CYS in the context of the full-length protein, we assessed Scm3 centromere targeting in the absence of the Mis16 protein. We expressed GFP-Scm3 FL in the temperature-sensitive mis16-53 mutant strain, wherein Scm3 presumably relies solely on CYS-dependent targeting. Consistent with previous studies ( 16 , 18 , 28 ), we did not detect any centromeric GFP-Scm3 FL foci in mis16-53 cells grown at the restrictive temperature when Scm3 expression was at endogenous levels ( Supplementary Figure S9A ). Similarly, most cells did not exhibit a centromere focus when GFP-Scm3 FL was overexpressed ( Supplementary Figure S9B ). However, ∼14% of the nuclei still showed a dim single focus that colocalized with cen2-tetO-tdTomato , underscoring the contribution of CYS to Scm3 centromere targeting even in the absence of Mis16. As expected, the combination of mutations in both CYS (i.e. Scm3 4A ) and Mis16 abrogated the formation of Scm3 centromeric foci ( Supplementary Figure S7A ). Together, these findings indicate that Mis16-BD and CYS are functionally independent, but act in concert to promote Scm3 targeting to centromeres.

Expression of truncated Scm3 is toxic and results in kinetochore loss

When expressed at high levels, truncated proteins lacking the N-terminal CENP-A binding domain might compete with the endogenous Scm3 protein and have detrimental effects on centromere assembly and cell fitness. Indeed, we found that overexpression of GFP-Scm3-CT significantly reduced cell viability (Figure 6B ). Notably, the toxicity was correlated with expression levels, as cells expressing GFP-Scm3-CT at endogenous levels (see +Thiamine, Figure 6B ) grew similarly to the wild-type control. We confirmed that the toxicity was not caused by the presence of cen2-tetO-tdTomato in the strains used for analysis. Expression of GFP-Scm3-CT resulted in a growth defect even in cells where cen2 remained unaltered (Figure 6B ).

We then asked whether toxicity is associated with disruption of the kinetochore. Indeed, we observed extensive missegregation of chromosome II in cells expressing Scm3-CT, indicating that the toxicity is likely caused by centromere/kinetochore malfunction (Figure 6C and D). Remarkably, CENP-A, CENP-C and CENP-K foci were barely detectable in cells overexpressing GFP-Scm3-CT, indicating a severe kinetochore deficiency in those cells (Figure 7A – D ). Unlike Scm3-CT, overexpression of Scm3-CYS did not result in severe phenotypes, suggesting that it is not strong enough to displace endogenous Scm3 and disrupt the kinetochore (Figure 6B and D). Based on these results, it is likely that truncated Scm3 proteins harboring both Mis16-BD and CYS outcompete endogenous Scm3 resulting in loss of kinetochore integrity.

Figure 7.

Mutations in the CYS zinc-binding motif prevent the toxicity associated with Scm3-CT

Next, we tested whether introducing the 4A mutations into the Scm3-CT protein would prevent centromere localization. Notably, Scm3-CT 4A failed to localize to centromeres, as confirmed by live-cell imaging analyses and ChIP-qPCR (Figures 5B and 6A ), again underscoring the importance of CYS zinc-binding motif. We then tested whether mutating the motif would reduce the toxicity of the Scm3-CT construct. Scm3-CT 4A did not show any toxicity in serial dilution growth assays, nor did it exhibit chromosome II missegregation, even when overexpressed (Figure 6B and D). Moreover, the localization of kinetochore proteins was unaffected in these cells (Figure 7 ). These findings further support the crucial role of the CYS zinc-binding motif in the centromere localization of the Scm3 protein.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of a novel cysteine-rich domain in the fission yeast CENP-A chaperone Scm3. We discovered that this domain is essential for centromere targeting and function. In both vertebrates and fission yeast, the centromere targeting of Scm3/HJURP relies on the Mis18 holocomplex, which consists of Mis16, Mis18 and Mis19 subunits in S. pombe . While fission yeast Scm3 has a specific region that interacts with the Mis18 holocomplex called Mis16-BD ( 28 ), we identified CYS as an additional requirement for targeting Scm3 to centromeres. Importantly, we found that CYS can bind zinc, and disruption of the zinc-binding motif prevents centromere targeting.

Our results argue that the role of CYS is independent of Mis16-BD in centromere targeting. Although we cannot completely rule out the possibility that CYS and Mis16-BD work together to facilitate the interaction of Scm3 with Mis16 in S. pombe , the finding that the last 32 aa of the Scm3 protein, which contain CYS, are not required for interaction with Mis16 argues against this scenario ( 28 ). Additionally, multiple sequence alignments of proteins from different organisms show that the presence of Mis16-BD and CYS together is not required ( Supplementary Figure S10A ). Furthermore, CYS domains are present in both phyla of the fungal subkingdom Dikarya, such as Neurospora and Ustilago from Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, respectively ( 29 ). However, we could not find Mis16-BD outside of Schizosaccharomycetes , except in the budding yeast Komagataella phaffii (also known as Pichia pastoris ), where the Scm3 protein has lost the CYS domain ( Supplementary Figure S10B ).

If CYS does not interact with Mis16, it raises the question of what the target for CYS might be. The presence of various predicted DNA-binding motifs in the C-terminus of other fungal Scm3 proteins ( 29 ) suggests that DNA could be the target. Supporting this idea, we provide evidence that CYS binds centromeric DNA and can itself localize to the centromere (Figure 5 and Supplementary Figure S8 ), which may confer additional ‘anchoring’ of Scm3 to centromeres. Notably, S. cerevisiae Scm3, as well as CENP-C from both budding yeast and human, also displayed in vitro binding preference for AT-rich DNA ( 55 , 56 ) that is a feature of centromeres and neocentromeres ( 57 ). Another possibility, which is not mutually exclusive, is that CYS may interact with centromeric noncoding RNA (ncRNA). In this regard, ncRNAs from CENP-A chromatin regions have been detected in S. pombe ( 58 ). Moreover, a long centromeric ncRNA has been shown to interact with HJURP/CENP-A complex in humans ( 59 ), while an optimal level of transcription is required for a functional point centromere in budding yeast ( 60 ). Alternatively, CYS might potentially recognize specific chromatin features, such as unmodified or hypoacetylated histones. Given that Mis16 depletion leads to increased levels of histone acetylation at centromeres ( 32 ), this scenario would explain the observed requirement for Mis16 in Scm3 localization.

Finally, Scm3 overexpression is toxic in various centromere/kinetochore temperature-sensitive mutants, including cnp1 CENP-A , mis16 and mis18 . However, the specific molecular mechanisms behind such toxicity remain unclear ( 18 ). In our study, we show that overexpression of a truncated version of Scm3, harboring CYS but lacking the CENP-A interacting domain, is extremely toxic. This leads to loss of kinetochore integrity, as evidenced by the absence of CENP-A/C/K centromere foci (Figure 7 ). This finding may have relevance to the reported overexpression and mutation of HJURP in various cancers ( 61 , 62 ). Indeed, HJURP is a significant biomarker, as its overexpression has been observed in many cancer types, including breast, gliomas and pancreatic cancer, and it is associated with poor prognosis ( 62–64 ). The penetrance of the deleterious phenotype may be influenced by the nature of HJURP mutations. For example, nonfunctional HJURP proteins lacking CENP-A could localize to centromeres and displace endogenous counterparts, as observed in our study. This would result in loss of kinetochore integrity and chromosomal instability, a hallmark of cancer progression.
