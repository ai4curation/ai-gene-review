# Structure and function of the S. pombe III-IV-cyt c supercomplex.

The respiratory chain in aerobic organisms is composed of a number of
membrane-bound protein complexes that link electron transfer to proton
translocation across the membrane. In mitochondria, the final electron acceptor,
complex IV (CIV), receives electrons from dimeric complex III (CIII2), via a
mobile electron carrier, cytochrome c. In the present study, we isolated the
CIII2CIV supercomplex from the fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe and
determined its structure with bound cyt. c using single-particle electron
cryomicroscopy. A respiratory supercomplex factor 2 was found to be bound at CIV
distally positioned in the supercomplex. In addition to the redox-active metal
sites, we found a metal ion, presumably Zn2+, coordinated in the CIII subunit
Cor1, which is encoded by the same gene (qcr1) as the mitochondrial-processing
peptidase subunit β. Our data show that the isolated CIII2CIV supercomplex
displays proteolytic activity suggesting a dual role of CIII2 in S. pombe. As in
the supercomplex from S. cerevisiae, subunit Cox5 of CIV faces towards one CIII
monomer, but in S. pombe, the two complexes are rotated relative to each other
by ~45°. This orientation yields equal distances between the cyt. c binding
sites at CIV and at each of the two CIII monomers. The structure shows cyt. c
bound at four positions, but only along one of the two symmetrical branches.
Overall, this combined structural and functional study reveals the integration
of peptidase activity with the CIII2 respiratory system and indicates a
two-dimensional cyt. c diffusion mechanism within the CIII2-CIV supercomplex.

## Full Text

In the final steps of energy conversion in aerobic organisms, electrons are transferred from Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) and succinate to O2, through a series of membrane-bound protein complexes, also known as the respiratory chain. The free energy released during this electron-transfer process is linked to proton translocation across the membrane, which results in the formation of a transmembrane proton electrochemical gradient that powers the production of ATP and transmembrane transport. In eukaryotes, the respiratory complexes are found in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

The first component of the respiratory chain, NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase (complex I, CI), receives electrons from NADH and reduces membrane-soluble quinone (Q) to quinol (QH2). The electron-transfer reaction is linked to translocation of protons from the mitochondrial matrix to the intermembrane space (IMS). Many yeast species such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe lack CI and instead harbor nonproton translocating alternative NADH dehydrogenases (1–3). Reduced QH2 diffuses in the membrane to donate electrons to cytochrome bc1, which forms an obligate homodimer, denoted CIII2 (dimeric complex III) (4). Quinol binds in the QP site (also called Qo), located towards the more positive (p) side of CIII2. Electrons are transferred along two branches referred to as the c- and b-chains, respectively. Transfer of electrons from QH2 in the QP site along the c-chain results in consecutive reduction of the Rieske iron–sulfur center and cytochrome (cyt.) c1, located at the p side surface of each CIII monomer. Cyt. c1 reduces water-soluble cyt. c located in the IMS of mitochondria. Oxidation of QH2 is linked to proton release to the p side. The second electron from the resulting semiquinone in the QP site is transferred along the b chain consecutively to heme bL, heme bH to reach a Q bound in the QN (or Qi) site, located toward the more negative (n) side of CIII2. The sequence of reactions is repeated, which results in two-electron reduction of the Q in the QN site. This reduction reaction is linked to uptake of two protons from the n side resulting in formation of QH2 that is released into the membrane phase. The overall energy-conservation process is referred to as the proton motive Q cycle, which results in oxidation of two QH2 at the QP site, release of 4 protons to the p side, and reduction of one Q in the QN site and uptake of two protons from the n side (4–10). Reduced cyt. c donates its electron to a copper center, CuA, located near the p side in cytochrome c oxidase [CIV (complex IV)]. Electrons are then transferred consecutively to heme a and the heme a3-CuB catalytic site, where after transfer of four electrons, O2 is reduced to H2O. Because electrons from cyt. c are transferred from the p side and protons are taken up from the n side, the vectorial reaction results in a charge separation across the membrane. In addition, each electron transfer to the catalytic site of CIV is linked to pumping one proton across the membrane, from the n to the p side of the membrane yielding an overall charge-separation stoichiometry that is equivalent of two translocated charges per electron transferred to O2 (11, 12).

Initially, it was anticipated that each respiratory complex functions in isolation and results from experimental studies were interpreted to indicate that these complexes diffuse independently in the mitochondrial inner membrane (13). However, the use of mild detergents in later studies allowed isolation from a wide range of organisms of supramolecular assemblies composed of two or more respiratory complexes (14–17). In addition, the use of cryo-electron tomography showed that these supercomplexes are found also in native membranes from mammals, yeast, and plants (18). The use of single-particle cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has revealed several high-resolution structures (for review, see refs. 19 and 20). These structures show that the relative orientation of CIII2 and CIV is highly variable in supercomplexes from different organisms and that the only common feature appears to be a minimal distance between the complexes, which is relevant for cyt. c-mediated electron transfer. Accordingly, studies of the cryo-EM structure and electron-transfer kinetics in the S. cerevisiae supercomplex, in which CIII2 is flanked by one or two monomeric CIVs (CIII2CIV1/2), suggested a functional role of respiratory supercomplexes in funneling surface electron transfer by two-dimensional (2D) diffusion [(21), see also refs. 22–24)].

The fission yeast S. pombe is an alternative model organism for studying the respiratory chain (25). S. pombe and S. cerevisiae are evolutionary highly diverged from each other and neither of them is closer to mammals than the other (25–27). S. pombe shares many characteristics with higher eukaryotes, presumably because it retains many features of the common ancient yeast ancestor (25, 28–31). In addition, S. pombe harbors only one isoform of the CIV subunit Cox5, as compared to the two isoforms Cox5a/b in S. cerevisiae (32, 33), found at the CIII–CIV interface of the supercomplex. Moreover, only one isoform of cyt. c is found in S. pombe, compared to two isoforms of cyt. c in S. cerevisiae.

In S. pombe, each subunit Cor1 of CIII2 harbors a conserved Zn2+-binding motif (34). This subunit is homologous to the β-subunit of the mitochondrial processing peptidase (MPP) and in S. pombe Cor1 and β-MPP protein are encoded by the same gene. Subunit Cor2 of S. pombe, located adjacent to Cor1 in CIII2 is homologous to the α-MPP subunit. In plants, MPP constitutes an integral part of CIII2 and displays proteolytic activity (35). Plants lack genes for an additional soluble MPP enzyme, i.e., all MPP activity is provided by the MPP subunits of CIII2. In other words, CIII2 in plants is not only a respiratory enzyme but also serves as the sole MPP peptidase (36–40). The zinc-binding motif, which is the active site of MPP, is not conserved in S. cerevisiae and no Zn2+ ion has been found in the structures of CIII2 (41, 42). Instead, S. cerevisiae harbors a soluble, enzymatically active heterodimer in the mitochondrial matrix, composed of the β-MPP and α-MPP subunits. Similarly, mammalian CIII2 has a modified zinc binding motif and no metal ion has been found in this binding motif (43).

In the current research, we combined structural and functional studies to investigate and understand the role of mitochondrial respiratory supercomplexes. We purified the active CIII2CIV1/2 supercomplexes from S. pombe and determined a structure of the cyt. c-CIII2CIV1 co-complex using cryo-EM. As with the S. cerevisiae supercomplex, subunit Cox5 of CIV faces CIII, but in S. pombe the two complexes are rotated ~45° relative to each other. Rcf2 (respiratory supercomplex factor 2) is bound at CIV in the supercomplex at the same position as in isolated CIV (44). Furthermore, the structural data show cyt. c bound at four positions along a line spanning the distance between CIV and one of the CIII2 monomers. These results suggest that 2D diffusion is possible in supercomplexes with different relative orientations of CIII2 and CIV indicating a general mechanism and functional significance of respiratory supercomplexes. Furthermore, we observed density of a metal ion in each Zn2+-binding motif in subunits Cor1 of CIII2. Consistent with this observation, the isolated S. pombe supercomplex displayed an in vitro MPP peptidase activity showing a dual role of CIII2 also in fission yeast S. pombe.

Analysis of the reduced minus oxidized difference spectra (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C and D) of the preparation showed absorbance maxima at 430, 550, and 560 nm characteristic for CIII2, as well as absorbance maxima at 445 and 607 nm corresponding to CIV. The surface-bound cyt. c is in equilibrium with the cyt. c pool in solution (74) and the mechanism of electron transfer is determined by the relative rates of 2D diffusion and equilibration of the surface-bound cyt. c with the cyt. c pool. The QH2:O2 oxidoreductase activity is dependent on the average distance between CIII and CIV (21, 24), in particular at the low cyt. c:supercomplex ratio of 2 to 3 found in S. cerevisiae (24) and S. pombe (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D) mitochondria. Hence, the 2D cyt. c diffusion mechanism in the supercomplex would result in increasing the rate of the rate-limiting electron transfer between each CIII monomer and CIV. In addition, the 2D diffusion mechanism could also have evolved to outcompete nonspecific binding of the positively charged cyt. c to other negatively charged proteins (75). The 2D diffusion mechanism appears to be common to the S. cerevisiae and S. pombe but it is not universal. For example, in bovine heart mitochondria, a major fraction of CIV is not part of supercomplexes (76). Furthermore, in, e.g., Tetrahymena thermophila (77), cyt. c is negatively charged and interacts with positively charged patches that are restricted only to the cyt. c binding sites at CIII2 and CIV, respectively.

In the S. cerevisiae supercomplex subunit Cox5 of CIV, which faces CIII2, exposes a matrix domain that closely interacts with Cor1. Even though in the V. radiata supercomplex the relative position of CIII2 and CIV is similar to that in S. cerevisiae, the plant supercomplex has a shorter Cox5 subunit, which leaves an open space between Cor1 of CIII2 and CIV. As discussed previously (67), the geometry found in the S. cerevisiae supercomplex would restrict Cor1–Cor2 dynamics necessary to allow access of peptides to the Cor1 catalytic site. We speculate that the shorter Cox5 as in V. radiata or the different CIV position as in S. pombe would allow unrestricted dynamics facilitating the enzymatic activity of the Cor1–Cor2 pair.

In S. pombe, β-MPP is identical to the Cor1 subunit and they are encoded by the same gene (qcr1, pombase.org). However, in contrast to vascular plants, S. pombe also harbors a gene encoding the α-MPP subunit (mas2), which is distinct from CIII2. Mass spectrometry data showed that the α-MPP subunit (mas2) was not present in the supercomplex preparation. In other words, the data from the present study indicate that the α-MPP subunit (product of mas2) is not absolutely required for supporting the MPP activity of Cor1 (β-MPP) of CIII2. Most likely, this activity is supported by the adjacent α-MPP homolog Cor2. Of note, in the S. pombe database (pombase.org), deletions of qcr1 or mas2 are noted as inviable. Because the catalytic site of MPP is located in the β-MPP subunit (qcr1), these observations probably indicate that the gene product of qcr1 comprises two protein populations, one that is the Cor1 subunit of CIII2 and another that is water soluble β-MPP subunit. The latter form interacts with the α-MPP subunit (mas2) to form an active, water-soluble α-β MPP peptidase. Interestingly, supporting such a dual role of qcr1, Herbert et al. (68) (see also pombase.org) found that the product of qcr1 in S. pombe is 10 to 20 times more abundant than that of mas2. Similarly, Hawlitschek et al. (69) found that in N. crassa mitochondria the equivalent of Cor1 (β-MPP) is 15-fold more abundant than α-MPP. Furthermore, the authors observed that 75% of the total β-MPP is attached to the inner membrane surface but is not embedded in the phospholipid bilayer. The equivalent of the α-MPP subunit was not associated with the membrane. A similar scenario would also support a dual role of the qcr1 product in S. pombe. It has been speculated that the MPP and the Cor proteins originate from a processing peptidase that was integrated into CIII2 (38). In some organisms such as S. cerevisiae and in mammals the two MPP subunits detached from CIII2 by gene duplication in order to independently regulate protein processing and respiration. N. crassa and S. pombe would represent intermediate stages in this process (38).

Results from earlier studies have revealed a glycine-rich region of the α-MPP subunit, which is found in proximity to the β-MPP catalytic site. In vitro deletion or site-directed mutagenesis studies of the role of residues in this region revealed that it is essential for binding and cleavage of the precursor proteins (70). In S. pombe CIII2 a loop of Cor2 at the same position faces the Zn2+-catalytic site in Cor1. However, residues previously shown to be essential for supporting the proteolytic activity (70) are not conserved in S. pombe Cor2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). It appears that the arrangement of Cor1 and Cor2 in S. pombe is optimized to provide an overall geometry that allows MPP activity while using a different combination of residues around and in the catalytic site. Alternatively, the lower activity of the structural variants in the earlier study (70) may be caused by a weaker binding of the two MPP subunits.

ConclusionsWe isolated the CIII2CIV supercomplex from S. pombe and determined its structure with bound cyt. c using cryo-EM. In CIV, subunit Cox5 faces CIII2, but at a position that is rotated by ~45° compared to that in S. cerevisiae. This orientation yields equal distances between the cyt. c binding sites at each CIII monomer and at CIV. Cyt. c was found to be bound at four positions along one of the two symmetrical branches connecting the cyt. c binding sites at CIII and CIV. These results suggest that even though the relative orientation of CIII and CIV is different from that in S. cerevisiae, 2D diffusion along the supercomplex surface is possible, indicating a general mechanism and functional significance of respiratory supercomplexes. A Zn2+-containing β-MPP homologous Cor1 subunit of each CIII, presumably together with the α-MPP homolog Cor2 subunit, displayed MPP proteolytic activity, which suggests a dual role of CIII2 in respiration and protein processing in S. pombe. The rotation of CIII2 and CIV relative to each other may facilitate the MPP activity by allowing Cor1–Cor2 dynamics that is required for functional MPP. Overall, our current combined structural and functional study provides unique insights into yeast respiration.

The cleared lysate was concentrated with a 100-kDa molecular weight cutoff concentrator (Merck Millipore) to approximately 10 mL, diluted fivefold in detergent-free buffer, and concentrated again to 10 mL before applied to a StrepTactin superflow column (IBA life sciences) preequilibrated with 25 mM KH2PO4, pH 8 and 150 mM KCl. The column was washed with 25 mM KH2PO4, pH 8, 150 mM KCl, and 0.01% GDN before elution in the same buffer, supplemented with 5 mM desthiobiotin. The membrane supernatant, flow through, wash, and elution fractions were all analyzed spectrophotometrically (Cary100, Agilent) to determine their protein and CIV content at 280 nm and 420 nm, respectively. Eluted supercomplexes were concentrated with a 100-kDa molecular weight cutoff concentrator (Merck Millipore) and further purified by size exclusion chromatography with an Äkta Pure M25 (GE Healthcare) operated at 4 °C with UV detection at 280 nm and 415 nm. The sample was loaded on a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with 25 mM KH2PO4, pH 8.0, 150 mM KCl, 0.01% GDN at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Fractions of 250 μL were collected, and fractions containing both CIII and CIV were pooled and concentrated as described above. The concentration of CIII and CIV was determined from the reduced-minus-oxidized spectra using ε554 = 21 mM–1cm–1 (79), ε562 = 51.2 mM–1cm–1 (80) and ε607 = 24 mM–1cm–1 (81).

Grid Preparation and cryo-EM.Purified supercomplexes (3 µL) at a concentration of ~8 mg mL–1, supplemented with S. cerevisiae cyt. c (Sigma-Aldrich; aerobically grown S. cerevisiae contains 95% isoform-1 cyt. c) (83) at a ∼1:12 molar ratio, was applied to holey carbon film coated copper EM grids (C flat 2/2 3C T50) that had been glow-discharged in air (120 s, 20 mA using PELCO easiGlow). Grids were blotted for 3 s at 4 °C and 100% humidity before rapid freezing in liquid ethane with a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cryo-EM data were collected using a Titan Krios G3 electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 kV, equipped with a Gatan BioQuantum energy filter and a K3 Summit direct electron detector (AMETEK). Automated data collection was done with the EPU software package (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A dataset of 10,347 movies, each consisting of 40 exposure fractions was collected at a nominal magnification of 105,000×, corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 0.85 Å. The camera exposure rate and the total exposure of the specimen were 15.2 e–/pixel/s and ~41 e–/Å2, respectively (SI Appendix, Table S1).

Cryo-EM Image Processing.All image analysis was performed with cryoSPARC v2 (84). Movies were aligned with MotionCor2 (85), and contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated in patches with a 7 × 7 grid. The dataset was manually curated to remove movies with devitrification, large cracks in the ice, or poor CTF fit parameters, reducing the dataset size to 9,226 movies. Templates for particle selection were generated by 2D classification of manually selected particle images. A total of 690,302 particles were selected, and images were corrected for local motion (86) and extracted in 512 × 512 pixel boxes. The dataset was cleaned with several rounds of ab initio 3D classification and heterogeneous refinement, taking only the classes that corresponded to supercomplexes after each round. This procedure further reduced the size of the dataset to 125,752 particle images, which were subject to nonuniform refinement (87) to produce the refined CIII2CIV1 map. The map was then analyzed with 3D variability analysis (71) with a mask over the region of the map corresponding to cyt. c. Cluster analysis was used to generate four maps that served as initial references in a heterogeneous refinement.
