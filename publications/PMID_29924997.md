# Analysis of Drosophila STING Reveals an Evolutionarily Conserved Antimicrobial Function.

The vertebrate protein STING, an intracellular sensor of cyclic dinucleotides,
is critical to the innate immune response and the induction of type I interferon
during pathogenic infection. Here, we show that a STING ortholog (dmSTING)
exists in Drosophila, which, similar to vertebrate STING, associates with cyclic
dinucleotides to initiate an innate immune response. Following infection with
Listeria monocytogenes, dmSTING activates an innate immune response via
activation of the NF-κB transcription factor Relish, part of the immune
deficiency (IMD) pathway. DmSTING-mediated activation of the immune response
reduces the levels of Listeria-induced lethality and bacterial load in the host.
Of significance, dmSTING triggers an innate immune response in the absence of a
known functional cyclic guanosine monophosphate (GMP)-AMP synthase (cGAS)
ortholog in the fly. Together, our results demonstrate that STING is an
evolutionarily conserved antimicrobial effector between flies and mammals, and
it comprises a key component of host defense against pathogenic infection in
Drosophila.

INTRODUCTION

Pathogenic infection of Drosophila induces the secretion of antimicrobial peptides by the fat body, an organ analogous to the mammalian liver, which accumulate in the hemolymph (Lemaitre and Hoffmann, 2007). Antimicrobial peptides are small, cationic molecules that are capable of killing bacteria and fungi (Imler and Bulet, 2005). Like mammals, flies encode a number of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that recognize conserved pathogen motifs called pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) (Sabin et al., 2010). The recognition of pathogens in Drosophila initiates a signaling cascade where one of the termination points is the induction of antimicrobial peptides (Lemaitre and Hoffmann, 2007).

As innate immunity is an ancient, evolutionarily conserved form of host defense, there is a high degree of similarity in the innate immune responses between flies and mammals. Mammalian PRRs consist of Toll-like receptors (TLR) and RIG-I-like receptors (RLR), among other families of PRRs (Kawai and Akira, 2011). Activation of PRRs with their respective PAMPs leads to an innate immune response via the nuclear translocation of NF-κB or interferon (IFN) regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) (Sharma et al., 2003). This cascade culminates with the induction of hundreds of IRF-responsive genes, including IFN-β, a cytokine produced during the early stages of infection and the host defense response, which binds to the IFN-α/β receptor and induces IFN-stimulated gene expression (Sato et al., 2000). In Drosophila, two classical innate immune pathways function through either Toll or IMD (immune deficient) (Gottar et al., 2002; Lemaitre et al., 1996). During Gram-positive bacteria infection, activation of peptidoglycan recognition protein SA (PGRP-SA), the serine protease Persephone, and Gram-negative binding protein 1 (GNBP1) lead to proteolytically processing of Spätzle and stimulation of the Toll receptor, which activates dMyD88, Tube, Pelle, and the NF-κB homolog DIF (dorsal-related immunity factor) (Gobert et al., 2003; Ligoxygakis et al., 2002). The IMD pathway is stimulated by PGRP-LE and PGRP-LC, which recognize diaminopimelic acid (DAP) type peptidoglycan (PGN) on the surface of bacteria and activate autophagy or the IMD pathway through the NF-κB molecule Relish (Gottar et al., 2002; Kaneko et al., 2006; Yano et al., 2008). Together, the Toll and IMD pathways make up two NF-κB pathways in Drosophila that function in the humoral response to pathogenic infection. Signaling through Drosophila NF-κB pathways is similar to the mammalian TLR pathways, both in pathway structure and the proteins involved in signaling (Ganesan et al., 2011). Antimicrobial peptide genes induced as part of the Toll and IMD pathways include Drosomycin (Drs), AttacinA (AttA), and CecropinA2 (CecA2), among others. Each pathway preferentially induces its own set of antimicrobial peptides, and mutations in the Toll-mediated NF-κB molecule DIF render flies susceptible and unable to induce Toll-mediated antimicrobial peptides during Gram-positive bacteria or fungal infections (Gottar et al., 2002; Ligoxygakis et al., 2002; Michel et al., 2001; Rutschmann et al., 2000), while mutations in IMD or Relish render flies susceptible and unable to induce IMD-mediated antimicrobial peptides during Gram-negative bacteria infections (Hedengren et al., 1999; Lemaitre et al., 1995). However, there is crosstalk between the Toll and IMD pathways, resulting in the sets of peptides being stimulated together (Tanji et al., 2007). Ultimately, the fruit fly innate immune response must be fully functional for the proper secretion of antimicrobial peptides into the hemolymph to neutralize the pathogenic infection and curb mortality.

Another class of PAMPs in mammals that has not been extensively studied in Drosophila is one that recognizes cytosolic DNA or cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs). In mammals, these molecules trigger signaling pathways controlled by STING (stimulator of interferon genes) and lead to NF-κB and IRF3 activation and ultimately the induction of IFN-β (Ishikawa and Barber, 2008). STING is a transmembrane protein that activates an innate immune response during viral or bacterial infection. STING activation in response to Listeria monocytogenes functions through CDNs, which are byproducts of Listeria infection known to induce IFN-β (Burdette et al., 2011; Woodward et al., 2010). Recent studies have also identified cyclic diguanosine monophosphate (di-GMP) as a major signaling molecule in the Listeria life cycle (Chen et al., 2014; Köseoğlu et al., 2015) that is able to activate STING (Burdette et al., 2011). Indeed, during infection with Chlamydia trachomatis, bacterial CDNs directly activate STING to activate a type I IFN response (Barker et al., 2013). Cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) signals upstream of STING by binding to cytosolic DNA, triggering cGAS to metabolize ATP and GTP into non-canonical cyclic-GMP-AMP (cGAMP) containing 2′-5′ and 3′-5′ mixed phosphodiester linkages, which are then able to activate STING (Ablasser et al., 2013; Diner et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2013). While the roles of STING and cGAS in sensing cytosolic nucleic acids have been comprehensively studied in mammalian immunity, less is known about their role in invertebrate immunity.

To date, the major nucleic acid sensors that have been identified in Drosophila are the Dicer proteins involved in the RNAi pathway (Deddouche et al., 2008; van Rij et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2006). Dicer-2 is a pathogen-recognition receptor that senses viral nucleic acids and initiates the RNAi pathway to mount innate and antiviral responses to DNA viruses such as invertebrate iridescent virus 6 (IIV6) (Bronkhorst et al., 2012). Furthermore, Dicer-2 and the mammalian proteins MDA5 and RIG-I share sequence similarity at their RNA-binding helicase domains (Deddouche et al., 2008). While vertebrates sense cytosolic DNA with a variety of proteins, including IFI16, AIM2, and cGAS (Hornung et al., 2009; Sun et al., 2013; Unterholzner et al., 2010), only cGAS has an ortholog in Drosophila, namely CG7194. However, CG7194 lacks the zinc-ribbon domain and a positively charged N terminus, which are functionally important for DNA binding (Wu et al., 2014). In this study, we sought to identify a CDN-binding protein in Drosophila, and we found that the Drosophila protein CG1667, henceforth referred to as dmSTING, is orthologous to the vertebrate STING protein. DmSTING retains its ability to bind cyclic di-GMP, leading to the induction of innate immune response genes. Knockdown of dmSTING during Listeria infection led to a loss of innate immune gene induction, increased bacterial burden, and consequent animal mortality. Conversely, overexpression of dmSTING led to increased antimicrobial peptide induction and activation of the Drosophila NF-κB homolog Relish. Interestingly, dmSTING was functional in mammalian cells, and it was able to induce mammalian NF-κB. Finally, epistasis analysis in flies indicated that dmSTING functioned predominantly through the IMD pathway and Relish to achieve antimicrobial peptide induction. Taken together, our results indicate that STING functions through an evolutionarily conserved host defense pathway, whose antimicrobial function, along with the RNAi, Toll, and JAK-STAT pathways, protects the invertebrate host against microbial infection.

RESULTSDmSTING Binds to CDNsHuman STING (hsSTING) binds cyclic di-GMP (c-di-GMP) (Burdette et al., 2011), and crystallographic studies have shown that c-di-GMP binding requires three major regions in STING (Ouyang et al., 2012; Shang et al., 2012; Shu et al., 2012; Yin et al., 2012). A BLAST search using the three hsSTING c-di-GMP-binding domains as input revealed a high degree of similarity with domains of a Drosophila melanogaster protein denoted CG1667 (Figure 1A). The residues of hsSTING that interact with c-di-GMP are highlighted, and the greatest amino acid similarity is observed at regions 1 and 3. Additionally, searching across multiple databases (Hu et al., 2011) and performing deep alignment with STING orthologs from both vertebrate and invertebrate species showed conservation across the three c-di-GMP-binding regions and identified the same putative STING ortholog, CG1667 (Figure S1A). Interestingly, no STING ortholog was found in Caenorhabditis elegans. Based on the conservation of the c-di-GMP-binding domains in dmSTING, we performed in vitro pull-down studies, which indicated that the C-terminal region of dmSTING, namely amino acids 134–343, associated with c-di-GMP, whereas the N-terminal region (amino acids 1–133) did not (Figure 1B). GFP is used as a negative control for c-di-GMP binding. We next performed deletion mutagenesis in the c-di-GMP-binding regions to ask how deletion of each region influenced c-di-GMP association in our in vitro system. We found that the deletion of regions 1 and 3, which had the greatest similarity to hsSTING at the amino acid level, reduced c-di-GMP association. Here, the deletion of region 2 did not impact c-di-GMP’s association with dmSTING (Figure 1C). In addition to c-di-GMP, hsSTING is also able to bind c-di-AMP and the cGAS product cGAMP. Since region 2 of hsSTING contains residues that are essential for forming the lid of four β sheets when hsSTING is bound to cyclic-GMP-AMP (cGAMP) (Zhang et al., 2013), we asked whether dmSTING was also able to bind cGAMP. While dmSTING was able to bind cGAMP and c-di-AMP in mild pull-down conditions, at more stringent salt conditions, dmSTING only bound to c-di-GMP (Figure S1B). Thus, we used c-di-GMP for subsequent experiments with dmSTING and to test its function.To further evaluate the capacity of dmSTING to associate with CDNs, we purified the soluble carboxy region of dmSTING (amino acids 147–343) from Escherichia coli as well as deletion mutants lacking each of the three CDN-binding regions (Figure 1D). This N-terminal truncation does not contain any of the hydrophobic transmembrane domains, which limit the solubility of dmSTING. The purified C-terminal region of dmSTING associated with biotin-labeled c-di-GMP, and the addition of unlabeled c-di-GMP inhibited the ability of dmSTING to bind to the biotin-labeled molecule (Figure 1E). Furthermore, while purified protein products lacking CDN-binding regions 1 and 3 were unable to bind c-di-GMP, the loss of CDN-binding region 2 did not render the protein completely unable to bind c-di-GMP (Figure 1F). Densitometry analysis of input and pull-down bands indicated that while 5.6% of dmSTING 147–343 was pulled down with c-di-GMP, only 3.1% of the ΔCDN2 mutant was pulled down with c-di-GMP, suggesting that the requirement for region 2 is not as great as that for regions 1 and 3 in CDN binding.DmSTING Mediates a Global Antimicrobial Transcriptional ResponseNext, to evaluate the contribution of dmSTING to antimicrobial transcriptional responses, we knocked down dmSTING in S2 cells by RNAi. Cells were then transfected with c-di-GMP for microarray analysis. We generated a signature of genes that were at least two-fold upregulated in scrambled RNAi cells, as compared to dmSTING RNAi cells following c-di-GMP transfection and normalization to mock-transfected cells (Figure 2A). Knockdown of dmSTING in S2 cells was ~10-fold (Figure 2B). We also examined the induction of the IMD-specific genes AttA and CecA2 and the Toll-specific gene Drs at 6 and 24 hr post-transfection. We observed induction of AttA and CecA2, but not Drs, by 24 hr (Figure 2C–2E). We then used the c-di-GMP-specific gene list in Figure 2A to interrogate gene expression data from Listeria-infected adult flies that had been knocked down for dmSTING. Upon applying the gene list from c-di-GMP stimulation in S2 cells (Figure 2A), we found that ten of the same genes from that gene list were also at least 1.5-fold upregulated following Listeria infection in control flies as compared to dmSTING knockdown flies (Figure 2F, red font). Many of these genes function in the innate immune response to pathogenic infection, such as Cecropin and Rudra/pirk/PIMS, resulting from IMD pathway activation (Aggarwal et al., 2008; Kleino et al., 2008; Lhocine et al., 2008). Additionally, we observed increased upregulation of Listericin, spirit, and Zip3 during both c-di-GMP stimulation and Listeria infection when dmSTING was present, indicating the role of dmSTING in the immune response (De Gregorio et al., 2002; Goto et al., 2010; Kambris et al., 2006; Okado et al., 2009).Next, we used a functional genomics approach to ask how c-di-GMP stimulation and Listeria infection affected gene expression in S2 cells and flies knocked down for dmSTING. With the entire gene expression datasets from c-di-GMP-transfected cells or Listeria-infected flies used to generate Figures 2A and 2F, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), which determines how distinct phenotypes or experimental conditions differ in their gene expression profiles by ranking the significance of the gene ontology (GO) classifications identified. The significance values of the GO classifications describe the phenotypic distinction among the sets (Subramanian et al., 2005). Notably, dmSTING knockdown cells and flies exhibited a decreased magnitude of significance for enrichment of GO classifications related to the immune response following c-di-GMP transfection (Figure 2G, red bars; Tables S2 and S3) or Listeria infection (Figure 2H, red bars; Tables S4 and S5), in contrast to cells transfected with scrambled RNAi or control flies (Figures 2G and 2H, black bars). This analysis indicated that fewer GO classifications were significantly (p < 0.001) regulated in the absence of dmSTING. For example, the defense response (GO: 0006952), humoral immune response (GO: 0006959), and innate immune response (GO: 0045087) as well as their subclassifications, including the defense response to Gram-negative/positive bacterium (GO: 0050829/0050830) and antibacterial humoral response (GO: 0019731), were less significantly enriched when dmSTING was knocked down following transfection or infection. We also took advantage of IMD- and Toll-specific gene signatures generated by De Gregorio et al. (2002) to examine our transcriptomic data. Genes upregulated during c-di-GMP stimulation in S2 cells were part of the IMD/Relish induction signature, and their induction was decreased when dmSTING was knocked down (Figure S2A). Since Listeria infection is a less specific stimulus, we also observed induction of genes that are part of the Toll pathway, and induction of these genes was ablated when dmSTING was knocked down in flies (Figure S2B). Together, these data highlight the enrichment and upregulation of immune responses in Drosophila containing normal levels of dmSTING in response to c-di-GMP stimulation or Listeria infection. Collectively, our data indicate that knockdown of dmSTING results in a significant loss of host defense gene induction following CDN stimulation and reduced enrichment levels, as noted by p values, of certain defense response GO classifications during Listeria infection.DmSTING Controls Listeria Infection through the Induction of Antimicrobial PeptidesGiven our transcriptomic results, we next sought to characterize the effect that dmSTING knockdown had in Listeria-infected adult flies. Using two different dmSTING RNAi lines, we found that flies expressing decreased levels of dmSTING were significantly more susceptible to Listeria infection than control flies (Figures 3A and S3A). Additionally, we observed increased bacterial load in flies with reduced levels of dmSTING (Figures 3B and S3B). We confirmed reduced levels of dmSTING (Figure 3C). Upon demonstrating that dmSTING conferred protection against Listeria infection, we sought to understand the mechanism of action. Thus, at 24 hr post-infection (p.i.), we analyzed levels of antimicrobial peptides in the infected flies. As compared to mock-infected flies, AttA, CecA2, and Drs mRNA levels were increased in Listeria-infected flies (Figures 3D–3F). However, there was decreased induction of antimicrobial peptides putatively controlled by the IMD pathway in dmSTING RNAi flies as compared to control flies, namely AttA and CecA2 (Figures 3D and 3E). Levels of the Toll pathway readout Drs were not significantly different when dmSTING was knocked down (Figure 3F). We also measured CecA2 and Drs induction during Listeria infection in flies knocked down for dmSTING or Relish in the fat body. While CecA2 induction was significantly reduced following infection in dmSTING RNAi flies as compared to control flies, the knockdown of Relish resulted in further reduction of CecA2 induction (Figure 3G). Knockdown of dmSTING or Relish did not significantly affect Drs induction following Listeria infection (Figure 3H). Because the knockdown of dmSTING resulted in decreased levels of antimicrobial peptides associated with the IMD pathway, and because Listeria expresses a DAP-type PGN that activates the PGRP-LC/LE receptors and induces Relish-mediated antimicrobial peptides (Kaneko et al., 2006; Yano et al., 2008), we next examined the levels of Relish activation during infection. When activated, full-length Relish is proteolytically cleaved, and the 68 kDa N-terminal product translocates to the nucleus while the 49-kDa C-terminal product remains cytoplasmic (Stöven et al., 2000). Western blot analysis of fly protein extracts indicates that when dmSTING is knocked down during Listeria infection, there is decreased Relish activation, as indicated by a decrease in the 49-kDa cleavage product (Figure 3I).To corroborate the results we observed regarding mortality and antimicrobial peptide induction in flies knocked down for dmSTING, we generated loss-of-function dmSTING flies using clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/Cas9-mediated deletion of base pairs 1,381–1,993 of the dmSTING gene, which includes a majority of the second exon. Listeria infection of two distinct founder lines exhibited increased mortality as compared to the parental nos-Cas9 control line (Figure 4A). The loss of dmSTING in each founder line (Figure 4B) also resulted in a loss of IMD-mediated antimicrobial peptide induction (Figures 4C and 4D), but not a loss in Drs induction, during Listeria infection (Figure 4E). Notably, the ΔRG3 line exhibited a greater loss in AttA induction and reduced survival as compared to the ΔRG5 line. Therefore, to test whether the loss-of-function lines included any off-target effects that contribute to the observed phenotype, we crossed the founder lines with a balanced deficiency line, BSC133/CyO, that lacks a contiguous ~40 kbp of genomic DNA, including the dmSTING gene. We observed that flies heterozygous for the BSC133 deficiency and the CRISPR/Cas9 dmSTING deletion displayed a similar loss of AttA and CecA2 induction, but not a loss of Drs induction, during Listeria infection (Figures 4F–4I).In contrast to the reduced survival and increased pathogen load in Listeria-infected flies when dmSTING was knocked down using one RNAi line, we did not observe any difference in survival or pathogen load between knockdown and control flies infected with the dsDNA virus IIV6 (Figures S4A and S4B). Additionally, we measured antimicrobial peptide induction in response to infection with IIV6. Regardless of dmSTING expression levels (Figure S4C), we only observed a statistically significant loss of the antimicrobial peptide AttA during IIV6 infection (Figures S4D–S4F). However, the magnitude of AttA induction during IIV6 infection in control flies was greater than 10-fold less than its induction during Listeria infection, suggesting that this statistically significant decrease in AttA induction is not biologically significant.Since our results thus far indicate that dmSTING is acting through the IMD pathway via a CDN-mediated mechanism during Listeria infection, we next tested whether the knockdown of dmSTING affected the response to infection with the extracellular Gram-negative bacteria E. coli, which activates the IMD pathway during infection (Lemaitre et al., 1997). While we observed induction of both AttA and Drs during E. coli infection, there was no loss of induction when dmSTING was knocked down (Figure S4G and S4H). Finally, we tested whether the knockdown of dmSTING affected the response of the RNA virus Cricket paralysis virus (CrPV), since IMD loss-of-function mutants are more sensitive to CrPV infection (Costa et al., 2009). We observed no differences in rates of mortality to CrPV infection (Figure S5A), nor did we observe induction of antimicrobial peptides during infection (Figures S5B–S5D).Considering that infection with a dsDNA virus did not affect a dmSTING-mediated response with regards to mortality, virus replication, and antimicrobial peptide induction (Figure S4), we next sought to test the role of the putative cGAS homolog in flies, CG7194 (Hancks et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2014). To this end, we tested the response to both Listeria and IIV6 infection in two different CG7194 P element lines, f00723 and MB10883, in comparison to the isogenic control line, w1118. Compared to the control line, CG7194 expression is reduced by 2.3-fold and 37-fold in lines f00723 and MB10883, respectively (Figure S6A). Following infection with Listeria or IIV6, there were no differences in mortality rates of either line compared to the control line (Figures S6B and S6C). Furthermore, knockdown of CG7194 by 39-fold using RNAi did not affect flies’ mortality to either Listeria or IIV6 infection (Figures S6D–S6F), suggesting that CG7194 does not play a role during infection with these microbes.To further examine the role of dmSTING during Listeria infection and to validate our knockdown experiments, we generated dmSTING overexpression fly lines using the Actin5C-GAL4 gene to ubiquitously drive dmSTING cDNA with an upstream activator sequence (UAS) element. Overexpressing flies were less susceptible to mortality induced by Listeria infection (Figure 5A) and had decreased bacterial load levels (Figure 5B). DmSTING overexpression was confirmed (Figure 5C). Additionally, the dmSTING overexpression flies exhibited significantly higher levels of AttA and CecA2 mRNA, but not Drs, 24 hr following Listeria infection (Figures 5D–5F). Overexpression of dmSTING in the fat body also resulted in increased CecA2 induction following Listeria infection (Figure 5G), but Drs induction was not significantly affected (Figure 5H). This N-terminal truncation does not contain any of the hydrophobic transmembrane domains, which limit the solubility of dmSTING. The purified C-terminal region of dmSTING associated with biotin-labeled c-di-GMP, and the addition of unlabeled c-di-GMP inhibited the ability of dmSTING to bind to the biotin-labeled molecule (Figure 1E). Furthermore, while purified protein products lacking CDN-binding regions 1 and 3 were unable to bind c-di-GMP, the loss of CDN-binding region 2 did not render the protein completely unable to bind c-di-GMP (Figure 1F). Together, these data highlight the enrichment and upregulation of immune responses in Drosophila containing normal levels of dmSTING in response to c-di-GMP stimulation or Listeria infection. Collectively, our data indicate that knockdown of dmSTING results in a significant loss of host defense gene induction following CDN stimulation and reduced enrichment levels, as noted by p values, of certain defense response GO classifications during Listeria infection.

The loss of dmSTING in each founder line (Figure 4B) also resulted in a loss of IMD-mediated antimicrobial peptide induction (Figures 4C and 4D), but not a loss in Drs induction, during Listeria infection (Figure 4E). Notably, the ΔRG3 line exhibited a greater loss in AttA induction and reduced survival as compared to the ΔRG5 line. Therefore, to test whether the loss-of-function lines included any off-target effects that contribute to the observed phenotype, we crossed the founder lines with a balanced deficiency line, BSC133/CyO, that lacks a contiguous ~40 kbp of genomic DNA, including the dmSTING gene. We observed that flies heterozygous for the BSC133 deficiency and the CRISPR/Cas9 dmSTING deletion displayed a similar loss of AttA and CecA2 induction, but not a loss of Drs induction, during Listeria infection (Figures 4F–4I).

In contrast to the reduced survival and increased pathogen load in Listeria-infected flies when dmSTING was knocked down using one RNAi line, we did not observe any difference in survival or pathogen load between knockdown and control flies infected with the dsDNA virus IIV6 (Figures S4A and S4B). Additionally, we measured antimicrobial peptide induction in response to infection with IIV6. Regardless of dmSTING expression levels (Figure S4C), we only observed a statistically significant loss of the antimicrobial peptide AttA during IIV6 infection (Figures S4D–S4F). However, the magnitude of AttA induction during IIV6 infection in control flies was greater than 10-fold less than its induction during Listeria infection, suggesting that this statistically significant decrease in AttA induction is not biologically significant.

Since our results thus far indicate that dmSTING is acting through the IMD pathway via a CDN-mediated mechanism during Listeria infection, we next tested whether the knockdown of dmSTING affected the response to infection with the extracellular Gram-negative bacteria E. coli, which activates the IMD pathway during infection (Lemaitre et al., 1997). While we observed induction of both AttA and Drs during E. coli infection, there was no loss of induction when dmSTING was knocked down (Figure S4G and S4H). Finally, we tested whether the knockdown of dmSTING affected the response of the RNA virus Cricket paralysis virus (CrPV), since IMD loss-of-function mutants are more sensitive to CrPV infection (Costa et al., 2009). We observed no differences in rates of mortality to CrPV infection (Figure S5A), nor did we observe induction of antimicrobial peptides during infection (Figures S5B–S5D).

Considering that infection with a dsDNA virus did not affect a dmSTING-mediated response with regards to mortality, virus replication, and antimicrobial peptide induction (Figure S4), we next sought to test the role of the putative cGAS homolog in flies, CG7194 (Hancks et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2014). To this end, we tested the response to both Listeria and IIV6 infection in two different CG7194 P element lines, f00723 and MB10883, in comparison to the isogenic control line, w1118. Compared to the control line, CG7194 expression is reduced by 2.3-fold and 37-fold in lines f00723 and MB10883, respectively (Figure S6A). Following infection with Listeria or IIV6, there were no differences in mortality rates of either line compared to the control line (Figures S6B and S6C). Furthermore, knockdown of CG7194 by 39-fold using RNAi did not affect flies’ mortality to either Listeria or IIV6 infection (Figures S6D–S6F), suggesting that CG7194 does not play a role during infection with these microbes.

To further examine the role of dmSTING during Listeria infection and to validate our knockdown experiments, we generated dmSTING overexpression fly lines using the Actin5C-GAL4 gene to ubiquitously drive dmSTING cDNA with an upstream activator sequence (UAS) element. Overexpressing flies were less susceptible to mortality induced by Listeria infection (Figure 5A) and had decreased bacterial load levels (Figure 5B). DmSTING overexpression was confirmed (Figure 5C). Additionally, the dmSTING overexpression flies exhibited significantly higher levels of AttA and CecA2 mRNA, but not Drs, 24 hr following Listeria infection (Figures 5D–5F). Overexpression of dmSTING in the fat body also resulted in increased CecA2 induction following Listeria infection (Figure 5G), but Drs induction was not significantly affected (Figure 5H). Inverse to the results we observed when dmSTING was knocked down by RNAi, when dmSTING is over-expressed during Listeria infection, there is increased Relish activation (Figure 5I). Finally, we tested the effects of dmSTING over-expression on CrPV infection, and we did not observe any differences in mortality rates or antimicrobial peptide induction (Figure S5E–S5H). Taken together, these data further establish that dmSTING upregulates the induction of antimicrobial peptides that are part of the IMD/Relish pathway following Listeria infection, but not infection with IIV6, E. coli, or CrPV.

DISCUSSIONOur results indicate that Drosophila CG1667 is a functional ortholog of the mammalian STING protein. In Drosophila, dmSTING is conserved at the amino acid level (22% identity and 57% similarity) with hsSTING, especially at regions that are crucial for binding to CDNs. Recent studies have performed evolutionary analyses to confirm that functional cGAS orthologs do not exist in insects (Hancks et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2014). Interestingly, a functional cGAS-STING pathway exists in the sea anemone Nematostella vectensis. However, purified sea anemone cGAS is not active in vitro but will function in human cells, suggesting that there are additional co-factors that are required for cGAS activity (Kranzusch et al., 2015). Our experiments using dsDNA virus infection in flies containing P elements in CG7194, a putative cGAS ortholog in Drosophila (Wu et al., 2014), further suggest the lack of a cGAS-STING axis in Drosophila since we did not observe the induction of defense response genes or the dependence of dmSTING on survival to IIV6 infection. Rather, Drosophila Dicer-2, which contains a RIG-I-like helicase domain, activates an antiviral response to IIV6 through the RNAi pathway, and Dicer-2 plays a role in the defense response to both RNA and DNA virus infection in Drosophila through viral RNA sensing and subsequent degradation to protect the host (Bronkhorst et al., 2012; Deddouche et al., 2008). Taken together, our results suggest that in Drosophila, STING senses CDNs, and in the absence of a functional cGAS molecule, bacterial CDNs directly lead to STING activation and a subsequent innate immune response. We contend that dmSTING signals through the IMD pathway; however, gene expression analyses did show that some Toll-specific genes, including Relish, were less induced during Listeria infection when dmSTING was knocked down (Figure S2B), likely due to synergism between the two pathways (Tanji et al., 2007).From an evolutionary standpoint, invertebrates utilize the RNAi pathway as a defense response to exogenous DNA and RNA encountered during viral infections, whereas RNAi plays less of a role, if any, in the defense response to viral infection in vertebrates (Benitez et al., 2015; Bogerd et al., 2014). Rather, in vertebrates, antiviral immunity is mediated primarily through the RLR-MAVS axis for RNA viruses. Regarding DNA virus infection in vertebrate hosts, cGAS gained functionality in its ability to bind cytosolic DNA and metabolize CDNs as second messengers to activate STING and thus amplify antiviral immunity. As elegantly described by Kranzusch et al. (2015), while Nematostella vectensis contains a cGAS homolog (nv-cGAS) that stimulates STING signaling in human cells, nv-cGAS does not respond to dsDNA in vitro, also likely due to the absence of the zinc-ribbon domain and a positively charged N terminus, which are also lacking in CG7194 (Wu et al., 2014). Like the STING homolog in N. vectensis, the role of dmSTING in innate immunity is to sense CDNs in the absence of amplification via cGAS. It should be noted that Kranzusch et al. showed that insect STING orthologs, including dmSTING, did not associate with CDNs in their assay. However, in these experiments, a full-length dmSTING construct was used, containing the hydrophobic N-terminal transmembrane domains, which may inhibit CDN binding when the protein is not in its natural in vivo state. In addition to the classical Toll, IMD, JAK-STAT, and RNAi pathways of innate immunity in Drosophila, autophagy and apoptosis play major roles in the defense response to infection (Shelly et al., 2009; Yano et al., 2008). However, previous reports suggest that the pathway through which each function to induce a host defense response differs. For example, when chromosomal DNA escapes apoptotic degradation, there is induction of the IMD pathway, but not the Toll pathway (Mukae et al., 2002). However, in response to viral infection, an antiviral state is induced via hemocyte-mediated phagocytosis of virions and, to a minor extent, autophagy through Atg7, independent of the canonical Toll, IMD, and JAK-STAT pathways (Lamiable et al., 2016). With regards to Listeria infection, both the Toll and IMD pathways are important to combat infection (Mansfield et al., 2003), as well as autophagy (Yano et al., 2008). While both PGRP-LC and -LE induce antimicrobial peptides in response to monomeric PGN stimulation (Kaneko et al., 2006), only PGRP-LE controls autophagy during Listeria infection (Yano et al., 2008). Since mammalian STING contributes to autophagy (Konno et al., 2013), it would be prudent to test the role of dmSTING in autophagy.

Further understanding of STING function in Drosophila, and more importantly, how it functions during pathogenic infection, will have an important impact on how we develop methods to target STING for therapeutic intervention, particularly with regards to insect vector-borne diseases. Additionally, studies of dmSTING may help in uncovering evolutionarily conserved mechanisms of autoimmunity, since STING activity must be kept under tight control to prevent autoimmune disease in humans.

METHOD DETAILSCloning and cell transfectionsThe LP14056 (dmSTING) cDNA clone was obtained from the Drosophila Genomics Resource Center. For T7 transcription, S2 cell overexpression, and retroviral transformation, cloning of dmSTING cDNA into pcDNA3.1- (Invitrogen), pActin5.1B (Addgene), or pBABE-puro (Addgene (Morgenstern and Land, 1990)) was performed using standard techniques. For transgenic fly generation, dmSTING cDNA was cloned into pUAST (Drosophila Genomics Resource Center), which contains the white (w+) selection marker. Cell culture transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (for mammalian cells) or Cellfectin II (for S2 cells) (Invitrogen) as described by the product manuals using specified expression vectors or 5 μg/ml c-di-GMP (Biolog C057). Mock transfection is with transfection vehicle alone. Retroviral transformation of STING−/− MEFs was performed using the pBABE-puro vector and the Platinum E cell line for viral packaging (Cell Biolabs). Viral particles attached to STING−/− MEFs in the presence of polybrene (10 μg/ml) for 6 h, then the media containing virus was replaced with normal growth medium. 48 h p.i., puromycin (2 μg/ml) was added to the cells for selection. 48 h later, cells were passaged for further experimentation.Protein analysisProtein and immunoblot analysis was performed as previously described (Ahlers et al., 2016) using antibodies for HA (Sigma H3663), actin (Sigma A2006), IRF3 (Cell Signaling 4302S), P-IRF3 (Cell Signaling 4947S), IκB (Santa Cruz sc-371), P-IκB (Cell Signaling 2859S), and Relish-C (Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank 21F3). METHOD DETAILS

Cloning and cell transfectionsThe LP14056 (dmSTING) cDNA clone was obtained from the Drosophila Genomics Resource Center. For T7 transcription, S2 cell overexpression, and retroviral transformation, cloning of dmSTING cDNA into pcDNA3.1- (Invitrogen), pActin5.1B (Addgene), or pBABE-puro (Addgene (Morgenstern and Land, 1990)) was performed using standard techniques. For transgenic fly generation, dmSTING cDNA was cloned into pUAST (Drosophila Genomics Resource Center), which contains the white (w+) selection marker. Cell culture transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (for mammalian cells) or Cellfectin II (for S2 cells) (Invitrogen) as described by the product manuals using specified expression vectors or 5 μg/ml c-di-GMP (Biolog C057). Mock transfection is with transfection vehicle alone. Retroviral transformation of STING−/− MEFs was performed using the pBABE-puro vector and the Platinum E cell line for viral packaging (Cell Biolabs). Viral particles attached to STING−/− MEFs in the presence of polybrene (10 μg/ml) for 6 h, then the media containing virus was replaced with normal growth medium. 48 h p.i., puromycin (2 μg/ml) was added to the cells for selection. 48 h later, cells were passaged for further experimentation.
