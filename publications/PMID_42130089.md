# Evolutionary shifts in plant adaptation mediated by phytohormones.
**Authors:** Arabia A, Munné-Bosch S
**Journal:** Plant Commun (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.xplc.2026.101892](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xplc.2026.101892)

## Content

The transition of plants from aquatic to terrestrial environments imposed unprecedented ecological and physiological challenges, necessitating the evolution of sophisticated perception and response mechanisms. Phytohormones played a central role in this adaptive transition by orchestrating coordinated physiological and morphological adjustments that improved survival under variable terrestrial conditions. In this review, we examine the evolutionary origin, diversification, and functional specialization of the 10 classical phytohormones-auxin, cytokinins, gibberellins, abscisic acid, ethylene, jasmonates, salicylic acid, strigolactones, brassinosteroids, and peptide hormones-from the algal ancestors of land plants to angiosperms. The timing of the emergence of each hormonal signaling system is considered in parallel with the evolving ecological and physiological demands of terrestrial habitats, highlighting their roles in environmental sensing, stress responses across lineages, and the successful colonization of terrestrial environments and adaptation to life on land. Furthermore, we discuss how the sequential establishment and integration of these networks increased physiological complexity, coordinated growth and development, and provided competitive advantages under fluctuating conditions, underscoring the central role of integrated hormonal signaling in the evolutionary success of land plants.

The transition of plants from aquatic to terrestrial environments represents one of the most significant evolutionary events in Earth’s history, reshaping ecosystems and laying the foundation for complex terrestrial life. This transition originated more than 500 million years ago in freshwater streptophyte green algae, a lineage within Viridiplantae (green plants) that gave rise to all land plants (embryophytes) and is closely related to charophyte algae (Morris et al., 2018). Recent phylogenomic analyses have established a robust framework for understanding the evolutionary relationships among green plants and the genetic innovations underlying major evolutionary transitions in Viridiplantae (One Thousand Plant Transcriptomes Initiative, 2019). Modern land plants comprise two main lineages, bryophytes (non-vascular plants)—including mosses, liverworts, and hornworts—and tracheophytes (vascular plants)—including lycophytes and ferns—which subsequently diversified into seed plants, encompassing gymnosperms and angiosperms (Harris et al., 2022). The successful colonization of land required the evolution of sophisticated regulatory mechanisms that coordinated growth, development, and environmental responses. Among these, phytohormones play a central role, functioning at low concentrations through specific receptor-mediated pathways. The principal classes of phytohormones include auxin, cytokinins (CKs), gibberellins (GAs), abscisic acid (ABA), ethylene (ETH), salicylic acid (SA), jasmonates (JAs), brassinosteroids (BRs), peptide hormones (PEPs), and strigolactones (SLs), collectively referred to as the “classical ten” (Munné-Bosch, 2025; Table S1). Recent genomic and transcriptomic data from non-seed plant lineages have provided new insights into the origin and evolution of early land plants (Banks et al., 2011; Hori et al., 2014; Bowman et al., 2017; Wilhelmsson et al., 2017; Ali et al., 2025), revealing the progressive emergence of phytohormone signaling pathways (Figure 1).Figure 1Evolution of land plants and emergence of phytohormone signaling pathways.The phylogenetic tree depicts major green plant lineages (Viridiplantae). CKs and ETH are among the earliest-evolved hormone signaling systems, operating through two-component phosphorelay pathways that originated from ancestral bacterial signaling mechanisms. Components of CK biosynthesis, perception, and signal transduction, including homologs of histidine kinase (AHK) receptors and response regulators (RRs), were already present in streptophyte algae such as Klebsormidium flaccidum and Chara braunii (Hori et al., 2014; Nishiyama et al., 2018; Cheng et al., 2019) but have not been detected in chlorophytes (Wang et al., 2015; Bowman et al., 2017). Similarly, a functional ETH signaling system has been identified in the charophyte Spirogyra pratensis (Van de Poel et al., 2016). In addition, peptide-based signaling components are present in streptophyte algae (Ma et al., 2023), although functional PEP signaling appears to have emerged only in embryophytes (for an extensive review, see Furumizu and Shinohara, 2024). In contrast, F-box-mediated pathways represent later evolutionary innovations in plants. While auxin biosynthetic enzymes and polar transport components occur in charophytes (Żabka et al., 2016; Nishiyama et al., 2018), the intracellular auxin receptor TIR1/AFB evolved later, with its presence first established in bryophytes (Kato et al., 2018; Mutte et al., 2018). JA signaling can be traced back to early land plants, with functional COI1–JAZ–MYC–NINJA modules already present in bryophytes (Monte et al., 2018). However, in Marchantia polymorpha, the active ligand is dinor-12-oxo-10,15(Z)-phytodienoic acid (dn-OPDA) rather than jasmonoyl-isoleucine (JA-Ile), indicating that the canonical JA-Ile/COI1–JAZ module arose later in lycophytes (Chini et al., 2023). SLs are also present in bryophytes and possibly in charophytes (Delaux et al., 2012). Canonical SL signaling via D14 receptors is confirmed only in seed plants (Walker et al., 2019); however, DDK subfamily proteins within the D14/KAI2 clade in non-seed lineages may act as receptors, indicating that some form of SL perception arose early (Bythell-Douglas et al., 2017). GA signaling also relies on a small set of components, with DELLA proteins conserved across land plants (Yasumura et al., 2007). The GA receptor GID1 and a functional GA–GID1 module occur only in vascular plants, consistent with the absence of canonical bioactive GAs in bryophytes (Hirano et al., 2007). In contrast to F-box-mediated pathways, ABA signaling relies on a conserved module composed of PP2Cs, PYL receptors, and SnRK2 kinases. Although ABA has been detected in chlorophyte and streptophyte algae (Hori et al., 2014; Pichler et al., 2020), ligand-dependent modulation of PYL receptors arose in the common ancestor of land plants (Sun et al., 2019). Likewise, SA is widespread in green plants, including chlorophytes and streptophytes (Schmidt et al., 2024), but its signaling pathway was assembled stepwise, with TGA factors predating land plants and NPR receptors arising in the last common ancestor of land plants (Jia et al., 2023). Finally, BRs are among the most recently evolved phytohormones, with fully functional BRI1 receptors containing ligand-binding domains appearing only in seed plants (Wang and Mao, 2014; Zheng et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024). In this review, we explore how the emergence and diversification of phytohormone signaling pathways have shaped the evolution of stress perception and adaptive responses from streptophyte algae to angiosperms. By focusing on the 10 major phytohormones, we discuss how their stepwise appearance across plant lineages aligns with the evolving ecological and physiological demands of terrestrial environments, enabling plants to respond to diverse biotic and abiotic stresses.

Streptophyte algae represent the earliest-diverging photosynthetic lineages, and recent phylogenomic analyses have consistently identified the Zygnematophyceae as the closest algal relatives of land plants (Donoghue et al., 2021). This class is phylogenomically diverse, comprising five major orders—Spirogloeales, Serritaeniales, Zygnematales, Spirogyrales, and Desmidiales (Hess et al., 2022)—with high-quality genomes now available for species such as Spirogloea muscicola, Mesotaenium endlicherianum, Penium margaritaceum, and several Zygnema spp. (Cheng et al., 2019; Jiao et al., 2020; Feng et al., 2024). The streptophyte algal lineage also comprises Mesostigmatophyceae, Chlorokybophyceae, Klebsormidiophyceae, Charophyceae, and Coleochaetophyceae (Figure 2; Hess et al., 2022), which display diverse evolutionary trajectories and degrees of morphological complexity and provide important information on the emergence of key traits of land plants. For example, Klebsormidiophyceae include resilient, multicellular filamentous algae that have undergone several transitions between aquatic and terrestrial habitats, with early lineages likely colonizing land prior to embryophytes (Bierenbroodspot et al., 2024). In contrast, Coleochaetophyceae can develop complex thallus architectures, including branching filaments and discoid forms generated by periclinal and anticlinal cell divisions that are reminiscent of land plant morphogenesis, whereas Zygnematophyceae exhibit simpler body plans. These analyses further suggest that the most elaborate Coleochaete morphologies arose relatively recently within this lineage (Bierenbroodspot et al., 2025). Indeed, Charophyceae, exemplified by Chara, have long served as physiological models and retain numerous genes once thought to be specific to land plants (Kurtović et al., 2024). Importantly, rapid advances in genomic and multi-omic analyses of streptophyte algae now enable comparative and integrative systems biology approaches to identify conserved gene sets, shedding light on the evolutionary origins of key embryophyte traits and establishing Zygnematophyceae and related lineages as powerful comparative models for dissecting the evolution of phytohormone signaling (see Goldbecker and de Vries, 2025; Kunz et al., 2025).Figure 2Hormonal signaling components that predate the emergence of land plants.The upper panel illustrates representative marine, freshwater, and terrestrial habitats, indicating the types of streptophyte algae inhabiting each environment. CK perception and signaling rely on a bacterial-derived two-component system, in which cyclase/histidine kinase receptors activate RRs through histidine phosphotransfer proteins, ultimately modulating gene expression (Hwang and Sheen, 2001). This signaling system is ancestral, and classical CK receptors are highly conserved across several representative species, from the basal streptophyte alga K. flaccidum to the eudicot Arabidopsis (Li et al., 2022). Reference genomes of streptophyte algae reveal the presence of CK compounds and canonical signaling components, including tRNA-IPT and LOG enzymes for biosynthesis, cyclase/histidine kinase receptors and histidine phosphotransfer proteins, and type-B and type-A RRs (RRBs and RRAs; Figure 2). However, land-plant-specific biosynthetic genes such as IPT and CYP735A appear only in embryophytes (Jiao et al., 2020). Similarly, streptophyte algae contain mainly tRNA-derived CKs, including N6-(Δ2-isopentenyl)-adenine (iP), cis-zeatin (cZ), and methylthio derivatives (MeS-iP and MeS-cZ), whereas trans-zeatin (tZ) and cZ/tZ-O-glucosides are found only in land plants (Schmidt et al., 2024). This predominance of cZ in algae could reflect their limited biosynthetic capacity and suggests that full CK phytohormone identity arose with the earliest land plants. In non-vascular plants, such as mosses and liverworts, cZ and its conjugates remain dominant, as observed in some green algae (von Schwartzenberg et al., 2007; Aki et al., 2019), whereas vascular plants are dominated by tZ, followed by iP-type CKs (Ko et al., 2014). Functional studies in Coleochaetales show that treatments with classical CKs, such as tZ and iP, promote structured discoid growth, with tZ eliciting the strongest response. Interestingly, adenine, typically used as a negative control, also induces similar morphological changes, suggesting that the canonical CK signaling pathway is present but exhibits low ligand specificity in this algal group (Pinto et al., 2023). This low specificity is consistent with the broader evolutionary pattern, in which the specificity of CK receptor–ligand interactions increased over time, with land plants showing progressive specialization toward certain CK types, particularly tZ over cZ, reflecting a gradual refinement of the CK signaling system (Lomin et al., 2021).

Despite the long-standing presence of CK biosynthesis and signaling components in streptophytes, most studies have focused on their roles in land plants (Bartrina et al., 2025; Khanna et al., 2025), and relatively few studies have explored their function in the algal ancestors of terrestrial plants. However, early evidence indicates that CKs in green algae may play a role in coping with abiotic stresses. For instance, studies on the Zygnematophyceae representatives Mougeotia and Spirogyra have revealed pronounced changes in plastid morphology under heat stress, accompanied by significant shifts in central metabolites (de Vries et al., 2020). Both species encode AHK homologs involved in canonical CK signaling, including CHASE-domain-containing receptors, which are strongly differentially expressed under heat stress. Hence, it is conceivable that, in both species, AHK homologs modulate phosphorelay signal transduction, thereby contributing to the regulation of stress responses and temperature acclimation. Notably, the responsiveness of AHKs to cold stress has also been reported in these algae. For example, a CHASE-containing AHK ortholog in Zygnema circumcarinatum was upregulated 1.9-fold upon cold exposure (de Vries et al., 2018a). Together, these observations indicate that interconnections among CKs, AHKs, and temperature-related stress responses represent a conserved feature in Zygnematophyceae, highlighting a possible ancestral role of CKs in mediating tolerance to fluctuating thermal conditions, a trait likely crucial for the colonization of terrestrial habitats (Figure 2). Indeed, CK-mediated responses to temperature stress have also been reported in later-diverging lineages such as seed plants, in which CKs modulate the expression of phosphorelay components and CK response factors involved in stress tolerance (Jeon and Kim, 2013; Gupta and Rashotte, 2014). Furthermore, the transition from aquatic to terrestrial habitats also exposed algal ancestors of land plants to heterogeneous nutrient availability, particularly nitrate, phosphate, and carbon sources. Streptophyte algae such as Klebsormidium nitens respond to nutrient fluctuations at the cellular level, whereas in M. polymorpha, CK-responsive networks coordinate systemic responses across specialized cell types, promoting adaptive growth under nutrient limitation (Dong et al., 2024). This suggests that nutrient sensing and transcriptional adjustment were already present in streptophyte algae, but their integration into organism-wide CK-mediated regulation evolved in land plants, a mechanism that continues to operate in higher plants to coordinate uptake and distribution of essential nutrients during environmental stress (Argueso and Kieber, 2024).

PEPs are small, secreted polypeptide molecules that act as intercellular signaling agents, typically ranging from a few to around 250 amino acids in length. Most well-characterized PEPs are secreted peptides that are perceived by leucine-rich repeat (LRR) receptors at the plasma membrane. These include cysteine-rich peptides (CRPs), which are stabilized by disulfide bridges, and post-translationally modified peptides (see Zhang et al., 2025b). The ancestry of several peptide families predates the divergence of vascular plants and bryophytes, suggesting that some signaling peptides may have been present in ancestral algal lineages. Consistent with this idea, bioinformatic searches have identified CLAVATA3/EMBRYO SURROUNDING REGION-related-like sequences in unicellular chlorophyte algae, including Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, Coccomyxa subellipsoidea, and Dunaliella salina (Oelkers et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2020b). In addition, several post-translational modification enzymes required for peptide maturation, such as tyrosylprotein sulfotransferases and subtilases, are conserved in charophytes and chlorophytes (Xu et al., 2019; Furumizu and Shinohara, 2024). LRR–RLK receptors are also broadly conserved in land plants and most charophytes, although their occurrence in chlorophytes is sporadic (Furumizu and Shinohara, 2024; Figure 2). Moreover, Furumizu et al. (2021) identified LRR–RLKs containing more than 20 LRRs in streptophyte algae and proposed that changes in peptide signaling systems might be linked to cell-to-cell communication pathways that facilitated the evolution from ancestral algae to land plants. Moreover, CRPs, which have broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, are also widely distributed across plant lineages, from algae to eudicots, although comparative genomics shows that CRP gene copy numbers vary widely among species and are often associated with genome size, reflecting expansions through whole-genome and tandem duplications (Ma et al., 2023). Despite the lack of functional data on algal PEPs, their conserved sequences and the presence of modification enzymes and receptors in streptophyte algae suggest that peptide signaling mechanisms were already emerging before land colonization (Figure 2). Nevertheless, further experimental studies are required to determine their biosynthesis, concentrations, and peptide types in these algae lineages.

The role of the gaseous phytohormone ETH in abiotic stress, well documented in late-diverging plants, is also observed in liverworts, indicating that the stress-related role of ETH production and signaling likely predates the divergence of bryophytes from the land plant lineage, at least 450 million years ago (Bharadwaj et al., 2022). Ethylene biosynthesis and signaling pathways are particularly well characterized in angiosperms (Dubois et al., 2018), but ETH signaling has also been detected in streptophyte algae such as Spirogyra (Zygnematophyceae) (Timme and Delwiche, 2010), K. flaccidum (Klebsormidiophyceae) (Hori et al., 2014; Ju et al., 2015), and K. crenulatum (Holzinger et al., 2014). A cyanobacterial origin has been proposed for ETH receptors (Mount and Chang, 2002), suggesting that they were derived from the plastid ancestor and retained in charophytes and land plants, but lost in chlorophytes and red algae. Key elements of this pathway show a clear evolutionary pattern: homologs of ETHYLENE INSENSITIVE 2 are absent in chlorophytes and early-diverging streptophytes (Chlorokybophyceae and Mesostigmatophyceae; Wang et al., 2020) but present in Zygnematophyceae (Jiao et al., 2020), indicating that a complete ETH signaling module was established in late-diverging streptophyte algae closely related to embryophytes (Figure 2). This conservation raises the question about the roles of ETH in Zygnematophyceae, which may provide insights into its functions in algal growth and stress responses. The predominance of streptophyte algae in freshwater and semi-terrestrial environments is consistent with the hypothesis that early photosynthetic eukaryotes originated in low-salinity habitats (Sánchez-Baracaldo et al., 2017), where fluctuations in water availability, light intensity, and nutrient conditions would have imposed significant physiological challenges (Figure 2). Unlike land plants, these organisms lack vascular tissues and specialized organs, and their stress responses occur largely at the level of individual cells or filaments (Permann et al., 2022). Water availability has remained a major constraint throughout plant evolution as early land plants, such as bryophytes, are small and shallow-rooted, exposing them to recurrent drought and flooding, while terrestrial plants evolved adaptive responses to submergence. ETH signaling plays a central role in these responses. ETR-HKs are essential for submergence and osmostress responses in both Zygnematophyceae (Rieseberg et al., 2025) and early land plants such as the moss Physcomitrella patens (Toriyama et al., 2022), highlighting their evolutionary conservation. Indeed, in Zygnematophyceae, ETH promotes cell elongation within filaments under abiotic stresses, including salinity, pH change, light limitation, and nutrient or osmotic stress, as demonstrated in Spirogyra (Van de Poel et al., 2016). This elongation likely represents an adaptive response, allowing filaments to access improved light and oxygen conditions, and is accompanied by the upregulation of cell-wall remodeling enzymes and stress-responsive genes, such as expansins and xyloglucan endotransglucosylases/hydrolases. Similar adaptive elongation responses are observed in mosses, as illustrated by the effect of ETH on filament architecture and the expression of aquaporin-encoding genes (PpPIP2;2 and PpPIP2;3), paralleling the effects of submergence (Yasumura et al., 2012). Thus, ETH-promoted cell elongation is a conserved streptophyte response that contributes to abiotic stress adaptation and supports developmental growth. Experimental evidence collected over several decades has established ETH-promoted underwater elongation as one of the most compelling examples of hormone-mediated stress adaptation in plants (Jackson, 2008). This conserved function was later co-opted and diversified in land plants to support developmental growth, including rachis elongation in ferns (Cookson and Osborne, 1979), coleoptile and internode elongation in monocots such as deep-water rice (Van der Straeten et al., 2001; Qiao et al., 2024), hypocotyl elongation in Arabidopsis (Ma et al., 2018), and basal fruit elongation (Xin et al., 2019).

In addition to regulating cell elongation, ETH downregulates photosynthetic capacity and chlorophyll biosynthesis in Spirogyra under abiotic stresses (Van de Poel et al., 2016). Likewise, transcriptomic analyses in the zygnematophyte Mesotaenium revealed a conserved regulatory hub linking plastids, photosynthesis, and ETH-associated factors (Dadras et al., 2023). The control of photosynthesis is one of the primary and most conserved processes governed by ETH across evolutionarily distant organisms, from cyanobacteria to streptophyte algae and land plants (Lacey and Binder, 2016; Ceusters and Van de Poel, 2018; Bharadwaj et al., 2022), where ETH exerts species-specific effects on photosynthesis, often promoting leaf senescence under stress to optimize resource remobilization (Mohorović et al., 2024). This underscores the long-standing role of ETH in coordinating photosynthetic activity and stress resilience alongside cell elongation.

ABA, JAs, and SA are master regulators of plant stress responses, integrating environmental and metabolic signals to coordinate tolerance to abiotic stresses, including drought, salinity, and temperature fluctuations, as well as defense against biotic challenges (Qi et al., 2016; Duhan et al., 2025; He et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). A shared feature of these phytohormones is that their biosynthesis occurs partially or entirely within plastids (Iuchi et al., 2001; Wildermuth et al., 2001; Chini et al., 2018), underscoring the deep evolutionary integration between primary metabolism and stress signaling. Land plants exert tight regulatory control over plastids, which originated from an ancient endosymbiotic event and subsequently transferred most of their genome to the nucleus, resulting in their semi-autonomy and functional dependence on nuclear gene expression (Pogson and Albrecht, 2011; see Figure 3A). In return, chloroplasts serve as metabolic hubs that are essential for plant growth, defense responses, and stress adaptation. Their activity is coordinated with the nucleus through retrograde (plastid-to-nucleus) and anterograde (nucleus-to-plastid) signaling (Jiang and Dehesh, 2021). Terrestrial stressors, such as drought and excessive light, posed inescapable threats to land plants by disrupting photosynthetic electron transport, redox homeostasis, and key metabolic processes (Chan et al., 2016; de Vries et al., 2016). In response, chloroplasts act as environmental sensors that initiate retrograde signaling to fine-tune nuclear gene expression. The carotenoid-derived signal ABA has been known for decades as one of the major stress-responsive phytohormones. Upon ABA perception via PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors, PP2C phosphatases are inhibited, thereby activating SnRK2 kinases that phosphorylate transcription factors, such as AREB/ABFs, and regulate hundreds of stress-responsive nuclear genes (Umezawa et al., 2009; Yamburenko et al., 2015). Emerging evidence shows that plastid-to-nucleus communication systems were established prior to the divergence of vascular plants. Zhao et al. (2019) showed that the core SAL1 (nucleotide phosphatase)-PAP (3′-phosphoadenosine-5′-phosphate) retrograde signaling pathway originated in streptophyte algae, predating land colonization. Comparative analyses across mosses, ferns, and angiosperms indicate that this pathway is highly conserved, and functional assays revealed that PAP regulates stomatal closure through conserved secondary messengers and ion transport mechanisms. As stomata arose early during terrestrialization, an ancestral SAL1–PAP module could have linked chloroplast redox and metabolic status to stomatal behavior, intersecting with core ABA signaling (Zhao et al., 2019) and thereby facilitated plant adaptation to fluctuating terrestrial environments, including the management of water stress. Consistent with this view, transcriptomic and comparative genomic analyses indicate that plastid-centered stress-signaling networks were already sophisticated in the algal relatives of land plants. The streptophyte algae Zygnema deploy hallmark land-plant stress-response genes under high light and cold and allocate a disproportionately large fraction of their transcriptional budget to plastid-targeted proteins (de Vries et al., 2018a). These algae also encode an unexpectedly rich set of anterograde and retrograde signaling components, including a PYR/PYL/RCAR-like receptor. This suggests that the regulatory framework connecting plastid stress to nuclear gene expression arose before plants colonized land. However, these ancestral receptors function ligand-independently and lack the high affinity ABA binding seen in land-plant receptors, indicating that canonical ABA sensing evolved later (Sun et al., 2019). Together, these findings imply that the algal precursors of land plants possessed proto-ABA signaling modules and plastid-nucleus communication pathways already responsive to drought-associated cues such as high light and oxidative stress. The integration of these ancestral networks with bona fide ABA perception during early embryophyte evolution likely provided a crucial foundation for terrestrial drought adaptation. Furthermore, ABA biosynthesis and several core signaling components, such as PP2C, ABI3, and ABI1 are well characterized in bryophytes (Khandelwal et al., 2010; Komatsu et al., 2013). Consistently, functional studies in these early-diverging land plants demonstrate that these modules are conserved and operational. For example, in P. patens, disruption of PpABA1 reduces osmotic acclimation and dehydration tolerance (Takezawa et al., 2015), while PpSnRK2 and PpABI3 mutants show altered ABA-mediated non-photochemical quenching (NPQ) and xanthophyll cycle responses (Maeng et al., 2025). Vegetative tissues in mosses thus exhibit a conserved functional link between ABA, ABI3, and desiccation tolerance analogous to that observed in angiosperms (Khandelwal et al., 2010; Bedi et al., 2016). Likewise, in M. polymorpha, Mpaba1 lines display reduced desiccation tolerance and altered sugar responses (Khatun et al., 2023), reflecting ABA–sugar crosstalk. Thus, in liverworts, ABA-induced accumulation of sugars and LEA-like proteins helps protect cells from dehydration (Jahan et al., 2019; Khatun et al., 2023), a mechanism that is well documented in angiosperms through several ABA-biosynthetic and ABA-insensitive Arabidopsis mutants showing altered sugar responses (Arenas-Huertero et al., 2000; Laby et al., 2000; Dekkers et al., 2008; Zheng et al., 2010). Moreover, MpPYL1 overexpression enhances ABA sensitivity and stress tolerance (Jahan et al., 2019), and similar phenotypic outputs are observed in angiosperms such as tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) and rice (Oryza sativa), in which overexpression of PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors enhances drought tolerance (Kim et al., 2012a; González-Guzmán et al., 2014), highlighting the evolutionary conservation of ABA signaling pathways.

SA accumulation is also strongly induced by chloroplast perturbations, including redox imbalances and reactive oxygen species generation (Wu et al., 2012). SA then activates downstream signaling cascades, notably through the NPR1 receptor, which translocates to the nucleus to regulate pathogenesis-related and defense genes (Ding et al., 2018). Major retrograde signaling pathways differentially regulate SA biosynthesis and signaling to activate growth inhibition and acclimation responses. Comparative genomic analyses indicate that NPR genes emerged in land plants, with homologs present in mosses and liverworts but absent from hornworts, suggesting that they were lost in this lineage (Jia et al., 2023). Functional studies in the liverwort M. polymorpha provide critical insights into the early evolution of this pathway. AtNPR1 and MpNPR share partially conserved biochemical properties, including SA-binding activity, yet MpNPR negatively regulates bacterial resistance in this species (Jeon et al., 2024). Remarkably, Mpnpr mutants exhibit enhanced thermomorphogenesis, reduced far-red-induced shade avoidance, and delayed far-red-dependent gametangiophore formation. Comparable thermomorphogenic phenotypes are observed in Atnpr1, suggesting that NPR-mediated regulation of temperature and light-quality responses is conserved across mosses, liverworts, and vascular plants, thus representing an ancient function (Jeon et al., 2024). These findings suggest that NPR genes were likely first recruited to modulate abiotic (rather than biotic) stress pathways, particularly responses to fluctuating environmental conditions, which were crucial for the successful colonization of terrestrial habitats. During the transition from deep sea to shallow waters and from the aquatic environment to land, the ancestors of green plants encountered stronger and more variable light and temperature regimes (Zhang et al., 2022). Building on this, Jia et al. (2023) demonstrated that high light triggers SA accumulation across representative green plant species, including the chlorophyte C. reinhardtii, the bryophyte M. polymorpha, and angiosperms such as Arabidopsis thaliana and O. sativa. In all cases, exposure to high light significantly increases SA levels. High irradiance also reduces the maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II photochemistry (Fv/Fm), which can be partially rescued by exogenous SA. SA-deficient mutants, including Craim1 (C. reinhardtii) and Osaim1 (O. sativa), exhibit a significantly lower Fv/Fm than wild-type plants, confirming the key role of SA in photoprotection. These findings indicate that SA-mediated responses to high light are conserved across chlorophytes and land plants, with the AIM1-dependent β-oxidation pathway being essential for photoprotection and the adaptation of early-diverging green algae to terrestrial environments. The emergence of the NPR–TGA signaling module in land plants strengthened SA-dependent responses, and subsequent functional diversification of NPR proteins during spermatophyte evolution allowed the assembly of more complex stress-response networks, thereby integrating SA signaling with nuclear gene regulation to enhance environmental acclimation. In Arabidopsis, this integration is exemplified by retrograde signaling triggered by damaged photosystem II cores, which promotes SA accumulation and activates SA-responsive nuclear genes, linking chloroplast photoinhibition directly to nuclear transcriptional responses (Duan et al., 2019). A parallel increase in signaling complexity is evident in the evolution of stomatal responses. In seed plants, SA induces stomatal closure through an NPR-dependent pathway (Prodhan et al., 2020), a mechanism that enhances tolerance to high light stress and desiccation in addition to pathogen attack. In contrast, fern and lycophyte stomata do not respond to SA because they lack several key downstream components of this response, including SA-induced NO accumulation, K+ efflux, and vacuolar ion compartmentation, found only in seed plants (Zhang et al., 2025a). This pattern suggests that the integration of SA into guard-cell signaling is a later evolutionary innovation of spermatophytes, reinforcing the progressively expanded role of SA in environmental acclimation during land-plant evolution.

JAs are oxylipin phytohormones that orchestrate plant defense responses against herbivores and necrotrophic pathogens and regulate various aspects of development as well as acclimation to abiotic stress (Wasternack and Song, 2017). JA biosynthesis begins in plastids from α-linolenic acid via the OPDA pathway, after which intermediates are converted to JA and subsequently to the bioactive conjugate JA-Ile in the peroxisome and cytosol, respectively. As JA-Ile accumulates in the cytosol and enters the nucleus, it is perceived by the COI1–JAZ co-receptor complex, whose activation triggers JAZ degradation and releases transcription factors, predominantly bHLHs and MYBs, to activate JA-responsive defense genes (Howe et al., 2018; Kılıç et al., 2025). COI1–JAZ modules are conserved across land plants, although ligand specificity has diversified, with bryophytes and lycophytes predominantly perceiving dn-cis-OPDA and dn-iso-OPDA, whereas the lycophyte Selaginella moellendorffii is so far the only species known to synthesize both dn-iso-OPDA and JA-Ile (Pratiwi et al., 2017; Monte et al., 2018). Additional ligands have been found in certain plant lineages, such as the eicosapentaenoic acid derivative Δ4-dn-OPDA in Marchantia and the moss Polytrichastrum formosum (Kneeshaw et al., 2022; Chini et al., 2023). The evolution of adaptive interactions with microbes was a key factor enabling plant terrestrialization. In M. polymorpha, necrotrophic fungal pathogens such as Irpex lacteus trigger JA-type defenses, strongly inducing the oxylipin pathway and the bioactive liverwort JA dn-OPDA. This response depends on the conserved COI1–JAZ module, as mutants impaired in JA perception show increased susceptibility (Matsui et al., 2020). Similarly, Mpcoi1 and Mpdes6fad5 mutants, which are deficient in long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acid (LCPUFA)-derived Δ4-dn-OPDA biosynthesis, are more susceptible to herbivory, with Spodoptera exigua larvae growing faster than on wild type (Kneeshaw et al., 2022). By contrast, Mpcoi1 mutants display no altered susceptibility to the hemibiotroph Pseudomonas syringae DC3000, although exogenous OPDA induces hypersusceptibility in a COI1-dependent manner (Giménez-Ibáñez et al., 2019), paralleling that observed in A. thaliana Atcoi1 mutants, which show enhanced resistance to Fusarium oxysporum (Thatcher et al., 2009). These results suggest that JA signaling mediates a trade-off between defenses against necrotrophs and hemibiotrophs, a regulatory strategy conserved across land plants. Furthermore, in seed-plant defense responses, JA and SA signals have generally been shown to be antagonistic (Van der Does et al., 2013; Nomoto et al., 2021), and similar antagonism has been observed in bryophytes. For instance, in M. polymorpha, SA and dn-OPDA signals antagonize one another, as treatment with OPDA compromises SA-dependent gene expression and promotes susceptibility to P. syringae pv. tomato DC3000, whereas SA treatment suppresses dn-OPDA-dependent gene activation (Giménez-Ibáñez et al., 2019). Similarly, SA treatment promotes infection by a necrotrophic fungus, an effect that is mitigated by co-application of dn-OPDA (Matsui et al., 2020). These findings indicate that antagonistic crosstalk between SA and oxylipin pathways represents an ancient mechanism that likely fine-tuned immunity in the last common ancestor of land plants, even when the specific JA hormone differed. Beyond bryophytes, ferns such as Adiantum capillus-veneris possess a complete JA biosynthesis pathway, including the conversion of OPDA to JA-Ile, which acts as a bioactive JA (Fang et al., 2022). Wounding rapidly triggers JA accumulation in ferns, contributing to their notable resistance to pathogens, which is generally greater than that of flowering plants (Antonovics, 2020). Interestingly, although JA-Ile biosynthesis from OPDA is more pronounced in ferns than in lycophytes, the OPDA-to-JA-Ile ratio remains higher than in seed plants, indicating comparatively slower conversion and suggesting evolutionary modulation of JA signaling strength across lineages (Fang et al., 2022). However, SA/JA antagonism is less studied in ferns, and it was not detected in the water fern Azolla filiculoides (de Vries et al., 2018b). In addition to their roles in pathogen and herbivore defense, dn-OPDA and OPDA activate a COI1-independent pathway that regulates thermotolerance genes and protects plants against heat stress. This signaling mechanism is conserved across streptophytes (including M. polymorpha), A. thaliana, and the charophyte alga K. nitens, and predates the COI1-dependent JA pathway (Monte et al., 2020). Hence, these findings indicate that cyclopentenone-regulated COI1-independent signaling represents an ancient mechanism that enhanced plant survival under high temperatures and was likely important for stress adaptation.

The emergence of bryophytes marked a major evolutionary transition, introducing new levels of developmental complexity and traits such as multicellular embryos, persistent apical meristems, rhizoid-based anchorage, and protective cuticles and stomata that support stable terrestrial growth and resilience to desiccation (Ligrone et al., 2012). Their dominant, free-living gametophytes display a striking diversity of growth forms (Figure 4A; Wang et al., 2022). These innovations established the developmental contexts in which hormonal signaling networks began to coordinate more complex patterns of growth and stress adaptation. Auxin is widely regarded as one of the key molecular advances that enabled land plants to generate increasingly complex body plans (Mutte et al., 2018). Interestingly, some components of auxin signaling, including directional transport mediated by PIN carriers, are already present in complex streptophyte algae such as C. braunii (Kurtović et al., 2025). Moreover, AUX/IAA repressors and ARF transcription factors are detectable in charophytes, but the TIR1/AFB receptors, which establish the canonical auxin-signaling module, originated in the common ancestor of embryophytes (Bowman et al., 2017; Flores-Sandoval et al., 2018). A distinctive feature of auxin signaling is its capacity to form directional fluxes through the polarized activity of PIN efflux carriers, guiding cell expansion, cell differentiation, and developmental patterning. Recent work in the liverwort M. polymorpha demonstrates that even a minimal auxin signaling system can control three-dimensional plant architecture (Suzuki et al., 2023). For example, MpTIR1 loss-of-function mutants retain the ability to undergo cell division but fail to form organs, instead producing undifferentiated cell masses. This phenotype is consistent with earlier observations in Marchantia (Kato et al., 2015) and with findings in A. thaliana, where simultaneous loss of all six TIR1/AFB homologs impairs embryogenesis (Prigge et al., 2020). Similarly, in Physcomitrium patens, impaired auxin signaling suppresses caulonemal differentiation, altering protonemal patterning (Prigge et al., 2010). Together, these findings indicate that, across bryophytes, auxin is essential for spatial organization, stem cell maintenance, and complex three-dimensional growth, highlighting a conserved role in shaping plant architecture early in land-plant evolution (Figure 4B). Furthermore, auxin regulates rhizoid emergence and positioning in bryophytes, with high levels of auxin promoting rhizoid formation and impaired auxin signaling causing ectopic rhizoids (Sakakibara et al., 2003; Lavy and Estelle, 2016). Basipetal auxin transport, partly mediated by PIN proteins, establishes local auxin maxima that determine the sites of rhizoid initiation, whereas downstream transcription factors such as PpRSL and PpLRL drive differentiation (Sakakibara et al., 2003; Jang and Dolan, 2011; Thelander et al., 2018). Branching is another key aspect of plant architecture, shaping morphology and resource acquisition, and allowing plants to adapt to ecological niches (Barthélémy and Caraglio, 2007). In vascular plants, auxin gradients generated by PIN-mediated polar transport create minima in leaf axils, defining sites for lateral branch initiation and maintaining apical dominance (Ferguson and Beveridge, 2009; Wang et al., 2014; Yadav et al., 2023). Recent work in the moss P. patens has shown that similar principles operate in bryophytes, although via mechanisms adapted to their small, modular growth forms (Abitbol-Spangaro et al., 2025). Branches arise in the axils of specific phyllids, whereas apical, inter-branch, and basal zones remain branch-free. Auxin is produced both at the apices and at the gametophore base, and the auxin biosynthesis-deficient TAR and YUC mutants exhibit reduced apical dominance and increased branching (Thelander et al., 2022; Abitbol-Spangaro et al., 2025). Consistently, A. thaliana TAA1/TAR and YUC mutants display reduced apical dominance and enhanced branching (Yadav et al., 2023). In mosses, however, branching is regulated mainly by symplastic auxin diffusion through plasmodesmata, with local PIN-mediated gradients establishing branch-inhibition zones rather than the long-range PIN-mediated transport as in vascular plants, reflecting adaptations to plant size and morphology (Figure 4C). Previous work in P. patens demonstrated that PIN-mediated auxin transport is also essential for gametophore development and contributes to sporophyte architecture, with loss-of-function mutants exhibiting ectopic branching (Bennett et al., 2014). Together, these regulatory mechanisms enable bryophytes to modulate apical dominance and branching, thereby shaping colony architecture that is critical for survival, as it optimizes light capture, hydration, and space occupation. This developmental plasticity is broadly conserved across land plants and underpins the capacity to adjust growth in response to environmental constraints (Michaud et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2024).

Auxin alone does not fully explain apical dominance and branching control, which also involve other signaling molecules, particularly SLs and CKs (Figure 4B). Auxin stimulates SL biosynthesis, which suppresses branch outgrowth, whereas CKs generally promote it (Ferguson and Beveridge, 2009; Bertheloot et al., 2020). In mosses, SLs act as local and inter-colony signals, controlling protonemal branching and colony expansion. The ccd8 knockout mutants fail to respond to neighboring colonies, a phenotype rescued by exogenous SLs (Proust et al., 2011). Similarly, in vascular plants, SLs integrate environmental cues such as light quality and nutrient availability to modulate branching (Drummond et al., 2015). SLs are also key regulators of rhizoid elongation in bryophytes (Delaux et al., 2012). In addition, ABA acts as a key regulator of growth under environmental stress. In vascular plants, ABA accumulation under low red light, a signal of competitive shading, suppresses local auxin biosynthesis and transport in axillary buds, thereby reinforcing bud inhibition (Yao and Finlayson, 2015). In bryophytes, ABA similarly restricts vegetative growth under stress by inhibiting cell polarity and asymmetric expansion (Figure 4B; Beier et al., 2023). The ABA-responsive transcription factor PpABI3 partially mediates ABA-dependent inhibition of vegetative growth by regulating auxin synthesis, transport, and signaling. Δabi3 mutants display elevated auxin levels and accelerated chloronema-to-caulonema differentiation, highlighting ABA–auxin crosstalk in moss development (Zhao et al., 2018).

Plant architecture is also shaped by organ orientation and spatial arrangement, which are crucial for optimizing resource acquisition. In angiosperms, PIN-mediated auxin redistribution underlies both phototropic and gravitropic growth (Kleine-Vehn et al., 2010; Rakusová et al., 2016). In M. polymorpha, MpPIN1 establishes directional auxin fluxes that orient gametangiophore growth in response to light and gravity (Fisher et al., 2023). Beyond endogenous developmental regulation, auxin and SLs also act as rhizosphere signaling molecules to mediate plant–microbe interactions. The SL bryosymbiol, identified in the liverwort Marchantia paleacea and conserved in vascular plants, indicates that the rhizosphere-signaling function of SLs originated in the common ancestor of land plants and is enhanced under arbuscular mycorrhizal-permissive conditions (Kodama et al., 2022). In M. paleacea, SLs are synthesized in basal ventral tissues and rhizoids, where they promote communication with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and phosphate uptake (Yoda et al., 2025). In higher plants, low root auxin levels are associated with reduced SL production, highlighting conserved crosstalk between developmental and environmental signals (Foo, 2013; Li et al., 2023). Hence, rhizosphere signaling is an ancestral function of SLs modulated by auxin, contributing to nutrient acquisition and plant–microbe interactions (Figure 4B).

The evolution of vascular plants marked another major advance in land-plant diversification. Unlike bryophytes, early tracheophytes developed roots and vascular systems, enabling efficient transport, structural support, and the development of larger, more complex sporophytes with branched fronds (Woudenberg et al., 2022). In bryophytes, the gametophyte is the dominant, long-lived photosynthetic generation, whereas the sporophyte remains small, short-lived, and nutritionally dependent. The evolution of vascular tissues allowed the sporophyte to become dominant, introducing new developmental and regulatory challenges (Plackett et al., 2015). This shift paved the way for seed plants, in which the sporophyte became even larger and more complex, and the gametophyte was reduced and fully enclosed within it. These changes required more sophisticated hormonal and developmental control to coordinate growth, reproduction, and environmental responses. Among classical plant growth hormones, GAs emerged later in vascular plants (Yoshida et al., 2018). The evolution of GA biosynthesis, together with the canonical GA–DELLA signaling module first detected in lycophytes dating to approximately 430 million years ago (Yasumura et al., 2007; Gao et al., 2008), provided key regulatory control over cell elongation, organ growth, and reproductive development (Cheng et al., 2004; Shi et al., 2020; Fukazawa et al., 2021). However, the possibility that DELLA proteins are regulated non-canonically in non-vascular plants cannot be excluded, as recently demonstrated in the liverwort M. polymorpha, where inositol pyrophosphate messengers mediate DELLA degradation and thereby promote thallus development (Rana et al., 2025). DELLA proteins also act as conserved transcriptional hubs. Their capacity to interact with multiple transcription factors was already present in ancestral and bryophyte lineages (Hernández-García et al., 2024) and has been maintained across land plants, underpinning the broad diversity of GA-regulated processes (Briones-Moreno et al., 2023). This suggests that alternative regulatory mechanisms for DELLA predated GA-dependent signaling, which later became central in vascular plants. In an evolutionary context, the emergence of GA signaling in vascular plants coincided with the evolution of larger, more complex sporophytes, suggesting that this pathway was a key driver of their morphological and developmental innovation. Moreover, GA-mediated growth is sensitive to environmental cues such as light quality and intensity, enabling plants to adjust organ elongation accordingly (De Lucas et al., 2008; Griffiths et al., 2024). GA signaling also acts in concert with earlier-evolved phytohormones, such as auxin, CK, and SLs, to coordinate organ elongation and enhance overall plant vigor (Figure 5). Furthermore, sporophyte dominance in ferns underscores the need for tightly coordinated developmental transitions, such as spore germination and gametophyte differentiation, in which GA signaling plays a central regulatory role. In Anemia phyllitidis, inhibition of GA biosynthesis with AMO-1618 strongly suppresses light-induced germination, and this effect is fully rescued by exogenous GA (Weinberg and Voeller, 1969). GA also activates stable mRNAs in dry spores, providing templates for the synthesis of proteins necessary for the early events of germination (Raghavan, 1977). Indeed, in Lygodium japonicum, red light triggers GA biosynthesis via the phytochrome system, which in turn promotes spore germination (Kagawa and Michizo, 1991). In seed plants, GA-dependent mechanisms observed in fern spore germination are retained but embedded in a seed-based framework, in which embryos enter ABA-dependent dormancy until environmental cues (such as light, temperature, and moisture) modulate GA signaling and trigger germination (Oh et al., 2007; Seo et al., 2016; Vaistij et al., 2018; Zhang and Zhu, 2022). Thus, compared with ferns and lycophytes, seed plants evolved a more tightly regulated and environmentally responsive germination program, underscoring the evolutionary significance of seeds.

After germination, GAs continue to play crucial roles during the gametophyte phase, in which sexual differentiation must be tightly regulated to ensure successful fertilization and maintain genetic diversity in seed-free vascular plants. In lycophytes such as Selaginella, GA treatments promote proper microspore wall formation, whereas GA-biosynthesis inhibitors disrupt this process (Aya et al., 2011). In ferns, GA pathways have been co-opted into an antheridiogen-based communication system that regulates population-level sex ratios. Many homosporous fern species release GA-related antheridiogens that induce male development in younger gametophytes, thereby promoting outcrossing. The best-characterized example is L. japonicum, in which the antheridiogen GA9 methyl ester is taken up by immature gametophytes and converted into bioactive GA4 to trigger antheridium formation (Tanaka et al., 2014). Consistent with this, inhibition of GA biosynthesis reduces male differentiation in Ceratopteris (Warne and Hickok, 1989), and GA3 accumulates during the formation of both archegonia and antheridia (Kosakivska et al., 2020). These findings indicate that GA-related pathways, originally regulate spore and microspore development in lycophytes, also control sexual differentiation in ferns through antheridiogen-dependent signaling. In addition, ABA suppresses antheridiogen-induced male development in Ceratopteris (Hickok, 1983), suggesting potential antagonism between ABA and GA in fern sex determination, analogous to their antagonistic roles in seed germination, although this possibility remains to be explored.

Beyond the gametophyte, GAs also promote reproductive competence at the sporophyte level. For example, exogenous GA treatments in Ceratopteris thalictroides accelerate fertile frond development and shorten the life cycle (Stein, 1971), indicating that GA-mediated control of reproductive development operates across both generations. Reproductive development is one of the most important physiological processes involving GAs, and a comparable central role for GA is observed in seed plants. In seed plants, GA signaling is critical for male reproductive development, regulating microsporogenesis, pollen maturation, pollen tube growth, and anther dehiscence, which together ensure pollen viability and successful fertilization (Liu et al., 2017; Marciniak and Przedniczek, 2021). GAs also contribute to female reproductive development by promoting ovule initiation and development (Barro-Trastoy et al., 2022). With the evolution of flowers in angiosperms, GA signaling assumed a central role in promoting the transition to flowering by activating key floral identity genes such as FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT), LEAFY (LFY), and SUPPRESSOR OF OVEREXPRESSION OF CONSTANS1 (SOC1) (Fukazawa et al., 2021). GAs also drive fruit set by stimulating ovary growth and tissue expansion (Shinozaki et al., 2020), even in the absence of fertilization, as observed in species such as tomato and cucumber (Martí et al., 2007; Su et al., 2025). Importantly, GA-mediated regulation of flowering and fruit set is highly responsive to environmental conditions, including water availability and temperature. Environmental stress can alter GA biosynthesis and signaling, thereby limiting GA-promoted reproductive development and highlighting the plasticity of hormonal control in coordinating reproductive success in response to the environment.

BRs are a group of polyhydroxylated steroidal hormones that regulate nearly every aspect of plant growth and development (Planas-Riverola et al., 2019). The first BR to be isolated was brassinolide (BL) from rapeseed pollen, but subsequent research has identified around 60 additional BRs, with castasterone (CS) and BL recognized as the most biologically active (Kutschera and Wang, 2012). The presence of a complete BR receptor system in both angiosperms and gymnosperms indicates that BR-mediated signaling originated early in seed plant evolution but represents a relatively late acquisition in land plants overall (Wang and Mao, 2014). Nevertheless, gymnosperms appear to synthesize only CS, the second most active BR, whereas basal angiosperms and monocots rely primarily on CS, and only dicots produce BL, the most active BR (Kim et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2021). These lineage-specific differences parallel the diversification of BR receptors: gymnosperms primarily use BRL1, whereas angiosperms progressively incorporated the more potent BRI1 receptor (Figure 5). Recent phylogenomic analyses indicate that this receptor expansion was shaped by ancient whole-genome duplications (Liu et al., 2024). Accordingly, the gymnosperm Picea abies exhibits a weaker BR signaling system based on CS and BRL1, whereas angiosperms evolved progressively stronger CS–BRI1 and BL–BRI1 modules, reflecting the stepwise co-evolution of BR ligands and receptors (Wang et al., 2021). Mutations in genes encoding major components of BR biosynthesis and signaling pathways result in severe dwarfism, impaired organ growth and development, and limited plant fertility (Yan et al., 2020; Xiang et al., 2024). In woody plants, slower growth is frequently linked to enhanced stress tolerance, reflecting trade-offs in resource allocation (Tao et al., 2024). In conifers, BR signaling is generally weaker than in angiosperms but remains functional through BRL1, with BR-mediated drought responses during seed germination representing one clear example of its activity (Kuneš et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2021). Consistently, BRL1/3 overexpression in Arabidopsis confers drought tolerance without compromising growth, whereas loss of function of the ubiquitously expressed BRI1 receptor enhances drought resistance at the expense of growth (Fàbregas et al., 2018). These findings suggest that reduced BR signaling intensity may not be a limitation but rather an adaptive strategy that supports the persistence of conifers in extreme environments while maintaining their characteristic slow growth. However, the physiological roles of BRs in gymnosperms remain largely unexplored.

Complementing their adaptive functions in stress resilience, BRs also play a pivotal role in vascular tissue development. In Arabidopsis, BR signaling regulates the differentiation of xylem elements and influences phloem organization, ensuring proper vascular patterning and connectivity (Lozano-Elena and Caño-Delgado, 2019). Efficient vascular development was a major innovation during land-plant evolution. Most seed plants possess eusteles and secondary cambia for radial growth and wood formation. Angiosperms combine tracheids and vessel elements, allowing faster water conduction than gymnosperms, which rely predominantly on tracheids (Spicer and Groover, 2010). Thus, angiosperms possess the most efficient hydraulic system (Sperry et al., 2006), combining highly conductive xylem with finely regulated stomata, whose development and movement are also modulated by BRs (Kim et al., 2012b; Gudesblat et al., 2012; Inoue et al., 2017). Liu et al. (2024) proposed that the evolutionary shift from a BRL1/3-based system in gymnosperms to a BRI1-centered system in angiosperms likely enhanced the precision and coordination of vascular development, increasing water transport capacity and supporting angiosperm diversification, ecological expansion, and stress tolerance. Moreover, BRs contribute to secondary growth and wood formation, processes that are critical for radial expansion, mechanical support, and efficient water transport in woody plants (Jiang et al., 2021). Genetic manipulation of BR levels in poplar has shown that elevated BR levels promote secondary xylem development and tension wood formation, whereas BR inhibition reduces growth and secondary vascular differentiation (Du et al., 2020). Hence, BRs may enhance resilience to environmental stress, allowing trees to maintain structural integrity and efficient hydraulic function under challenging conditions. However, the precise sites of BR synthesis and their interactions with other hormones during secondary growth remain unknown.

BRs play a pivotal role in plant reproduction, regulating the development of flowers and fruits, two key innovations underlying the ecological dominance of angiosperms (Chai et al., 2013; Ji et al., 2021). Recent work in A. thaliana shows that both BL and CS accumulate in pistils and pollen, with BL promoting pollen tube capacitation and guidance, whereas disruption of BRI1 in female tissues impairs guidance, indicating that BR-mediated regulation in both gametophytes is essential for fertilization (Matsuura-Tokita et al., 2025). Given the conservation of steroid-derived hormones across plants and animals, reproduction may represent one of the ancestral roles of BRs. Their elaboration in angiosperms parallels the evolution of complex physiology and reproductive systems, reflecting the need for precise regulation of growth, vascular development, and reproductive processes.

Here, we have examined how plant hormonal signaling has shaped the evolution of land plants from early streptophytes to angiosperms (Figure 5). Among the earliest complete signaling pathways, ETH and CKs were already present in the algal ancestors of land plants, likely contributing to the perception and integration of environmental cues, whereas PEPs appear to have emerged during a similar evolutionary window, although functional evidence in algae is primarily inferred from genomic and transcriptomic analyses. The transition to terrestrial habitats coincided with the emergence of most phytohormones recognized today, including auxin, SLs, and the principal stress-related hormones, providing bryophytes, the earliest-diverging extant land plants exposed to multiple new abiotic and biotic stresses, with a remarkably integrated signaling network. Auxin, through its activity in primary meristems, governed apical dominance, organ differentiation, and spatial patterning, establishing foundational control of plant architecture. In bryophytes, auxin was already essential for coordinating growth in simple plant bodies lacking true roots, stems, or leaves, and this role was further amplified in later lineages as polar auxin transport became increasingly prominent, allowing auxin to regulate physiological processes throughout the plant. Early auxin activity was complemented by the already present CKs, which promoted cell division, expansion, and branching, with SLs acting in specific developmental contexts, demonstrating that effective growth control relied on hormonal crosstalk from the earliest stages of land-plant evolution. Simultaneously, ABA, JAs, SA, and ETH enabled these early plants to perceive and respond to environmental fluctuations, coordinating adaptive responses at the cellular and organ levels. The emergence of GA signaling in vascular plants marked a decisive evolutionary step, enabling enhanced structural and reproductive complexity and greater developmental flexibility. GAs integrated with auxin and CKs to orchestrate organ elongation, branching, and reproductive system development, while also interacting with ABA to regulate germination and early developmental timing, establishing a regulatory system increasingly reminiscent of that observed in modern plants. Angiosperms subsequently achieved the highest level of regulatory sophistication with the emergence of BRs, which further refined plant growth control by acting in concert with auxin and other hormones. This hormonal interplay added a flexible regulatory layer that enhanced vascular development, mechanical support, and reproductive efficiency, contributing to the structural and functional complexity characteristic of flowering plants.

Although much has been learned about the evolution of plant hormones, fundamental questions remain. First, detailed studies of hormonal signaling in streptophyte algae are essential, as these early pathways likely played a major role in the colonization of terrestrial environments, positioning these algae as valuable models for systems biology and comparative approaches aimed at unraveling the evolutionary origins of plant hormonal regulation. Indeed, recent advances in genomic and multi-omic analyses of streptophyte algae now provide unprecedented opportunities to investigate these pathways in an evolutionary context. The functional roles of CKs, ETH, and particularly PEPs in these algae remain poorly characterized. Comparative analyses across streptophyte clades, considering the sequential acquisition of signaling components and lineage-specific physiological requirements, will be crucial. Functional studies are particularly needed to complement genomic and transcriptomic data and to clarify the presence, diversity, and roles of PEPs in algae. In early land plants such as bryophytes, the mechanisms underlying hormonal crosstalk are still largely unknown, despite their importance in coordinating growth, development, and stress responses. The emergence of GA signaling represented a major evolutionary innovation. While GAs and their interactions with other hormones are well characterized in higher plants, little is known about their roles in early vascular plants such as lycophytes and ferns. Current knowledge of GA function in gametophyte and sporophyte growth in these lineages is limited, as studies have primarily focused on sexual differentiation. Investigating GA signaling and its interplay with auxin in these plants is therefore essential for understanding the evolution of complex growth regulation. Furthermore, the diversity, functional specialization, and integration of BRs, SLs, and PEPs across plant lineages remain poorly understood, particularly in gymnosperms, where the roles and mechanisms of BRs are still enigmatic. Finally, although hormones are known to mediate responses to drought, light and temperature fluctuations, and nutrient stress, the mechanisms by which these pathways integrate multiple environmental signals remain unresolved in different plant lineages. Addressing these gaps is essential for a comprehensive understanding of how integrated hormonal networks evolved to coordinate growth, reproduction, and environmental resilience, thereby underpinning the evolutionary success of land plants.
