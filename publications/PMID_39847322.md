Lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) exerts its physiological roles through the
endothelialdifferentiation gene (EDG) family LPA receptors (LPAR1-3) or the
non-EDG family LPA receptors (LPAR4-6). LPAR6 plays crucial roles in hair loss
and cancer progression, yet its structural information is very limited. Here, we
report the cryoelectron microscopy structure of LPA-bound human LPAR6 in complex
with a mini G13 or Gq protein. These structures reveal a distinct ligand binding
and recognition mode that differs significantly from that of LPAR1.
Specifically, LPA uses its charged head to form an extensive polar interaction
network with key polar residues on the extracellular side of transmembrane helix
5-6 and the extracellular loop 2. Structural comparisons and homology analysis
suggest that the EDG and non-EDG families use two distinct modes for LPA
binding. The structural observations are validated through functional
mutagenesis studies. We further uncover the mechanisms of LPAR6 activation and
principles of G-protein coupling. The structural information revealed by our
study lays the groundwork for understanding LPAR6 signaling and provides a
rational basis for designing compounds targeting LPAR6.

## Full Text

Lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) is a lipid signaling molecule consisting of a phosphate group, a glycerol moiety, and a single acyl chain of varying length and saturation (1). The acyl chain can be attached at either the sn-1 or sn-2 position of the glycerol backbone. LPA is generated from lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) by autotaxin via its lysophospholipase D activity (2), or from phosphatidic acid (PA) by membrane-bound PA-selective phospholipase A1α (3). LPA plays crucial roles in a wide range of physiological processes, including cell growth, migration, differentiation, immune response, pain sensation, and tumorigenesis (1).

LPA exerts its physiological effects by binding to and activating LPA receptors, which are members of the G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) superfamily. There are six LPA receptors (LPAR1-6), divided into two subfamilies: the endothelial differentiation gene (EDG) family (LPAR1-3) (1, 4) and the non-EDG family (LPAR4-6) (1, 5). LPAR1, the first identified LPA receptor, is highly expressed in the central nervous system and is associated with neuropathic pain. It can couple with multiple G-proteins (Gαi, Gαq, and Gα13) but primarily signals through Gαi pathways, making it a target for fibrosis and cancer treatments (4, 6).

In contrast, LPAR6 is the most recently identified LPA receptor and belongs to the P2Y purinergic GPCR cluster (7). LPAR6 is closely associated with hair loss, as mutations in LPAR6 or the upstream LPA-producing enzyme PA-PLA1α cause autosomal recessive woolly hair/hypotrichosis (8, 9). LPAR6 has been linked to Gα13, Gαi, and Gαs activities, but primarily activates Gα13 signaling (1, 7). Notably, LPAR6 prefers ligands with an acyl chain at the sn-2 position (2-acyl-LPA) (7), whereas LPAR1 favors 1-acyl-LPA (1). LPAR6 also plays crucial roles in regulating the chemorepulsion of T cells in tumors, making it an attractive target for treating hair loss and cancer (1, 10).

A key question in LPA signaling is whether the ligand recognition mode is conserved among LPA receptors. Structural advancements in LPAR1 have greatly enhanced our understanding of its signaling and facilitated related drug discovery, for example, the crystal structure of LPAR1 bound with various antagonists (11), as well as later cryoelectronmicroscopy (cryo-EM) structures of LPA-bound LPAR1 (12, 13), have provided mechanistic insights into LPA’s ligand recognition mode. However, there is limited structural information on the non-EDG family of LPA receptors. The only available structure, an inactive apo crystal structure of zebrafish LPAR6 (14), offers little information about the ligand recognition mode of LPAR6. This lack of structural information has also heavily hindered drug development targeting LPAR6, resulting in far fewer available LPAR6-specific modulators compared to LPAR1. Given the significant potential of LPAR6 in treating hair loss and other diseases, we aimed to solve the cryo-EM structure of LPAR6 bound to its native ligand, LPA.

We also include a thermostabilized apocytochrome b562RIL from Escherichia coli (M7W, H102I, and R106L) (BRIL) (17) to the N terminus of LPAR6 to further increase the stability and expression of the receptor (for details, see Materials and Methods). We used a miniGαs/q (18) which contain crucial far end Gαq sequence receptor interaction for the LPAR6/Gq complex assembling. Similarly, we used a miniGα13 (19) for the assembling of LPAR6/G13 complex. Furthermore, a single-chain variable fragment (scFv16) (20) targeting the N terminus of Gαi (residues 1 to 29) was included in the assembly of the LPAR6/miniGαi/13 complex. Nb35 (21) was used in the LPAR6/miniGs/q complex to introduce additional features for cryo-EM determination and enhance stability, as detailed in the Methods section. Employing miniG proteins aids in the structural determination of GPCR/G-protein complexes. Various studies have shown that the G-protein engagement conformation derived from miniG proteins aligns with that of conventional G-proteins (22, 23). To simplify comparisons, we will refer to LPAR6/miniGs/q and LPAR6/miniG13 as LPAR6/Gq and LPAR6/G13, respectively. We coexpressed all components of the complex, receptor LPAR6, Gα, Gβ, and Gγ in insect Sf9 cell via the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system (for details, see Materials and Methods). The purified LPAR6/G-protein complexes display distinct and well-defined target peaks in the size exclusion chromatography profile (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D).

We made an effort to obtain LPAR6/G-protein complexes bound with 2-acyl-LPA, a preferred ligand for LPAR6 (1, 7). However, the extreme instability of 2-acyl-LPA during the in vitro protein purification process made this highly challenging. Consequently, we switched to using conventional 1-acyl-LPA (18:1) for structural determination. We resolved the structures of the LPA-bound LPAR6/G13 and LPA-bound LPAR6/Gq complexes at resolutions of 3.06 Å and 2.89 Å, respectively, using single particle cryo-EM analysis (SI Appendix, Figs. These complexes exhibit the classical GPCR/G-protein engagement conformations, where the G-protein interacts with the receptor primarily through the far end of its Gα subunit (Fig. 1). The receptor side of the G13 complex shows better density compared to the Gq complex (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), enabling us to model intracellular loop 3 (ICL3) and part of Helix 8 (H8), which are not visible in the Gq complex. In the ECL2 region of the receptor, residues 158 to 164 are missing in the G13 complex, and residues 158 to 166 are absent in the Gq complex due to the high flexibility of this region (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Two pairs of disulfide bonds, C91.24/C2637.25 and C893.25/C168ECL2, are found on the extracellular side of the receptor. The BRIL fusion segment is not discernible in the cryo-EM map due to the flexibility of this region. We also modeled three cholesterols and one palmitic acid around the transmembrane helices (Fig. 1).

DiscussionAn interesting and frequently occurring phenomenon in GPCRs is that a single ligand can bind to different receptors, yet the binding poses can vary even among closely related receptors. For example, LysoPS was initially expected to have the same binding pose across the LysoPS receptor family after the structural determination of LysoPS-bound GPR174. However, subsequent studies revealed that the binding mode of LysoPS in GPR34 is significantly different from that in GPR174 (28).The LPA receptors are divided into the EDG family (LPAR1-3) and the non-EDG family (LPAR4-6). Following the same logic as with the LysoPS receptors, where GPR174 and P2Y10 have high homology, while GPR34 does not, we anticipated that the ligand binding mode of LPAR6 would differ from the previously reported LPAR1. Indeed, our structural study reveals a distinct LPA binding mode that is completely different from that of LPAR1. These results indicate that the ligand binding mode is dictated by differences in the ligand-binding pocket, rather than specific feature of the ligand. This information is crucial for the accurate design of drugs targeting LPAR6. First, the shape of the ligand-binding pocket in LPAR6 differs significantly from that of LPAR1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C), providing a precise model for initial shape-fitting screenings or template-based design. Second, the models of the phosphate head group engagement are also quite distinct (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D), allowing for a more accurate mapping of polar interactions to optimize the charge groups of candidate compounds for better interaction with the receptor. Overall, the unique ligand binding mode of LPAR6 identified in our study presents a rational framework for precise drug design targeting LPAR6.It is intriguing that the outward movement of TM6 upon activation in LPAR6 is minimal compared to other class A GPCRs. We investigated whether this small outward movement is an inherent characteristic of the receptor. A comparison of the active conformation of GPR174 (26) and P2Y10 (29), which are phylogenetically closest to LPAR6, shows that all of them have a similar TM6 outward movement (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). In contrast, β2AR shows a significantly greater outward movement upon activation, displacing TM6 by 6.9 Å more than LPAR6, measured by the Cα of LPAR6/T2256.31 and β2AR/H2696.31. We conducted an in-depth comparison of the active conformation of LPAR6 with β2AR in the context of G-protein binding and identified two key differences. First, M228G.H5.24 of Gα13 inserts into a hydrophobic pocket created by M562.43/F421.57/I298ICL2, pulling αH5 toward the TM7 and H8 regions (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Second, the salt bridge formed between H2346.40 and R1143.50 draws TM6 inward toward the receptor’s center, preventing the significant outward movement observed in the active β2AR structure (21). These analyses elucidate why the outward movement of TM6 is less pronounced compared to that observed in β2AR, indicating that this limited outward movement upon activation is an intrinsic feature of LPAR6 and its closest receptors.Taken together, our study reveals a unique LPA binding mode in LPAR6, providing mechanistic insights into LPAR6 activation and G-protein coupling. In fact, comparing to LPAR1, there are very little or no LPAR6 specific agonists or modulators available for research due to the lack of structural information of LPAR6. This translational information from our study offers a rational basis for precise drug design targeting LPAR6.

In contrast, β2AR shows a significantly greater outward movement upon activation, displacing TM6 by 6.9 Å more than LPAR6, measured by the Cα of LPAR6/T2256.31 and β2AR/H2696.31. We conducted an in-depth comparison of the active conformation of LPAR6 with β2AR in the context of G-protein binding and identified two key differences. First, M228G.H5.24 of Gα13 inserts into a hydrophobic pocket created by M562.43/F421.57/I298ICL2, pulling αH5 toward the TM7 and H8 regions (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Second, the salt bridge formed between H2346.40 and R1143.50 draws TM6 inward toward the receptor’s center, preventing the significant outward movement observed in the active β2AR structure (21). These analyses elucidate why the outward movement of TM6 is less pronounced compared to that observed in β2AR, indicating that this limited outward movement upon activation is an intrinsic feature of LPAR6 and its closest receptors.

Taken together, our study reveals a unique LPA binding mode in LPAR6, providing mechanistic insights into LPAR6 activation and G-protein coupling. In fact, comparing to LPAR1, there are very little or no LPAR6 specific agonists or modulators available for research due to the lack of structural information of LPAR6. This translational information from our study offers a rational basis for precise drug design targeting LPAR6.

The cells were then washed with HBSS (Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution) and incubated with 10 μM furimazine for 3 min at 37 °C. The BRET ratio was measured in triplicate reads using an EnVision multimode plate reader (PerkinElmer), followed by the addition of ligand or vehicle control solutions. Subsequent BRET readings were taken to monitor ligand-induced changes in BRET. The signal was quantified as the ratio of 460/40 nm (gain = 3,600) to cpVenus emission (535/30 nm, gain = 4,000), with an integration time of 0.3 s for both channels.

To assess the surface expression levels of both WT LPAR6 and its mutants, AD293 cells were transiently transfected with 1,000 ng of plasmids encoding the target receptors or a vehicle plasmid (pcDNA3) using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen) in a 6-well plate. The cells were incubated for 24 h, after which they were reseeded into 48-well plates and cultured for an additional 24 h. The cells were then fixed with 500 μL of 4% formaldehyde in PBS for 20 min at room temperature with light orbital shaking. Following fixation, the cells were washed twice with PBS. After washing, the cells were blocked with 500 μL of DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium) containing 5% FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum) for 1 h at 37 °C with light orbital shaking. The blocking solution was removed, and the cells were incubated with 100 μL of DMEM containing 5% FBS and Anti-HA-peroxidase antibody (1:1,000, ABclonal) for 1 h at room temperature under light orbital shaking. Washing was repeated, and any remaining PBS was removed to ensure the wells were dry. Next, 200 μL of TMB (3,3’,5,5’-Tetramethylbenzidine) substrate solution was added to each well and incubated in the dark for 20 min at room temperature. The reaction was stopped by adding 100 μL of 3 M sulfuric acid per well. The reaction solution (200 μL) was transferred to a 96-well transparent plate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.
