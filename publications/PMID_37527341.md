# The structure of Plasmodium falciparum multidrug resistance protein 1 reveals an N-terminal regulatory domain.

Plasmodium falciparum multidrug resistance protein 1 (PfMDR1), an adenosine
triphosphate (ATP)-binding cassette (ABC) transporter on the digestive vacuole
(DV) membrane of the parasite, is associated with the resistance to antimalarial
drugs. To understand the mechanisms of PfMDR1, we determined the cryo-electron
microscopy structures of this transporter in different states. The transporter
in the apo state shows an inward-facing conformation with a large cavity opening
to the cytoplasm. Upon ATP binding and dimerization of the nucleotide-binding
domains (NBDs), PfMDR1 displays an outward-facing conformation with a cavity
toward the DV lumen. Drug resistance-associated mutations were investigated in
both structures for their effects, and Y184F was identified as an allosteric
activity-enhancing mutation. The amphiphilic substrate-binding site of PfMDR1
was revealed by the complex structure with the antimalarial drug mefloquine and
confirmed by mutagenesis studies. Remarkably, a helical structure was found to
hinder NBD dimerization and inhibit PfMDR1 activity. The location of this
regulatory domain in the N terminus is different from the well-studied R domain
in the internal linker region of other ABC transporter family members. The lack
of the phosphorylation site of this domain also suggests a different regulation
mechanism.

## Full Text

The high prevalence of malaria in developing countries remains one of the most severe challenges in public health and a major reason for mortality in the population. The World Health Organization estimates that 241 million cases of malaria occurred worldwide and resulted in 627,000 deaths in 2020, increasing significantly from the previous year (1). Antimalarial drugs are effective means of fighting malaria, but the early antimalarial drugs quinine and chloroquine (CQ) gradually led to drug resistance in plasmodium. Artemisinin (Art) and its derivatives have been profoundly fruitful in the treatment of malaria in recent decades. As the half-life of these drugs in the human body is relatively short, their effects are well enhanced when used in combination with other long-acting drugs. Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) have been suggested by the World Health Organization as the preferred treatment for malaria and have saved a large number of lives around the world (2). Companion drugs frequently used in ACTs include lumefantrine (LMF), mefloquine (MFQ), amodiaquine (ADQ), and piperaquine (PPQ) (3, 4). However, with the widespread use of ACT therapies, resistance to the drugs used in ACTs has emerged in plasmodium in some regions, particularly in South East Asia (5–7).

It has been found that the resistance to Art is caused by mutations in the Kelch 13 gene (8), while the resistance to some other antimalarial drugs is associated with transport proteins on the digestive vacuole (DV) membrane. CQ, ADQ, or PPQ accumulates in the DV and prevents heme detoxification. Certain mutant Plasmodium falciparum chloroquine resistance transporter isoforms can transport these drugs out of the DV and decrease the susceptibility to them. The resistance to drugs such as MFQ, halofantrine (HF), and LMF is associated with another transporter resident on the DV membrane, P. falciparum multidrug resistance protein 1 (PfMDR1) (3), which is proposed to transport drugs into the DV (9). The main targets of these drugs are considered to be in the cytosol, so they become less effective once transported by PfMDR1 into the DV (6, 9, 10). The copy number of PfMDR1 significantly affects the sensitivity of the parasite to these drugs. Increased pfmdr1 copy number is particularly common in South East Asia and is closely related to the failures of MFQ and LMF treatment (11, 12). Increased pfmdr1 copy number was also found in some countries in South America (13–15) and East and West Africa (16–18). Single nucleotide polymorphism in the pfmdr1 gene is related to in vivo and in vitro parasite reactions to ADQ (19, 20), MFQ (6, 21, 22), LMF (23, 24), and Art (21, 22). Five globally prevalent missense mutations have been identified in PfMDR1. Parasites harboring N86Y and Y184F mutations are more common in individuals of African and Asian descent (25–28), whereas mutations S1034C, N1042D, and D1246Y occur with high frequency in South American isolates (29–31).

PfMDR1 is a homolog of the human MDR1 P-glycoprotein (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), which is an ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter. Sequence analysis shows that PfMDR1 contains two transmembrane domains (TMDs) and two conserved nucleotide-binding domains (NBDs), following a typical TMD-NBD-TMD-NBD arrangement of ABC transporter (32). Extensive clinical investigations and functional studies have been done to elucidate the roles of PfMDR1 in malaria drug resistance (5, 29, 33). However, the three-dimensional structure of PfMDR1 is not available to date. The structural information of PfMDR1 is critical to understand the drug resistance mechanisms and will help to develop drugs for fighting malaria. Here, we present the cryo-EM structures of PfMDR1 in the inward-facing apo state, in the ATP-bound, outward-facing state, and in complexes with antimalarial drugs MFQ. These structures, together with functional analysis, provide insights into the substrate recognition and transport mechanisms of PfMDR1 and the effects of the prevalent mutations on drug resistance. Notably, there is a regulatory domain (R domain) on the N terminus of PfMDR1, significantly different from the well-studied R domain of cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) (34) and other ABC transporters (35), which is in the internal linker region. The lack of conserved phosphorylation sites suggests different regulatory mechanisms of this domain. This phenomenon enriches our understanding of the regulation of ABC transporters.

Sequence alignment reveals that the N terminus of PfMDR1, particularly the first two helices, shows very low similarity to human and mouse homologs (Fig. 2A), although there is an overall 30% identity for the full-length transporters (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The N terminus of human or mouse MDR1 protein contains only one helix which is the structural counterpart of the H3 helix of PfMDR1 (Fig. 2B). The H2 helix of PfMDR1, together with the linker region following it, wraps around TM6 through hydrophobic interactions with residues from TM6 (Fig. 2C). The N terminus of H2 points to the opening between the two TMDs of PfMDR1, where the upstream H1 domain is positioned.The location of the H1 helix in between the two TMDs of PfMDR1 (Fig. 1D) suggests that it may interfere with the formation of the NBD dimer and therefore hinder the transport activity of PfMDR1. A similar helical structure has been previously reported in the CFTR channel, a member of the ABCC transporter family (34). This helix belongs to the R domain of CFTR, an internal linker region connecting the two halves and regulates CFTR channel activity. To investigate whether the N-terminal domain of PfMDR1 has a similar regulatory effect on the activity as the R domain of CFTR does, we truncated the first two helices (residue 1 to 37) from the full-length PfMDR1 and found that the truncated protein has 80% higher ATPase activity than the full-length PfMDR1 (Fig. 2D), confirming the inhibitory role of the N-terminal domain. Accordingly, we refer to this N-terminal region as PfMDR1’s R domain.A close-up view of the structure of the H1 helix of PfMDR1 reveals that it predominantly interacts with TM4 (Fig. 2E). L224 in TM4 is responsible for forming hydrophobic interactions with V19 and L23 in the H1 helix. The H1 helix of PfMDR1 is highly enriched in negatively charged glutamates including E17, E18, E20, and E22 (Fig. 2A), which suggests that electrostatic forces play roles for the R domain binding. Surface electrostatic potential analysis of the PfMDR1 atomic model without the H1 helix supports this hypothesis, as the area where the H1 helix binds is highly positively charged (Fig. 2F). There is a cluster of lysine composed of K217, K220, and K221 on TM4, which is a unique feature of PfMDR1 that does not exist in human and mouse MDR1 (Fig. 2G). The lysine side chains contribute to the creation of positive surface potential. To test whether disrupting the electrostatic interaction between the H1 helix and TM4 could remove the inhibition on PfMDR1, we made a mutant transporter 3Q by replacing K217, K220, and K221 with neutral amino acid glutamine. The mutant protein showed a significantly higher ATPase activity than wild-type PfMDR1 protein (Fig. 2H), indicating that electrostatic attraction is critical for positioning of the H1 helix to inhibit the transporter. The interaction between the H1 helix and the TMD domain should be relatively weak to allow a dynamic binding of the H1 helix, indicated by the coexistence of two reconstructions out of particles from the same sample preparation, which represents a conformational equilibrium of PfMDR1 between the inhibited and noninhibited states.The Outward-Facing Structure of PfMDR1.According to a canonical transport mechanism of the ABC transporters, after the substrate and ATP bind to the inward-facing transporter, the two NBDs dimerize and induce a rearrangement of the TMDs to form an outward-facing conformation (36, 37). During this conformational transition, the substrate moves through the transmembrane region from the cytoplasmic side to the extracellular opening and is released into outer space (corresponding to the DV lumen in the case of PfMDR1). ATP hydrolysis causes dissociation of the NBD dimer and restores the transporter to the inward-facing state. The last step does not happen when the ATP hydrolytic activity is disrupted by mutating the key glutamates in the catalytic sites to glutamines so that the mutant transporter remains in an outward-facing conformation (38–41). We mutated the catalytic glutamates E588 and E1337 of PfMDR1 to glutamines to abolish its ATPase activity (Fig. 2D). After an initial unsuccessful attempt to obtain the outward-facing structure using a full-length protein, we truncated the N-terminal R domain (37 amino acids, containing H1 and H2 helices) to eliminate the interference of this region on the dimerization of NBDs. We were able to obtain the outward-facing structure of PfMDR1 at a resolution of 2.94 Å by using this modified protein and with the presence of ATP, Mg2+, and a substrate MFQ (SI Appendix, Table S1 and Fig. S4). On the other hand, in the outward-facing state, the two residues are buried among transmembrane helices and are surrounded by a number of other residues. S1034C and N1042D mutations would change the hydrophobicity and electrostatic properties of their side chains, interfere with their interactions with surrounding residues, and alter the activity of PfMDR1. S1034C and N1042D mutations are frequently observed together in P. falciparum resistance strains. However, there are also strains carrying only one of them. ATPase activity measurement (Fig. 4B) shows that the single S1034C has a significant decrease of ATPase activity. The N1042D mutation causes an even greater decrease of ATPase activity, suggesting that the negative charge brought by this mutation has a more severe impact on the transporter. During this conformational transition, the substrate moves through the transmembrane region from the cytoplasmic side to the extracellular opening and is released into outer space (corresponding to the DV lumen in the case of PfMDR1). ATP hydrolysis causes dissociation of the NBD dimer and restores the transporter to the inward-facing state. The last step does not happen when the ATP hydrolytic activity is disrupted by mutating the key glutamates in the catalytic sites to glutamines so that the mutant transporter remains in an outward-facing conformation (38–41). We mutated the catalytic glutamates E588 and E1337 of PfMDR1 to glutamines to abolish its ATPase activity (Fig. 2D). After an initial unsuccessful attempt to obtain the outward-facing structure using a full-length protein, we truncated the N-terminal R domain (37 amino acids, containing H1 and H2 helices) to eliminate the interference of this region on the dimerization of NBDs. We were able to obtain the outward-facing structure of PfMDR1 at a resolution of 2.94 Å by using this modified protein and with the presence of ATP, Mg2+, and a substrate MFQ (SI Appendix, Table S1 and Fig. S4). During this conformational transition, the substrate moves through the transmembrane region from the cytoplasmic side to the extracellular opening and is released into outer space (corresponding to the DV lumen in the case of PfMDR1). ATP hydrolysis causes dissociation of the NBD dimer and restores the transporter to the inward-facing state. The last step does not happen when the ATP hydrolytic activity is disrupted by mutating the key glutamates in the catalytic sites to glutamines so that the mutant transporter remains in an outward-facing conformation (38–41). We mutated the catalytic glutamates E588 and E1337 of PfMDR1 to glutamines to abolish its ATPase activity (Fig. 2D). After an initial unsuccessful attempt to obtain the outward-facing structure using a full-length protein, we truncated the N-terminal R domain (37 amino acids, containing H1 and H2 helices) to eliminate the interference of this region on the dimerization of NBDs. We were able to obtain the outward-facing structure of PfMDR1 at a resolution of 2.94 Å by using this modified protein and with the presence of ATP, Mg2+, and a substrate MFQ (SI Appendix, Table S1 and Fig. S4). The density of ATP molecules and Mg2+ is very well resolved (Fig. 3G), while the density of MFQ is not observed as it may have been released to the solution.

The overall structure of PfMDR1 in the outward-facing conformation is significantly different from its inward-facing conformation (Fig. 3 A and B). In the inward-facing conformation, the two NBDs are well separated from each other with a distance of more than 20 Å. However, they make extensive contacts in the outward-facing conformation and form a “head-tail” dimer (Fig. 3 B and G), which is a typical feature of ABC transporters (40, 41). The cytoplasmic regions of the TMDs from both halves of the PfMDR1 molecule move together with the NBDs, tightly packed with no space between them (Fig. 3B). The drug-binding cavity in the membrane inner leaflet observed in the inward-facing conformation disappears in the outward-facing conformation (Fig. 3 C and E). Viewed from the DV lumen, the ends of the 12 transmembrane helices form two clusters. One cluster consists of TM3-TM8, and the other cluster consists of TM1-TM2 and TM9-TM12. The two clusters form a tightly packed structure in the inward-facing conformation (Fig. 3D). However, in the outward-facing conformation, they move away from each other, creating a cavity between them that faces the DV lumen (Fig. 3F). The overall conformational change is mostly accomplished by tilting and rigid-body rotation of the two helix clusters, which move in opposite directions with a slight counterclockwise twist. Subtle rearrangements also occur among the transmembrane helices within each cluster.

The structure of NBDs of PfMDR1 can be well aligned to that of human MDR1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A), except for an extended helix (α16) and an extra helix (α17, SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The two ATP molecules are located at the interface of the two NBDs, where they interact with the Walkers A motif and A-loop of one NBD and the ABC signature motif of the other NBD (Fig. 3G). The interactions of ATPs with PfMDR1 are similar to those observed in human MDR1 (41), such as the interactions between the adenosine moieties and the conserved tyrosines on the A-loop, and the coordination bonds among magnesium ion, the γ-phosphate of ATP, and the conserved glutamines on the Q-loop (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B).

DiscussionOur studies provide structural insights into how PfMDR1 mediated the transport of the antimalarial drug across the DV membrane. The amphiphilic and vast substrate binding site of PfMDR1 enables it to accommodate various molecules, including antimalarial drugs and presumably other biologically active substances. Regulation of the PfMDR1 activity must be crucial for the parasite to survive in different conditions. The regulation can occur at the DNA level as the increased copy number of the pfmdr1 gene (6, 16) and missense mutations in pfmdr1 gene have been identified in resistant parasites (25, 26). The expression of the pfmdr1 gene can also be regulated at the transcriptional level (43, 44). Posttranslational regulation may also be important for PfMDR1 function. Phosphorylation as one of the posttranslational regulations has been found to regulate proteins in ABC transporter family members, such as ABCB1 (45), ABCC1 (46), and CFTR(ABCC7) (34, 40, 47–49). The R domain of CFTR, a flexible internal linker region connecting the two halves of the CFTR molecule, is responsible for the regulation by phosphorylation (50). A helical structure in this region is wedged between the two TMDs and inhibits CFTR activity (40). Phosphorylation changes the conformation of the R domain and removes its inhibition on CFTR. Some other ABC transporters also contain such internal linkers, but their regulatory roles remain to be understood (35).The cryo-EM structures of PfMDR1 reveal the presence of a R domain that contains a helical structure wedged between the two TMDs in the inward-facing conformation. This domain has an inhibitory effect on the activity of PfMDR1. Either truncation of the R domain or disruption of its binding to the TMD causes elevated ATPase activity. However, the R domain of PfMDR1 is in the N terminus, while the R domain of CFTR is in the internal linker, and there is no sequence homology between them. For CFTR, the helical structure in the R domain is proposed to correspond to residues 825 to 843 that connect to the elbow helix in TMD2, and multiple PKA recognition sites have been identified in its upstream region (34). The recently resolved structures of yeast glutathione transporter Ycf1 assigned the sequence of the internal inhibitory helical structure and visualized the phosphorylated residues (51). The phosphorylated residues are situated in the upstream region of the inhibitory helix and interact with NBD1. According to phylogenetic analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), PfMDR1 belongs to the ABCB subfamily. No regulatory domain that inhibits transporter activity has been previously revealed in this subfamily. In the R domain of PfMDR1, there are only three phosphorylatable residues (S14, S27, and T28), and none of these residues are predicted as the PKA recognition site. Only one of them is on the N-terminal end of the inhibitory H1 helix. Whether these residues can be phosphorylated by certain P. falciparum kinase to regulate the activity of PfMDR1 requires additional investigation. Other possible mechanisms for removal of the inhibition by the R domain of PfMDR1 include other type of posttranslational modification such as SUMOylation (52), proteolytic cleavage, or interaction with partner proteins. It is also possible that the R domain interacts with lipids on the DV membrane in some instances, resulting in the removal of its inhibition.The different locations of the R domains of PfMDR1 (in the N terminus) and CFTR (in the internal linker region) and the lack of sequence homology between them indicate independent evolution of the regulatory machinery in ABC proteins. They are also different from the structural point of view: The H1 helix in the R domain of PfMDR1 is wedged between TM4 and TM6, while the inhibitory helix in the R domain of CFTR is wedged between TM10 and TM12. However, rotating the PfMDR1 molecule 180º along the pseudosymmetric axis would place the H1 helix in a similar position of the inhibitory helix of CFTR (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). TM4 of PfMDR1 would align with TM10 of CFTR, and TM6 of PfMDR1 would align with TM12 of CFTR. The two inhibitory helices also share a common electrostatic mechanism for their interaction with the TMDs. Both the H1 helix of PfMDR1 and the regulatory helix of CFTR are negatively charged. The positively charged TM4 of PfMDR1 is important for binding to the H1 helix. Although TM10 of CFTR is not rich in positive residues, R1158, R1162, and K1165 on TM12 and K968, R975, and K978 on TM9 provide a positive surface potential for the electrostatic interactions (40) (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). The observation on the mechanistic similarity between the R domains of PfMDR1 and CFTR provides an example for convergent evolution at the molecular level of ABC proteins.

Our studies provide structural insights into how PfMDR1 mediated the transport of the antimalarial drug across the DV membrane. The amphiphilic and vast substrate binding site of PfMDR1 enables it to accommodate various molecules, including antimalarial drugs and presumably other biologically active substances. Regulation of the PfMDR1 activity must be crucial for the parasite to survive in different conditions. The regulation can occur at the DNA level as the increased copy number of the pfmdr1 gene (6, 16) and missense mutations in pfmdr1 gene have been identified in resistant parasites (25, 26). The expression of the pfmdr1 gene can also be regulated at the transcriptional level (43, 44). Posttranslational regulation may also be important for PfMDR1 function. Phosphorylation as one of the posttranslational regulations has been found to regulate proteins in ABC transporter family members, such as ABCB1 (45), ABCC1 (46), and CFTR(ABCC7) (34, 40, 47–49). The R domain of CFTR, a flexible internal linker region connecting the two halves of the CFTR molecule, is responsible for the regulation by phosphorylation (50). A helical structure in this region is wedged between the two TMDs and inhibits CFTR activity (40). Phosphorylation changes the conformation of the R domain and removes its inhibition on CFTR. Some other ABC transporters also contain such internal linkers, but their regulatory roles remain to be understood (35).

The cryo-EM structures of PfMDR1 reveal the presence of a R domain that contains a helical structure wedged between the two TMDs in the inward-facing conformation. This domain has an inhibitory effect on the activity of PfMDR1. Either truncation of the R domain or disruption of its binding to the TMD causes elevated ATPase activity. However, the R domain of PfMDR1 is in the N terminus, while the R domain of CFTR is in the internal linker, and there is no sequence homology between them. For CFTR, the helical structure in the R domain is proposed to correspond to residues 825 to 843 that connect to the elbow helix in TMD2, and multiple PKA recognition sites have been identified in its upstream region (34). The recently resolved structures of yeast glutathione transporter Ycf1 assigned the sequence of the internal inhibitory helical structure and visualized the phosphorylated residues (51). The phosphorylated residues are situated in the upstream region of the inhibitory helix and interact with NBD1. According to phylogenetic analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), PfMDR1 belongs to the ABCB subfamily. No regulatory domain that inhibits transporter activity has been previously revealed in this subfamily. In the R domain of PfMDR1, there are only three phosphorylatable residues (S14, S27, and T28), and none of these residues are predicted as the PKA recognition site. Only one of them is on the N-terminal end of the inhibitory H1 helix. Whether these residues can be phosphorylated by certain P. falciparum kinase to regulate the activity of PfMDR1 requires additional investigation. Other possible mechanisms for removal of the inhibition by the R domain of PfMDR1 include other type of posttranslational modification such as SUMOylation (52), proteolytic cleavage, or interaction with partner proteins. It is also possible that the R domain interacts with lipids on the DV membrane in some instances, resulting in the removal of its inhibition.

The different locations of the R domains of PfMDR1 (in the N terminus) and CFTR (in the internal linker region) and the lack of sequence homology between them indicate independent evolution of the regulatory machinery in ABC proteins. They are also different from the structural point of view: The H1 helix in the R domain of PfMDR1 is wedged between TM4 and TM6, while the inhibitory helix in the R domain of CFTR is wedged between TM10 and TM12. However, rotating the PfMDR1 molecule 180º along the pseudosymmetric axis would place the H1 helix in a similar position of the inhibitory helix of CFTR (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). TM4 of PfMDR1 would align with TM10 of CFTR, and TM6 of PfMDR1 would align with TM12 of CFTR. The two inhibitory helices also share a common electrostatic mechanism for their interaction with the TMDs. Both the H1 helix of PfMDR1 and the regulatory helix of CFTR are negatively charged. The positively charged TM4 of PfMDR1 is important for binding to the H1 helix. Although TM10 of CFTR is not rich in positive residues, R1158, R1162, and K1165 on TM12 and K968, R975, and K978 on TM9 provide a positive surface potential for the electrostatic interactions (40) (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). The observation on the mechanistic similarity between the R domains of PfMDR1 and CFTR provides an example for convergent evolution at the molecular level of ABC proteins.
