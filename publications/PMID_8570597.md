Phototransduction systems in vertebrates and invertebrates share a great deal of
similarity in overall strategy but differ significantly in the underlying
molecular machinery. Both are rhodopsin-based G protein-coupled signaling
cascades displaying exquisite sensitivity and broad dynamic range. However,
light activation of vertebrate photoreceptors leads to activation of a
cGMP-phosphodiesterase effector and the generation of a hyperpolarizing
response. In contrast, activation of invertebrate photoreceptors, like
Drosophila, leads to stimulation of phospholipase C and the generation of a
depolarizing receptor potential. The comparative study of these two systems of
phototransduction offers the opportunity to understand how similar biological
problems may be solved by different molecular mechanisms of signal transduction.
The study of this process in Drosophila, a system ideally suited to genetic and
molecular manipulation, allows us to dissect the function and regulation of such
a complex signaling cascade in its normal cellular environment. In this
manuscript I review some of our recent findings and the strategies used to
dissect this process.
