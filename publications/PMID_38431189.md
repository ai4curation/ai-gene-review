Three glucose-6-phosphatase catalytic subunits, that hydrolyze
glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) to glucose and inorganic phosphate, have been
identified, designated G6PC1-3, but only G6PC1 and G6PC2 have been implicated in
the regulation of fasting blood glucose (FBG). Elevated FBG has been associated
with multiple adverse clinical outcomes, including increased risk for type 2
diabetes and various cancers. Therefore, G6PC1 and G6PC2 inhibitors that lower
FBG may be of prophylactic value for the prevention of multiple conditions. The
studies described here characterize a G6PC2 inhibitor, designated VU0945627,
previously identified as Compound 3. We show that VU0945627 preferentially
inhibits human G6PC2 versus human G6PC1 but activates human G6PC3. VU0945627 is
a mixed G6PC2 inhibitor, increasing the Km but reducing the Vmax for G6P
hydrolysis. PyRx virtual docking to an AlphaFold2-derived G6PC2 structural model
suggests VU0945627 binds two sites in human G6PC2. Mutation of residues in these
sites reduces the inhibitory effect of VU0945627. VU0945627 does not inhibit
mouse G6PC2 despite its 84% sequence identity with human G6PC2. Mutagenesis
studies suggest this lack of inhibition of mouse G6PC2 is due, in part, to a
change in residue 318 from histidine in human G6PC2 to proline in mouse G6PC2.
Surprisingly, VU0945627 still inhibited glucose cycling in the mouse
islet-derived βTC-3 cell line. Studies using intact mouse liver microsomes and
PyRx docking suggest that this observation can be explained by an ability of
VU0945627 to also inhibit the G6P transporter SLC37A4. These data will inform
future computational modeling studies designed to identify G6PC isoform-specific
inhibitors.

Introduction

Glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase) is located in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane where it catalyzes the hydrolysis of glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) to glucose and inorganic phosphate (1,2). It is thought to exist as a multi-component enzyme system in which a G6P transporter delivers substrate from the cytosol to the active site of a glucose-6-phosphatase catalytic subunit (G6PC) in the lumen of the ER, with transporters for inorganic phosphate and glucose returning the reaction products back to the cytosol (1,2). The G6PC gene family is comprised of three members, namely G6PC1, G6PC2 and G6PC3 (1). G6PC1, also known as G6Pase and G6PC, is highly expressed in liver, kidney and intestine. G6PC2, also known as IGRP, is selectively expressed in pancreatic islets, with expression approximately 5 fold higher in beta than alpha cells (3-5). G6PC3, also known as UGRP, is widely expressed, with especially high expression in kidney, testis, skeletal muscle and brain (1).

The function of G6PC1 is to promote glucose production through catalysis of the terminal step in the gluconeogenic and glycogenolytic pathways (1). While G6PC3 can hydrolyze G6P, it appears that it primarily evolved to hydrolyze, and thus inactivate, the toxic metabolite 1,5-anhydroglucitol-6-phosphate (6). In contrast, G6PC2 acts in conjunction with glucokinase, the beta cell glucose sensor, to create a futile substrate cycle that fine-tunes the sensitivity of glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) to glucose by modulating the rate of beta cell glycolytic flux (7-10). Multiple studies have shown that this futile cycle contributes to the ability of beta cells to regulate fasting blood glucose (FBG) (7-10). For example, deletion of G6pc2 results in a leftward shift in the dose response curve for GSIS (9) such that under fasting conditions, insulin levels are the same in wild type (WT) and G6pc2 KO mice but FBG is reduced in KO mice (9-12). These observations are consistent with genome wide association studies (GWAS) showing that single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in GCK, the gene encoding glucokinase, and G6PC2 are linked to variations in FBG (13,14).

Elevated FBG has been associated with multiple adverse clinical outcomes, specifically increased risk for cardiovascular-associated mortality (CAM) (15-18), heart disease (19,20), atherosclerosis (21), brain atrophy (22), stroke (23), type 2 diabetes (T2D) (24) and various cancers (25-27). With respect to T2D, experimental data strongly suggest that elevated blood glucose drives beta cell failure (28,29), and hence the development of T2D (30-33). However, in most cases the link between elevated FBG and adverse clinical outcomes remains an association rather than a causal link. Nonetheless, the simplest hypothesis is that elevated FBG, leading to increased glycolytic flux, is causal based on the well-established negative effects of reactive oxygen species (ROS) on cell function (34,35).

Based on the association of elevated FBG with adverse clinical outcomes, therapies designed to lower FBG may be of prophylactic value for prevention of multiple conditions. Compounds that inhibit G6PC2 and/or partially inhibit G6PC1 are obvious candidates for such a therapy. While deletion of G6pc2 lowers FBG by ~20 mg/dl (9-12), deletion of G6pc1 results in lethality in ~90% of mice by 5 weeks of age (36). G6pc1 knockout mice have severe hypoglycemia, similar to humans with inactivating mutations in G6PC1, the cause of glycogen storage disease type 1a (37). These observations indicate that while a competitive G6PC2 inhibitor might be beneficial, allosteric G6PC1 inhibitors that only partially reduce enzyme activity will be required to avoid hypoglycemia. In addition, such inhibitors would need to avoid inhibiting the ability G6PC3 to hydrolyze 1,5-AG6P (6) because, while deletion of G6pc3 does not affect FBG (38), inactivating mutations in G6PC3 in humans are associated with multiple negative consequences (39).

The studies described here, facilitated by insights gained from the recently available AlphaFold2-predicted structural model for G6PC2, characterize a low affinity G6PC2 inhibitor, designated VU0945627, that was previously identified as Compound 3 (40), with a long-term goal of designing optimized G6PC2-specific inhibitors. We show that VU0945627 inhibits human G6PC2 at a lower concentration than human G6PC1 but activates human G6PC3. Surprisingly, the data suggest that, while VU0945627 inhibits G6PC2, it also inhibits the G6P transporter SLC37A4.

The previously reported Km values, measured at pH 6.5, following overexpression of these proteins in COS cells were 1.77 mM (G6PC1; (57)), 0.45 mM (G6PC2; (40)) and 2.22 mM (G6PC3; (58)), which are of similar magnitude to the values obtained following expression in 832/13 cells (Figs. 2A-C). Also consistent with previous studies (40,58), at low 2 mM G6P concentrations both G6PC1 and G6PC3 are more active at pH 6.5 than pH 8.0 whereas the activity of G6PC2 is insensitive to pH over this range (Figs. 2A-C). Finally, following expression in 831/13 cells, the observed Vmax for G6P hydrolysis by G6PC1 was considerably greater than that for G6PC2 and G6PC3, with G6PC3 also having a higher Vmax for G6P hydrolysis relative to G6PC2, similar to results obtained following transient transfection of COS cells (40,58) (Figs. 2A-C).

3.3VU0945627 inhibits Human G6PC1 and G6PC2 but Activates G6PC3Figure 3 shows that VU0945627 has selective effects on the hydrolysis of G6P by the three G6PC isoforms. Since VU0945627 was suggested to be selective for G6PC2 we initially screened the effects of VU0945627 on both G6PC1 and G6PC3 at a single high 150 μM concentration whereas we screened the effects of VU0945627 on G6PC2 at a range of concentrations encompassing the published EC50 (40). Rather than being selective for G6PC2 as previously reported (40), at a concentration of 150 μM, VU0945627 increased the Km for G6P hydrolysis by G6PC1 without affecting the Vmax (Fig. 3A), suggesting a competitive mode of inhibition. As the concentration of VU0945627 increased, a simultaneous increase in the Km and decrease in the Vmax for G6P hydrolysis by G6PC2 was observed (Figs. 3B & C), indicating a mixed mode of inhibition. Surprisingly, at a concentration of 150 μM, VU0945627 increased both the Km and Vmax for G6P hydrolysis by G6PC3 indicating that it acts as an activator rather than an inhibitor (Fig. 3D). Investigation of the effects of VU0945627 over a range of concentrations shows that the compound exhibits a higher EC50 for inhibition of human G6PC1 (Fig. 4A) than G6PC2 (Fig. 4B). In addition, the EC50 for activation of G6PC3 by VU0945627 is similar to the EC50 for inhibition of G6PC2 (Fig. 4C).

3.4VU0945627 is Predicted to Bind to Two Sites in G6PC2To explore the mechanism whereby VU0945627 inhibits G6PC2, PyRx computational docking (48) was used to computationally dock this molecule to the AlphaFold2-predicted model of human G6PC2 (Fig. 5). An agnostic blinded docking was performed that included all regions of human G6PC2, regardless of whether the regions may exist within or outside the membrane. Ten iterations of the docking analysis were performed, each of which generated nine low energy conformations of VU0945627 for a total of 90 protein-ligand binding predictions. The results suggested the existence of two distinct binding sites for VU0945627 in human G6PC2 (Fig. 5A), designated Site 1 (Fig. 5B) and Site 2 (Fig. 5C), consistent with the kinetic data suggesting that VU0945627 is a mixed inhibitor of G6PC2 (Fig. 3B). This analysis found that 41 of 90 VU0945627 poses were in position to interact with residues in Site 1, whereas 49 of 90 VU0945627 poses were in position to interact with residues in Site 2. The lowest energy VU0945627 binding pose of each iteration was positioned to interact with residues in Site 1 and had an average relative binding energy of −8.8 kcal/mol (Supplemental Table 1). These data suggest VU0945627 is capable of binding to multiple sites in G6PC2, as evaluated by virtual molecular docking.Table 1 shows the ten G6PC2 residues in each site most frequently in a position to interact with VU0945627. To evaluate this docking model, multiple residues within Site 1 and Site 2 were mutated to alanine to determine whether the variants affected G6PC2 protein expression (Fig. 6A), whether they retained G6Pase activity (Fig. 6B) and whether they were no longer inhibited by VU0945627 (Fig. 6C). As a control, the effects of mutating two residues outside Site 1 and Site 2, which we previously demonstrated did not alter protein expression or G6Pase activity (41), were also investigated. Based on the AlphaFold2-predicted model for G6PC2 and mutational analyses, Site 1 is located near the G6PC2 substrate cavity whereas Site 2 is located near a cholesterol binding motif (47). Mutation of the control residues outside Site 1 and Site 2 did not affect G6PC2 expression, activity or inhibition by VU0945627 (Figs. 6A-C; gray bars). In contrast, mutation of some residues in both Site 1 (Figs. 6A-C; blue bars) and Site 2 (Figs. 6A-C; red bars) affected G6PC2 expression, activity and/or inhibition by VU0945627. While these data broadly support the hypothesis generated from PyRx analyses, namely that residues in both Site 1 and Site 2 are required for maximal inhibition of G6PC2 by VU0945627, the data also indicate that the mechanism of G6PC2 inhibition by VU0945627 is likely complex. This conclusion is reinforced by the observation that combined mutation of residues in both Sites 1 and 2, that individually reduce inhibition of G6PC2 activity by VU0945627, did not have an additive effect (Fig. 6C; purple bars). Thus, the data suggest that although maximal inhibition of G6PC2 by VU0945627 requires the presence of intact residues in Sites 1 and 2, VU0945627 is still capable of partially inhibiting G6PC2 when residues in these Sites are mutated.

3.6VU0945627 Blocks G6P Hydrolysis in Intact MicrosomesThe studies described above were all performed on disrupted microsomes from cells expressing G6PC1 or G6PC2. Since VU0945627 is hydrophobic and Sites 1 and 2 are buried in the ER membrane we hypothesized that it would be equally effective at inhibiting G6PC enzyme activity in intact microsomal membranes. To explore that possibility, we compared the ability of VU0945627 to inhibit the hydrolysis of G6P and mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) by G6PC1 in intact versus disrupted mouse liver microsomes. SLC37A4, the most abundant G6P transporter in liver does not transport M6P whereas G6PC1 hydrolyzes both G6P and M6P (2,59). As such, the relative degree of G6P and M6P hydrolysis observed in microsome preparations is taken to reflect the degree to which the microsomes are intact versus disrupted (2). Figure 8A shows that the rate of G6P hydrolysis is much greater than the rate of M6P hydrolysis in intact liver microsomes, as expected (2), but surprisingly VU0945627 inhibited G6P hydrolysis in these intact microsomes at a concentration as low as 12.5 μM. In contrast, in disrupted microsomes, the rate of G6P hydrolysis was similar to the rate of M6P hydrolysis, again as expected (2), and inhibition of G6P hydrolysis was only observed at a higher concentration of 50 μM VU0945627 (Fig. 8B), identical to the concentration of VU0945627 that inhibited mouse G6PC1 following expression in 832/13 cells (Fig. 8C). These data suggest that our hypothesis was incorrect and instead of being equally effective at inhibiting G6PC enzyme activity in intact and disrupted microsomal membranes, VU0945627 is more effective at inhibiting G6P hydrolysis in intact microsomal membranes.

3.9VU0945627 Inhibits Purified G6PC2Due to the existence of a possible physical interaction between G6PC isoforms and SLC37A4 (61), it was possible that the inhibition of G6PC2 observed in disrupted microsomes was entirely due to an indirect effect of VU0945627 binding to SLC37A4 and indirectly affecting the activity of G6PC2. To address this possibility, we investigated the ability of VU0945627 to inhibit purified G6PC2. In addition, because in humans G6PC2 residue 219 is the site of a common non-synonymous single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), rs492594 (41) and alleles encoding a leucine or valine at residue 219 are in approximately equal abundance in the European population (62), we purified both variants.G6PC2 residue 219 is also the first residue in a putative cholesterol recognition amino acid consensus (CRAC) motif (47). We have recently shown that, in microsomal membrane preparations, the L219 variant has greater activity than the V219 variant (41), but when G6PC2 is purified in detergent micelles the L219 and V219 variants are equally active (47). The reason why a difference in activity is observed in microsomal membranes appears to be due to a differential association of the two variants with cholesterol, which is lost following purification. This conclusion is based on the observation that the addition of cholesteryl hemi-succinate (CHS) to the purified enzymes results in a substantial decrease in the Vmax of the V219 variant but a modest effect on the L219 variant (47). Figure 11A shows that VU0945627 inhibited the activity of both the V219 and L219 purified G6PC2 variants (Fig. 11A) and the magnitude of this inhibition was not affected by the amino acid at position 219 or CHS (Fig. 11B). These data indicate that the inhibitory effect of VU0945627 on the G6Pase activity of G6PC2 is not dependent on ER microsomal lipids or the presence of SLC37A4. Together, these data suggest VU0945627 directly inhibits G6PC2 and requires intact amino acids in Sites 1 and 2 for the maximal response, and that VU0945627 is also capable of inhibiting G6P transport by SLC37A4.

4.DiscussionThis study focused on characterizing a G6PC2 inhibitor, VU0945627, previously identified as Compound 3. Kinetic analyses reveal that VU0945627 is a mixed G6PC2 inhibitor, both increasing the Km and lowering the Vmax for G6P hydrolysis (Fig. 3). However, VU0945627 is not selective for G6PC2; inhibition of G6P hydrolysis by human G6PC1 is observed at much higher inhibitor concentrations and VU0945627 activates, rather than inhibits, G6P hydrolysis by human G6PC3 (Fig. 3 & 4).PyRx docking studies suggest that VU0945627 binds allosterically to two sites in G6PC2, designated Site 1 and Site 2. The simplest hypothesis is that the binding of VU0945627 to Site 1 in G6PC2, which is near the substrate cavity, affects the Km for G6P hydrolysis, acting like a competitive inhibitor, whereas binding of VU0945627 to Site 2 in G6PC2 affects the Vmax, acting like a noncompetitive inhibitor. The intricacies of these predictions were not assessed here, however, as our mutagenesis experiments only screened mutants at single G6P and inhibitor concentrations. This approach is therefore only able to determine whether residues in these Sites contribute to inhibitor binding under the specific conditions tested. As such, our experiments are unable to distinguish whether the mutations made have marked effects on the Km and/or Vmax for G6P hydrolysis. Importantly, these experiments also do not negate the possibility that the inhibitory action of VU0945627 is being indirectly affected by a conformational changes in the protein induced by mutations. As such, we also cannot rule out the possibility that the PyRx docking analysis is flawed such that VU0945627 binding to a single site in G6PC2 yields the observed mixed inhibition. The only way to definitively generate compelling evidence supporting the two-site binding model would be to obtain a crystal or Cryo-EM structure of G6PC2 bound to VU0945627. However, our priority is to optimize VU0945627 so as to generate a high-affinity G6PC2-specific inhibitor before performing such studies.The AF2-model has very high confidence (pLDDT > 90) for the regions of G6PC2 that are predicted to interact with VU0945627 and high confidence (90 > pLDDT > 70) for the regions of SLC37A4 that are predicted to interact with VU0945627. While we chose to perform the docking analysis in the absence of a membrane, the AF2 algorithm determines protein structure with an implicit knowledge of how proteins fold within a membrane. This is supported by molecular dynamics modeling on the closely related G6PC1 protein that suggests that the protein is almost entirely embedded in the membrane and shows low variability from the AF2-predicted model (63). Given the hydrophobic nature of the inhibitory compound, we expect it to be capable of traversing the membrane and reaching the hydrophobic Sites that it is predicted to interact with in these proteins.Experiments in mice suggest that the nature of the physiological benefits associated with G6PC2 are that it confers a transient, beneficial elevation in FBG during periods of stress (64,65) and that it protects against mild hypoglycemia in response to a ketogenic diet or prolonged fasting (66). Previous biobank and molecular analyses have demonstrated an association between reduced G6PC2 activity and reduced FBG with no consistent evidence for negative consequences (10,41). In one biobank study, performed using the rs560887 G6PC2 SNP that has a mild effect on G6PC2 RNA splicing (67), reduced G6PC2 expression was linked to an increased risk for acute pancreatitis (10). However, a second study examining the impact non-synonymous G6PC2 SNPs that have a major effect on G6PC2 protein expression and/or activity did not replicate this association (41). The global deletion of G6pc2 in mice lowers FBG by ~20 mg/dl (9-12). We have no evidence for negative long-term consequences of G6pc2 deletion in mice though demonstrating the predicted benefits of reduced FBG, based on human studies, is likely to be challenging in mice given their much shorter life span. Overall, these observations suggest that future studies should be designed to optimize inhibitors that completely, rather than partially block G6PC2 activity. This study focused on characterizing a G6PC2 inhibitor, VU0945627, previously identified as Compound 3. Kinetic analyses reveal that VU0945627 is a mixed G6PC2 inhibitor, both increasing the Km and lowering the Vmax for G6P hydrolysis (Fig. 3). However, VU0945627 is not selective for G6PC2; inhibition of G6P hydrolysis by human G6PC1 is observed at much higher inhibitor concentrations and VU0945627 activates, rather than inhibits, G6P hydrolysis by human G6PC3 (Fig. 3 & 4).

PyRx docking studies suggest that VU0945627 binds allosterically to two sites in G6PC2, designated Site 1 and Site 2. The simplest hypothesis is that the binding of VU0945627 to Site 1 in G6PC2, which is near the substrate cavity, affects the Km for G6P hydrolysis, acting like a competitive inhibitor, whereas binding of VU0945627 to Site 2 in G6PC2 affects the Vmax, acting like a noncompetitive inhibitor. The intricacies of these predictions were not assessed here, however, as our mutagenesis experiments only screened mutants at single G6P and inhibitor concentrations. This approach is therefore only able to determine whether residues in these Sites contribute to inhibitor binding under the specific conditions tested. As such, our experiments are unable to distinguish whether the mutations made have marked effects on the Km and/or Vmax for G6P hydrolysis. Importantly, these experiments also do not negate the possibility that the inhibitory action of VU0945627 is being indirectly affected by a conformational changes in the protein induced by mutations. As such, we also cannot rule out the possibility that the PyRx docking analysis is flawed such that VU0945627 binding to a single site in G6PC2 yields the observed mixed inhibition. The only way to definitively generate compelling evidence supporting the two-site binding model would be to obtain a crystal or Cryo-EM structure of G6PC2 bound to VU0945627. However, our priority is to optimize VU0945627 so as to generate a high-affinity G6PC2-specific inhibitor before performing such studies.

The AF2-model has very high confidence (pLDDT > 90) for the regions of G6PC2 that are predicted to interact with VU0945627 and high confidence (90 > pLDDT > 70) for the regions of SLC37A4 that are predicted to interact with VU0945627. While we chose to perform the docking analysis in the absence of a membrane, the AF2 algorithm determines protein structure with an implicit knowledge of how proteins fold within a membrane. This is supported by molecular dynamics modeling on the closely related G6PC1 protein that suggests that the protein is almost entirely embedded in the membrane and shows low variability from the AF2-predicted model (63). Given the hydrophobic nature of the inhibitory compound, we expect it to be capable of traversing the membrane and reaching the hydrophobic Sites that it is predicted to interact with in these proteins.

Experiments in mice suggest that the nature of the physiological benefits associated with G6PC2 are that it confers a transient, beneficial elevation in FBG during periods of stress (64,65) and that it protects against mild hypoglycemia in response to a ketogenic diet or prolonged fasting (66). Previous biobank and molecular analyses have demonstrated an association between reduced G6PC2 activity and reduced FBG with no consistent evidence for negative consequences (10,41). In one biobank study, performed using the rs560887 G6PC2 SNP that has a mild effect on G6PC2 RNA splicing (67), reduced G6PC2 expression was linked to an increased risk for acute pancreatitis (10). However, a second study examining the impact non-synonymous G6PC2 SNPs that have a major effect on G6PC2 protein expression and/or activity did not replicate this association (41).
