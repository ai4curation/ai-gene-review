Tissue-resident memory CD8 T (TRM) cells provide critical antiviral and
antitumor immunity, but the molecular pathways guiding their development are not
fully defined. Here, we identify the G protein-coupled receptor GPR25, induced
by TGF-β signaling, as a regulator of TRM cell formation. Using adoptive
transfer, we found that Gpr25-deficient T cells infiltrated tissues normally
after viral infection but failed to efficiently develop into TRM cells. In a
tumor challenge model, Gpr25 deficiency impaired TRM cell expansion and tumor
control. Single-cell transcriptomics revealed defective acquisition of stem-like
TRM cell features, including expression of T cell factor 1 (TCF1). After antigen
rechallenge, Gpr25-deficient TRM cells showed impaired secondary TRM cell
differentiation and maintenance. Moreover, Gpr25-deficient T cells displayed
negative enrichment of TGF-β signature genes and impaired responses to TGF-β,
indicating that GPR25 enhances TGF-β signaling to promote TRM cell development.
Our findings suggest that modulating GPR25 function may provide a therapeutic
strategy to improve TRM cell responses in infection and cancer.

INTRODUCTION

Immunological memory is a hallmark of adaptive immunity and the basis of vaccination (1). Tissue-resident memory CD8 T (TRM) cells are a distinct population of memory T cells that primarily reside within tissues and respond immediately against pathogens invading barrier tissues, thus representing the first-line of defense (2, 3). TRM cells are phenotypically, functionally, transcriptionally, and metabolically distinct from circulating memory T cell subsets (4–8) like central memory T (TCM) and effector memory T (TEM) cells. For example, TRM cells lack expression of molecules required for tissue egress and trafficking to lymph nodes like Sphingosine-1-Phosphate Receptor (S1PR)1, S1PR5, C-C motif chemokine receptor (CCR)7 and L-selectin (CD62L) (3, 9), and mostly express high levels of CD69, CD49a, and C-X-C motif chemokine receptor (CXCR)6, which play important roles in the tissue retention and localization of TRM cells (10–12). The majority of TRM cells that reside within the epithelium also express the ⍺E integrin, CD103 (4, 13), whereas, murine TRM cells that reside in non-epithelial tissues, such as liver (6, 11) and kidney (14, 15) do not express high levels of CD103 (6, 11, 16, 17). Although the molecules that play an important role in the development and function of TRM cells are not fully understood, several studies have defined the key transcriptional regulators such as Hobit, Blimp-1, Runx3, Etv5, Id3, fatty acid binding proteins (FABPs) (6, 16–19), and cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-15 and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) that contribute to TRM cell development and maintenance (20–23).

Besides their protective role in infections, it is now well-appreciated that TRM cells also play a critical role in cancer immunosurveillance (24, 25). Several studies have shown a positive association between tumor TRM cells and favorable clinical outcome in patients with breast cancer (26, 27), cervical cancer (28), esophageal cancer (29), gastric cancer (30), liver cancer (31), head and neck cancer (32), lung cancer (33), melanoma (34), and pancreatic cancer (35). These results suggest that the abundance of TRM cells in the tumor tissue is a prognostic indicator for survival outcomes. Functional studies in murine tumor models have further confirmed the pivotal role of TRM cells in driving anti-tumor immune responses (18, 19). Thus, targets that modulate the magnitude and quality of TRM cell responses are likely to have a potential therapeutic role in certain infections and cancer types as well as in autoimmune diseases, where TRM cells play a pathogenic role (36, 37).

Unbiased gene expression studies in humans and mice have discovered a number of genes that are highly enriched for their expression in TRM cells isolated from various tissues and tumor types (6, 16, 18, 19, 21, 27, 32, 33, 38). However, the functional role of the molecules encoded by TRM cell-enriched genes is not well-defined. One such molecule is a poorly characterized G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), called GPR25 (39). GPCRs are the largest superfamily of transmembrane receptors in the genome (40), they interact with a large number of extracellular ligands (41) and transmit intracellular signals by activating heterotrimeric guanine nucleotide-binding proteins (42). The structure and cellular localization of GPCRs make them attractive targets for drug development (43). Our recent transcriptomic study showed that GPR25 is expressed at significantly higher levels in TRM cells compared with non-TRM cells in the human lung and tumor tissues (33). GPR25 has long been considered an orphan receptor with no known endogenous ligand. However, a recent study reported that C-X-C motif chemokine 17 (CXCL17), a chemokine expressed mainly by epithelial cells in the respiratory tract and non-intestinal mucosae tissue, functions as a ligand for GPR25 and mediates lymphocyte homing to these tissues (44). Additionally, Gpr25 also has been reported to have a ligand-independent constitutive activity (45). In this study, using genetic models, we demonstrate that Gpr25 plays an important role in the development and function of TRM cells in the liver and lung.

RESULTSTGF-β induces expression of GPR25 in CD8 T cellsTRM cells play a central role in local immune surveillance and protection and we had previously reported that GPR25 expression was significantly increased in both lung and tumor-infiltrating CD8 TRM cells when compared to non-TRM cells (33). The expression of GPR25 was strongly correlated with the expression of several key TRM cell-associated genes, such as ZNF683, RBPJ, ITGAE, ITGA1, S1PR1 and S1PR5 (33). Analysis of the DICE dataset (46) confirmed that expression of GPR25 was enriched in lung TRM cells when compared to non-TRM cell subsets and circulating CD8 T cell subsets (Fig. 1, A and B). As expected, TRM cell-associated genes were enriched in lung TRM cells (Fig. 1A). Because TGF-β is known to regulate the expression of many TRM cell-associated genes (23), we tested its role in regulating the expression of GPR25. TGF-β treatment resulted in rapid induction of GPR25 in primary human CD8 T cells that were polyclonally activated in vitro by using anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies (Fig. 1C). To determine how TGF-β signaling regulates expression of GPR25, we analyzed assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) profile of the GPR25 locus in human CD8 T cell subsets (33, 47). Three regions (C1, C2, C3) upstream of GPR25 transcription start site (TSS) displayed prominent transposase accessible peaks (ATAC-seq peaks) in tumor-infiltrating TRM cells compared to non-TRM cells (Fig. 1D). The C2 region, however, displayed prominent transposase accessibility in activated CD8 T cells. Our analysis suggested that these three cis-regulatory regions are likely to be involved in the regulation of GPR25 with C1, C2, and C3 regions associated with TRM cells and C2 region with T cell receptor (TCR) activation. The analysis of ENCODE transcription factor chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-seq data (48) showed that C1, C2, and C3 cis-regulatory regions bind to nuclear factor of activated T-cells (NFAT) and suppressor of mothers against decapentaplegic (SMAD)1, key transcription factors downstream of TCR (49) and TGF-β signaling (50), respectively, suggesting that expression of GPR25 was likely to be coregulated by these signaling pathways (Fig. 1D).We next assessed suitability of mouse models for testing the role of GPR25 in the development of TRM cells in vivo. We noted that GPR25 is highly conserved across species with 75% sequence homology with the murine ortholog (fig. S1A). Similar to the observation in human T cells, Gpr25 expression was readily induced by TGF-β in murine CD8 T cells that were activated in vitro by crosslinking TCR and CD28, but its expression gradually declined over time (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, in unmanipulated wildtype C57BL/6 mice (> 90 days old), CD8 T cells in non-lymphoid organs such as small intestinal intra-epithelial lymphocytes (siIEL) and liver, especially those expressing the TRM cell marker CD69 (6, 11, 16), expressed higher levels of Gpr25 transcripts. In contrast, splenic CD8 T cells, which comprise mainly of circulating naïve and central memory T cells (TCM cells) showed minimal Gpr25 expression (Fig. 1F and fig. S1B). As controls, cohorts of CD8KO mice that did not receive T cells were also included to specifically assess the contribution of transferred OT-I T cells on outcomes. As expected, we found that CD8KO mice receiving Gpr25-deficient OT-I cells had poor survival outcomes, with nearly 50% death rate, whereas most CD8KO mice receiving Gpr25-sufficient OT-I cells survived at the time of analysis (Fig. 7F). Additionally, the number of metastatic nodules was significantly lower in CD8KO mice that received Gpr25-sufficient OT-I T cells when compared to those that received Gpr25-deficient OT-I cells or had no transfer of OT-I T cells (Fig. 7G). To assess whether Gpr25-deficient lung TRM cells exhibit impaired cytotoxicity, we isolated CD69+ WT and KO OT-I T cells from lungs 30 days after LCMV-OVA infection and co-cultured them with OVA-expressing B16F10 melanoma cells. In this in vitro killing assay, no significant differences were observed in tumor-killing capacity between Gpr25-sufficient and Gpr25-deficient T cells (Fig. S6D). These findings suggest that impaired metastasis control of Gpr25-deficient T cells is not due to intrinsic cytotoxic defects, but rather to compromised TRM stemness, which limits their expansion and persistence upon tumor challenge and thereby reduces their ability to control lung metastases. Thus, Gpr25 plays an important T cell-intrinsic role in sustaining TRM cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target to enhance tumor control.

For the lung dataset, Gpr25+/+ (WT; CD45.1.2) and Gpr25−/− (KO; CD45.2) OT-I T cells isolated from lungs of 6 recipient mice at 30 days post infection were sorted and pooled. Sorted cells were processed using the 10x Genomics 3’ Tag v3.0 chemistry kit following manufacturer’s instruction (10x Genomics), and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Sequence data were processed by mapping reads to the Mus musculus mm10 genome (v3.0.0) using the Cell Ranger (v3.1.0) (10x Genomics), followed by aggregation and analysis in R with Seurat (v4.1.1) as described (89–92). Doublets were removed using Scrublet (v0.2.3) (93). Low-quality cells were excluded from further analyses based on these criteria: < 300 and > 5,000 unique genes, < 200 and > 30,000 total UMI content, and > 10% of mitochondrial UMIs for liver dataset; and < 300 and > 5,000 unique genes, < 200 and > 20,000 total UMI content, and > 10% of mitochondrial UMIs for lung dataset. Unbiased clustering analysis was performed using Seurat software (v4.1.1). Variable genes were detected with the variance stabilizing transformation (VST) method and most variable genes (UMI mean > 0.01) accounting for the tope 25% of the total standardized variance were selected for downstream analysis. Cells were clustered using Seurat’s functions FindNeighbors and FindClusters with a resolution of 0.2 and 0.4 for the liver and lung datasets, respectively. Pairwise single-cell differential gene expression analysis was performed using MAST (v1.14.0) (94) after normalizing the data to log2 counts per million (log2(CPM+1)). A gene was considered as differentially expressed if its Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P value (false discovery rate, FDR) < 0.05 and log2 fold change > 0.25. Cluster-specific markers were determined by MAST using the Seurat function FindAllMarkers with default parameters.

ATAC-seq data analysis.ATAC-seq data of resting and activated naïve CD8 T cells, TRM and non-TRM CD8 T cells were analyzed as described previously using ATACproc (33, 47). Briefly, single-end ATAC-seq reads were aligned to hg19 reference genome using Bowtie2 (version 2.3.3.1) (95, 96), with parameters “-k 4 --mm –threads 8 –X 2000”. Reads mapped to mitochondrial and chromosome Y were excluded. Uniquely mapped reads with mapping quality ≥ 30 were retained using SAMtools (version 1.6) (97). Duplicate reads were removed by MarkDuplicates. To correct for Tn5 transposase insetion (98), all reads were shifted by + 4 bp (positive strand) or −5 bp (negative strand), using alignmentSieve (99). Reads overlapping ENCODE blacklisted regions were removed. Coverage tracks were normalized to bins per million mapped reads (BPM), using BamCoverage (99) (--binSize 50 --normalizeUsing BPM –extendReads). Peaks calling was performed using MACS2 (version 2.1.0) (-g hs –q 0.05 -- nomodel --nolambda --keep-dup all --call-summits --shift −100 --extsize 200) (100). Tracks were visualized using the UCSC Genome Browser.

Statistical analysis.Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Data are presented as the mean, means ± SEM or means ± SD, as indicated in each figure legend. No data were excluded. All experiments were independently replicated at least twice. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used to compare two groups. The Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test was used for survival analysis. Statistical significance was defined as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. Details of the statistical tests employed are provided in each figure legends.
