## Full Text

Abstract

RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) is formed during RNA interference (RNAi), whereas stress granules (SG) are assembled in response to cellular stress. Here, we demonstrate an interesting connection between RISC and SG that may involve argonaute 2 (Ago2), a core component of RISC. We analyzed SG induction by arsenite, the commonly used SG inducer. SG formation was suppressed in heat shock transcription factor 1 (Hsf1) or hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (Hif1α) shRNA-transfected cells but not in Hsf1 or Hif1α-knockout cells, suggesting that RNAi per se (rather than gene deficiency) may account for the suppressive effect on SG. In support, the suppressive effect of RNAi on SG formation was reversed by the RISC-loading inhibitor aurintricarboxylic acid. In non-RNAi cells, arsenite induced the accumulation of Ago2 in SGs as shown by its colocalization and coimmunoprecipitation with SG proteins, but Ago2 was not recruited to SG in the cells with RNAi. Consistently, arsenite induced the dissociation of Ago2 from RISC proteins in non-RNAi cells but not in RNAi cells. CRISPR-Cas9-medicated ablation of Ago2 attenuated SG formation during arsenite treatment, suggesting a critical role of Ago2 in SG assembly. Together, these results indicate that RISC and SG may compete for some key components, such as Ago2. In response to cellular stress, Ago2 is recruited for SG assembly; however, during RNAi, Ago2 is held in RISC, becoming unavailable for SG formation.

Keywords: argonaute 2, arsenite, RNA-induced silencing complex, RNA interference, stress granule

INTRODUCTION

Stress granules (SG) are cytosol aggregates of proteins and RNAs that are formed in response to cellular stress ( 17 , 19 ). Functionally, SG may protect RNAs as an adaptive response. There is a dynamic process for SG formation and disassembly ( 4 , 9 ). which is regulated by mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and eukaryotic translation initiation factor-2α kinase 3 (PERK) signaling pathways ( 10 , 20 ). Following that, many RNA-binding proteins are recruited, including eukaryotic initiation factor 3 (eIF3) ( 16 ), Ras-GAP SH3 domain-binding protein (G3BP) ( 23 ), protein kinase C-α (PKCα) ( 11 ), human antigen R (HuR) ( 3 ), and others ( 17 , 19 ).

RNA interference (RNAi) is a fundamental cell biologic process that inhibits gene expression by inducing target RNA degradation and/or translation repression. RNAi can be induced by exogenous small-hairpin RNAs (shRNA) and small-interfering RNA (siRNA) or by endogenous microRNA (miRNA). In cells, shRNAs are cleaved by Dicer, creating ~22-nt double-stranded siRNAs ( 5 ). siRNAs recognize the target mRNAs in a sequence-specific manner and bring them to the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), resulting in target RNA degradation. Argonaute 2 (Ago2) is a core component of, and the key endonuclease in, RISC that is responsible for siRNA-induced RNA degradation.

Although both SG and RISC are protein/RNA complexes, their relationship is unclear. Nonetheless, they may share some common components. For example, Ago2 is well recognized as a core protein of RISC, but it has also been localized in SG ( 12 , 18 ). In 2011, Detzer and colleagues ( 6 ) further reported that cellular stress led to Ago2 accumulation in SG, which remarkably was accompanied by a decreased RNAi efficacy of siRNAs in these cells, suggesting the possible existence of an antagonizing relationship between SG and RISC. However, in 2015, Lopez-Orozco and colleagues ( 14 ) showed that serine 798 mutation of Ago2 abrogated its association with SG but did not affect gene-silencing activity. Moreover, it remains unknown whether RNAi may affect SG formation.

In this study, we show that siRNA-mediated RNAi suppresses SG formation during cellular stress. Mechanistically, we demonstrate an important role of Ago2 in the antagonizing relationship between RNAi and SG formation. We show that Ago2 is recruited to SG during cellular stress. In RNAi, Ago2 is sequestered in RISC, becoming unavailable for SG formation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cells and plasmids.

The rat kidney proximal tubular cell (RPTC) line was originally obtained from Dr. Hopfer (Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, OH) and maintained as described previously ( 7 , 22 , 28 ). The human embryonic kidney 293(HEK293) cell was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. The Boston University mouse proximal tubule cell (BUMPT-306) line was originally obtained from Drs. Lieberthal and Schwartz at Boston University and grown in DMEM medium (cat. no. 11965-092; ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) with 10% FBS. RPTC cell lines stably transfected with heat shock transcription factor 1 (Hsf1) shRNA, hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (Hif1α) shRNA, or negative control sequences were generated in our previous work ( 15 , 24 ). Wild-type (WT) and Hif1α-null mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) were obtained from Dr. Gregg Semenza (Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, Baltimore, MD) ( 29 ). Hsf1 knockout (KO) MEF cells were a kind gift from Dr. Ivor J. Benjamin (Medical College of Wisconsin, Milwaukee, WI) ( 26 ). Plasmids encoding enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP)-Hsf1 and Flag-Ago2 were constructed in our laboratory.

Antibodies and reagents.

The antibodies used in this study were from the following sources: rabbit monoclonal antibodies anti-Hif1α (cat. no. 14179), anti-Ago2 (cat. no. 2897), and anti-phospho-eIF2α (Ser51) (cat. no. 3597), rabbit polyclonal antibodies anti-Hsf1 (cat. no. 4356s), anti-PKCα (cat. no. 2056), and anti-phospho-mTOR (cat. no. 2971), and mouse monoclonal anti-phospho-p70s6k (Thr389) antibody (cat. no. 9206) from Cell Signaling Technology (Beverly, MA); mouse monoclonal antibodies anti-β-actin (cat. no. SAB2102429) from Sigma (St. Louis, MO); goat polyclonal anti-eIF3 antibody (cat. no. sc-16377) and mouse monoclonal antibodies anti-trans-activation-responsive RNA-binding protein (anti-TRBP2; cat. no. sc-514124) and anti-HuR (cat. no. sc-5261) from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA); peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (cat. no. 111-035-003) or goat anti-mouse (cat. no. 115-035-003) polyclonal antibodies, FITC-conjugated donkey anti-mouse polyclonal antibody (cat. no. 715-095-150), and Cy3-conjugated donkey anti-goat polyclonal antibody (cat. no. 705-165-147) from Jackson ImmunoResearch (West Grove, PA). The antibody information is also detailed in Supplemental Table S1 (Supplemental Material for this article is available online at the Journal website). Sodium arsenite (cat. no. S9663), aurintricarboxylic acid (ATA) (cat. no. A1895), and PD98059 (cat. no. P215) were purchased from Sigma. Puromycin (cat. no. 631305) was purchased from Clontech (San Jose, CA). Hygromycin B (50 mg/ml) (cat. no. 10687010) was purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific.

Arsenite treatment.

Cells were treated with 0.5 mM sodium arsenite for the indicated time, ranging from 0 to 60 min. Another group of cells was left untreated as control. For most experiments, arsenite treatment started just before cells reached confluence.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy.

Stress granules were revealed in cells by immunofluorescence staining of marker proteins as described by Drs. Kedersha and Anderson ( 8 ). Briefly, after arsenite treatment, cells were washed with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (cat. no. P6148; Sigma) in PBS for 15 min and then immediately permeabilized with −20°C precooled methanol for 10 min, then washed with PBS. The cells were incubated in blocking buffer (5% normal horse serum in PBS) for 1 h. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies (anti-eIF3α with or without anti-HuR) diluted in 0.1% Tween-20/PBS (PBST) containing 5% BSA (cat. no. A4628; Sigma) overnight at 4°C. After being washed with PBST three times, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies (FITC-donkey anti-mouse or Cy3-donkey anti-goat IgG) for 1 h at room temperature. Coverslips were mounted in Prolong Gold Antifade mounting media with DAPI (cat. no. P36931 ; ThermoFisher Scientific). Samples were visualized with a ×63 oil-immersion LSM 700 confocal laser-scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The operators were blinded to the treatments in all morphological assessments.

Coimmunoprecipitation.

After treatment, cells were rinsed with PBS and scratch collected in PBS-containing protease inhibitor cocktails (cat. no. 04693159001; Roche, Mannheim, Germany). The cells were then freeze-thawed four times in liquid nitrogen. After centrifugation, the cell supernatant was incubated with protein A/G agarose beads (cat. no. sc-2003; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 20 min to remove the nonspecific binding. The precleaned supernatants were incubated with a specific immunoprecipitation antibody (or with the corresponding IgG as control) at 4°C. After overnight incubation, equal amounts of protein A/G agarose beads were added to each tube and were incubated for another 4 h at 4°C. The beads were washed three times with PBS, boiled at 100°C for 10 min in 2× loading buffer, and finally analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blot.

Ago2 knockout HEK cell construction by CRISPR/Cas9 system.

HEK293 cells were grown in DMEM media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (cat. no. 10100147; ThermoFisher Scientific), antibiotic-antimycotic (100×) (cat. no. 15240062; ThermoFisher Scientific), and 1% l -glutamate (cat. no. 25030081; ThermoFisher Scientific). Ago2 KO was performed by using the CRISPR/Cas9 system (cat. no. KN218078 ; Origene, Rockville, MD). Briefly, 1 µg of donor plasmid pUC containing GFP and puromycin-resistance cassette was cotransfected into HEK293 cells with 1 µg of pCas-Guide-containing guide RNA sequence or scramble sequence using Lipofectamine 2000 (cat. no. 11668019; ThermoFisher Scientific). Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were split 1:10 to fresh medium, grown for 3 days, and then split 1:10 an additional six times. The cells were then selected with 2 µg/ml puromycin (cat. no. 631305; Clontech). The puromycin-resistant single-cell clones were seeded in 96-well plates by serial dilution. The expression levels of Ago2 in knockout cells were compared with wild-type cells by Western blotting using anti-Ago2 antibody.

Quantification of SGs.

The original raw confocal images were converted to .tif files. The number of SG-bearing cells was quantified using the ImageJ software. The plug-in “Analyze particles” was used to count the number of SGs. The percentage of cells with SGs was calculated by counting the number of cells displaying SGs and expressing them as 100 × [(number of cells with SGs)/(total number of cells)]. Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad prism software 6. Student’s t -test was used to compare two groups of data. For multiple groups, statistical significance was analyzed by one-way ANOVA (Bonferroni’s multiple-comparison test). Statistical analysis was performed with a confidence interval of P < 0.05. The standard error of the mean (SE) was chosen to estimate the statistical variability.

RESULTS

Arsenite-induced SG formation is decreased in Hsf1 knockdown cells but not in Hsf1 knockout cells.

The original goal of this study was to determine whether stress response transcription factors like Hsf1 regulate SG formation. To this end, we compared renal proximal tubule cells (RPTC) that were stably transfected with Hsf1 shRNA (knockdown, KD) with scrambled control sequence shRNA (negative control, NC) ( 15 ). The cells were treated with arsenite, a commonly used inducer of SG. As shown in Fig. 1 A , Hsf1 KD RPTC cells formed fewer SGs than negative control cells. In quantification, SG-positive cells were decreased by 42.7% in Hsf1 KD cells (35.4%, 12/34) compared with NC cells (78.1%, 29/37) ( Fig. 1 B ).

To confirm the role of Hsf1 in SG formation, we further examined Hsf1 knockout (KO) and wild-type (WT) MEF cells. To our surprise, no significant difference was observed in SG formation in these two cell lines ( Fig. 1 A ). In quantification, SG was induced in ~90% of cells in both cell lines ( Fig. 1 B ). We verified the lack of Hsf1 in Hsf1 KO MEF cells and the decreased Hsf1 expression in Hsf1 KD RPTC cells ( Fig. 1 C ). These results suggested that the decreased SG formation in Hsf1 KD RPTC cells was not caused by Hsf1 deficiency.

To extend the finding to other cell types, we transiently transfected HEK293, BUMPT, and RPTC cells with Hsf1 shRNA plasmids or control sequence plasmids and then treated them with arsenite. Compared with negative control miRNA sequence transfection (NC), transient transfection of Hsf1 shRNA (KD) resulted in significant decreases in stress granule formation, 49.5% in HEK cells (from 29/35 to 9/27), 64.8% in BUMPT cells (from 32/35 to 8/30), and 67.9% (from 30/35 to 5/28) in RPTC cells ( Fig. 2 and Supplemental Fig. S1).

Arsenite-induced SG formation is decreased in Hif1α knockdown cells but not in Hif1α knockout cells.

We then determined whether shRNAs targeting other genes than Hsf1 could also block SG formation. For this purpose, we compared Hif1α-shRNA (KD)-transfected RPTC cells with negative control sequence shRNA (NC)-transfected RPTC cells ( 24 ). SG formation was minimal in both cell lines without stimulation. Following arsenite treatment, most NC cells produced SGs, whereas some of the KD cells did not ( Fig. 3 A ). Cell counting indicated that the ~30% Hif1α KD cells (8/24) did not form SGs during arsenite treatment ( Fig. 3 B ). We also compared SG formation in Hif1α knockout cells and wild-type cells and did not find significant differences ( Fig. 3 , A and B ). Hif1α expression in these cell lines was verified by immunoblotting ( Fig. 3 C ).

We further examined the effects of shRNAs against autophagy-related gene 5 (Atg5) and Beclin1. The shRNA sequences were cloned into pEGFP plasmids for transfection to label the successfully transfected cells. As shown in Supplemental Fig. S2, arsenite induced SGs in EGFP-scramble sequence-transfected RPTC cells (puncta in two green fluorescent cells); however, SG formation was suppressed in EGFP-siAtg5 or EGFP-siBeclin1-transfected cells.

SG formation and related phenotypic changes in shRNA-transfected cells is restored by RISC-loading inhibitor ATA.

The above results suggested that shRNA transfection, and not knockdown of specific genes, might lead to the decrease of SG formation. Following transfection into cells, shRNAs induce the formation of RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), which contains multiple RNA-binding proteins. Interestingly, in the literature, there are scattered reports about the involvement of some of the key RISC proteins in SG assembly, including Ago family protein ( 12 ) and HuR ( 2 ). We therefore hypothesized that, in shRNA-transfected cells, these proteins may accumulate in the RISC complex and become unavailable or less available for SG formation. To test this possibility, we initially examined the effect of aurintricarboxylic acid (ATA), a RISC-loading inhibitor ( 21 ). The rationale was that, by inhibiting RISC loading, ATA would prevent RISC assembly and make Ago and HuR proteins available for SG formation. ATA was added to cells 2 h before arsenite treatment. As shown in Fig. 4 , A and B , ATA restored SG formation in Hsf1 shRNA-transfected cells. Similarly, ATA restored SG formation in Hif1α shRNA-transfected cells (Supplemental Fig. S3, A and B ). As expected, ATA improved Hsf1 or Hif1α expression in Hsf1 or Hif1α shRNA-transfected cells, verifying that ATA inhibited RISC formation or activity ( Figs. 4 C and Supplemental Fig. S3 C ).

In addition to SG formation, we observed that RPTC cells treated with sodium arsenite were shrunken, showing gaps between cells. The gaps of RPTC Hsf1 KD cells were obviously bigger than that of Hsf1 NC cells following arsenite treatment ( Fig. 5 A ). The gaps were mainly caused by the detachment of cells. Arsenite treatment for 4 h caused the detachment of 31.1% of cells in the RPTC Hsf1 KD group, which was suppressed to 12.3% by ATA ( Fig. 5 B ).

Accumulation of Ago2 to SGs that is inhibited by shRNA transfection.

Ago2 is a core component of the RISC complex, which cleaves the target mRNA with complete complementarity across the center of the complementary siRNA or shRNA strand. In RPTC cells, arsenite treatment induced the colocalization of Ago2 (green) with the SG marker eIF3 (red), suggesting the recruitment of Ago2 to SG ( Fig. 6 A ). We further analyzed the protein complexes by coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP). In control negative-transfected cells (NC), Ago2 showed co-IP with RISC (HuR) and SG (eIF3, PKCα) proteins. Arsenite treatment increased the co-IP of Ago2 with PKCα. In shRNA-transfected cells, arsenite treatment did not seem to increase Ago2 co-IP with PKCα; instead, it increased the co-IP of Ago2 with HuR. These results suggested that Ago2 may move to SG upon arsenite treatment, and this may be inhibited by RISC formation ( Fig. 6 B ). In addition, by co-IP assay we verified the interaction of Ago2 with other SG proteins, such as Tia1 ( Fig. 6 C ).

The data presented above suggest an interesting possibility that Ago2 accumulates in RISC during RNAi, but, in the absence of si/shRNAs, Ago2 may move to SG upon arsenite treatment. To have some preliminary information about the kinetics of Ago2 release from RISC during arsenite treatment, we analyzed the co-IP of Ago2 with the RISC marker protein TRBP2. RPTC cells with control or Hsf1 ( Fig. 7 ) or Hif1α shRNA transfection were treated with arsenite for a time course to collect cell lysate for co-IP using anti-TRBP2 antibody. In negative control (NC) transfection cells, co-IP between Ago2 and TRBP2 decreased in an arsenite treatment time-dependent manner, starting from 15 min ( Fig. 7 A ; Supplemental Fig. S4). However, in RPTC Hsf1 or Hif1α shRNA-transfected KD cells, co-IP of Ago2 with TRBP2 did not change significantly during arsenite treatment. Quantification ( Fig. 7 B ) further supports the kinetics of Ago2 dissociation from TRBP2 in RISC during arsenite treatment in normal cells but not in shRNA-transfected cells. These data suggest that Ago2 may be held in RISC in the presence of si/shRNAs, preventing it from accumulating in SG in response to cellular stress.

SG formation is reduced in Ago2-deficient cells.

What is the function of Ago2 in SG? To address this question, we generated Ago2-deficient HEK293 cells by using the CRISPR-Cas9 genome-editing technology. Two Ago2-specific guide sequences (G1: CACGGGCAGGAGCGCGCGAA and G2: CCGGTGAGTCCTGGCGCTCG) were selected for guiding Cas9 loading and cutting the ago2 gene from the genome. The genome was then repaired by donor template DNA containing the functional GFP and puromycin-resistance cassette with homologous arms (left and right) ( Fig. 8 A ). The ago2 knockout effect was confirmed by immunoblotting ( Fig. 8 B ). We compared wild-type cells and ago2 knockout cells for SG formation. Upon arsenite treatment, SGs were detected in most wild-type cells (95%) but not so in Ago2 knockout cells (25.7%) ( Fig. 8 , C and D ), supporting a critical role of Ago2 in SG formation.

DISCUSSION

This study was originally designed to investigate the role of Hsf1 in the regulation of SG. Our initial experiment showed that Hsf1 knockdown via shRNA blocked SG formation, apparently supporting the regulation of SG by Hsf1. However, this idea was invalidated by the subsequent observation that SG formation persisted in Hsf1 knockout cells. According to this result, we proposed a new hypothesis that the process of shRNA-induced RNAi per se, rather than Hsf1 deficiency, might be the key to defective SG formation in Hsf1-shRNA transfected cells. To test this possibility, we examined shRNA transfection in three cell types (RPTC, HEK, BUMPT). We also tested four specific gene (Hsf1, Hif1α, autophagy protein 5, Beclin1) shRNAs. In addition, we tested both stable and transient transfection. Consistently, transfection of gene-specific shRNAs into cells suppressed SG formation. Thus the effect of shRNA-mediated RNAi on SG formation is not dependent on certain cell types or a specific target gene. Together, these results suggest that the process of RNAi is inhibitory of SG formation during cellular stress.

Arsenite is the classic and most commonly used inducer of SG. We tested the effect of rapamycin, the pharmacological inhibitor of mTOR. As shown in Supplemental Fig. S5, rapamycin did not affect SG formation during arsenite treatment. In addition, rapamycin alone did not induce obvious SG formation or influence the phosphorylation of eIF2α.

In 2011, Detzer and colleagues ( 6 ) reported that different types of cellular stress led to Ago2 accumulation in SG, which remarkably was accompanied by a decreased RNAi efficacy of siRNAs in these cells. The results of our present study further demonstrate that the process of RNAi is inhibitory of SG formation during cellular stress. These studies collectively support the existence of an antagonizing relationship between SG formation and RNAi. What mechanism is responsible for this antagonizing relationship? In this aspect, both studies suggest the involvement of shared components of SG and RNAi-induced RISC. It is known that both SG and RISC are protein/RNA complexes, and, interestingly, they share some common components. For example, Ago2 is well recognized as a core protein of RISC, but it has also been localized in SG ( 12 , 18 ). Detzer and colleagues ( 6 ) showed that the accumulation of Ago2 in SG during cellular stress resulted in lower RNAi efficacy. We have now provided the evidence that RNAi-induced sequestration of Ago2 in RISC suppresses SG formation during cellular stress. Thus both studies have pinpointed Ago2 as one of the key molecules shared by SGs and RISC that may underlie the antagonizing relationship between SG formation and RNAi.

Ago2 has also been localized in SG ( 12 , 18 ). However, the role of Ago2 in SG formation remains unclear. In the present study, we tested the effect of ATA, a pharmacological inhibitor of siRNA loading to Ago2 ( 21 ). The rationale was that inhibition of Ago2 loading would prevent its accumulation to RISC and become available for SG formation. Indeed, ATA restored SG formation in both cell lines stably transfected with Hsf1 and Hif1α shRNAs ( Fig. 4 ). Our co-IP assay further showed that Ago2 increased its interaction with SG proteins like PKCα during arsenite treatment, while decreasing its interaction with RISC proteins such as TRBP2 ( Figs. 6 and 7 ). Finally, Ago2 knockout HEK cells constructed using CRISPR-Cas9 system exhibited significantly decreased SG-forming ability. Altogether, these data indicate that Ago2, not only accumulates to SG during cellular stress, but also plays an important role in SG formation, suggesting a competitive relationship between RISC and SG for key protein components ( Fig. 9 ).

SGs are the sites of untranslated mRNA storage, reinitiation, or degradation ( 1 ) although it remains uncertain whether SGs are important for mRNA stabilization during cellular stress ( 2 ). It is possible that, after cellular stress, some of the mRNAs are exported from SG to sites of decay or degradation. The observation of the “antagonizing” interaction between SGs and RISC is consistent with the possibility of mRNA moving from SG to RISC. In our study, shRNA-mediated RNAi reduced SG formation during arsenite stress, which was restored by ATA, an inhibitor of RNA loading to RISC. Moreover, Ago2 may move from SG to RISC when it is needed to degrade target RNA. At the moment, we do not know how mRNAs are transferred from SG to RISC. However, as Ago2 and HuR are components of both SGs and RISC, these RNA-binding proteins may bind to mRNAs to facilitate their move from SG to RISC for degradation.

RNAi may be triggered by siRNAs as well as miRNAs. To determine whether miRNAs may prevent SG formation as siRNAs, we examined arsenite-induced SG formation in miRNA-overexpressing cells. We tested two stable cell lines that were generated in our recent work to overexpress miRNA-219 and miRNA-668, respectively. We also tested the effect of transient transfection of these miRNAs. Somewhat to our surprise, expression of these miRNAs did not affect arsenite-induced SG formation (Supplemental Fig. S6). The differential effects of siRNAs and miRNAs on SG formation may be explained by the difference in their requirement of Ago2. SiRNAs bind to its target with complete complementarity, leading to mRNA degradation, whereas miRNAs bind to their targets with partial complementarity for translation repression. Ago2 is the only protein with endonuclease activity in the mammalian Ago family ( 27 ), and it is a core component of the RISC complex in siRNA-induced RNAi. However, miRNAs may not require Ago2 for their RNAi function. In Ago2-deficient cells, miRNA-mediated translational silencing remains unaffected ( 13 ), suggesting that other nonnucleolytic Ago proteins may predominate in miRNA silencing. Recently, other Ago family members, such as Ago1, were identified to be the main effector during miRNA-mediated translation repression. In addition, Ago3 appeared to be the most potent translational repressor when tethered to a reporter mRNA ( 25 ). On the basis of these considerations, we speculate that siRNA-induced RISC competes with SG for Ago2, resulting in an antagonizing relationship in which miRNAs induce Ago2-independent RISC and therefore do not affect SG formation during cellular stress.
