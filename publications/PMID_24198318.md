## Full Text

An F-box protein regulates drought and osmotic stress responses in a strigolactone-independent manner in Arabidopsis .

Abstract

MAX2 (for MORE AXILLARY GROWTH2) has been shown to regulate diverse biological processes, including plant architecture, photomorphogenesis, senescence, and karrikin signaling. Although karrikin is a smoke-derived abiotic signal, a role for MAX2 in abiotic stress response pathways is least investigated. Here, we show that the max2 mutant is strongly hypersensitive to drought stress compared with wild-type Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ). Stomatal closure of max2 was less sensitive to abscisic acid ( ABA ) than that of the wild type. Cuticle thickness of max2 was significantly thinner than that of the wild type. Both of these phenotypes of max2 mutant plants correlate with the increased water loss and drought-sensitive phenotype. Quantitative real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction analyses showed that the expression of stress-responsive genes and ABA biosynthesis, catabolism, transport, and signaling genes was impaired in max2 compared with wild-type seedlings in response to drought stress. Double mutant analysis of max2 with the ABA -insensitive mutants abi3 and abi5 indicated that MAX2 may function upstream of these genes. The expression of ABA -regulated genes was enhanced in imbibed max2 seeds. In addition, max2 mutant seedlings were hypersensitive to ABA and osmotic stress, including NaCl, mannitol, and glucose. Interestingly, ABA , osmotic stress, and drought-sensitive phenotypes were restricted to max2 , and the strigolactone biosynthetic pathway mutants max1 , max3 , and max4 did not display any defects in these responses. Taken together, these results uncover an important role for MAX2 in plant responses to abiotic stress conditions.

Environmental stresses greatly affect plant growth and development and harm crop productivity heavily. Among these, drought and salinity are the most common forms of environmental stresses. The plant hormone abscisic acid ( ABA ) plays central roles in plant drought stress responses ( Finkelstein et al., 2002 ; Cutler et al., 2010 ). Upon drought treatment, ABA content quickly elevates, which promotes the formation of ternary complexes of ABA , PYRABACTIN RESISTANCE1-LIKE/REGULATORY COMPONENTS OF ABA RECEPTORS ABA receptors of the START protein family, and type 2C protein phosphatase (PP2C) proteins ( Fujii et al., 2009 ; Ma et al., 2009 ; Nishimura et al., 2009 , 2010 ; Park et al., 2009 ; Santiago et al., 2009 ; Brandt et al., 2012 ). These could release Snf1-Related Protein Kinase1 from the inhibition of PP2C protein ( Ma et al., 2009 ; Melcher et al., 2009 ). The activated Snf1-Related Protein Kinase1 can phosphorylate downstream ion channels and transcription factors that can bind the ABA response element and regulate the expression of ABA -responsive genes ( Lopez-Molina et al., 2001 ; Brandt et al., 2012 ; Fujita et al., 2013 ). ABA -induced stomatal closure is promoted for maintaining water status in plant cells under water-deficit conditions. In addition, a number of dehydration proteins accumulate to protect plants from the damage of stress conditions ( Finkelstein et al., 2002 ; Mustilli et al., 2002 ; Raghavendra et al., 2010 ; Umezawa et al., 2010 ; Hauser et al., 2011 ).

F-box proteins, which can form SCF (for S-Phase Kinase-Associated Protein1/Cullin or Cell Division Cycle53/F-box protein) E3 ubiquitin ligases ( Deshaies, 1999 ), have been shown to play essential roles in plant growth and development, including multiple phytohormone-signaling pathways, such as auxin, gibberellin, ethylene, jasmonic acid, and strigolactone ( Ruegger et al., 1998 ; Xie et al., 1998 ; Dill et al., 2004 ; Gagne et al., 2004 ; Gomez-Roldan et al., 2008 ; Umehara et al., 2008 ) The Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ) genome encodes more than 700 putative F-box proteins ( Gagne et al., 2002 ). However, only a few F-box proteins are reported to be involved in plant response to abiotic stress, such as ABA and drought response pathways. DROUGHT TOLERANCE REPRESSOR1 (DOR1) is an F-box protein that is expressed specifically in guard cells ( Zhang et al., 2008 ). Consistent with the drought-tolerant phenotype, the dor1 mutant is hypersensitive to ABA -induced stomatal closure and has higher ABA content. But dor1 did not show any ABA -related phenotypes during seed germination and the early seedling development stage ( Zhang et al., 2008 ). The Empfindlicher im Dunkelroten Licht1-Like Protein3 is another ABA -induced F-box protein that can interact with multiple Arabidopsis S-Phase Kinase-Associated Protein1-like proteins with differential strength. Biological assays showed that Empfindlicher im Dunkelroten Licht1-Like Protein3 positively regulates ABA inhibition of seed germination, early seedling development, and root growth ( Koops et al., 2011 ).

MAX2 (for MORE AXILLARY GROWTH2), an F-box protein, has been previously shown to function in strigolactone-mediated regulation of branching, karrikin signaling, temperature signaling, and senescence pathways ( Woo et al., 2001 ; Stirnberg et al., 2002 ; Shen et al., 2007 ; Gomez-Roldan et al., 2008 ; Umehara et al., 2008 ; Nelson et al., 2011 ; Toh et al., 2012 ; Brewer et al., 2013 ). It also positively regulates photomorphogenesis under all three (blue, red, and far-red) light conditions ( Stirnberg et al., 2002 ; Shen et al., 2007 ; Nelson et al., 2011 ). Although all the max mutants ( max1 , max2 , max3 , and max4 ) share an increased shoot-branching phenotype ( Stirnberg et al., 2002 ; Sorefan et al., 2003 ; Booker et al., 2004 , 2005 ), only MAX2 but not the other MAX proteins (MAX1, MAX3, and MAX4) involved in strigolactone biosynthesis can affect plant photomorphogenesis ( Stirnberg et al., 2002 ; Shen et al., 2007 , 2012 ; Nelson et al., 2011 ). In addition, only max2 and not the other max mutants displayed defects in karrikin signaling ( Nelson et al., 2011 ), implying that MAX2 has diverse roles in other pathways not regulated by strigolactone. Recently, Arabidopsis D14 and its paralog KAI2 (for KARRIKIN INSENSITIVE2), which belongs to the α/β-hydrolase family of proteins, have been shown to display shared functions with MAX2 ( Waters et al., 2012 ). These analyses showed that D14 predominantly regulates branching, while KAI2 regulates karrikin signaling and possibly other pathways. Exogenous application of nonphysiological levels of the strigolactone analog (GR24) promoted photomorphogenesis, possibly by inducing nuclear exclusion of Constitutive Photomorphogenic1 and thereby stabilizing Long Hypocotyl5, a key transcription factor that promotes photomorphogenesis ( Tsuchiya et al., 2010 ). However, a recent report also showed that KAI2- and MAX2-mediated responses to karrikins and strigolactones are largely independent of Long Hypocotyl5 ( Waters and Smith, 2013 ). In addition, GR24 promoted interactions between PhMAX2 and petunia ( Petunia hybrida ) Decreased Apical Dominance2, a homolog of Arabidopsis and rice ( Oryza sativa ) D14, in a yeast two-hybrid system ( Hamiaux et al., 2012 ). These data suggest that MAX2 might interact with multiple proteins, including α/β-hydrolase family members, to regulate diverse biological processes.

In an effort to understand MAX2’s role in photomorphogenesis, we have shown that multiple hormones, including ABA , are defective in the max2 mutant compared with wild-type seeds and seedlings ( Shen et al., 2012 ). In this study, we show that MAX2 is also playing a key role in plant responses to abiotic stress. At the seedling and adult stages, three alleles of max2 displayed strong hypersensitivity to drought stress. All max2 alleles also showed hypersensitive phenotypes to ABA and osmotic stress during seed germination and seedling growth. Moreover, we show that the ABA and drought response phenotypes were specific to max2 , as the strigolactone biosynthetic mutants ( max1 , max3 , and max4 ) were not defective in these responses.

RESULTS

max2 Is Hypersensitive to Drought Stress Compared with the Wild Type

In a recent report, we showed that the max2 mutant was hypersensitive to ABA during seed germination ( Shen et al., 2012 ). In this study, we examined vegetative drought response phenotypes of the max2 mutants and wild-type plants at the adult stage. To investigate the drought response of max2 plants, we grew wild-type and max2 plants for 10 d on soil and then withdrawn water for 7 d. After inducing drought conditions, we rewatered the plants and monitored their growth for 2 d. Strikingly, max2 plants showed very high sensitivity to drought conditions compared with wild-type plants ( Fig. 1A ). Almost 100% of max2 seedlings did not recover, while 100% of the wild-type plants recovered from drought conditions after rewatering ( Fig. 1A ). These data suggest that max2 is highly sensitive to drought stress compared with wild-type plants.

Figure 1.

The Rate of Water Evaporation Is Higher in max2 Compared with the Wild Type

To investigate the cause of the hypersensitivity of max2 mutants under drought conditions, we compared the rate of water evaporation between max2 and wild-type plants. The results show that the rate of water evaporation from the excised leaves of all three max2 alleles is much faster than that of wild-type plants ( Fig. 1B ). max2 leaves lost turgor within 2 h, while the wild-type leaves were still soft and turgid even after 3 h of excision from the plants. These data suggest that the increased water evaporation might contribute to the higher drought sensitivity of the max2 plants.

max2 Is Hyposensitive to ABA -Induced Stomatal Closure Compared with Wild-Type Plants

Plants lose water mainly through stomata. Because max2 loses water faster than the wild type ( Fig. 1B ), we examined the stomatal size and density in the max2 mutant compared with the wild type. The results showed that there was no significant difference in the average stomatal apertures and densities between the wild type and the max2 mutant ( Fig. 1, C and D ; Supplemental Fig. S1A ). These data suggest that the observed hypersensitivity to drought conditions for max2 is not due to a difference in stomatal architecture in max2 .

ABA , the stress hormone, plays a prominent role in inducing stomatal closure to prevent water loss and thereby contributes to tolerance to drought conditions ( Hauser et al., 2011 ). Because water evaporation is higher in max2 plants compared with wild-type plants, we investigated whether max2 is defective in stomatal closure in response to ABA . The epidermal peels of max2-2 , pleiotropic photosignaling ( pps ), and the wild type were incubated in a buffer solution under white light exposure to open the stomata. Then, the peels were treated with different concentrations of ABA for 2 h. The ratio of stomatal width to length indicates the degree of stomatal closure, since the size of individual stomata varies in Arabidopsis ( Siegel et al., 2009 ). max2-2 , pps , and the wild type showed comparable ratios of width to length of fully opened stomata without and/or at low concentrations of ABA ( Fig. 1C ). However, in the presence of high concentrations of ABA , the ratio of width to length of pps and max2-2 was significantly larger than that of the wild type ( Fig. 1C ). These data suggest that the increased rate of water evaporation from the max2 plants ( Fig. 1, A and B ) correlates with the decreased sensitivity to ABA in the induction of stomatal closure compared with wild-type plants.

The above data showing stomatal response to ABA were from excised epidermal peels pretreated with white light. To obtain direct evidence of whether stomata from max2 plants exposed to drought stress are more open than those of wild-type plants, we also measured the ratios of width to length of stomata from detached leaves of plants that were exposed to drought stress to mimic the natural drought condition. The width-to-length ratios of max2-1 stomata were significantly larger than those of the wild type during an actual drought stress over time ( Fig. 1D ). These data provide a strong correlation between the increased water evaporation from max2 plants and the more open stomata of max2 plants under drought stress.

The Cuticle Membrane Thickness of max2 Is Thinner Than That of Wild-Type Plants

The striking drought-sensitive phenotypes of the max2 mutant prompted us to explore additional mechanisms by which MAX2 might be involved in the drought response. It has been reported that membrane cuticle thickness is associated with drought response ( Aharoni et al., 2004 ; Islam et al., 2009 ). Transmission electron microscopy ( TEM ) analyses were performed to observe and measure the cuticle thickness of max2 and wild-type plants. These analyses showed that the cuticle layer of max2-1 was significantly thinner than that of the wild type in both leaves and stems ( Fig. 2, A and B ). These data suggest that MAX2 is involved in regulating cuticle thickness.

Figure 2.

To test whether the thinner cuticle layer of the max2 mutant is related to membrane permeability, a chlorophyll-leaching assay was conducted. The data show that the rate of chlorophyll leaching is much faster for max2 compared with the wild type ( Fig. 2C ). However, the total chlorophyll content in the wild type and max2 is comparable ( Supplemental Fig. S1B ). Taken together, these data suggest that the reduced cuticle thickness of max2 could contribute to the drought-sensitive phenotype of the max2 mutant.

Stress-Inducible Gene Expression Is Impaired in the max2 Mutant

Because max2 plants showed drought-sensitive phenotypes at the adult vegetative stage, we examined whether the expression of drought-responsive genes is altered in max2 compared with wild-type plants at the adult stage. To verify this possibility, we selected several stress-inducible genes and compared their expression between max2 and the wild type in response to dehydration treatment. Quantitative real-time reverse transcription ( qRT )-PCR assays indicated that dehydration treatment led to an increased expression of all the ABA -inducible marker genes, including Responsive to ABA , Responsive to Dehydration29A , Responsive to Dehydration29B , Kinase1 , and Cold-Regulated Protein47 , ( Kurkela and Borg-Franck, 1992 ; Lång and Palva, 1992 ; Abe et al., 2003 ). The expression levels of all these genes were reduced in max2 compared with wild-type plants during both 1 and 3 h after dehydration treatment ( Fig. 3 ). These data suggest that the expression of stress-inducible marker genes is impaired in the max2 mutant, which might contribute, at least partly, to the drought-sensitive phenotypes of max2 .

Figure 3.

The Expression of ABA Biosynthetic, Catabolic, Transport, and Signaling Genes Is Impaired in max2

Because max2 showed a dramatic drought-sensitive phenotype, we examined the expression levels of genes involved in the ABA biosynthesis, catabolism, transport, and signaling pathways, including Nine Cis-Epoxycarotenoid Dioxygenase3 , Cytochrome P450 707A3 , ABC Transporter G Family Protein22 , ABI1 , ABA Insensitive1 ( ABI1 ), and Hypersensitive to ABA1 ( Leung et al., 1994 , 1998 ; Meyer et al., 1994 ; Leube et al., 1998 ; Rodriguez et al., 1998 ; Iuchi et al., 2001 ; Saez et al., 2004 ; Umezawa et al., 2006 ; Kuromori et al., 2011 ). qRT -PCR showed that the expression of all the above genes was induced significantly by drought treatment ( Fig. 4 ). However, the drought-induced expression levels of these genes in the max2 mutant were much lower than in the wild type. These data suggest that max2 is hyposensitive to the drought-induced expression of ABA pathway genes compared with the wild type.

Figure 4.

Since the expression of ABA biosynthetic, catabolic, transport, and signaling pathway genes is impaired in the max2 mutant in response to drought stress, we measured ABA content in the wild type and max2 during the drought treatment. The results show that the ABA content is dramatically induced under drought stress in both the wild type and max2 . However, the amount of ABA is similar between the wild type and max2 after drought treatment ( Supplemental Fig. S1C ), suggesting that the increased drought sensitivity of the max2 mutant is not due to a difference in the ABA contents of wild-type and max2 plants. Alternatively, the induction of ABA biosynthesis, catabolic, and transport genes could contribute to the transient accumulation of ABA in a specific tissue, which was not detected by our experimental conditions.

Overexpression of MAX2 Did Not Show ABA - and Drought-Related Phenotypes

To examine whether MAX2 overexpression results in a drought-tolerance phenotype, we selected two transgenic lines in which MAX2 is overexpressed for phenotypic analysis ( Shen et al., 2007 ). Water loss and drought treatment assays showed similar phenotypes for the MAX2 overexpression and wild-type plants ( Supplemental Fig. S2 ; data not shown). In addition, no difference in ABA -regulated seed germination or early seedling greening was observed for these overexpression lines compared with the wild type (data not shown). These results suggest that MAX2 may not be a limiting factor under these conditions for abiotic stress responses. In addition, MAX2 might require other proteins for its function in ABA -regulated phenotypes.

max2 Is Hypersensitive to ABA during Seed Germination and Early Seedling Development

In a recent report ( Shen et al., 2012 ), we showed that the max2 mutant was hypersensitive to ABA during seed germination. To understand the roles of max2 in ABA signaling more fully, we further investigated the function of MAX2 in the ABA -mediated inhibition of early seedling development. The cotyledon-greening phenotype was used as an indicator of early seedling development. The wild type and three max2 mutant alleles ( max2-1 , max2-2 , and pps ) were sown on ABA -free and ABA -containing medium. In the absence of ABA , max2-1 , max2-2 , pps , and the wild type showed similar cotyledon-greening kinetics ( Supplemental Fig. S3A ; data not shown). When sown on medium containing 0.5 µ m ABA , the rate of cotyledon greening of max2 seedlings was significantly lower than that of wild-type seedlings ( Fig. 5A ). In an ABA dose-response assay, seeds were sown on medium containing different concentrations of ABA and the cotyledon-greening rate was scored at 3 d after germination. As shown in Figure 5B , max2 mutants showed lower cotyledon greening than the wild type in the presence of different concentrations of ABA . Because max2 displayed reduced seed germination in response to ABA ( Shen et al., 2012 ), the above hypersensitive phenotype for cotyledon greening might be due to a residual effect of delayed seed germination. To examine this possibility, we transferred germinated seeds from a Murashige and Skoog ( MS ) plate without ABA to MS plates containing increasing concentrations of ABA and scored the cotyledon-greening phenotype over time. Interestingly, the rates of cotyledon greening of the wild type and max2-1 were similar at low concentrations of ABA (0.3–0.5 µ m ; Supplemental Fig. S3, B–E ). However, at higher concentrations of ABA (0.8–1.0 µ m ), the max2-1 mutant was still hypersensitive to ABA for cotyledon greening compared with wild-type seedlings ( Supplemental Fig. S3, B and C ). These results demonstrated that max2 mutant plants are more sensitive to ABA than wild-type plants, suggesting that MAX2 acts as a negative regulator of ABA signaling during early seedling establishment.

Figure 5.

To investigate whether MAX2 was involved in ABA -mediated inhibition of seedling root growth, wild-type and max2 plants were grown on MS medium. When the primary root was about 1 cm long, the similar-sized plants were transferred to MS medium containing different concentrations of ABA , and then the root length was measured after 7 d of growth. Consistent with the ABA hypersensitivity for cotyledon greening, the root growth of max2-1 was more inhibited by ABA than that of the wild type ( Fig. 5, C and D ). Taken together, these and our previous data suggest that the max2 mutant is hypersensitive to ABA during seed germination and early seedling growth.

Genetic Analysis of max2 with Other ABA -Responsive Mutants

ABI3 and ABI5 are two key transcription factors that specifically control the seed germination and early seedling development processes in response to ABA ( Lopez-Molina et al., 2001 , 2002 ). To further investigate the function of MAX2 in the ABA inhibition of the early seedling stage, we crossed max2-1 with the ABA -insensitive mutants abi3-8 and abi5-7 and obtained the double mutants max2-1/abi3-8 and max2-1/abi5-7 . When grown under continuous white light, double mutants displayed longer hypocotyls at the young seedling stage and multiple branches at the adult stage compared with the wild type and abi3-8 and abi5-7 single mutants. These phenotypes are similar to the max2 single mutant under these conditions ( Fig. 6A ; data not shown). In response to ABA treatment, the germinative cotyledon-greening rate of the double mutants max2-1/abi3-8 and max2-1/abi5-7 was similar to that of the corresponding abi3 and abi5 single mutants ( Fig. 6, B and C ). These results suggest that MAX2 might act upstream of ABI3 and ABI5 in the ABA -responsive pathway.

Figure 6.

ABA -Regulated Genes Display Enhanced Expression in max2 during Seed Germination

Since max2 showed hypersensitivity to ABA during seed germination and early seedling development ( Shen et al., 2012 ), we investigated whether the ABA -regulated gene expression was affected in the max2 mutant at this stage. To this end, we examined the expression of two ABA -regulated transcription factors, ABI3 and ABI5 , and their downstream target gene Arabidopsis Thaliana Late Embryogenesis Abundant1 ( AtEM1 ), which have been shown to play key roles in the ABA inhibition of seed germination and early seedling development ( Lopez-Molina et al., 2001 , 2002 ; Carles et al., 2002 ). The results show that the expression of all three genes was highest in the dry seed and then decreased during seed imbibition ( Fig. 7 ). ABA treatment stimulated the expression of AtEM1 especially after 48 h. In the dry seed, the expression of these ABA -regulated genes was comparable between the wild type and the max2 mutant, but during seed imbibition with and without ABA treatment for 24 h and 48 h, ABI3 , ABI5 , and AtEM1 displayed enhanced expression in max2 compared with the wild type ( Fig. 7 ). The enhanced expression of these genes in max2 might contribute to the ABA -hypersensitive phenotype of max2 during seed germination and early seedling development.

Figure 7.

The Expression of MAX2 Is Decreased during Seed Imbibition and by ABA Treatment at the Seedling Stage

Because MAX2 is involved in ABA signaling pathways, we examined whether ABA can affect the expression of MAX2 using qRT -PCR assays. MAX2 transcript was abundant in dry seeds and significantly reduced when seeds were imbibed for 24 and 48 h ( Fig. 8A ). The expression pattern of MAX2 in the dry seed and imbibition stages was similar to that of AtEM1 , a gene highly expressed in seeds ( Carles et al., 2002 ; Bu et al., 2009 ). ABA treatment during seed imbibition largely did not affect the expression of MAX2 , in contrast to that of AtEM1 , which was induced by ABA ( Fig. 8A ). During the seedling stage, the expression of MAX2 was down-regulated by ABA treatment ( Fig. 8B ). Moreover, we also examined the expression of MAX2 in dry seeds of different abi mutant backgrounds. Figure 8C shows that the expression of MAX2 was reduced in abi3 and abi5 mutants compared with that in the wild type, but the deficiency of ABI4 did not affect MAX2 expression. These data suggest that ABA regulates the expression of MAX2 at both seed and seedling stages, and this regulation may contribute to MAX2’s role in ABA signaling.

Figure 8.

max2 Is Hypersensitive to Osmotic Stress during Seed Germination

Salt and other osmotic stresses inhibit seed germination and early seedling development in both ABA -dependent and ABA -independent manners ( Zhu, 2002 ). Since MAX2 is involved in ABA signaling during seed germination, we examined whether MAX2 affects the plant responses to salt stress. Seeds of max2 mutant alleles and the wild type were sown on medium containing different concentrations of NaCl, and germinative cotyledon greening was scored. In medium supplemented with 100 to 200 m m NaCl, the percentages of max2 seedlings with green cotyledons were significantly lower than those of wild-type seedlings ( Fig. 9, A and B ). To distinguish whether MAX2 is involved in salt-specific or general osmotic responses, we compared the responses of different genotypes to the osmotic reagent mannitol and Glc. The percentage of max2 seedlings with green cotyledons was lower than that of the wild type at various days of growth and in the presence of different concentrations of mannitol ( Fig. 9, C and D ) and Glc ( Fig. 9, E and F ). To verify whether the lower cotyledon greening in response to osmotic stress is due to delayed seed germination, as observed previously ( Shen et al., 2012 ), we transferred the germinated seeds from an MS plate to an osmotic reagent-containing plate and scored the cotyledon-greening rate. The results show that max2 is still hypertensive to osmotic stress for cotyledon greening at the seedling stage ( Supplemental Fig. S3, F–H ). These data suggest that MAX2 negatively regulates osmotic stress responses during both seed germination and seedling development.

Figure 9.

Strigolactone Is Not Involved in the ABA and Drought Response Pathways

Strigolactones are terpenoid-derived new plant hormones. MAX2 is an F-box protein necessary for strigolactone signaling ( Stirnberg et al., 2002 ). Three other genes in the MAX pathway, MAX1 , MAX3 , and MAX4 , encode strigolactone biosynthetic enzymes in Arabidopsis ( Sorefan et al., 2003 ; Booker et al., 2004 , 2005 ). To examine whether strigolactone is involved in ABA and drought response pathways, we investigated the early seedling development of max1 , max3 , and max4 mutants using max2 and the wild type as controls. Interestingly, only max2 displayed hypersensitive phenotypes to ABA ( Fig. 10, A–C ) and osmotic stress ( Fig. 10, A and D–G ) in seed germination, while max1 , max3 , and max4 showed similar responses to wild-type plants. In addition, we also examined the drought response and detached leaf water loss of max1 , max3 , and max4 mutants compared with the wild type. As shown above, the max2 mutant showed a dramatic drought-sensitive phenotype and rapid water loss compared with the wild type. However, max1 , max3 , and max4 did not display any phenotypic difference compared with the wild type under these conditions ( Fig. 11 ). Taken together, these data suggest that ABA - and drought-hypersensitive phenotypes are restricted to the max2 mutant, and the strigolactone biosynthetic pathway controlled by MAX1, MAX3, and MAX4 may not directly affect the ABA and drought response pathways.

DISCUSSION

Previously, MAX2 has been shown to regulate diverse biological processes, including plant architecture, senescence, karrikin signaling, temperature responses, and photomorphogenesis ( Woo et al., 2001 ; Stirnberg et al., 2002 ; Shen et al., 2007 ; Gomez-Roldan et al., 2008 ; Umehara et al., 2008 ; Nelson et al., 2011 ; Toh et al., 2012 ; Waters et al., 2012 ; Brewer et al., 2013 ). The data presented here show a novel function of MAX2 in ABA and drought responses, expanding its roles in the regulation of plant growth and development as well as in responding to abiotic stress conditions.

The physiological, genetic, and molecular data presented here provide evidence that MAX2 is involved in ABA and drought responses. First, max2 mutants were drought sensitive and lost water faster than the wild type at the adult stage ( Fig. 1, A and B ). Second, max2 mutants were less sensitive to ABA -induced stomatal closure and displayed larger stomatal aperture compared with the wild type under drought conditions ( Fig. 1, C and D ). Third, max2 mutants were impaired in stress-inducible gene expression ( Figs. 3 and 4 ). Fourth, max2 mutants showed reduced cuticle thickness resulting in increased membrane permeability ( Fig. 2 ). Fifth, max2 mutants displayed hypersensitivity to ABA , NaCl, mannitol, and Glc at the seed germination and early seedling stages ( Figs. 5 and 9 ). These data strongly suggest that max2 is involved in regulating abiotic stress responses at both seedling and adult stages.

The plant drought response is a complex process and regulated by multiple molecular and cellular pathways. In Arabidopsis, many positive or negative regulators have been identified and characterized as key components in ABA or drought signaling ( Shinozaki and Yamaguchi-Shinozaki, 2007 ; Hauser et al., 2011 ). ABA sensitivity in early seedling stages and drought sensitivity at the adult vegetative stage do not always correlate. Some genes only affect the ABA -mediated inhibition of seed germination and early seedling development processes but not the ABA -induced closure of stomata and stress-induced gene expression at the adult stage. For example, RING-H2 FingerA2a and Supersensitive to ABA and Drought1 are hypersensitive to ABA at the early seedling stage ( Xiong et al., 2001 ; Bu et al., 2009 ). However, the ABA hypersensitivity at the early stage was not accompanied by drought tolerance at the adult stage. In contrast, another F-box protein mutant, dor1 , did not show an ABA -related phenotype during seed germination and the early seedling development stage but displayed hypersensitivity to ABA -induced stomatal closure and drought-resistant phenotypes ( Zhang et al., 2008 ). Moreover, RING-H2 FingerA2b and ABA-Overly Sensitive1 displayed ABA hypersensitivity in the early seedling stage as well as the adult stage ( Chen et al., 2006 ; Li et al., 2011 ). Phenotypically, MAX2 appears to behave similarly to ABA-Overly Sensitive3, a WRKY family transcription factor, which plays a negative role at the seed and seedling stages but a positive role at the adult stage ( Ren et al., 2010 ).

MAX2’s role in ABA signaling appears to be developmentally regulated. It plays a negative role in ABA - and osmotic stress-induced inhibition of seed germination and early seedling growth ( Figs. 5 and 9 ; Shen et al., 2012 ) but plays a positive role in ABA -mediated stomatal closure and the expression of stress-inducible genes ( Figs. 1 , 3 , and 4 ). MAX2 expression is induced by ABI3 and ABI5 at the seed stage, while ABA slightly down-regulates MAX2 expression at the adult stage ( Fig. 8 ). Previously, it was shown that multiple hormone pathways are defective in max2 during seed germination and seedling development. It is possible that the negative role at the seed and seedling stages is due to defects in multiple hormones ( Nelson et al., 2011 ; Shen et al., 2012 ).

As addressed previously in relation to light and karrikin signaling pathways ( Nelson et al., 2011 ; Shen et al., 2012 ), one important question is whether strigolactone is involved in ABA signaling and drought responses. The results show that the ABA - and drought-sensitive phenotypes were specific to max2 and that the strigolactone biosynthetic mutants ( max1 , max3 , and max4 ) did not display any defects in ABA , osmotic stress, and drought responses ( Figs. 10 and 11 ). Because strigolactone biosynthetic mutants (e.g. max3 and max4 ) still have detectable amounts of strigolactone ( Umehara et al., 2008 ), these data suggest that ABA sensitivity and drought responses might be extremely sensitive to strigolactone levels. Alternatively, strigolactone may not be necessary for these responses, but only MAX2 regulates these responses in a strigolactone-independent manner, as observed previously for light and karrikin signaling ( Nelson et al., 2011 ; Shen et al., 2012 ).

In summary, we show novel functions of MAX2 in regulating ABA signaling, osmotic stress, and a very strong drought sensitivity. MAX2 also regulates membrane cuticle thickness in addition to its established roles in the regulation of shoot branching, senescence, karrikin signaling, temperature responses, and photomorphogenesis ( Supplemental Fig. S4 ; Woo et al., 2001 ; Stirnberg et al., 2002 ; Shen et al., 2007 ; Gomez-Roldan et al., 2008 ; Umehara et al., 2008 ; Nelson et al., 2011 ; Toh et al., 2012 ; Waters et al., 2012 ; Brewer et al., 2013 ). How MAX2 regulates these diverse responses at different developmental stages is still unknown. However, recent data shed light on how MAX2’s specificity might be achieved. AtD14 and its homolog KAI2, the Arabidopsis ortholog of rice DWARF14, have been shown to specifically regulate strigolactone and karrikin signaling, respectively ( Waters et al., 2012 ). Both of these pathways are regulated by MAX2 ( Waters et al., 2012 ). Furthermore, GR24 promotes interactions between PhMAX2 and petunia Decreased Apical Dominance2, a homolog of Arabidopsis and rice D14, in a yeast two-hybrid system ( Hamiaux et al., 2012 ). These data suggest that MAX2 might interact with diverse proteins at different developmental stages in a stimulus-specific manner to regulate these pathways. Identification and characterization of MAX2 target proteins will help in understanding the mechanisms by which MAX2 regulates these diverse processes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant Growth Conditions

Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Columbia [ Col-0 ]) was used as the wild type in this study. All the mutants ( max2-1 , max2-2 , pps , max1 , max3 , max4 , abi3-8 , abi4-1 , and abi5-7 ) used in this study are in the Col-0 background ( Finkelstein et al., 1998 ; Nambara et al., 2002 ; Stirnberg et al., 2002 ; Sorefan et al., 2003 ; Booker et al., 2004 , 2005 ; Tamura et al., 2006 ; Shen et al., 2007 ). Plant growth conditions were as described ( Shen et al., 2012 ). Briefly, plants of each genotype were sown and grown under identical conditions. Seeds were surface sterilized and plated on MS medium. After 3 d of stratification at 4°C, the plates were transferred to a growth chamber for 10 d, then the seedling were transplanted on soil and grown in a growth room at constant temperature and light. Seeds were harvested, dried for 1 month in Drierite (W.A. Hammond Drierite Co. Ltd.), and then stored at room temperature. ABA in the form of (±)-cis,trans- ABA (Sigma A1049] was used in this article.

Seed Germination, Cotyledon Greening, and Root Growth Assays

Plants of different genotypes were grown under the same conditions, and seeds were collected at the same time. For each comparison, seeds were planted on the same plate containing MS medium (one-half-strength MS salts, 1% Suc, and 0.8% agar) without or with different concentrations of osmotic stresses as indicated. Plates were chilled at 4°C in the dark for 3 d for stratification and moved to 22°C with a 16-h-light/8-h-dark cycle. Germinative greening rate, which indicated the percentage of total seeds that germinated and produced green cotyledons, was scored at the indicated times. This experiment was repeated independently three times.

For the root growth assay, seeds was germinated and grown on MS medium. When the root length was about 1 cm long, seedlings of the wild type and max2-1 were transferred to MS medium containing different concentrations of ABA . After 7 d of growth, photographs were taken and the root length was measured using the publicly available ImageJ software. At least 20 seedlings were measured for each genotype.

Drought Tolerance Assays, Water Loss Measurements, and Stomatal Aperture Bioassays

For drought stress, plants of various genotypes were grown in Metro-Mix 200 soil under 24 h of light at 24°C ± 0.5°C. These plants were watered twice per week for 10 d. To induce drought conditions, water was withheld for the days indicated in the figures. These plants were then rewatered, and their growth was monitored after 2 d from rewatering. These experiments were repeated at least three times.

For the water loss assays, rosette leaves of mutant and wild-type plants (about 0.5 g of leaves from the same stage of different plants) growing in the growth room for 3 weeks were detached and weighed immediately on a piece of weighing paper. The leaves were then placed on a laboratory bench and weighed at designated times. The loss of fresh weight was calculated on the basis of the initial weight of the detached leaves, and the percentage of water loss was calculated. The experiment was repeated at least three times.

Stomatal closure assays were conducted as described ( Kwak et al., 2002 ). Briefly, rosette leaf peels were floated in solutions containing 10 m m KCl, 7.5 m m iminodiacetic acid, and 10 m m MES-Tris, pH 6.15, and exposed to light (150 µmol m −2 s −l ) for 2 h. Subsequently, the indicated concentrations of ABA were added to the solution for stomatal closing response analyses. After ABA treatment for 2 h, stomatal apertures were imaged and measured with ImageJ software. Experiments were performed double blindly (with the experimenter not knowing the genotypes and ABA concentrations). Values are means ± se , n = 3 experiments, and each experiment analyzed 45 stomatal apertures.

To examine the stomatal aperture from drought-stressed plants, leaves of the same developmental stages were excised from 2-week-old seedlings and left on the bench to mimic the drought condition. The leaf epidermises were peeled at the indicated times after detachment to observe and measure the stomatal aperture with a microscope. Values are means ± sd of three individual plants per genotype. At least 50 stomata were measured for each plant. The experiments were repeated at least three times with similar results.

TEM and Chlorophyll Efflux Assay

For TEM , sections of stems and leaves were collected, prepared as described previously ( Lee et al., 2009 ), and viewed with a TEM apparatus (Hitachi H-7500). The thickness of the cuticle was measured and analyzed using ImageJ software. For the chlorophyll efflux assay, rosette leaves of 4-week-old plants grown in soil were used; about 2 g of each leaf sample was incubated on ice for 30 min and then immersed in 30 mL of 80% ethanol in 50-mL conical tubes at room temperature. A series of 100-μL aliquots were taken every 10 min after initial immersion and subjected to spectrophotometry (absorption measured at 647 and 664 nm) to quantify the amount of chlorophyll leached. The concentration (µ m ) of chlorophyll in the leachate was calculated using the following equation (7.93 × A 664 + 19.53 × A 647 ) as described previously ( Lolle et al., 1997 ). This experiment was repeated five times.

qRT -PCR Analysis

Two-week-old seedlings grown on plates were taken from the agar and exposed to dehydration for the indicated times as described previously ( Umezawa et al., 2006 ). Total RNA was extracted from various samples using the Spectrum plant total RNA kit (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For qRT -PCR, total RNA samples (2 µg per reaction) were reverse transcribed into complementary DNAs by Moloney murine leukemia virus (MMLV) reverse transcriptase, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen 28025-021). Then, the complementary DNAs were used as templates in qRT -PCR with the gene-specific primers listed in Supplemental Table S1 . qRT -PCR was performed on 96-well plates with SYBR Green Master Mix in a total volume of 20 µL using the Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR system. The reactions were performed in biological triplicate using RNA samples extracted from three independent plant materials. Expression levels for all candidate genes were determined using the 2 ^△CT (for change in critical cycle threshold) method, and the relative transcript levels were calculated.

Construction of max2-1/abi3-8 and max2-1/abi5-7 Double Mutants

To generate max2-1/abi3-8 and max2-1/abi5-7 double mutants, max2-1 was crossed to abi3-8 and abi5-7 plants. F2 seeds were plated and grown on MS medium containing 2 µ m ABA , and seedlings displaying the abi3-8 or abi5-7 phenotype were selected. Plants showing longer hypocotyls at the seedling stage and multiple branches at the adult stage were selected as max2-1/abi3-8 and max2-1/abi5-7 candidates. Homozygous mutations were further confirmed by sequencing the PCR products of the genomic DNA of MAX2 , ABI3 , and ABI5 genes from these candidates.

Measurement of ABA Content

Two-week-old seedlings growing on plates were taken from the agar and exposed to dehydration for the indicated times as described previously ( Umezawa et al., 2006 ). ABA extraction and measurement were carried out using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry as described ( Owen and Abrams, 2009 ). ABA content was measured from six independent biological samples for each time point. ABA content is shown as ng g −1 fresh weight of seedlings before the drought treatment.
