Endonuclease G (ENDOG) is involved in DNA replication, mitochondrial DNA
maintenance, and late apoptosis. Its role in genome maintenance and cell death
necessitates strict regulation to prevent unwanted DNA damage. Although
inhibitors of ENDOG have been identified in nonmammalian species, no such
inhibitors have been discovered in mammals. Here, we identify KU80 as a novel
negative regulator of ENDOG in human cells. The KU80 C-terminal domain (CTD)
shares structural homology with Drosophila EndoG inhibitor (EGI), directly binds
ENDOG and inhibits its nuclease activity in vitro. In silico modeling and
immunoprecipitation reveal the KU80 core domain mediates ENDOG interaction.
Depletion of KU80 increases nuclear ENDOG accumulation and DNA damage under
normal conditions, evidenced by elevated phosphorylated histone H2AX (γH2AX)
levels. Conversely, ENDOG-deficient cells show reduced DNA damage and increased
chemotherapeutic resistance. We uncover a mechanism where KU80 maintains genomic
stability by inhibiting ENDOG activity and nuclear translocation. This study
establishes KU80 as a key DNA damage response regulator, offering insights into
potential therapies for diseases linked to dysregulated ENDOG activity.

Introduction

Genomic stability is fundamental to cellular function and organismal survival [1, 2]. Cells face constant threats to genomic integrity from both intrinsic factors like replication errors and metabolic byproducts, and extrinsic factors, such as ionizing radiation, UV light, and chemical carcinogens [3, 4]. To maintain genomic fidelity, cells have developed sophisticated mechanisms coordinating cellular metabolism, DNA repair, and cell cycle progression. If DNA damage is beyond repair, cells execute programmed cell death. These are coordinated processes, where the cell cycle is paused and apoptosis is inhibited to ensure sufficient time for DNA repair. Endonucleases play pivotal roles in these processes, participating in DNA repair as well as apoptosis. However, their ability to cleave phosphodiester bonds in DNA and RNA necessitates stringent regulation to prevent unintended nucleic acid degradation and maintain cellular homeostasis [5, 6]. Apoptosis, a programmed cell death process, is crucial for normal embryonic development, tissue homeostasis maintenance, and appropriate physiological responses to tissue injury. A hallmark of late‐stage apoptosis is inter‐nucleosomal DNA fragmentation [7]. Upon apoptotic activation, various factors are released from mitochondria, including cytochrome c, procaspases, Smac/DIABLO, apoptosis‐inducing factor (AIF), and Endonuclease G (ENDOG) [8].

ENDOG, a mitochondrial enzyme, translocates to the nucleus in response to apoptotic stimuli, cleaving chromosomal DNA via a caspase‐independent pathway [9, 10]. Initially identified in chicken erythrocyte nuclei, ENDOG efficiently cleaves double‐stranded DNA rich in cytosine and guanine stretches. Synthesized as a 33 kDa precursor protein with a mitochondrial localization sequence (MLS), ENDOG is directed to mitochondria where it is cleaved to produce the active 28 kDa mature nuclease [11, 12]. This mitochondrial compartmentalization under normal conditions prevents indiscriminate nucleic acid degradation [13]. ENDOG belongs to a large family of DNA/RNA nonspecific nucleases characterized by a ββα‐Me‐finger motif, shared among well‐conserved nucleases across microbial and eukaryotic organisms [14]. Under physiological conditions, ENDOG exhibits low nuclease activity and is believed to require additional cofactors for regulation, such as nuclear proteins like AIF, flap endonuclease 1 (FEN‐1), and heat shock protein 70 (Hsp70) [15, 16].

Previous biochemical and structural studies have identified an endogenous ENDOG inhibitor (EGI) in Drosophila melanogaster. EGI, localized in the nucleus, protects cells from chromosomal damage by directly binding to the active sites of ENDOG [17, 18]. However, mammalian genomes lack proteins with sequence homology to EGI, suggesting alternative regulatory mechanisms for ENDOG in higher organisms. Comparative structural analyses have suggested that the human protein KU80 (also known as XRCC5) may serve a similar inhibitory function to EGI in mammals [17]. KU80, an 86 kDa protein with three conserved domains, traditionally functions as a heterodimer with KU70 in the nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) pathway of DNA double‐strand break (DSB) repair [19, 20]. Emerging evidence indicates that KU80 can exist as a monomer in the cytoplasm and may have functions independent of KU70 and DNA repair [21].

In this study, we investigate the potential regulatory role of KU80 on ENDOG activity. We demonstrate that both active and inactive forms of ENDOG interact with the core domain of KU80. Our findings reveal that in the absence of KU80, ENDOG translocates into the nucleus, potentially contributing to DNA degradation. Notably, KU80‐deficient cells exhibit increased DNA damage sensitivity, evidenced by elevated levels of phosphorylated H2AX (γH2AX), whereas ENDOG‐deficient cells show reduced γH2AX intensity. These results suggest that ENDOG is capable of inducing DNA DSB, and that KU80 is the negative regulator of ENDOG. Inhibition is achieved by directly interacting with ENDOG and preventing its nuclear translocation, thereby playing a crucial role in maintaining genomic stability. Our work provides new insights into the regulation mechanism of apoptotic nuclease ENDOG, opening avenues for potential therapeutic interventions in diseases characterized by dysregulated apoptosis and DNA damage responses.

KU80 CTD inhibits mammalian ENDOG activityTo identify potential regulators of mammalian ENDOG, we employed a structure‐based approach using the ENDOG–EGI complex as a template (PDB ID: 3ISM). Structural similarity searches conducted with Foldseek revealed that the C‐terminal domain (CTD) of human KU80 (PDB ID: 1Q2Z) exhibits a significant structural resemblance to EGI, achieving a similarity score of 89 and an E‐value of 6.35e‐2 (Fig. 1A) [22]. Remarkably, this structural homology exists despite a low sequence identity of only 15.5% between the two proteins. Superimposition of the KU80 CTD onto EGI demonstrated alignment across nearly all regions, including the critical ENDOG binding interface, with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 1.941 Å (Fig. 1B). To assess whether the KU80 CTD could inhibit ENDOG activity, we cloned and purified the KU80 CTD encompassing amino acids 590–709. In vitro DNase activity inhibition assays were performed using purified ENDOG and varying concentrations of the KU80 CTD, with plasmid DNA serving as the substrate. The results indicated that the KU80 CTD effectively inhibited ENDOG‐mediated DNA cleavage in a dose‐dependent manner, exhibiting inhibitory potency comparable to that of EGI (Fig. 1C). To confirm a direct interaction between ENDOG and the KU80 CTD, we conducted isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) experiments. The ITC analysis revealed a dissociation constant (K
d) of 78.1 nm, indicative of a high‐affinity binding interaction between the two proteins (Fig. 1D). KU80 interacts with the ENDOG through its core domainBecause the nuclease activity of ENDOG was suppressed by KU80 and in silico analysis elucidated the protein–protein complex, we next decided to directly validate the interaction between ENDOG and KU80 using an IP assay and determine the critical domain for this interaction. First, the mutagenesis plasmids ENDOG and KU80 were used to clone the truncation mutants of each protein. Afterward, a co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assay was conducted to identify the essential domains for interaction (Fig. 3A). The plasmids were labeled with HA and Myc tags to facilitate the specific isolation of proteins of interest from protein complexes. Co‐IP assay revealed that both the mature and preprotein forms of ENDOG were associated with KU80 (Fig. 3B). Additionally, the interaction between endogenous ENDOG and KU80 was confirmed (Fig. S1A,B). Next, we investigated which KU80 domain was critical for its interaction with ENDOG. It has been demonstrated that KU80 has three main active domains, including an N‐terminal, von Willebrand factor type A (vWA) domain (1–235 aa), a core domain (236–564 aa), and a C‐terminal domain (565–732 aa). The vWA domain is responsible for DNA binding, whereas the core domain mediates protein–protein interactions [20, 25]. Co‐IP results demonstrated that the core domain of KU80 played a critical role in this interaction (Fig. 3C). This result was supported by an additional IP assay with deletion mutants of KU80 and full‐length ENDOG. All deletion mutants interacted with ENDOG, except for the core domain deletion mutant (Fig. 3D). As the previous results revealed a direct interaction between ENDOG and KU80, the next step was to investigate the specific mechanism of this interaction. Previous studies have reported that ENDOG translocates to the nucleus after being released from the mitochondria during apoptosis and contributes to nuclear DNA fragmentation [12, 26]. To replicate this scenario, cells were treated with ETO to induce DNA damage, which induced apoptosis via DNA DSBs [27, 28]. Cells were transfected with ENDOG‐ or KU80‐targeting siRNAs and treated with ETO for the indicated times. Remarkably, IB analysis revealed that ENDOG and KU80 deficiency did not exert discernible effects on the reciprocal protein expression. No significant differences were observed in KU80 expression between ENDOG‐depleted cells under normal and apoptotic conditions (Fig. 3E). Correspondingly, ENDOG expression did not change in the absence of KU80 under nonapoptotic or ETO‐treated conditions (Fig. 3F). DiscussionEndonuclease regulation is critical for cell viability and apoptosis. Despite the recognized role of ENDOG in apoptotic DNA degradation in mammalian cells, its regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely characterized. Here, we reveal a direct interaction between mammalian ENDOG and KU80, uncovering a novel inhibitory function of KU80. The CTD of KU80 directly inhibits ENDOG activity, while the core domain mediates protein interaction. This finding expands the functional repertoire of KU80 beyond its established role in NHEJ DNA repair. Structural analysis revealed that the solenoid domain of KU80 exhibits remarkable homology to Drosophila EGI, with comprehensive domain searches identifying KU80 (but not KU70, DNA‐PKcs, XRCC4, or Ligase IV) as the sole mammalian protein structurally analogous to EGI, demonstrating comparable inhibitory potency against ENDOG. Although the KU80 CTD shares a very low sequence identity of just 15.5% with the Drosophila EGI, it exhibits a highly conserved structural topology that aligns almost perfectly across the critical ENDOG binding interface [17, 18]. Functionally, this structural mimicry allows the KU80 CTD to perform an equivalent regulatory role to EGI, directly binding to ENDOG and suppressing its nuclease activity with comparable dose‐dependent potency. Thus, despite their phylogenetic divergence in primary sequence, the striking structural and functional parallels between the KU80 CTD and EGI highlight a conserved mechanism for restraining apoptotic nucleases to preserve genomic integrity. Furthermore, in silico modeling and immunoprecipitation experiments revealed that the KU80 core domain also plays an essential role in ENDOG interaction.Our findings suggest that KU80 regulates ENDOG through direct protein–protein interactions, not by altering its expression level. This regulatory relationship represents a previously unrecognized but functionally conserved mechanism for genome protection, with KU80 serving as the mammalian guardian against aberrant ENDOG activity, functionally equivalent to Drosophila EGI. Our data highlight ENDOG inhibition by KU80 mainly in the nucleus, as shown by the increased accumulation of ENDOG and decreased γH2AX. Based on the control condition, KU80 most likely sequesters ENDOG in the cytosol, preventing nuclear translocation and subsequent DNA degradation. This is because a very little to undetectable amount of ENDOG is normally present in the cytosol or in the nucleus as shown in Figs 4 and 5. Knock down of KU80 dramatically increasing the nuclear localization of ENDOG strongly implies that there must be a basal level inhibition of its migration to the nucleus which involves KU80. This cytosolic inhibition is crucial given the nucleic acid cleavage activity of ENDOG and its role in mitochondrial DNA replication primer provision.Our findings support a coordinated mechanism whereby KU80 maintains genome integrity by simultaneously promoting DNA repair and restraining apoptosis. While our in vitro DNase inhibition assays and ITC experiments demonstrated that isolated KU80 CTD directly binds to ENDOG and inhibits its nuclease activity, Co‐IP experiments in cellular contexts failed to detect CTD‐ENDOG interaction when CTD was expressed alone. This apparent discrepancy likely reflects the requirement for proper protein context and cellular localization signals present in full‐length KU80 but absent in isolated CTD, suggesting that while CTD harbors the intrinsic ENDOG‐inhibitory function, its effective engagement in vivo depends on structural elements provided by the complete KU80 protein. These observations underscore the importance of considering both domain‐intrinsic biochemical capabilities and cellular contextual requirements when interpreting protein–protein interaction data. Under sub‐lethal genotoxic stress, KU80 functions as a KU70/KU80 heterodimer positioned predominantly in the nucleus, rapidly binding to DNA breaks and facilitating classical NHEJ. This ‘repair‐first’ bias is reinforced by KU‐dependent suppression of apoptotic signaling. KU binds to a stem loop in the p53 mRNA 5′UTR under normal conditions to repress p53 translation, thereby preventing unnecessary p53‐mediated cell death when damage is repairable [33]. KU80‐deficient mammalian cells exhibited increased ENDOG nuclear translocation, leading to DNA damage evidenced by elevated γH2AX levels. Conversely, ENDOG‐deficient cells showed decreased γH2AX levels under normal and apoptotic conditions, with increased chemotherapeutic resistance.However, under severe or sustained DNA damage conditions, KU80 undergoes stress‐triggered modifications and relocalization that shift its role from ‘guardian’ to ‘executioner’. High‐intensity stress or prolonged DNA damage activates post‐translational modifications on KU80 and KU70 that attenuate the repair regime. Upon significant genotoxic stress, KU70 becomes acetylated, disrupting its interaction with the pro‐apoptotic factor Bax. More definitively, proteolytic cleavage of KU80 represents an early event in apoptosis. KU80 is cleaved at its C terminus by caspase/calpain activity in the nucleus, disabling the DNA‐PK‐KU complex [34]. The cleaved KU80 fragments redistribute to the cytoplasm as the nucleus fragments. This loss of intact KU80 not only halts NHEJ repair but also releases the restraint on apoptotic nucleases. Endonuclease regulation is critical for cell viability and apoptosis. Despite the recognized role of ENDOG in apoptotic DNA degradation in mammalian cells, its regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely characterized. Here, we reveal a direct interaction between mammalian ENDOG and KU80, uncovering a novel inhibitory function of KU80. The CTD of KU80 directly inhibits ENDOG activity, while the core domain mediates protein interaction. This finding expands the functional repertoire of KU80 beyond its established role in NHEJ DNA repair. Structural analysis revealed that the solenoid domain of KU80 exhibits remarkable homology to Drosophila EGI, with comprehensive domain searches identifying KU80 (but not KU70, DNA‐PKcs, XRCC4, or Ligase IV) as the sole mammalian protein structurally analogous to EGI, demonstrating comparable inhibitory potency against ENDOG. Although the KU80 CTD shares a very low sequence identity of just 15.5% with the Drosophila EGI, it exhibits a highly conserved structural topology that aligns almost perfectly across the critical ENDOG binding interface [17, 18]. Functionally, this structural mimicry allows the KU80 CTD to perform an equivalent regulatory role to EGI, directly binding to ENDOG and suppressing its nuclease activity with comparable dose‐dependent potency. Thus, despite their phylogenetic divergence in primary sequence, the striking structural and functional parallels between the KU80 CTD and EGI highlight a conserved mechanism for restraining apoptotic nucleases to preserve genomic integrity. Furthermore, in silico modeling and immunoprecipitation experiments revealed that the KU80 core domain also plays an essential role in ENDOG interaction.

Our findings suggest that KU80 regulates ENDOG through direct protein–protein interactions, not by altering its expression level. This regulatory relationship represents a previously unrecognized but functionally conserved mechanism for genome protection, with KU80 serving as the mammalian guardian against aberrant ENDOG activity, functionally equivalent to Drosophila EGI. Our data highlight ENDOG inhibition by KU80 mainly in the nucleus, as shown by the increased accumulation of ENDOG and decreased γH2AX. Based on the control condition, KU80 most likely sequesters ENDOG in the cytosol, preventing nuclear translocation and subsequent DNA degradation. This is because a very little to undetectable amount of ENDOG is normally present in the cytosol or in the nucleus as shown in Figs 4 and 5. Knock down of KU80 dramatically increasing the nuclear localization of ENDOG strongly implies that there must be a basal level inhibition of its migration to the nucleus which involves KU80. This cytosolic inhibition is crucial given the nucleic acid cleavage activity of ENDOG and its role in mitochondrial DNA replication primer provision.

Our findings support a coordinated mechanism whereby KU80 maintains genome integrity by simultaneously promoting DNA repair and restraining apoptosis. While our in vitro DNase inhibition assays and ITC experiments demonstrated that isolated KU80 CTD directly binds to ENDOG and inhibits its nuclease activity, Co‐IP experiments in cellular contexts failed to detect CTD‐ENDOG interaction when CTD was expressed alone. This apparent discrepancy likely reflects the requirement for proper protein context and cellular localization signals present in full‐length KU80 but absent in isolated CTD, suggesting that while CTD harbors the intrinsic ENDOG‐inhibitory function, its effective engagement in vivo depends on structural elements provided by the complete KU80 protein. These observations underscore the importance of considering both domain‐intrinsic biochemical capabilities and cellular contextual requirements when interpreting protein–protein interaction data. Under sub‐lethal genotoxic stress, KU80 functions as a KU70/KU80 heterodimer positioned predominantly in the nucleus, rapidly binding to DNA breaks and facilitating classical NHEJ. This ‘repair‐first’ bias is reinforced by KU‐dependent suppression of apoptotic signaling. KU binds to a stem loop in the p53 mRNA 5′UTR under normal conditions to repress p53 translation, thereby preventing unnecessary p53‐mediated cell death when damage is repairable [33]. KU80‐deficient mammalian cells exhibited increased ENDOG nuclear translocation, leading to DNA damage evidenced by elevated γH2AX levels. Conversely, ENDOG‐deficient cells showed decreased γH2AX levels under normal and apoptotic conditions, with increased chemotherapeutic resistance.

However, under severe or sustained DNA damage conditions, KU80 undergoes stress‐triggered modifications and relocalization that shift its role from ‘guardian’ to ‘executioner’. High‐intensity stress or prolonged DNA damage activates post‐translational modifications on KU80 and KU70 that attenuate the repair regime. Upon significant genotoxic stress, KU70 becomes acetylated, disrupting its interaction with the pro‐apoptotic factor Bax. More definitively, proteolytic cleavage of KU80 represents an early event in apoptosis. KU80 is cleaved at its C terminus by caspase/calpain activity in the nucleus, disabling the DNA‐PK‐KU complex [34]. The cleaved KU80 fragments redistribute to the cytoplasm as the nucleus fragments. This loss of intact KU80 not only halts NHEJ repair but also releases the restraint on apoptotic nucleases. These findings align with previous observations of ENDOG activation through subcellular translocation during late apoptosis in mammalian systems. In Drosophila, apoptosis induction reduces and relocalizes inhibitor levels to the cytoplasm. Similarly, mammalian KU80 undergoes cleavage and cytoplasmic redistribution during early apoptosis. Notably, ENDOG nuclear translocation under normal conditions in KU80‐absent cells suggests a regulatory role for KU80 in mammalian ENDOG activation.

Reverse transfection of siRNA was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Newark, CA, Waltham, MA, USA) concurrently with cell seeding, following the manufacturer's protocol. The following day, forward transfection of siRNA was performed to enhance the efficacy of siRNA transfection [36]. Twenty‐four to forty‐eight hours after forward transfection, the cells were harvested for subsequent experiments, including IF and IB. Negative control siRNA (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as a negative control. siRNA sequence for ENDOG and KU80 is found in Table S2.

 for 20 min. The resulting supernatants were subjected to Bradford assay to determine protein concentrations. Next, the cell lysates were buffered with sample buffer and boiled at 100 °C for 10 min. After transfer, the membrane was blocked with a 5% skim milk solution (skim milk powder and Tween 20 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature and rinsed with 1× PBST (Tween 20 in PBS) for a short time. Subsequently, the primary antibodies were completely removed and blots were washed with 1× PBST for 15 min each. The membrane was next incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in 5% skim milk dissolved in 1× PBST and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After incubation, the unattached antibodies were washed away with 1× PBST for 10 min and membranes were developed using the Lumi Femto or Pico reagent (Daeil Lab Service, Seoul, South Korea). Drug sensitivity assays/cell clonogenic assayA total of 1 × 103 HeLa cells were seeded in 6‐well plates 24 h before drug treatment. The cells were treated with ETO and CPT at concentrations of 0, 25, 50, 100 nm and 0, 2, 4, and 8 nm, respectively, for 10 days to allow colony formation. The cells were rinsed once with ice‐cold 1× PBS and fixed with a 3.7% formaldehyde solution for 15 min. After fixation, the cells were rinsed with 1× PBS again and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (100% ethanol) for 15 min, followed by the removal of the crystal violet solution with 1× PBS. The cell colonies were air‐dried overnight. Colonies were measured using the imagej software.
