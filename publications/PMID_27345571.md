It is well established that eukaryotic genomes are pervasively transcribed
producing cryptic unstable transcripts (CUTs). However, the mechanisms
regulating pervasive transcription are not well understood. Here, we report that
the fission yeast CENP-B homolog Abp1 plays an important role in preventing
pervasive transcription. We show that loss of abp1 results in the accumulation
of CUTs, which are targeted for degradation by the exosome pathway. These CUTs
originate from different types of genomic features, but the highest increase
corresponds to Tf2 retrotransposons and rDNA repeats, where they map along the
entire elements. In the absence of abp1, increased RNAPII-Ser5P occupancy is
observed throughout the Tf2 coding region and, unexpectedly, RNAPII-Ser5P is
enriched at rDNA repeats. Loss of abp1 also results in Tf2 derepression and
increased nucleolus size. Altogether these results suggest that Abp1 prevents
pervasive RNAPII transcription of repetitive DNA elements (i.e., Tf2 and rDNA
repeats) from internal cryptic sites.

Bioinformatics and Biostatistics analysesIllumina ChIPseq data for Abp1 and RNAPII-Ser5, together with their respective Input samples, and strand-specific RNAseq data for short RNAs from wt and abp1Δ cells were aligned against the reference S. pombe genome (Sanger release 09052011) using Bowtie 0.12.5, allowing for 2 (ChIPseq) and 0 (RNAseq) mismatches in the read seed. In order to compare read proportions to estimate enrichment and depletion in feature types with multiple copies along the genome (i.e. transposons, rRNA units), all possible alignments for multiple hits were considered for all samples and controls. For ChIPseq data, strand shift bias and duplicated reads were estimated and removed with the htSeqTools package [35] using the filterDuplReads functions with default options, and significantly enriched regions were detected with the enrichedRegions function, considering only regions within the IP sample with a coverage above 2.5 times the first quartile, and using a Benjamini Hochberg p-value threshold of 0.05. Afterwards the enrichedPeaks function was used to detect significantly enriched peaks (putative binding sites), setting the minimum coverage difference between IP and Input at 200. For RNAseq data, abp1Δ and wt replicates were pooled, and significantly enriched regions in short RNAseq in abp1Δ and wt samples independently (without control) were detected using the enrichedRegions function from the htSeqTools package, using the one tailed option and a Benjamini Hochbergh pvalue threshold of 0.05, and leaving the other settings as default. Afterwards, significantly enriched and depleted regions in small RNAseq in abp1Δ mutant against wt (thus, up and down-regulated short RNAs in abp1Δ) were detected using the enrichedRegions function from the htSeqTools package, using the two tailed option and a Benjamini Hochbergh pvalue threshold of 0.05, and leaving the other settings as default.Annotation of all resulting enriched/depleted regions (RNAseq) and putative binding sites (ChIPseq) against the S. pombe annotation 294 v2.20 [36] was performed using the AnnotatePeakInBatch function from the ChIPpeakAnno package [37], and setting the maxgap parameter at 100 bp. ChIPseq (IP/Input) and short RNAs (abp1Δ/wt) log2 ratios were computed in R, and peak location, mean coverage and coverage ratio plots were produced with the PeakLocation, plotMeanCoverage and RegionsCoverage functions of the htSeqTools package respectively, normalizing with respect to the mean value across genomic feature length when necessary. Unless otherwise stated, all downstream statistical analysis of ChIPseq and RNAseq data was performed in R version 2.15.3. Microarray data for abp1Δ de-regulated probesets was retrieved from NCBI GEO accession GSE39404 and probeset chromosomal location was annotated against the S. pombe annotation 294 v2.20 genome.

RESULTS and DISCUSSION3.1. abp1Δ cells accumulate CUTs from Tf2 retrotransposons and rDNA repeatsCapillary gel electrophoresis analyses of total RNAs obtained from abp1Δ cells showed an accumulation of short RNA molecules of sizes ranging between 50 nt and 200 nt (Figure 1). No such increase was observed upon deletion of the other two S. pombe CENP-B homologs cbh1 or cbh2 (Figure 1). Furthermore, no synergistic effects were detected in abp1Δ/cbh1Δ or abp1Δ/cbh2Δ double mutants (Figure 1), suggesting that Cbh1/2 have only minor effects on this accumulation.RNAseq analyses showed that, as expected from their length, a significant proportion of short RNAs detected in abp1Δ corresponded to short non-coding RNAs species, such as 5S and 5.8S rRNAs, and tRNAs (Figure 2A). In addition, we also detected short RNAs corresponding to genomic features of larger size, such as protein-coding genes and, in particular, Tf2 retrotransposons and 28S and 18S rDNAs, their full-length forms being respectively of 4000 nt, 3485 nt and 1842 nt (Figure 2A), suggesting that a large proportion of these short RNAs corresponded to CUTs species. Note that, although the highest proportion of short RNAs mapped to protein-coding genes (~55%), we detected them in only <5% of the S. pombe protein-coding genes (not shown). From the RNAseq data we defined significantly enriched regions in short RNAs in abp1Δ and wt samples (see material and method). The short-RNAs enriched regions found in abp1Δ were smaller in size than the one found in wt (Supplementary Figure S3A). We observed that ~65% of the genomic regions enriched in short RNAs were detected in both abp1Δ and wt cells and 35% (169) were present in abp1Δ only (Figure 2B). Compared with wt and abp1Δ, the 169 short-RNAs enriched regions specific to abp1Δ contained a higher proportions of regions mapping to tRNA but were depleted in regions corresponding to the rDNA repeats (Supplementary Figure S3B). Overall, the short RNAs were generally enriched in abp1Δ cells in comparison to wt cells (Figure 2C), with the highest accumulation corresponding to Tf2 retrotransposons and 28S rDNAs (Figure 2C). We confirmed this accumulation by RT-qPCR analyses on gel purified-RNAs of sizes <500 nt (Figure 2D). Tf2 and rDNA constitute two major types of repetitive DNA elements in the S. pombe genome, which contains thirteen Tf2 copies scattered along the genome [38] and approximately 100–150 rDNA repeats located at both ends of chromosome III [39]. CUTs are usually degraded through the exosome pathway [1–3]. Indeed, we observed that dis3-54, a mutation that impairs ribonuclease activity of the essential Dis3 exosome subunit [40], and deletion of cid14, a poly(A)polymerase targeting nuclear RNAs to the exosome [41], showed synergistic effects with abp1Δ regarding the accumulation of CUTs from both Tf2 and rDNA elements (Figure 2E). Altogether, these results suggest that Abp1 prevents the accumulation of CUTs from repetitive DNAs, mainly Tf2 and rDNA elements. Abp1 has recently been shown to collaborate with Set1 to silence sense and antisense Tf2 transcription [42]. We tested whether Set1 had an effect on the accumulation of short RNAs seen in the abp1Δ strain but we saw no increase in CUTs proportion in set1Δ compared to wt or in the double mutant set1Δ/abp1Δ compared to abp1Δ (Supplementary Figure S2A and B), indicating that the Abp1-mediated silencing of cryptic transcription is independent of Set1.ChIPseq analyses showed a significant co-occurrence of CUTs with Abp1 binding sites (Fisher’s test p< 10−42) (Figure 3A), suggesting a direct contribution of Abp1 to their regulation. Actually, ~50% of CUTs-enriched regions localized at Abp1 binding sites (Figure 3B). The other ~50% of the CUTs don’t appear to be directly associated with an Abp1 binding sites, either because we could not detect the binding sites or because Abp1 was acting on the CUTs production from a bigger distance. In particular, Abp1 binding was detected at the LTRs of Tf2 elements (Figure 3C, left) [20, 25] and all along the rDNA repeats (Figure 3C, right). ChIP-qPCR experiments confirmed binding of Abp1 at LTRs of Tf2 elements and at the 28S rDNA gene (Figure 3D). Abp1 binding was also detected at tRNA and 5S rDNA genes, and at ~10% of the S. pombe protein-coding genes (Supplementary Figure S1A). Except in the rDNA repeats, Abp1 binding was generally restricted to the 5′ region of the corresponding genomic feature (Supplementary Figure S1B).3.2. In the absence of Abp1, RNAPII transcribes Tf2 and rDNA elements from multiple internal cryptic sitesAt Tf2 elements, CUTs were enriched along the entire element including the LTRs (Figure 4A). Similarly, CUTs enrichment was also detected all along the rDNA repeats (Figure 5A), with the highest enrichment observed at the 28S rDNA gene. Notice that at Tf2 elements CUTs were mostly sense, with antisense CUTs restricted to the LTRs (Figure 4A). On the other hand, in rDNA repeats, we detected both sense and antisense CUTs, the later being slightly more abundant (Figure 5A). Furthermore, in ChIPseq experiments, we detected the active RNAPolII form phosphorylated in Serine 5 (RNAPII-Ser5P), which is normally restricted to promoters (reviewed in [43]), all along Tf2 elements (Figure 4B). Most remarkably, we also detected RNAPII-Ser5P across rDNA repeats, which are normally transcribed by RNAPI [44] (Figure 5B). ChIP-qPCR analyses confirmed these results (Figures 4C and 5C). Altogether these results suggest that, in the absence of Abp1, RNAPII initiates transcription from multiple internal cryptic sites distributed along Tf2 elements and rDNA repeats.We observed that most differentially expressed Abp1 target genes were upregulated in abp1Δ cells (Supplementary Figure S1C). A third of the differentially affected genes were not localized near an Abp1-binding site (Supplementary Figure S1C), suggesting that the absence of Abp1 could also have an indirect role on gene expression, for example through it’s ability to regulate nuclear organization ([20, 42]). Transcription from internal cryptic sites generally occurs when genes are actively transcribed [45, 46]. In this regard, full-length Tf2 mRNA levels increased in abp1Δ cells (Figure 2D, left) [20, 47]. Abp1 has been shown to repress transcription by promoting histone deacetylation through the recruitment of the HDACs Crl3 and Clr6 [20, 42, 47, 48]. Thus, the function of Abp1 in preventing cryptic transcription of Tf2 elements might reflect its role in establishing a repressive chromatin state. Consistent with this hypothesis, CUTs also accumulated in crl3Δ/clr6Δ mutants as well as in the hip1Δ and hip3Δ mutants, which are histones chaperones belonging to the HIRA complex involved in nucleosome assembly and repressing Tf2 and cryptic transcriptions [45, 49] (Figures 4D and 4E). The extent of CUTs accumulation generally correlated with the extent of Tf2 activation (Figures 4D and 4E). H3K9Ac was nevertheless increased within the coding sequence of Tf2 in the clr6-1 mutant, indicating that this chromatin mark could mediate the increased transcription of CUTs in this strain (Figure 4E). This model could potentially explain the unexpected lack of increased H3 acetylation in abp1Δ. We measured the presence of H3K36me on Tf2 elements, but observed no changes in abp1Δ, clr3 or clr6 mutants. This suggests that the short abortive transcription of CUTs does not stably recruit Set2, possibly because RNAPII does not reach a ser2-phosphorylated elongating form.In contrast to Tf2, expression of full-length 28S RNAs was unaltered in abp1Δ cells (Figure 2D), suggesting that transcription by RNAPl was not affected. However, as shown above (Figure 5B), CUTs accumulation at rDNA repeats is associated with cryptic transcription by RNAPII, which normally is not detected at rDNAs repeats. H3K14ac is significantly increased over the rDNA gene in abp1Δ, supporting a model of Abp1-mediated recruitment of Clr3 to repress CUTs (Figure 5F) [52]. However, neither clr3 nor clr6 mutants phenocopied abp1Δ in this respect, and unlike clr3Δ [52], abp1Δ mutants do not lose silencing of a ura4+ reporter gene embedded in the rDNA (See Supplementary Figure S3). Altogether this indicates that other HDACs might be implicated in Abp1-mediated CUT repression in rDNA. Also, in contrast to the case of Tf2, rDNA genes accumulated high levels of H3K36me (Figure 5G). Together, these results suggest that abp1 represses CUT expression through different mechanisms in Tf2 and rDNA.Moreover, we observed that nucleolus size is enlarged by ~20% in abp1Δ cells (Figure 5D). The augmentation in nucleolus size in abp1Δ is not due to an increase in rDNA copy number, because the length of the rDNA repeats on chromosome III remains unchanged in abp1Δ ([25] and our unpublished results). Altogether these results suggest that nucleolus organization is disturbed in the absence of Abp1 and that this alteration does not affect RNAPI transcription but results in deregulated RNAPII binding and transcription. Interestingly, although normally transcribed by RNAPI, cryptic RNAPII transcription of rDNAs genes has been reported in both budding and fission yeasts, and is believed to be important for stability and expression of the rDNA array [53, 54]. The nucleolus constitutes a highly structured nuclear compartment [55]. However, the factors and mechanisms that determine nucleolus organization are still poorly understood. In this regard, our results suggest a contribution of Abp1 to the structural organization of nucleolar chromatin. Interestingly, Abp1 is known to mediate clustering of Tf2 elements [20] and it locates at 5S rRNA and tRNA genes that, being transcribed by RNAPIII, also form clusters in S. pombe [56–58]. Altogether these observations suggest that Abp1 might play a general structural role in the organization of the S. pombe genome.3.3. ConclusionsHere, we have shown that deletion of abp1 induces the accumulation of CUTs from Tf2 and rDNA repeats. These CUTs are product of RNAPII transcription, initiate at multiple internal cryptic sites and are degraded by the exosome pathway. Altogether, these results suggest that Abp1 prevents pervasive RNAPII transcription at Tf2 elements and rDNA repeats. Abp1 is also detected at promoters of ~10% of protein-coding genes, and at most 5S rRNA and tRNA genes. Whether Abp1 is also preventing pervasive transcription at these sites remains to be determined.

3.2. In the absence of Abp1, RNAPII transcribes Tf2 and rDNA elements from multiple internal cryptic sites

At Tf2 elements, CUTs were enriched along the entire element including the LTRs (Figure 4A). Similarly, CUTs enrichment was also detected all along the rDNA repeats (Figure 5A), with the highest enrichment observed at the 28S rDNA gene. Notice that at Tf2 elements CUTs were mostly sense, with antisense CUTs restricted to the LTRs (Figure 4A). On the other hand, in rDNA repeats, we detected both sense and antisense CUTs, the later being slightly more abundant (Figure 5A). Furthermore, in ChIPseq experiments, we detected the active RNAPolII form phosphorylated in Serine 5 (RNAPII-Ser5P), which is normally restricted to promoters (reviewed in [43]), all along Tf2 elements (Figure 4B). Most remarkably, we also detected RNAPII-Ser5P across rDNA repeats, which are normally transcribed by RNAPI [44] (Figure 5B). ChIP-qPCR analyses confirmed these results (Figures 4C and 5C). Altogether these results suggest that, in the absence of Abp1, RNAPII initiates transcription from multiple internal cryptic sites distributed along Tf2 elements and rDNA repeats.

We observed that most differentially expressed Abp1 target genes were upregulated in abp1Δ cells (Supplementary Figure S1C). A third of the differentially affected genes were not localized near an Abp1-binding site (Supplementary Figure S1C), suggesting that the absence of Abp1 could also have an indirect role on gene expression, for example through it’s ability to regulate nuclear organization ([20, 42]). Transcription from internal cryptic sites generally occurs when genes are actively transcribed [45, 46]. In this regard, full-length Tf2 mRNA levels increased in abp1Δ cells (Figure 2D, left) [20, 47]. Abp1 has been shown to repress transcription by promoting histone deacetylation through the recruitment of the HDACs Crl3 and Clr6 [20, 42, 47, 48]. Thus, the function of Abp1 in preventing cryptic transcription of Tf2 elements might reflect its role in establishing a repressive chromatin state. Consistent with this hypothesis, CUTs also accumulated in crl3Δ/clr6Δ mutants as well as in the hip1Δ and hip3Δ mutants, which are histones chaperones belonging to the HIRA complex involved in nucleosome assembly and repressing Tf2 and cryptic transcriptions [45, 49] (Figures 4D and 4E). The extent of CUTs accumulation generally correlated with the extent of Tf2 activation (Figures 4D and 4E). We explored the state of H3K9 and H3K14 acetylation in Tf2 (Figures 4F and 4G), but did not observe an increase in acetylation in the abp1Δ mutant. H3K9Ac was nevertheless increased within the coding sequence of Tf2 in the clr6-1 mutant, indicating that this chromatin mark could mediate the increased transcription of CUTs in this strain (Figure 4E). Increased RNAPII transcription in abp1Δ might recruit Set2, resulting in H3K36me deposition that in turn could recruit the Clr6 complex II to repress CUT expression [50, 51]. This model could potentially explain the unexpected lack of increased H3 acetylation in abp1Δ. We measured the presence of H3K36me on Tf2 elements, but observed no changes in abp1Δ, clr3 or clr6 mutants. This suggests that the short abortive transcription of CUTs does not stably recruit Set2, possibly because RNAPII does not reach a ser2-phosphorylated elongating form.

In contrast to Tf2, expression of full-length 28S RNAs was unaltered in abp1Δ cells (Figure 2D), suggesting that transcription by RNAPl was not affected. However, as shown above (Figure 5B), CUTs accumulation at rDNA repeats is associated with cryptic transcription by RNAPII, which normally is not detected at rDNAs repeats. H3K14ac is significantly increased over the rDNA gene in abp1Δ, supporting a model of Abp1-mediated recruitment of Clr3 to repress CUTs (Figure 5F) [52]. However, neither clr3 nor clr6 mutants phenocopied abp1Δ in this respect, and unlike clr3Δ [52], abp1Δ mutants do not lose silencing of a ura4+ reporter gene embedded in the rDNA (See Supplementary Figure S3). Altogether this indicates that other HDACs might be implicated in Abp1-mediated CUT repression in rDNA. Also, in contrast to the case of Tf2, rDNA genes accumulated high levels of H3K36me (Figure 5G). Together, these results suggest that abp1 represses CUT expression through different mechanisms in Tf2 and rDNA.

Moreover, we observed that nucleolus size is enlarged by ~20% in abp1Δ cells (Figure 5D). The augmentation in nucleolus size in abp1Δ is not due to an increase in rDNA copy number, because the length of the rDNA repeats on chromosome III remains unchanged in abp1Δ ([25] and our unpublished results). Altogether these results suggest that nucleolus organization is disturbed in the absence of Abp1 and that this alteration does not affect RNAPI transcription but results in deregulated RNAPII binding and transcription. Interestingly, although normally transcribed by RNAPI, cryptic RNAPII transcription of rDNAs genes has been reported in both budding and fission yeasts, and is believed to be important for stability and expression of the rDNA array [53, 54]. The nucleolus constitutes a highly structured nuclear compartment [55]. However, the factors and mechanisms that determine nucleolus organization are still poorly understood. In this regard, our results suggest a contribution of Abp1 to the structural organization of nucleolar chromatin. Interestingly, Abp1 is known to mediate clustering of Tf2 elements [20] and it locates at 5S rRNA and tRNA genes that, being transcribed by RNAPIII, also form clusters in S. pombe [56–58]. Altogether these observations suggest that Abp1 might play a general structural role in the organization of the S. pombe genome.

3.3. ConclusionsHere, we have shown that deletion of abp1 induces the accumulation of CUTs from Tf2 and rDNA repeats. These CUTs are product of RNAPII transcription, initiate at multiple internal cryptic sites and are degraded by the exosome pathway. Altogether, these results suggest that Abp1 prevents pervasive RNAPII transcription at Tf2 elements and rDNA repeats. Abp1 is also detected at promoters of ~10% of protein-coding genes, and at most 5S rRNA and tRNA genes. Whether Abp1 is also preventing pervasive transcription at these sites remains to be determined.
