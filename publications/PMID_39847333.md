The glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase) is an integral membrane protein that
catalyzes the hydrolysis of glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) in the endoplasmic
reticulum lumen and plays a vital role in glucose homeostasis. Dysregulation or
genetic mutations of G6Pase are associated with diabetes and glycogen storage
disease 1a (GSD-1a). Studies have characterized the biophysical and biochemical
properties of G6Pase; however, the structure and substrate recognition mechanism
of G6Pase remain unclear. Here, we present two cryo-EM structures of the 40-kDa
human G6Pase: a wild-type apo form and a mutant G6Pase-H176A with G6P bound,
elucidating the structural basis for substrate recognition and hydrolysis.
G6Pase comprises nine transmembrane helices and possesses a large catalytic
pocket facing the lumen. Unexpectedly, G6P binding induces substantial
conformational rearrangements in the catalytic pocket, which facilitate the
binding of the sugar moiety. In conjunction with functional analyses, this study
provides critical insights into the structure, substrate recognition, catalytic
mechanism, and pathology of G6Pase.

## Full Text

Glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) is the first intermediate of glucose metabolism. It acts as a central hub for major carbohydrate metabolic pathways, including glycolysis, glycogen synthesis, the pentose phosphate pathway, the hexosamine pathway, and glucose production (1). Cytosolic G6P is transported by the G6P transporter G6PT1 (encoded by SLC37A4) into the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where G6P is broken down into glucose and inorganic phosphate (Pi) by glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase, encoded by G6PC1-3), the final step of both gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis (2–4). Consequently, G6Pase serves as the gatekeeper of glycogen breakdown and de novo glucose production, thus playing an indispensable role in blood glucose homeostasis between meals (5–7). The expression of G6Pase is regulated by glucose (8), glucocorticoid (9), glucagon (10), fatty acids (11), and insulin (12), suggesting that G6Pase has a role in diabetes (13, 14). Deficiency or dysfunction of G6Pase causes glycogen storage disease type 1a (GSD-1a) (also known as von Gierke disease) (15–18). GSD-1 consists of a group of autosomal recessive disorders with an incidence of approximately 1 in 100,000 individuals and is characterized by hypoglycemia, hepatomegaly, growth retardation, nephromegaly, hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, and lactic acidemia (2, 19). To date, dozens of G6Pase mutations have been identified in GSD-1a patients (2), highlighting the physiological and pharmacological importance of G6Pase.

In humans, G6Pase consists of three isoforms, G6PC1, G6PC2, and G6PC3, which share sequence identity of approximately 35 to 50% and have different tissue distributions (5, 6). G6PC1, also known as G6Pase-α, is predominantly expressed in gluconeogenic tissues, including the liver, kidney, and small intestine (5–7). G6PC2, which is expressed mainly in pancreatic islet-β cells, regulates basal glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (20–22). G6PC3, also called G6Pase-β, is expressed ubiquitously and is responsible for neutrophil/macrophage homeostasis and function (6, 23). G6Pase was initially proposed to have six transmembrane helices (TMs) (24). A 9-TM model for G6Pase was subsequently proposed (25), which accords with the predicted three-dimensional (3D) AlphaFold2 (23) model of G6Pase. Biochemical studies have shown that the C terminus of G6Pase is sensitive to protease digestion, whereas the N terminus is resistant to this treatment in intact microsomes, indicating that the N terminus is located on the lumen side and that the C terminus faces the cytoplasm (26). Sequence analysis revealed that the loops of G6Pase in the lumen harbor the signature phosphatase motif (KX6RP-PSGH-SRX5HX3D/Q) of the type 2 phosphatidic acid phosphatase (PAP2) superfamily (27). In this phosphatase motif of G6Pase, the two Arg residues are predicted to stabilize the phosphate moiety of G6P, and the two His residues may serve as proton donor and phosphate receptor during hydrolysis (3, 26). The digestion of a [32P] phosphate-G6Pase intermediate by cyanogen bromide demonstrated that His176 of the two His residues is the potential phosphate receptor covalently binding to the radioactive phosphate during catalysis (3). G6Pase displays G6P-specific hydrolysis activity in intact microsomes (28); however, when microsomes are disrupted, G6Pse can catalyze the hydrolysis of other sugar phosphates, including mannose-6-phosphate (M6P, a stereoisomer of G6P), fructose-6-phosphate (F6P), and ATP, suggesting that the catalytic specificity of G6Pase is determined by substrate access (27, 29). Although these biochemical studies, AlphaFold2 structural prediction, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and in silico predictions of thermodynamic stability have revealed key interactions governing substrate binding and GSD-1a mutations (30, 31), the experimental structure of human G6Pase is unknown, and the precise binding mode of G6P and the structural basis for GSD-1a associated mutations in G6Pase remain undefined.

In this study, we report the cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of the human wild-type (WT) G6PC1 apo form and the hydrolysis-deficient G6PC1-H176A mutant in complex with G6P. These structures reveal unexpected conformational changes in G6PC1 upon G6P binding and elucidate the detailed mechanism of G6P recognition and hydrolysis. Combined with in vitro biochemical assays, we investigated the effects of molecular disruption caused by known GSD-1a-related mutations on the structure, expression level, and catalytic activity of G6Pase, providing important insights into G6PC1 pathology.

To validate possible binding poses of these ligands in G6PC1, we utilized the G6PC1–H176A–G6P complex structure as a template and docked M6P, F6P, and ATP into the catalytic pocket, which demonstrated that the phosphate groups of M6P, F6P, and ATP could reside in a position essentially identical to that of G6P, whereas the sugar moieties are variable (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 E and F). In particular, M6P and F6P are similar in size to G6P and can be fully embedded in the pocket, but the binding pose of the larger ATP is limited, which is consistent with the lowest phosphohydrolase activity of G6PC1 on ATP (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Thus, G6PC1 can catalyze the hydrolysis of different phosphate esters when these substrates are accessible; the in vivo G6P specificity of G6PC1 is presumably because the catalytic site is enveloped in the ER lumen, where substrate accessibility relies on the G6P transporter SLC37A4 (41).G6P Binding Induced Conformational Changes.The superposition of the apo and G6P-bound G6PC1 structures revealed unexpected conformational changes upon G6P binding (Fig. 3A). Although their overall structures are similar, a closer look shows that Arg83, Ser117, His119, Arg170, and Ca of H176 of the reaction center display slight backbone shifts and side-chain rotations, suggesting the stability of the catalytic center; however, the long luminal loops of TM2-TM3 (L2) and TM6-TM7 (L3) undergo notable movements toward the reaction center by 2.0 Å (between Ca atoms of Glu110) and 10.1 Å (between Ca atoms of Thr255), respectively, and LH1 and the loop of TM8-TM9 also move slightly to the center (Fig. 3 A–C). As a result, the ligand binding pocket of the G6P-bound structure shrinks, facilitating the formation of proper interactions between the glucose moiety and key residues in the luminal loops (Fig. 3B). In particular, Pro248 and Pro257 in the luminal loop L3 undergo cis–trans isomerization between the apo and G6P-bound states, which enable large movements of the loops (Fig. 3C). Moreover, structural evidence suggests that the movements between the two luminal loops are coupled: Cys109 of L2 forms a disulfide bond with Cys245 of L3 in both structures (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, in the G6P-bound structure, Thr255 engages in hydrogen bond interactions with Thr111 of L2 and the carbonyl oxygen atom of His252, stabilizing the conformation of the luminal loops for substrate binding (Fig. 3 C and D). Consequently, the pocket is reshaped and is favorable for G6P binding. Catalytic Mechanism of G6PC1.

The structures of hG6PC1, EcPgpB (35, 43), EbNSAP (42), and CiCPO (45) revealed that the consensus motifs of this phosphatase superfamily are spatially organized in a remarkably similar catalytic center (Fig. 4 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3), suggesting a common two-step mechanism for phosphatase activity (36). Our hG6PC1–G6P structure clearly demonstrates that the positively charged Lys76 and Arg83 in the C1 motif and Arg170 in the C3 motif engage in electrostatic interactions with the phosphate group of G6P, indicating that these residues attract the phosphate group and bring it in close proximity to His176 of the C3 motif (Fig. 4C), which functions as a nucleophile and forms the G6PC1–G6P intermediate (36). Meanwhile, His119 of the C2 motif, which is very close to the phosphate ester bond, acts as a proton donor and facilitates the release of the glucose moiety. Next, a water molecule can attack the phosphate-histidine intermediate, resulting in the release of an inorganic phosphate from the protein (Fig. 4 C and E).

In the C3 motif of EcPgpB, EbNSAP, and CiCPO, the histidine forms a hydrogen bond with the aspartate at the end, which was suggested to function as a charge-relay system to promote the formation of a histidine–phosphate intermediate (35, 42, 45). These structural observations suggest that this conserved histidine may be polarized by a combination of these interacting residues to form the histidine–phosphate intermediate.

Interestingly, our structures reveal the dual roles of Glu110 in the catalytic reaction. In the apo form of hG6PC1, Glu110 interacts with both Arg83 and Arg170, suggesting that Glu110 may serve as a phosphate surrogate when the substrate is absent (30); in the complex structure, Glu110 rotates its side chain away from the two arginine residues and forms a hydrogen bond with the glucose moiety (Fig. 4D). Substituting Glu110 with a neutral glutamine or a positive lysine reduced the enzymatic activity by more than 80% (Fig. 2G), highlighting the crucial roles of Glu110 in G6PC1 activity.

DiscussionIn this study, we report structures of human G6PC1 in the apo and G6P-bound forms, which is a key enzyme in glucose homeostasis (6). Human G6PC1 is a small integral membrane protein with 357 residues and is embedded in the ER membrane, making large-scale purification and structural determination extremely difficult. By combining protein solubilization in LMNG detergents (32) and ALFA-tag fusion (34), we successfully determined the cryo-EM structure of hG6PC1 at 3.3 Å resolution, which could stimulate structural exploration of other important small membrane proteins by cryo-EM. By introducing the H176A mutation to fully eliminate enzymatic activity, we obtained the hG6PC1–G6P complex structure, revealing a substrate-bound structure in the PAP2 family. The TMD of hG6PC1 is surrounded by several lipid-like densities, one of which is located near the entrance of the ligand binding pocket in both structures (Figs. 1D and 2A), in line with the findings showing that lipids enhance the stability of mG6PC1 but reduce the Pi release rate (32). Structural comparison revealed that G6P binding induces marked conformational rearrangements on the luminal side of G6PC1, facilitating G6P recognition in G6PC1. However, the structural docking, thermostability shift, and Pi release results suggest that purified hG6PC1 can bind to and catalyze the hydrolysis of M6P, F6P, and ATP, although it is not as efficient as hydrolyzing G6P (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Arg83 and Arg170 hold the phosphate group of G6P close to the inner His176, catalyzing the formation of a histidine-phosphate intermediate; subsequently, the outer His119 donates a proton and liberates the glucose moiety (Fig. 4E). Additionally, Glu110 provides a negative charge to balance the positive Arg83 and Arg170 residues in the apo structure, suggesting an important role of Glu110 in regulating G6PC1 function. The charge–relay pair of Asp-His in the C3 motif was proposed to be important for phosphatase; however, we found that Gln180 replaces the Asp of other PAP2 members and forms a hydrogen bond with His176 in G6PC1. Furthermore, the conserved histidine is stabilized by two additional backbone carbonyl oxygen atoms in both EcPgpB and hG6PC1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C), suggesting that the histidine is polarized by a combination of these interacting residues and a negatively charged residue at the end of the C3 motif is not absolutely required for enzymatic activity.Human G6PC1 is vulnerable to pathogenic mutations (Fig. 5), which cause GSD-1a. Structural mapping demonstrated that the mutations are concentrated around the ligand binding pocket and in the TMD core. By probing expression and solution behavior, we showed that most pathogenic mutations dramatically reduce G6PC1 expression and protein stability, thus altering G6PC1 function. Although several mutations in the catalytic pocket, such as K70N, H119L, and R170Q, display comparable expression and stability to those of WT G6PC1, they directly disrupt the catalytic center. Thermostability Shift Assay.To assess the thermostability of G6PC1 with different substrates, we performed protein thermostability analysis via rotary qPCR. Purified G6PC1-H176A proteins were used because they can bind to substrates but cannot hydrolyze them. The thiol-specific fluorochrome 7-Diethylamino-3-(4’- Maleimidylphenyl)-4-Methylcoumarin (CPM, Invitrogen) was used as the fluorescence probe. When the temperature increases, the protein denatures, and CPM binds to the solvent-exposed cysteine. The volume of the reaction system was 25 μL. First, 0.1 mg/mL G6PC1-H176A was added to reaction buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.50), 150 mM NaCl, 0.005% (w/v) LMNG, and 0.0005% (w/v) CHS), with or without G6P, M6P, F6P, or ATP, and the mixture was incubated for 1 h on ice. The reactions were subsequently placed in the qPCR instrument and subjected to a program in which the temperature was increased at a rate of 1 °C/min from 25 °C to 95 °C. The first derivative of the denaturation curve was calculated in GraphPad Prism 8, and the melting temperatures (Tm) of G6PC1-H176A and G6PC1-H176A in complex with G6P, M6P, F6P, or ATP were estimated to be the peak temperature.

Pi Release Measurements.Ten milliliters of cultured HEK293F cells expressing GFP alone, the WT G6PC1, or G6PC1 variants were collected via centrifugation. The cell pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of hydrolysis reaction buffer (50 mM Mes pH 6.5, 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA) supplemented with 5 mM (0.5% w/v) LMNG and 1 mM PMSF and solubilized at 4 °C for 1 h. The experimental data were read using a microplate reader at 850 nm. The total nmol of released Pi was calculated from the 850 nm absorbance value fitted to a standard Pi content–850 nm absorbance curve. The background activity from the GFP-expressing cells was subtracted, and the fluorescence intensity of the supernatant for each G6PC1 variant was measured via a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-7100, Hitachi) and used for expression normalization.
