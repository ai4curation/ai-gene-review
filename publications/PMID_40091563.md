Human genetic variants of the orphan G protein-coupled receptor GPR50 are
suggested risk factors for neuropsychiatric disorders. However, the function of
GPR50 in the central nervous system (CNS) and its link to CNS disorders remain
poorly defined. Here, we generated GPR50 knockout (GPR50-KO) mice and show that
the absence of GPR50 increases neurite outgrowth, cell motility and migration of
isolated neural progenitor cells (NPCs) and hypothalamic radial glial cells
(tanycytes). These observations were phenocopied in NPCs and tanycytes from
wild-type mice treated with neutralizing antibodies the against the prototypical
neurite growth inhibitor Nogo-A. Treatment of NPCs and tanycytes from GPR50-KO
cells with neutralizing antibodies had no further, additive, effect. Inhibition
of neurite growth by GPR50 occurs through activation of the G12/13 protein-RhoA
pathway in a manner similar to, but independent of Nogo-A and its receptors.
Collectively, we show that GPR50 acts as an inhibitor of neurite growth and cell
migration in the brain by activating the G12/13 protein-RhoA pathway.

Introduction

Seven‐transmembrane‐spanning G protein‐coupled receptors (GPCRs) are involved in multiple cellular processes, and implicated in the pathogenesis and control of a broad range of psychiatric disorders [1, 2]. While some ~40% of approved drugs mediate their effects through GPCRs [1, 2, 3], many of the 400 potentially druggable human classes of GPCRs remain to be exploited. This is particularly true for more than 100 orphan GPCRs, for which no endogenous ligand has been identified yet [4, 5, 6].

GPR50 is an orphan GPCRs belonging to the melatonin receptor subfamily and represents 45% sequence homology with melatonin MT1 and MT2 receptors in humans but does not bind melatonin or any other known ligand [7]. The absence of key residues in the 2nd extracellular loop (ECL2) of GPR50, know to be important for melatonin binding in MT1 and MT2, most likely explain the absence of melatonin binding in GPR50 [8, 9]. Its function depends on the heterodimerization with other GPCRs [10], proteolytic cleavage of the C‐terminal domain of GPR50 [11, 12, 13] and possibly on the interaction with ADAM17 (A disintegrin and metalloprotease 17) [14]. Genetic studies indicate an association of GPR50 variants with autism spectrum disorders (ASD), depression, bipolar disorder and seasonal affective disorder [15, 16, 17, 18].

In the adult brain, GPR50 is widely expressed in dorsomedial hypothalamus, the hypothalamic median eminence and amygdaloid complex which controls stress and emotional responses [11, 19, 20]. GPR50 is highly expressed in tanycytes, which are specialized radial glial cells lining the third ventricle including the median eminence, and which are proposed as a local niche of hypothalamic neural stem cells and play a role in neurogenesis [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. GPR50 expression is highly regulated in the hypothalamus with variations depending on the energy status, the season and circadian rhythms [26, 27]. In NS‐1 cells, a subclonal line of PC12 cells derived from rat pheochromocytoma cells, pituitary adenylate cyclase‐activating polypeptide (PACAP) upregulates GPR50 expression [28]. GPR50 is also expressed during embryonic development of the mouse brain from E13 onward with a peak at E18 [20].

The functional role of GPR50 in CNS development is suggested by its capacity to promote self‐renewal and neuronal differentiation of neuronal progenitor cells (NPCs) [29], to modulate neuronal dendrites in primary hippocampal and cortex neurons of newborn mouse pups [30], to be involved in PACAP‐induced neuritogenesis in PC12 and NS‐1 cells [28] and to be negatively regulated by the PHD‐finger protein 3 (PHF3), an important effector of neuronal gene expression [31].

Neurite outgrowth and migration are two important cellular processes involved in brain development, maintenance, plasticity [32, 33, 34] and instrumental for injury‐induced neurogenesis and tissue regeneration [35, 36]. Neuronal migration depends on neurite extension and outgrowth, during which neurons dynamically generate and retract growth cones in search of extracellular guidance cues [37, 38]. Nogo‐A is the major inhibitor of neuritogenesis and neuronal migration by acting on several receptors (NgR) [39], the spingosine‐1‐phosphat receptor 2 (S1PR2) and heparin sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) [40, 41]. RhoA, a small GTPase, is a common downstream intermediate of these receptors and connecting them to neurite outgrowth and neuronal migration. In early neuritogenesis, RhoA activity is typically downregulated to facilitate neurite extension. During later maturation stages, its activation promotes cytoskeletal stabilization and growth cone collapse, refining neuronal connections [42, 43]. Additionally, RhoA is a key regulator of neuronal migration, where it modulates actomyosin contractility and focal adhesion dynamics to direct cellular movement in response to extracellular signals [44, 45].

To decipher the function of GPR50 in the central nervous system we studied the role of GPR50 in neurite growth and cell migration by applying loss‐ and gain‐of‐function in both in vitro and ex vivo systems. We used basic neuronal cell models like primary NPCs and neuroblastoma cells (NG108‐15) for neurite outgrowth and migration while hypothalamic radial glia‐like ependymal cells (tanycytes) were utilized for the study of cellular migration. We show that GPR50 activates the G12/13 protein‐RhoA pathway, thereby negatively regulating neurite growth and cellular migration in a manner similar to, but independent of Nogo‐A and its receptors.

By using a large panel of neuronal cells including primary cell cultures, we show that deletion of GPR50 significantly promotes neurite growth and migration in NPCs and tanycytes derived from GPR50KO mice, while this effect is rescued in human GPR50‐KI mice. The results obtained with NPCs expressing endogenous GPR50 and in transfected NG108‐15 cells indicate that GPR50 inhibited neurite outgrowth. Reports in NS‐1 cells and primary hippocampal and cortex neurons of newborn mouse pups suggested that GPR50 rather promotes neurite outgrowth [28, 30, 55]. The precise reason for these diverging observations is currently unknown but might depend on the expression level of GPR50 and on the cell type or particular developmental stage of the neurons. Indeed, NPCs represent an early developmental stage model (E14) and NG108‐15 cells are neuroblastoma cells whereas hippocampal and cortex neurons were obtained from newborn mouse pups and NS‐1 cells are derived from a pheochromocytoma. In addition, the pathway involved in neurite outgrowth might vary depending on the cell type. It can be Nogo‐A dependent or independent and with and without the involvement of G12/13 proteins [40] and of Gs proteins [28]. It might also require the integration of two distinct phases, a short, tyrosine kinase receptor dependent, and a prolonged Gs protein‐dependent activation at the level of ERK pathway as illustrated in NS‐1 cells.

In our study, we started to address the question of how GPR50 is regulating neurite outgrowth and migration. The results indicate that GPR50, like Nogo‐A, activates RhoA to inhibit neurite outgrowth and migration. However, the pathway by which RhoA is activated seems to be different between these two regulators. Indeed, the activation of RhoA by GPR50 is independent of Nogo‐A as ectopic expression of GPR50 in NG108‐15 cells is sufficient to recapitulate RhoA activation in the absence of Nogo‐A. Consistently, no further effect is observed upon coexpression with Nogo‐A. Additionally, we show that the effect of GPR50 is independent of the activation of the three reported Nogo‐A receptors (NgR, S1PR2 and HSPGs) indicating that GPR50 is not upstream of Nogo‐A in the signaling cascade. Interestingly, GPR50 activates G12/13 proteins that have been previously reported to be upstream of RhoA upon ligand‐induced activation of several GPCRs [49]. Convergence of Nogo‐A and GPR50 effects on RhoA and further downstream levels seem to be possible in tanycytes that express both GPR50 and a functional Nogo‐A/NgR/S1PR2 system. However, the existence of such a convergence is likely to be cell context dependent and unlikely to operate in NS‐1 cells, a model of PACAP‐induced neuritogenesis that is strictly dependent on GPR50 but independent of Nogo‐A [28]. An alternative mechanistic explanation has been provided by Liu et al. [30] proposing that mitochondrial localization of GPR50 is required to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis in developing neurons of newborn mouse pups as GPR50 facilitates the elimination of damaged mitochondria by autophagosomes.

Another level of convergence might exist between GPR50 and melatonin receptors, the latter being reported to also modulate the RhoA/ROCK pathway [56, 57]. As the RhoA pathway activation depends on the maturation stage of neurons [42, 43] and the cellular context as discussed above, the outcome of the potential GPR50 and melatonin receptor convergence is likely to be divers. An additional layer of regulation might exist in cells co‐expressing GPR50 and melatonin receptors as both proteins have been shown to form heteromeric complexes [10].

To conclude, the present data unveil a novel cellular mode of GPR50 signaling, namely constitutive recruitment of the G12/13‐RhoA axis (Figure 7). This action intervenes in its control of neurite outgrowth, motility and migration in a variety of cells and different experimental paradigms. Inasmuch as GPR50 is strongly expressed both developmentally and in the adult brain, these actions provide a molecular foundation for its potential implication in psychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders, and metabolism, roles awaiting further clarification. Interestingly, while the influence of GPR50 upon neurite outgrowth and migration mirrors the actions of Nogo‐A, which likewise acts via RhoA, their effects appear to be exerted independently. It will be interesting to further explore their respective roles in the control of neural plasticity in relation to cerebral development, and the pathogenesis of CNS disorders.

4.6Wound Healing AssayWound healing assay was performed as reported previously [65]. Twenty‐five thousand tanycytes contained in 100 μL of DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) donor calf serum (Invitrogen), 1% (v/v) l‐glutamine (Invitrogen), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin were plated on each well of a silicone insert with a defined cell‐free gap (ibidi, GmbH). Each silicone insert was placed on poly‐l‐lysine coated coverslips. After 6 h incubation at 37°C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2‐95% air, the cells formed two confluent rectangular monolayers on the surface of the coverslip separated by a defined cell‐free gap. Ibidi chambers were then removed, and 1 mL of tanycyte‐defined medium (TDM) consisting of DMEM/F12 (devoid of phenol red; Invitrogen) supplemented with 1% l‐glutamine, 2% penicillin/streptomycin, 5 μg/mL insulin (Sigma), and 100 μM putrescin (Sigma) with or without test substances was added to the culture. The cells were then cultured for 48 h. Pictures were taken at different time lapses, 17, 24, and 48 h. For morphological analysis, the cultures were fixed for 15 min at room temperature by adding 1 mL of 8% paraformaldehyde to the culture medium. Thereafter, the cells were gently rinsed in PBS and incubated in a blocking solution containing 0.3% Triton X‐100% and 4% of normal goat serum for 30 min at room temperature and then in Texas Red‐X phalloidin (1:200) prepared in blocking solution for 20 min at room temperature. After three rinses in PBS, the coverslips were mounted on glass slides using Mowiol (Sigma). The gap area occupied by attached cells was imaged using a Hamamatsu camera attached to a Zeiss fluorescence microscope. To analyze the surface occupied by the cells in the gap, Adobe Photoshop CS3 and a script in R were used. The number of cells was counted by using Adobe Photoshop CS3.
