# The SPL8-YAB3/TCP19 module integrates transcriptional and protein-protein regulatory mechanisms to orchestrate anther development in Arabidopsis.

SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN-LIKE 8 (SPL8) is a plant-specific
transcription factor essential for anther development, but its regulatory
partners and downstream mechanisms remain largely unknown. We identified 26
SPL8-interacting transcription factors by yeast two-hybrid screening and
bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays, with YABBY (YAB) and
TEOSINTE BRANCHED 1/CYCLOIDEA/PCF (TCP) family emerging as the predominant
interactors. Among these, YAB3 and TCP19 were selected for detailed
characterization. Their interactions with SPL8, as well as with each other, were
confirmed in plant nuclei by fluorescence resonance energy transfer-fluorescence
lifetime imaging microscopy (FRET-FLIM). Chromatin immunoprecipitation
sequencing (ChIP-seq), ChIP-qPCR, yeast one-hybrid, and dual-luciferase assays
demonstrated that SPL8 directly binds the regulatory regions of YAB3 and TCP19
and activates their transcription. Dexamethasone-inducible expression assays
further showed that this induction occurs independently of de novo protein
synthesis. Expression analyses revealed overlapping spatial and temporal
patterns of SPL8, YAB3, and TCP19 during early anther development, particularly
in tapetal cells, microspore mother cells, and vascular tissues. Genetic and
cellular analyses of promoter-driven SRDX repression lines showed that SPL8,
YAB3, and TCP19 regulate anther morphogenesis, tapetal differentiation, meiotic
progression, and microspore development. Although individual family members
exhibited functional redundancy, their repression caused severe developmental
defects and male sterility. Together, these findings establish the
SPL8-YAB3/TCP19 module as a multilayered regulatory system in which SPL8
controls anther development through both transcriptional activation of YAB3 and
TCP19 and physical interaction with these proteins, expanding the regulatory
framework of SPL-mediated reproductive development.

INTRODUCTION

The development of functional male gametophytes in flowering plants is essential for sexual reproduction and crop yield. In Arabidopsis thaliana, anther development follows a precisely ordered program that depends on the specification, differentiation, and coordinated development of multiple somatic and reproductive cell lineages (Chang & Ma, 2026; Sanders et al., 1999; Scott et al., 2004). This process is controlled by complex transcriptional regulatory networks, in which several core factors, including SPOROCYTELESS/NOZZLE (SPL/NZZ), EXCESS MICROSPOROCYTES 1/EXTRA SPOROGENOUS CELLS (EMS1/EXS), T
APETUM DETERMINANT 1 (TPD1), SOMATIC EMBRYOGENESIS RECEPTOR KINASE1/2 (SERK1/2), DYSFUNCTIONAL TAPETUM1 (DYT1), DEFECTIVE IN TAPETAL DEVELOPMENT AND FUNCTION 1 (TDF1), and ABORTED MICROSPORES (AMS), regulate key signaling cascades (Canales et al., 2002; Colcombet et al., 2005; Schiefthaler et al., 1999; Sorensen et al., 2003; Verma, 2019; Yang et al., 1999; Zhang et al., 2006; Zhao et al., 2002; Zhu et al., 2008).

In addition to these core regulators, several other gene families—including SBP‐box (also known as SPL), TEOSINTE BRANCHED 1/CYCLOIDEA/PCF (TCP), TGACG‐binding (TGA), ROXY, and YABBY (YAB)—also participate in early anther development (Chen et al., 1999; Li, Tian, et al., 2025; Siegfried et al., 1999; Tao et al., 2024; Unte et al., 2003; Xing et al., 2010; Xing & Zachgo, 2008). Unlike the severe defects observed in spl/nzz single mutants, most members of these families exhibit substantial functional redundancy. Single mutants typically display few or no detectable developmental abnormalities, whereas higher‐order mutants recapitulate phenotypes comparable to those of spl/nzz (Tao et al., 2024; Xing et al., 2010; Yang et al., 1999). Extensive regulatory interactions have been documented among these factors. For example, TCP proteins physically interact with SPL/NZZ to regulate ovule morphogenesis and directly repress transcription of TGA and ROXY family genes during early anther development (Tao et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2015), highlighting the complexity of the transcriptional regulatory network governing reproductive development.

Among these families, the SPL family (formerly known as the SBP‐box family) plays pleiotropic roles in plant growth, development, and responses to environmental stress (Bu et al., 2025; Huijser & Schmid, 2011; Wang et al., 2009). All SPL proteins contain a highly conserved SBP DNA‐binding domain that specifically recognizes the GTAC core cis‐regulatory element (Birkenbihl et al., 2005; Cardon et al., 1999). The Arabidopsis genome encodes 17 SPL proteins, which are phylogenetically divided into two groups. Group I members are not regulated by miR156, whereas most Group II members are post‐transcriptionally repressed by miR156, with SPL8 representing the only known exception within this group (Xing et al., 2010). Functional studies have shown that spl8 mutants exhibit partial male sterility in early‐arising flowers, a phenotype that gradually recovers during subsequent floral development. Specifically, spl8‐1 plants display reduced anther size, fewer anther locules, impaired pollen production, and shortened filaments, indicating that defects in male reproductive organ development underlie the early‐flower sterility phenotype (Unte et al., 2003). Subsequent studies demonstrated that multiple miR156‐targeted SPL genes (SPL2, SPL9, SPL10, SPL11, SPL13, and SPL15) also contribute to anther development. Ectopic expression of MIR156b in the spl8‐1 background suppresses these genes and results in complete male sterility, strongly supporting functional redundancy between SPL8 and the miR156‐targeted SPL genes during anther development (Xing et al., 2010). In addition, SPL8 cooperates with the brassinosteroid signaling effector BES1‐interacting Myc‐like1 (BIM1) to regulate early anther formation (Xing et al., 2013). Despite these advances, the molecular mechanisms by which SPL8 regulates anther development, particularly its downstream targets and interacting partners, remain largely unknown.

The YABBY and TCP transcription factor families represent two additional regulatory groups involved in plant morphogenesis. The six Arabidopsis YABBY genes encode proteins containing an N‐terminal C2C2 zinc finger domain and a conserved C‐terminal YABBY domain. Based on their biological functions, they are classified into vegetative (FILAMENTOUS FLOWER (FIL)/YAB1, YAB2, YAB3, and YAB5) and reproductive (INNER NO OUTER (INO/YAB4) and CRABS CLAW (CRC/YAB6)) subgroups (Bowman & Smyth, 1999; Chen et al., 1999; Sawa et al., 1999; Siegfried et al., 1999; Villanueva et al., 1999). Vegetative YABBY genes are predominantly expressed in the abaxial tissue of lateral organs. Although single mutants exhibit few obvious developmental defects, higher‐order mutants display severe abnormalities in leaf polarity and morphology, demonstrating extensive functional redundancy (Sarojam et al., 2010). Notably, mutation of FIL results in filamentous floral structures, suggesting an additional role in reproductive development (Chen et al., 1999; Sawa et al., 1999). In contrast, the reproductive YABBY genes INO and CRC specifically regulate the formation of the ovule outer integument and carpel development, respectively (Bowman & Smyth, 1999; Villanueva et al., 1999). However, the roles of YABBY genes in early anther development and their crosstalk with other transcription factor families remain largely unexplored.

The TCP family comprises 24 members in Arabidopsis and regulates a wide range of developmental and physiological processes (Manassero et al., 2013; Viola et al., 2023; Viola & Gonzalez, 2023). Similar to the YABBY family, TCP genes exhibit extensive functional redundancy, with pronounced developmental defects becoming apparent only in higher‐order tcp mutants (Wang et al., 2025). Recent studies have uncovered critical roles for miR319‐targeted TCP genes during early anther development. TCP2, TCP3, TCP4, TCP10, and TCP24 promote early anther development by activating the expression of TGA9, TGA10, and ROXY2 (Tao et al., 2024), whereas TCP24 additionally regulates anther dehiscence (Wang et al., 2015). Furthermore, TCP5, a homolog of the same subfamily, has recently been identified as a potential regulator of early anther development (Li, Tian, et al., 2025), further expanding the functional repertoire of TCP transcription factors in reproductive development. Nevertheless, the complete set of TCP family members involved in anther development, as well as their potential coordination with other transcription factor families, remains to be determined.

Although the individual roles of SPL, YABBY, and TCP transcription factor families in anther development have been increasingly recognized, whether and how these families coordinate to regulate this process through direct interactions remains elusive. Given that SPL/NZZ physically interacts with TCP proteins during ovule development and that YABBY genes function in close spatiotemporal coordination during anther morphogenesis (Goldshmidt et al., 2008; Wei et al., 2015), we hypothesized that SPL8 similarly participates in cross‐family regulatory interactions with specific YABBY and TCP proteins to regulate early anther development.

In this study, we identify and characterize a bimodal regulatory pathway underlying SPL8‐dependent anther development. Through biochemical, transcriptional, genetic, and spatiotemporal expression analyses, we define a previously unrecognized regulatory module, the SPL8–YAB3/TCP19 axis, in which SPL8 physically interacts with both YAB3 and TCP19 while directly activating their transcription. This regulatory module establishes a hierarchical transcription factor network that promotes anther morphogenesis, thereby providing new mechanistic insights into anther development and identifying potential molecular targets for crop improvement and hybrid breeding.

DISCUSSION
SPL8 physically interacts with multiple YABBY, TCP, and SPL transcription factorsAlthough transcription factors are traditionally characterized by their DNA‐binding activities, many also function through interactions with other regulatory proteins and multi‐protein complexes. Identifying transcription factor interactors is therefore essential for understanding their molecular mechanisms (Åstrand et al., 2021; Dickey et al., 2026; Gray & Grotewold, 2025; Masiero et al., 2011). Through Y2H screening and targeted BiFC validation, we identified a broad spectrum of SPL8‐associated transcription factors. Among the 26 validated SPL8‐interacting partners, 19 belong to the YABBY, TCP, or SPL families, including four YABBY members, ten TCP members, and five SPL members (Table S1). The enrichment of these three plant‐specific transcription factor families is notable given their established roles in regulating leaf, floral, and anther development (Chen et al., 2014; Nicolas & Cubas, 2016; Sieber et al., 2004; Tao et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2015; Xing et al., 2011). These findings position SPL8 at the center of a transcriptional regulatory hub integrating multiple conserved developmental regulators and suggest that previously unrecognized physical interactions among these families contribute to the coordinated control of anther development.Several features of the SPL8 interaction network are noteworthy. First, the SPL8‐SPL8 interaction observed in BiFC assays raises the possibility that SPL8 may function as a homodimer, a configuration that often enhances DNA‐binding affinity and target recognition in transcription factors (Boer et al., 2014; Jeong et al., 2009; Li, Chen, et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023). Future biochemical studies using purified proteins would help to further define the functional relevance of this self‐interaction. Second, the identification of YAB3 and TCP19 as SPL8 interactors, together with the enrichment of their respective families, suggests that SPL8 may function as a physical bridge between YABBY and TCP transcription factors, facilitating their coordinated assembly into shared transcriptional complexes. Although neither YAB3 nor TCP19 contains a canonical EAR motif (LXLXL/DLNXXP), both interacted with TPL in BiFC assays (Kagale et al., 2010) (Figure S1; Table S2), suggesting that they may participate in a transcriptional repression complex (Plant et al., 2021). A similar mechanism has been reported for SPL/NZZ (Chen et al., 2014; Wei et al., 2015). Third, the absence of detectable interactions between SPL8 and several anticipated candidates, including SPL/NZZ, ROXY1, PI, and AG (Table S2), is equally informative. Although these proteins are well‐established regulators of anther development (Verma, 2019; Xing et al., 2011; Yang et al., 1999), the results suggest that SPL8 functions independently of direct physical association with them, refining current models of the anther transcriptional network (Chang & Ma, 2026; Verma, 2019; Xing et al., 2011).

Inducible expression assays, combined with CHX treatment, demonstrated that SPL8 directly activates transcription of YAB3 and TCP19, placing these genes downstream of SPL8 in the regulatory hierarchy. ChIP‐seq analysis identified multiple SPL8‐binding sites within both loci, highlighting these genomic regions as candidates for further examination. Notably, we observed partial discrepancies between the ChIP‐seq and ChIP‐qPCR results: one genomic fragment originally selected as a negative control, which lacked a prominent ChIP‐seq peak, nevertheless exhibited significant SPL8 enrichment in ChIP‐qPCR assays (Figure 4A–D). These differences likely reflect the distinct resolution and analytical characteristics of the two methods. Specifically, ChIP‐seq peak‐calling algorithms apply genome‐wide statistical thresholds that may exclude weaker but biologically relevant binding events, whereas ChIP‐qPCR quantifies enrichment at predefined loci with greater sensitivity (Landt et al., 2012; Park, 2009). Importantly, the validated binding regions were further supported by Y1H and dual‐luciferase assays, confirming their functional relevance. Together, these complementary lines of evidence suggest that genome‐wide ChIP‐seq profiling alone may not fully capture the complete spectrum of SPL8 occupancy and underscore the value of targeted validation approaches.Interestingly, SPL8 binding within the TCP19 coding sequence (CDS) represents a non‐canonical mode of transcriptional regulation. Although transcription factors most commonly bind promoter‐proximal regions, intragenic binding has been increasingly recognized as a functionally important regulatory mechanism (Pal & Biswas, 2023). For example, SPL3 binds the LFY coding region to regulate its expression, while BZR1 frequently associates with gene bodies to modulate transcriptional elongation (Sun et al., 2010; Yamaguchi et al., 2009; Zhu et al., 2024). The association of SPL8 with the TCP19 CDS is therefore consistent with this emerging paradigm, suggesting that intragenic binding may contribute to transcriptional regulation beyond promoter occupancy. However, whether SPL8 binding to the TCP19 CDS influences transcriptional elongation, mRNA processing or stability, or other regulatory processes remains to be determined.Together, these findings reveal a regulatory circuit in which SPL8 not only physically interacts with YAB3 and TCP19 but also directly controls their transcription. Such dual‐level regulation may facilitate coordinated accumulation of these transcription factors during early anther development and promote the formation of functional regulatory complexes. YAB3 functions as a central regulator of anther morphogenesis. Previous studies have shown that YABBY family members cooperate to establish normal anther morphology, as exemplified by the filamentous and clavate stamens of the fil yab3 double mutant (Siegfried et al., 1999). Although severe vegetative defects in higher‐order YABBY mutants display severe vegetative defects that obscure anther phenotypes (Sarojam et al., 2010; Stahle et al., 2009), the consistent clavate anther defects observed in YAB1/FIL artificial microRNA lines and in pSPL8:YAB3‐SRDX and pYAB3:YAB3‐SRDX repressor lines further demonstrate a specific role of YAB3 in anther morphogenesis (Goldshmidt et al., 2008) (Figure 6H,I; Figure S7e1–f3). Given its prominent abaxial expression in anther primordia (Siegfried et al., 1999) (Figure 5K–N), YAB3 likely contributes to early anther patterning by regulating abaxial cell fate, analogous to its established role in leaf development (Palermo & Dornelas, 2021; Sarojam et al., 2010).Stage‐specific repression of YAB3 further revealed that its functions extend beyond early morphogenesis. SRDX‐mediated repression driven by anther‐specific promoters (DRL1, A9, and REM22) demonstrated that YAB3 is required for tapetum differentiation and microspore development, with the consequent failure of pollen formation. Among these lines, pREM22:YAB3‐SRDX exhibited the most severe phenotype, producing anthers with only two pollen sacs instead of the typical four (Figure S9c,j). This observation suggests that YAB3 may participate not only in anther patterning but also in determining the number of pollen sacs, raising the intriguing possibility that it contributes to an evolutionarily conserved mechanism controlling anther architecture (Åstrand et al., 2021; Romanova et al., 2021). Conversely, excessive YAB3 activity also profoundly altered floral development. As proposed for YAB3, TCP19 may function through interactions with the AP3/PI regulatory pathway, suggesting that TCP19 and YAB3 contribute cooperatively, potentially through shared downstream targets, to regulate floral organ identity.

TCP19 may also contribute to early anther patterning. The occasional reduction in pollen sac number observed in pREM22:TCP19‐SRDX lines resembles phenotypes observed in spl8‐1 and pREM22:YAB3‐SRDX plants (Unte et al., 2003) (Figure S9g–k,u). These shared developmental defects suggest that TCP19, together with YAB3 and SPL8, coordinately regulates early anther morphogenesis, in addition to its roles in maintaining anther identity and promoting meiosis and tapetum function.

SPL8 functions in anther development (Unte et al., 2003; Xing et al., 2010), as further confirmed by the pDRL1:SPL8‐SRDX phenotypes (Figure 7E–H). Interestingly, spl8 mutants exhibit defects in meiotic progression, whereas the SRDX lines undergo relatively normal meiosis but display impaired microspore release from tetrads. These findings suggest that SPL8 has a distinct role in promoting microspore release. The absence of meiotic defects in the pDRL1:SPL8‐SRDX lines may reflect differences in the spatiotemporal expression domains of the DRL1 promoter relative to endogenous SPL8, or it may indicate that the meiotic function of SPL8 is mediated through mechanisms that are not effectively targeted by the SRDX‐mediated repression.

Genetic analyses further showed that the spl8‐1 yab3 tcp19 triple mutant phenocopied spl8‐1 mutant, suggesting that YAB3 and TCP19 function downstream of SPL8. This epistatic relationship is consistent with previous observations of phenotypic convergence between SPL‐deficient and TCP‐deficient mutants. In spl8 mutants, reduced activity of miR156‐targeted SPL members (SPL2/9/10/11/13/15), as well as disruption of miR319‐targeted TCP genes, results in severe male sterility associated with the absence of pollen sacs, suggesting a potential genetic connection between the SPL and TCP regulatory pathways (Tao et al., 2024; Xing et al., 2010). Together, these findings provide functional evidence for a model in which SPL8 acts upstream of YAB3 and TCP19 to coordinate an integrated regulatory module controlling anther development.

Proposed model for SPL8‐mediated regulation of YAB3 and TCP19 during anther development.

This working model illustrates the SPL8‐centered regulatory network. In the active state (A), SPL8 homodimers bind GTAC‐containing cis‐elements in the regulatory regions of YAB3, TCP19, and other potential downstream genes (X), thereby activating their transcription. The resulting accumulation of YAB3, TCP19, and other target proteins promotes normal anther development. As YAB3 and TCP19 accumulate, they initiate a negative feedback loop (B). Individually or cooperatively, these proteins interact with SPL8 and recruit TPL co‐repressors to form transcriptional repressor complexes that suppress the expression of YAB3 and TCP19, thereby reducing protein accumulation. Upon dissociation of the repressor complexes, SPL8 is released to re‐form homodimers and reactivate target gene expression, restoring the active state (A). This reversible negative feedback mechanism fine‐tunes SPL8 activity and maintains the homeostasis of anther developmental gene expression, thereby ensuring proper anther development in A. thaliana.

In this model, the module transitions between two functional states. In State A, SPL8, likely functioning as a homodimer, binds to cis‐regulatory elements of YAB3, TCP19, and other downstream anther‐related genes to activate their transcription. As YAB3 and TCP19 proteins accumulate in the nucleus, they interact individually with SPL8, forming SPL8–YAB3 and SPL8–TCP19 heterodimers, and may also associate with each other to promote the assembly of higher‐order regulatory complexes. These complexes subsequently recruit the transcriptional corepressor TPL, resulting in the formation of repressive complexes.

In State B, these repressive complexes attenuate the expression of YAB3, TCP19, and other SPL8 target genes. Because both YAB3 and TCP19 are transcriptional targets of SPL8, their protein products may participate in feedback regulation by limiting SPL8‐dependent transcription through the formation of SPL8‐containing repressor complexes. This negative‐feedback mechanism provides a means to constrain the duration and magnitude of SPL8‐mediated transcriptional activation.

The dynamic equilibrium between these two states is likely determined by the abundance of YAB3 and TCP19 proteins. When their nuclear levels decrease due to reduced transcription and protein turnover, repressive complexes may dissociate, allowing SPL8 to reform active homodimers and reinitiate the transcription of YAB3, TCP19, and other downstream targets, thereby restoring State A. Such a self‐regulatory circuit may ensure that the expression of these key regulators is maintained within an optimal range, thereby preventing insufficient or excessive transcriptional activity.

Several aspects of this model require further experimental validation. First, the proposed dual‐layer feedback, in which YAB3 and TCP19 independently contribute to repression and may further influence complex assembly through their mutual interaction, could provide robustness and flexibility to the regulatory network. This feature may be particularly important for coordinating the precise temporal and spatial transcriptional changes required during anther development (Gray & Grotewold, 2025). Second, although interaction assays demonstrate that YAB3 and TCP19 can physically associate with SPL8 and with each other, the stoichiometry, composition, and assembly dynamics of the proposed multi‐protein complexes remain unclear. Whether these complexes assemble cooperatively or competitively, and whether their composition changes across developmental stages or cell types, are important questions for further investigation. Third, recruitment of TPL as a corepressor is supported by BiFC evidence showing interactions between YAB3–TPL and TCP19–TPL (Figure S1). Direct biochemical validation of TPL recruitment in native anther tissues, for example through co‐immunoprecipitation using anther extracts or in vitro pull‐down assays using purified proteins, would further strengthen this model.

In summary, the SPL8–YAB3/TCP19 module may function through a self‐balancing negative‐feedback mechanism that fine‐tunes the spatiotemporal expression of YAB3, TCP19, and other downstream anther‐associated genes, thereby ensuring proper anther development in Arabidopsis. This regulatory framework provides a mechanistic explanation for the coordinated functions of these transcription factors and illustrates how transcriptional regulation and protein–protein interactions can be integrated to achieve developmental precision.

The pFRET‐2in1‐CC constructs (pFRET‐2in1‐CC‐TCP19‐mCherry‐SPL8‐GFP, pFRET‐2in1‐CC‐YAB3‐mCherry‐SPL8‐GFP, and pFRET‐2in1‐CC‐YAB3‐mCherry‐TCP19‐GFP) were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation as described for the BiFC assay. In each construct, GFP served as the donor fluorophore and mCherry as the acceptor fluorophore.

FLIM imaging was performed using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope equipped with a time‐correlated single‐photon counting (TCSPC) system. The GFP donor was excited using a 488‐nm pulsed laser, and fluorescence emission was collected between 490 and 530 nm. mCherry fluorescence was detected between 565 and 610 nm following excitation at 561 nm to confirm acceptor expression. Imaging parameters were optimized to minimize spectral bleed‐through between donor and acceptor channels.

Fluorescence lifetime data were acquired using SymPhoTime 64 v2.1 (PicoQuant, Berlin, Germany). Images were collected until sufficient photon counts were obtained for reliable lifetime fitting. Donor fluorescence lifetimes were calculated from fluorescence decay curves, and pseudocolor lifetime maps were generated to visualize spatial variations in fluorescence lifetime. A reduction in the fluorescence lifetime of GFP in the presence of the mCherry‐tagged acceptor, relative to the control (bait‐GFP + free mCherry), was interpreted as evidence of FRET, indicating a direct protein–protein interaction.

Plasmid construction and generation of transgenic plants

All plasmids were constructed using conventional restriction enzyme‐mediated cloning or Gateway BP/LR recombination (File S1). Target CDS, promoter fragments, and other regulatory sequences were amplified from A. thaliana Col‐0 cDNA or genomic DNA. Synthetic DNA fragments were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). All constructs were validated by Sanger sequencing before use.

For protein–protein and protein–DNA interaction assays, the full‐length CDSs of SPL8, YAB3, and TCP19 were individually subcloned into the pGBKT7 and pGADT7 vectors for Y2H assays or into the pAbAi vector for Y1H assays. CRISPR/Cas9 constructs targeting TCP19 were generated by inserting guide RNA sequences into the HBp402 vector, followed by Gateway LR recombination with pDe‐Cas9. SRDX repressor constructs were generated by fusing the target gene's CDS to the SRDX repressive motif under the control of endogenous, ubiquitous, or tissue‐specific promoters.

All recombinant plasmids were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 for either transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves or stable transformation of Arabidopsis Col‐0 or spl8‐1 mutants using the floral dip method. Transgenic plants were screened based on Basta resistance or Fast Red fluorescence (Shimada et al., 2010), and positive lines were further confirmed by PCR.
