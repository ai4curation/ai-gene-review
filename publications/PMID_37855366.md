# G6PC1 and G6PC2 influence G6P flux but not HSD11B1 activity.

G6PC1 and G6PC2 influence G6P flux but not HSD11B1 activity.

Author information:

In the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) lumen, glucose-6-phosphatase catalytic subunit
1 and 2 (G6PC1; G6PC2) hydrolyze glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) to glucose and
inorganic phosphate whereas hexose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (H6PD) hydrolyzes
G6P to 6-phosphogluconate (6PG) in a reaction that generates NADPH.
11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 1 (HSD11B1) utilizes this NADPH to convert
inactive cortisone to cortisol. HSD11B1 inhibitors improve insulin sensitivity
whereas G6PC inhibitors are predicted to lower fasting blood glucose (FBG). This
study investigated whether G6PC1 and G6PC2 influence G6P flux through H6PD and
vice versa. Using a novel transcriptional assay that utilizes separate fusion
genes to quantitate glucocorticoid and glucose signaling, we show that
overexpression of H6PD and HSD11B1 in the islet-derived 832/13 cell line
activated glucocorticoid-stimulated fusion gene expression. Overexpression of
HSD11B1 blunted glucose-stimulated fusion gene expression independently of
altered G6P flux. While overexpression of G6PC1 and G6PC2 blunted
glucose-stimulated fusion gene expression, it had minimal effect on
glucocorticoid-stimulated fusion gene expression. In the liver-derived HepG2
cell line, overexpression of H6PD and HSD11B1 activated
glucocorticoid-stimulated fusion gene expression but overexpression of G6PC1 and
G6PC2 had no effect. In rodents, HSD11B1 converts 11-dehydrocorticosterone
(11-DHC) to corticosterone. Studies in wild-type and G6pc2 knockout mice treated
with 11-DHC for 5 weeks reveal metabolic changes unaffected by the absence of
G6PC2. These data suggest that HSD11B1 activity is not significantly affected by
the presence or absence of G6PC1 or G6PC2. As such, G6PC1 and G6PC2 inhibitors
are predicted to have beneficial effects by reducing FBG without causing a
deleterious increase in glucocorticoid signaling.

Glucose strongly activated the rat G6pc1 promoter with the induction still increasing at 30 mM glucose (Fig. 2B). These results suggest that the kinetics of G6P signaling through the pathways leading to activation of the glucocorticoid receptor and ChREBP/CREB are distinct (Fig. 2B).

To investigate the effect of G6PC overexpression on cortisone-stimulated fusion gene expression we utilized a luciferase fusion gene containing a multimerized GRE ligated to the thymidine kinase (TK) promoter that is markedly stimulated by glucocorticoids (Vander Kooi et al. 2005) (Fig. 1).In the presence of 30 mM glucose but absence of overexpressed H6PD and HSD11B1, cortisone treatment did not induce expression of the glucocorticoid-responsive GRE-TK-luciferase fusion gene (Fig. 6A). Overexpression of H6PD alone did not enhance cortisone-stimulated fusion gene expression (Fig. 6A). However, overexpression of HSD11B1 markedly enhanced cortisone-stimulated fusion gene expression, which implies that H6PD is endogenously expressed in HepG2 cells. This induction was further increased by co-expression of H6PD (Fig. 6A). Neither overexpression of G6PC1 nor G6PC2 reduced cortisone-stimulated GRE-TK-luciferase fusion gene expression (Fig. 6B). This result suggests that, as in 832/13 cells, the kinetics of G6P hydrolysis in HepG2 cells by G6PC1 and G6PC2 are not sufficient to strongly influence G6P flux through the H6PD pathway. We were unable to determine whether overexpression of G6PC1/G6PC2 or H6PD/HSD11B1 affect glucose signaling to the rat G6pc1 promoter (Fig. 1) because this pathway is inactive in HepG2 cells (data not shown), presumably due to the replacement of glucokinase by the low Km hexokinase 2 in this cell line (DeWaal, et al. 2018). However, we confirmed that overexpression of G6PC1 does increase glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase) activity in HepG2 cells (Fig. 6C), indicating that this does not explain why overexpression of G6PC1 fails to reduce cortisone-stimulated GRE-TK-luciferase fusion gene expression (Fig. 6B).

DiscussionWhile G6PC1 and G6PC2 share the same substrate with H6PD (Fig. 1), our data suggest that neither G6PC1 nor G6PC2 markedly influence G6P flux through H6PD and, therefore, do not modulate HSD11B1 activity. We speculate that these observations reflect a much lower Km of H6PD for G6P (0.4 μM) (Zhang, et al. 2009) relative to the higher Kms observed for G6PC1 (2.5 mM) and G6PC2 (0.45 mM) (Petrolonis, et al. 2004). These data suggest that inhibitors of G6PC1 and/or G6PC2 used to lower FBG will not have deleterious effects on glucocorticoid generation in liver or beta cells. This is important because glucocorticoids are thought to contribute to the increased hepatic glucose production characteristic of type 1 and type 2 diabetes (Kuo, et al. 2015) and have complex positive and negative effects on beta cell function (Rafacho, et al. 2014). Our results contrast with data suggesting that SLC37A4 preferentially delivers G6P to G6PC1 such that G6PC1 does not share G6P equally with H6PD (Wang, et al. 2016). However, those data were based on kinetic modeling of G6P flux rather than the intact cell approach taken here. Interestingly, previous studies have shown markedly reduced HSD11B1 protein in liver from G6pc1 KO mice, a model for glycogen storage disease (GSD) type 1a (Walker, et al. 2007). However, this reduction is presumably secondary to the profound changes in liver metabolism associated with GSD type 1a (Cho, et al. 2018).Glucocorticoids induce both G6pc2 and Slc37a4 expression (Boortz et al. 2016c; Hiraiwa and Chou 2001) with GREs identified in both the proximal G6pc2 (Boortz et al. 2016c) and Slc37a4 (Hiraiwa and Chou 2001) promoters. In C57BL/6J mice this GRE is mutated though glucocorticoids still induce G6pc2 expression through another, unknown mechanism (Boortz et al. 2016c). In our studies the induction of both G6pc2 and Slc37a4 expression by 11-DHC served as a positive control to confirm increased pancreatic glucocorticoid signaling. This observation would appear to support the existence of H6PD/HSD11B1 in islets but cannot address the controversial issue of expression in beta cells (see Introduction) since corticosterone generated in one islet cell type might diffuse and affect gene expression in neighboring cells.The observation that, in the absence of cortisone, overexpression of HSD11B1 blunted glucose-stimulated G6pc1-luciferase fusion gene expression (Fig. 4A) is highly unlikely to represent an action of HSD11B1 on G6P flux because, while overexpression of H6PD did enhance the stimulatory effect of HSD11B1 on cortisone-signaling (Fig. 2), it did not enhance the inhibitory effect of HSD11B1 on glucose-signaling (Fig. 4A). In addition, the effect is specific for the rat G6pc1 promoter (Fig. 4C). Due to the indirect nature of the assay and the fact that multiple transcription factors regulate G6pc1 promoter activity (Hutton and O’Brien 2009), this raised the interesting possibility that this action of HSD11B1 was not directly mediated through inhibition of the glucose-signaling pathway. For example, HSD11B1 has been shown to modulate the abundance of oxysterols (Beck, et al. 2019; Hult, et al. 2004; Schweizer, et al. 2004), known activators of the retinoid-related orphan receptor γ (RORγ) (Beck et al. 2019), a transcription factor that regulates G6PC1 promoter activity (Chopra, et al. 2008; Wang, et al. 2010). However, the fact that the repressive effect of HSD11B1 overexpression was also observed with catalytically impaired HSD11B1 (Fig. 4E) would appear to rule out this possibility. The observation that overexpression of HSD11B1 also partially repressed expression of the internal SV40-Renilla luciferase transfection control (data not shown) suggested that it may be activating an ER stress response, leading to a repression of the action of glucose on G6pc1 promoter activity (Fig. 4A), without affecting the ability of HSD11B1 to regulate cortisone-simulated gene expression (Fig. 4D & F). While ER stress has been shown to increase basal G6pc1 promoter activity (Wang, et al. 2006a) we would speculate that, in the context of glucose-stimulation of G6pc1 promoter activity, ER stress may have a repressive effect. Regardless of the mechanism of this repressive effect of HSD11B1 overexpression, which is observed in the absence of cortisone, in vivo, in the presence of cortisone, an increase in HSD11B1 activity is likely to lead to a stimulation of endogenous G6PC1 expression either through RORγ (Chopra et al. 2008; Wang et al. 2010) or the glucocorticoid receptor (Vander Kooi et al. 2005).
