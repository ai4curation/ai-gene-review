Solute carriers (SLCs) regulate cellular and organismal metabolism by
transporting small molecules and ions across membranes, yet the physiological
substrates of ∼20% remain elusive. To address this, we developed a
machine-learning platform to predict gene-metabolite associations. This approach
identifies UNC93A and SLC45A4 as candidate plasma membrane transporters for
acetylglucosamine and polyamines, respectively. Additionally, we uncover
SLC25A45 as a mitochondrial transporter linked to serum levels of methylated
basic amino acids, products of protein catabolism. Mechanistically, SLC25A45 is
necessary for the mitochondrial import of methylated basic amino acids,
including ADMA and TML, the latter serving as a precursor for carnitine
synthesis. In line with this observation, SLC25A45 loss impairs carnitine
synthesis and blunts upregulation of carnitine-containing metabolites under
fasted conditions. By facilitating mitochondrial TML import, SLC25A45 connects
protein catabolism to carnitine production, sustaining β-oxidation during
fasting. Altogether, our study identifies putative substrates for three SLCs and
provides a resource for transporter deorphanization.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONMachine learning predicts the function of metabolic genesTo fully leverage biochemical knowledge, we developed a supervised machine learning approach trained on experimentally validated gene-metabolite pairs to predict novel associations. This model assigns a probability score to each gene-metabolite interaction, providing a systematic framework for prioritizing candidate transporters and enzymes. A key advantage of this approach is its automated, unbiased feature weighing, followed by ranking associations based on probabilistic scoring as opposed to conventional sequential, feature-based statistical cutoffs. Building on our previous success with transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS)4,7 and GWAS in predicting physiological substrates of metabolic enzymes and transporters, we engineered features based on the METSIM8 and CLSA9 plasma metabolome GWAS datasets. Additionally, we incorporated gene-related properties, such as CADD pathogenicity scores10, as well as biochemical and functional similarity of metabolites to further enhance prediction accuracy and biological relevance. Following the feature engineering, we trained and fine-tuned machine learning models on a balanced dataset of 1,530 pairs that include known and randomly sampled gene-metabolite associations. The performance was assessed by 5-fold cross-validation and area under the curve (AUC) as a metric for the precision-recall balance (Figure 1A). Among the models tested, random forest (RF) demonstrated a marginally superior performance compared to gradient boosting (GB) and substantially outperformed neural networks and support vector machines (Figure 1B-C, Supplementary Figure 1A-B). Of note, RF utilized all 17 features with equal weights (Supplementary Figure 1C).We, therefore, employed RF to compute probabilistic associations across 2,426,064 gene-metabolite pairs. Consistent with the expectation that most associations are not biologically relevant, the distribution of probabilistic scores was skewed toward lower values (Figure 1D-E). Remarkably, given that we did not explicitly incorporate gene function into the model, the predicted probability scores distribution strongly correlated with known biological functions, particularly for solute carrier (SLC) transporters (Figure 1F). Accordingly, the pairs involving the metabolic genes were specifically enriched among the associations with the higher probability scores (Figure 1G). The entries with SLCs followed the same trend with respect to both all (Figure 1H) and associations with metabolic genes (Figure 1I). Building upon the robustness and biological relevance of machine learning predictions, we prioritized 430 top-scoring associations involving 160 SLCs (Figure 1J, Supplementary Figure 1D). These associations form a network with well-defined and biochemically meaningful Louvain communities (Figure 1K). Among the prioritized associations, we found supporting evidence in the literature for 36 out of 160 SLCs with established functions (Figure 2A). For instance, we identify SLC7A6, a cationic amino acid transporter11-13, associated with lysine metabolites. Additionally, our analysis links a lysophospholipid transporter MFSD2A14 to choline, phosphoethanolamine, oleate and stearate. Altogether, these results indicate that our model captures bona fide biological relationships between genes and metabolites.Substrate assignment for orphan solute carriersGiven our success in recapitulating known biology, we next sought to utilize this approach for uncovering novel substrates for transporters. For example, our analysis links the sugar transporter SLC45A4 to polyamine species that have not been recognized as substrates and cannot be explained by the known sugar transport (Figure 2A). To experimentally validate this association, we generated HEK293T SLC45A4-knockout cells expressing SLC45A4 complementary DNA (cDNA) or a vector control (Supplementary Figure 2A-C) and conducted a polar metabolite profiling by using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Consistent with the machine learning predictions, acetylspermidine levels were depleted by nearly 4-fold, while gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) accumulated by ~5.5 fold upon SLC45A4 expression (Figure 2B), indicating a role for SLC45A4 in controlling the intracellular levels of polyamine species. Of note, a possible explanation for GABA accumulation is an extracellular conversion of putrescine to 4-aminobutanatal (ABAL), which is imported back into the cells for GABA synthesis (Supplementary Figure 2D).15 Indeed, isotope labeled tracing with d6-spermidine and [13C4]putrescine showed a depletion of downstream metabolites, d6-acetylspermidine and [13C4]acetylputrescine upon SLC45A4 expression, respectively (Figure 2C-D, Supplementary Figure 2E-F). Furthermore, SLC45A4 expression resulted in elevated levels of [13C4]acetylputrescine in the media (Supplementary Figure 2G). In line with these findings, recent works identified SLC45A4 as a plasma membrane polyamine transporter by conducting a series of structural and biochemical assays.16,17We next rendered the list of associations with 28 orphan SLCs (Figure 2E) and focused on the pleiotropic genes (Supplementary Figure 2H). Among those, we identified two genes outside of the paralogous loci: SLC25A45 and UNC93A linked to methylated basic amino acids, and acetylglucosamine species, respectively (Supplementary Figure 2H). In serum data, UNC93A locus was significantly associated with two acetylglucosamine species: acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc)/acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) and GlcNAc-Asn (acetylglucosamine-asparagine) (Figure 2F-G).8,9 GlcNAc is involved in the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway, serving as a key nutrient sensor that regulates signaling and protein function.18 GlcNAc-Asn is a byproduct of glycoprotein degradation, released during lysosomal breakdown of glycoproteins.19 It is subsequently processed by specialized enzymes to recycle GlcNAc for cellular use.19 Furthermore, GlcNAc is a building block of hyaluronic acid, a critical glycosaminoglycan for skin health.20,21 Thus, in line with these known functions of GlcNAc, UNC93A has a restricted expression to the skin and liver22 with little to no expression in other tissues (Supplementary Figure 2I). Therefore, to study its function, we overexpressed UNC93A cDNA in HEK293T cells (Supplementary Figure 2J-K) and performed metabolite profiling. Consistent with its predicted localization, immunofluorescence analysis confirmed UNC93A localized at the plasma membrane (Supplementary Figure 2L). Whereas most metabolites were not substantially altered by UNC93A overexpression, in accordance with the GWAS data, GlcNAc was depleted by over 8-fold upon UNC93A expression (Figure 2H), raising the possibility that UNC93A can mediate cellular export of acetylglucosamine or relevant metabolites. To test this possibility, we conducted isotope labeled [13C6,15N]glucosamine tracing (Figure 2I). We observed that the level of [13C6,15N]GlcNAc was indeed substantially decreased in UNC93A-overexpressing cells (Figure 2J), without impacting those of [13C6,15N]UDP-GlcNAc and [13C6,15N]glucosamine (Supplementary Figure 2M-N). To further confirm this, we leveraged the characteristic property of SLCs as bidirectional transporters facilitating concentration gradient-dependent movement across membranes and measured uptake of [13C6,15N]GlcNAc and its conversion into UDP-GlcNAc, a downstream product (Figure 2K). Strikingly, cells overexpressing UNC93A displayed a 3.5-fold and 10000-fold increase in [13C6,15N]GlcNAc and [13C6,15N]UDP-GlcNAc, respectively (Figure 2L, Supplementary Figure 2O). We also tested the uptake of GlcNAc-Asn, another metabolite associated with UNC93A variants and found that UNC93A overexpression similarly resulted in a 10-fold accumulation (Supplementary Figure 2P-Q). Altogether, our data suggest that UNC93A mediates the transport of acetylated glucosamine species across the plasma membrane.SLC25A45 is required for carnitine biosynthesis in vivoBuilding on the success of our machine learning approach for SLC function discovery, we focused on another association predicted by our platform: SLC25A45 and methylated basic amino acids including TML, ADMA, and dimethyllysine (DML). Methylated amino acids are degradation products of proteins with posttranslational modifications and play important roles in metabolism and physiology. For example, ADMA acts as an endogenous inhibitor of nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, influencing cardiovascular health by modulating vascular tone.23 TML is a precursor in the biosynthesis of carnitine, a critical metabolite in mitochondrial long-chain fatty acids β-oxidation. Although carnitine is predominantly acquired through diet, approximately 25% is synthesized endogenously24, highlighting the physiological importance of understanding this metabolic route. Indeed, in the METSIM8 plasma GWAS, one of the most significant associations at the SLC25A45 locus were TML and deoxycarnitine, a downstream metabolic product of TML and precursor in carnitine synthesis (Figure 3A).To investigate these predicted associations in vivo, we first generated a whole-body Slc25a45-knockout mouse model using CRISPR (Supplementary Figure 3A-B) and performed serum metabolite profiling compared to littermate wild-type controls (Figure 3B). Of note, to minimize exogenous carnitine intake prior to the experiment, mice were fed a vegetarian chow for two weeks prior to the experiment, which did not affect body weight differences between genotypes (Supplementary Figure 3C). Consistent with the GWAS data, we observed significantly altered levels of monomethyllysine (MML) and DML in the serum of Slc25a45-knockout mice. Whereas most of carnitine biosynthesis pathway occurs in many organs, the terminal BBOX1-catalyzed reaction is predominantly restricted to liver and kidney.22 Thus, given the role of these organs in carnitine synthesis and high expression of SLC25A4525 (Supplementary Figure 3D), we examined how liver and kidney metabolism is affected by SLC25A45 loss. Consistent with serum profiles, we observed significant changes in the levels of methylated amino acids. Additionally, deoxycarnitine, a downstream product of TML in the carnitine biosynthesis pathway, was reduced supporting the findings from human GWAS serum data (Figure 3D, Supplementary Figure 4A). These results strongly suggest a role for SLC25A45 in carnitine biosynthesis in vivo.To formally test this possibility, we performed a d9-TML tracing into carnitine metabolites in Slc25a45 knockout and wild-type mice via tail vein injection, followed by serum and liver collection (Figure 3E-F). While we did not observe a difference in the labeled fraction of serum d9-TML (Supplementary Figure 4B), the incorporation of deuterium atoms into carnitine species were decreased up to 35-fold in Slc25a45-knockout mice (Figure 3G, Supplementary Figure 4C-D). Consistently, the [M+9] carnitine abundance was also reduced in the liver and kidneys harvested from Slc25a45-knockout mice (Figure 3H, Supplementary Figure 4E). Notably, the labeled fraction of d9-TML was elevated in the liver of Slc25a45-knockout mice (Supplementary Figure 4F), possibly indicating that SLC25A45 regulates a major TML consumption pathway. Altogether, these findings show that SLC25A45 is required for carnitine biosynthesis and controls a critical route of TML utilization.SLC25A45 supports carnitine synthesis under fasting conditionsDuring prolonged fasting and starvation, organisms rely on carnitine-dependent mitochondrial β-oxidation of fatty acids for energy. Acetylcarnitine, the most abundant carnitine derivative, facilitates this process by shuttling acetyl groups across the mitochondrial membrane and buffering acetyl-CoA availability.26 In line with this, d9-TML tracing in mice displayed a higher serum [M+9] acetylcarnitine/carnitine ratio under fasting conditions compared to ad libitum feeding in mice (Supplementary Figure 4G-H). Given the requirement of SLC25A45 for carnitine synthesis, we hypothesized that its function may become critical during fasting, when carnitine intake is limited, and demand is elevated. To test this, we subjected Slc25a45-knockout and wild-type mice to either a 24-hour fast or ad libitum feeding, followed by serum and liver collection for polar and lipidomic profiling (Figure 4A). Consistent with the increased utilization of fatty acid oxidation upon starvation27, we observed a dramatic increase of serum 3-hydroxybutyrate (3-HB) levels upon fasting (Supplementary Figure 4I). Additionally, the most altered serum metabolite between genotypes in response to fasting was acetylcarnitine (Supplementary Figure 4J-M). Slc25a45-knockout mice failed to mount the fasting-induced increase in acetylcarnitine, indicative of potential carnitine limitation (Figure 4B). Consistently, livers from fasted Slc25a45-knockout mice showed reduced levels of carnitine and propionylcarnitine (Supplementary Figure 4N-P). Lipidomic analysis further revealed significant reductions in several mid-to-long chain acylcarnitines with additional species trending lower in the livers from Slc25a45-knockout mice (Figure 4C, Supplementary Figure 4Q). Given the well-established transcriptional response to fasting, such as the upregulation of β-oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation pathways28,29, we next asked whether loss of Slc25a45 impairs this adaptive program. We, therefore, used gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to identify β-oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation as signatures enriched in genes with differential fasting responses between livers from wild-type and Slc25a45-knockout mice. Targeted analysis of these pathways showed that Slc25a45 loss attenuated the fasting-induced transcriptional upregulation of key genes involved in fatty acid oxidation and electron transport chain function (Figure 4D-F). This raises the possibility that SLC25A45 is essential for the induction of transcriptional responses upon fasting. Consistent with this, the previous work reported liver-specific Cpt1a-knockout mice that functionally mimic carnitine deficiency also fail to mount acetyl-carnitine levels during fasting and show a blunted transcriptional fasting response.30 Altogether, our data suggest a critical role for SLC25A45 in sustaining carnitine synthesis and fatty acid oxidation during fasting.SLC25A45 mediates mitochondrial methylated amino acid importTo investigate the mechanism by which SLC25A45 impacts carnitine metabolism, we next examined its role in mitochondrial metabolism given its mitochondrial localization (Figure 5A). We crossed Slc25a45-knockout mice with the MITO-Tag model31, which enables rapid immunopurification of mitochondria from tissues for multiomic analysis (Figure 5B). Using this system, we isolated mitochondria from liver and kidney for the metabolite profiling and confirmed enrichment of mitochondrial proteins and metabolites with minimal contamination from other organelles (Supplementary Figure 5A-D). LC-MS analysis revealed that mitochondria from Slc25a45−/− mice exhibited markedly reduced levels of TML and ADMA along with a significant decrease in saccharopine, a key intermediate in lysine degradation (Figure 5C-D). These results suggest that SLC25A45 regulates mitochondrial levels of methylated basic amino acids.To further confirm these results, we generated SLC25A45 knockout and cDNA complemented HeLa cells expressing a Mito-Tag construct (3×HA–OMP25–mCherry)32 (Supplementary Figure 6A), and profiled the polar metabolome of immunopurified mitochondria by LC–MS. Consistent with in vivo experiments, SLC25A45-knockout cells showed a 9-fold depletion in mitochondrial TML levels and a 4-fold reduction in ADMA compared to controls (Figure 5E). Notably, SLC25A45 loss did not substantially affect whole cell levels of these metabolites (Supplementary Figure 6B). We observed similar results in SLC25A45-knockout Jurkat cells as well as HEK293T and HeLa clonal knockouts complemented with SLC25A45 cDNA (Figure 5F, Supplementary Figure 6C-E). To directly assess the impact of SLC25A45-mediated TML import on carnitine production, we engineered HEK293T cells to express the carnitine biosynthetic enzyme BBOX1 (Supplementary Figure 6F). Consistent with in vivo tracing experiments, SLC25A45-knockout cells exhibited a 3-fold decrease in the incorporation of d9-TML into carnitine (Supplementary Figure 6G). These data raise the possibility that SLC25A45 may mediate mitochondrial import of methylated basic amino acids such as TML. To test this, we performed a mitochondrial uptake assay using isotope-labeled d9-TML and d7-ADMA in mitochondria isolated from control and SLC25A45-knockout cells (Figure 5G). Mitochondria immunopurified from SLC25A45-knockout cells exhibited a > 50-fold reduction in uptake of both substrates compared to control cells (Figure 5H-I) confirming a role for SLC25A45 in the mitochondrial import of methylated basic amino acids. To further assess substrate specificity, we conducted competitive uptake experiments with labeled d9-TML in the presence of excess unlabeled substrates (Supplementary Figure 6H). ADMA effectively competed with TML, followed by moderate and low competition from arginine and lysine, respectively (Supplementary Figure 6I). Supporting arginine as a potential substrate, tracing with [1,2-13C2]arginine revealed decreased levels of its downstream product, [M+2] ornithine, in mitochondria from SLC25A45-knockout cells (Supplementary Figure 6J-K). This data is also consistent with a depletion of hepatic guanidinoacetate, a mitochondrial intermediate of arginine metabolism and creatine biosynthesis33, in Slc25a45-knockout mice upon fasting (Supplementary Figure 6L).To gain a better insight into the transport mechanism, we sought to identify the residues necessary for the import of methylated amino acids. We generated a homology model of SLC25A45 by aligning a predicted AlphaFold34 model of SLC25A45 and the bovine ADP/ATP transporter35 structure. This analysis pinpointed two potential substrate-binding residues D121 and K124 which are conserved across multiple species (Figure 5J). While mutating these amino acids to alanine did not alter localization or expression (Supplementary Figure 6E, Supplementary Figure 6M-N), expression of mutant cDNAs failed to restore levels of mitochondrial TML (Figure 5K) and ADMA in SLC25A45 knockout cells, indicating the critical role of these residues in SLC25A45-mediated import (Figure 5L). Of note, we also tested the GWAS missense variant (rs34400381), which results in an R283C substitution in SLC25A45 and is associated with serum levels of methylated basic amino acids. Consistent with the GWAS findings, mutating R283 to cysteine, but not alanine, altered mitochondrial trimethyllysine levels, confirming the physiological relevance of this variant (Supplementary Figure 6O). Collectively, our data strongly indicate that SLC25A45 mediates the mitochondrial import of methylated basic amino acid and supports carnitine synthesis pathway.

Remarkably, given that we did not explicitly incorporate gene function into the model, the predicted probability scores distribution strongly correlated with known biological functions, particularly for solute carrier (SLC) transporters (Figure 1F). Accordingly, the pairs involving the metabolic genes were specifically enriched among the associations with the higher probability scores (Figure 1G). The entries with SLCs followed the same trend with respect to both all (Figure 1H) and associations with metabolic genes (Figure 1I). Building upon the robustness and biological relevance of machine learning predictions, we prioritized 430 top-scoring associations involving 160 SLCs (Figure 1J, Supplementary Figure 1D). These associations form a network with well-defined and biochemically meaningful Louvain communities (Figure 1K). Among the prioritized associations, we found supporting evidence in the literature for 36 out of 160 SLCs with established functions (Figure 2A). For instance, we identify SLC7A6, a cationic amino acid transporter11-13, associated with lysine metabolites. Additionally, our analysis links a lysophospholipid transporter MFSD2A14 to choline, phosphoethanolamine, oleate and stearate. Altogether, these results indicate that our model captures bona fide biological relationships between genes and metabolites.

Substrate assignment for orphan solute carriersGiven our success in recapitulating known biology, we next sought to utilize this approach for uncovering novel substrates for transporters. For example, our analysis links the sugar transporter SLC45A4 to polyamine species that have not been recognized as substrates and cannot be explained by the known sugar transport (Figure 2A). To experimentally validate this association, we generated HEK293T SLC45A4-knockout cells expressing SLC45A4 complementary DNA (cDNA) or a vector control (Supplementary Figure 2A-C) and conducted a polar metabolite profiling by using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Consistent with the machine learning predictions, acetylspermidine levels were depleted by nearly 4-fold, while gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) accumulated by ~5.5 fold upon SLC45A4 expression (Figure 2B), indicating a role for SLC45A4 in controlling the intracellular levels of polyamine species. Of note, a possible explanation for GABA accumulation is an extracellular conversion of putrescine to 4-aminobutanatal (ABAL), which is imported back into the cells for GABA synthesis (Supplementary Figure 2D).15 Indeed, isotope labeled tracing with d6-spermidine and [13C4]putrescine showed a depletion of downstream metabolites, d6-acetylspermidine and [13C4]acetylputrescine upon SLC45A4 expression, respectively (Figure 2C-D, Supplementary Figure 2E-F). Furthermore, SLC45A4 expression resulted in elevated levels of [13C4]acetylputrescine in the media (Supplementary Figure 2G). Among those, we identified two genes outside of the paralogous loci: SLC25A45 and UNC93A linked to methylated basic amino acids, and acetylglucosamine species, respectively (Supplementary Figure 2H). In serum data, UNC93A locus was significantly associated with two acetylglucosamine species: acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc)/acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) and GlcNAc-Asn (acetylglucosamine-asparagine) (Figure 2F-G).8,9 GlcNAc is involved in the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway, serving as a key nutrient sensor that regulates signaling and protein function.18 GlcNAc-Asn is a byproduct of glycoprotein degradation, released during lysosomal breakdown of glycoproteins.19 It is subsequently processed by specialized enzymes to recycle GlcNAc for cellular use.19 Furthermore, GlcNAc is a building block of hyaluronic acid, a critical glycosaminoglycan for skin health.20,21 Thus, in line with these known functions of GlcNAc, UNC93A has a restricted expression to the skin and liver22 with little to no expression in other tissues (Supplementary Figure 2I). Therefore, to study its function, we overexpressed UNC93A cDNA in HEK293T cells (Supplementary Figure 2J-K) and performed metabolite profiling. Consistent with its predicted localization, immunofluorescence analysis confirmed UNC93A localized at the plasma membrane (Supplementary Figure 2L). Whereas most metabolites were not substantially altered by UNC93A overexpression, in accordance with the GWAS data, GlcNAc was depleted by over 8-fold upon UNC93A expression (Figure 2H), raising the possibility that UNC93A can mediate cellular export of acetylglucosamine or relevant metabolites. To test this possibility, we conducted isotope labeled [13C6,15N]glucosamine tracing (Figure 2I). To further confirm this, we leveraged the characteristic property of SLCs as bidirectional transporters facilitating concentration gradient-dependent movement across membranes and measured uptake of [13C6,15N]GlcNAc and its conversion into UDP-GlcNAc, a downstream product (Figure 2K). Strikingly, cells overexpressing UNC93A displayed a 3.5-fold and 10000-fold increase in [13C6,15N]GlcNAc and [13C6,15N]UDP-GlcNAc, respectively (Figure 2L, Supplementary Figure 2O). We also tested the uptake of GlcNAc-Asn, another metabolite associated with UNC93A variants and found that UNC93A overexpression similarly resulted in a 10-fold accumulation (Supplementary Figure 2P-Q). Altogether, our data suggest that UNC93A mediates the transport of acetylated glucosamine species across the plasma membrane.

SLC25A45 supports carnitine synthesis under fasting conditionsDuring prolonged fasting and starvation, organisms rely on carnitine-dependent mitochondrial β-oxidation of fatty acids for energy. Acetylcarnitine, the most abundant carnitine derivative, facilitates this process by shuttling acetyl groups across the mitochondrial membrane and buffering acetyl-CoA availability.26 In line with this, d9-TML tracing in mice displayed a higher serum [M+9] acetylcarnitine/carnitine ratio under fasting conditions compared to ad libitum feeding in mice (Supplementary Figure 4G-H). Given the requirement of SLC25A45 for carnitine synthesis, we hypothesized that its function may become critical during fasting, when carnitine intake is limited, and demand is elevated. To test this, we subjected Slc25a45-knockout and wild-type mice to either a 24-hour fast or ad libitum feeding, followed by serum and liver collection for polar and lipidomic profiling (Figure 4A). Consistent with the increased utilization of fatty acid oxidation upon starvation27, we observed a dramatic increase of serum 3-hydroxybutyrate (3-HB) levels upon fasting (Supplementary Figure 4I). Additionally, the most altered serum metabolite between genotypes in response to fasting was acetylcarnitine (Supplementary Figure 4J-M). Slc25a45-knockout mice failed to mount the fasting-induced increase in acetylcarnitine, indicative of potential carnitine limitation (Figure 4B). Consistently, livers from fasted Slc25a45-knockout mice showed reduced levels of carnitine and propionylcarnitine (Supplementary Figure 4N-P). Lipidomic analysis further revealed significant reductions in several mid-to-long chain acylcarnitines with additional species trending lower in the livers from Slc25a45-knockout mice (Figure 4C, Supplementary Figure 4Q). Given the well-established transcriptional response to fasting, such as the upregulation of β-oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation pathways28,29, we next asked whether loss of Slc25a45 impairs this adaptive program. We, therefore, used gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to identify β-oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation as signatures enriched in genes with differential fasting responses between livers from wild-type and Slc25a45-knockout mice. Targeted analysis of these pathways showed that Slc25a45 loss attenuated the fasting-induced transcriptional upregulation of key genes involved in fatty acid oxidation and electron transport chain function (Figure 4D-F). This raises the possibility that SLC25A45 is essential for the induction of transcriptional responses upon fasting. Consistent with this, the previous work reported liver-specific Cpt1a-knockout mice that functionally mimic carnitine deficiency also fail to mount acetyl-carnitine levels during fasting and show a blunted transcriptional fasting response.30 Altogether, our data suggest a critical role for SLC25A45 in sustaining carnitine synthesis and fatty acid oxidation during fasting.

CONCLUSIONThe physiological substrates of many SLCs remain elusive, as traditional deorphanization approaches have relied on low-throughput candidate-based screening and comparative genomics. More recently, the serum metabolite GWAS has enabled an unbiased and high-throughput strategy to substrate discovery.3,4,36 A key advantage of this approach is its ability to detect subtle, physiologically relevant changes, including those masked by functional redundancy or tissue specific expression. Leveraging GWAS datasets, we developed a supervised machine learning prioritization framework that reduces dependence on stringent statistical filtering3,4 and integrates experimental metabolic pathway knowledge, rendering biologically interpretable predictions. For example, this approach prioritized an overlooked (by conventional GWAS) SLC45A4-acetylspermidine association that we experimentally validated to be a strong candidate for a polyamine transporter. We also identified UNC93A as a mediator of plasma membrane acetylglucosamine transport. Given its high expression in skin, we speculate that UNC93A may contribute to the regulation of hyaluronic acid metabolism, a pathway essential for skin homeostasis.20,21 Likewise, we discovered SLC25A45 as a mediator of methylated basic amino acids mitochondrial import, providing a missing transporter in the carnitine biosynthesis.Methylated amino acids have largely been regarded as inert byproducts of protein catabolism, with little consideration for their metabolic significance. Although carnitine synthesis from isotope labeled TML has been described in 196137, the molecular players underlying mitochondrial import of TML, the first step in this pathway, has remained elusive. Carnitine is primarily obtained through diet, but during nutrient deprivation and starvation, endogenous carnitine synthesis becomes critical, particularly to sustain β-oxidation. Consistent with this, SLC25A45 deficiency partially phenocopies the blunted hepatic acetylcarnitine accumulation as well as the transcriptional response seen in liver-specific Cpt1 knockout mice.30 Building upon this, we speculate that SLC25A45 confer an evolutionary advantage during starvation by channeling protein breakdown products into β-oxidation via carnitine synthesis (Figure 6). Future work needs to establish the potential regulatory mechanisms that coordinate protein catabolism, carnitine biosynthesis and β-oxidation in response to such stress conditions. The physiological substrates of many SLCs remain elusive, as traditional deorphanization approaches have relied on low-throughput candidate-based screening and comparative genomics. More recently, the serum metabolite GWAS has enabled an unbiased and high-throughput strategy to substrate discovery.3,4,36 A key advantage of this approach is its ability to detect subtle, physiologically relevant changes, including those masked by functional redundancy or tissue specific expression. Leveraging GWAS datasets, we developed a supervised machine learning prioritization framework that reduces dependence on stringent statistical filtering3,4 and integrates experimental metabolic pathway knowledge, rendering biologically interpretable predictions. For example, this approach prioritized an overlooked (by conventional GWAS) SLC45A4-acetylspermidine association that we experimentally validated to be a strong candidate for a polyamine transporter. STAR METHODSGeneration of the machine learning prediction pipeline.GWAS of plasma metabolites.We conducted the largest-to-date TWAS of the human metabolome. We utilized two large-scale blood metabolomic studies, leveraging GWAS summary statistics from the METSIM study (1391 metabolites in 6136 Finnish men) and the CLSA cohort (1,091 metabolite and 309 ratios, 8,299 individuals of European ancestry). Harmonization of metabolites was performed according to the study by Chen et al.Building the training dataset.We parsed the Human cyclopedia gene-metabolite associations45 into gene-metabolite associations. For the training dataset, we removed the associations that are not coming from the free metabolites (i.e. removing the compounds involved in the reactions bound to DNA, tRNA, protein, histone modifications, etc.) given they are unlikely to have a global effect on plasma metabolite levels. Additionally, we filtered out the frequent flyer metabolites that have associations with 30 or more genes and are likely to be non-specific cofactors to the considered protein.Building the reaction and chemical similarity map.For the reaction map, we considered all the associations including the ones that are not acting on a free metabolite as it will still be able to provide information on the mapping between the metabolites. We removed the associations from the map involving the metabolite that had over 20% of metabolites as neighbors to avoid inflating the significance. For the chemical similarity map, we relied on the name similarity of the metabolites: they should be either a substring of each other or share a common root. To build the dictionary of the roots, we identified the longest common substring between each pair of the metabolites. Then, we filtered out the entries that are substrings of more than 50 metabolites and manually curated the list.Minimum GWAS and TWAS p-values.CLSA and METSIM datasets were used for the analysis as indicated in the GeneMAP work.4 We defined the boundaries based on ensemble 98 +/− 15 Kb pairs for the gene and considered only protein coding genes. For TWAS, we utilized the minimum p-value across the expression model as in the GeneMAP work.4Pathogenicity score.The pathogenicity score was defined based on the gene mapping from CADD scores as described in the GeneMAP work.4Building the neighbors by metabolite reaction mapping.We searched for the genes in the reaction map that are associated with the considered metabolite. Afterwards, we generated a list of all the metabolites that are connected to the identified genes excluding the considered metabolite. Finally, we considered all the association of the gene in question with the generated list of metabolites and computed minimum GWAS and TWAS p-values across these entries.Building the neighbors by the chemical mapping.We considered all the association of the gene in question with the generated list of metabolites as outlined above. Then, we computed minimum GWAS and TWAS p-values across these entries excluding the considered metabolite.Building the feature matrix.We created a matrix with 17 features that include the CADD pathogenicity score for the gene of interest in both the CLSA and METSIM datasets. The matrix also includes the minimum TWAS and GWAS p-values for the gene-metabolite associations, as well as for the gene’s reaction and chemical network neighbors. Additionally, it lists the number of CLSA and METSIM gene-metabolite associations considered based on the reaction and chemical networks. Only pairs with all the entries present have been considered. The dataset was divided into training and validation sets using k-fold cross-validation (k=5), ensuring that each fold provided a balanced evaluation of model performance (the labels and features are discussed above). Within each fold, missing values in the feature columns were imputed using the median value calculated from the training data.Neural Network Training.To standardize the feature values, we applied z-score normalization to the training data, where each feature was centered to have a mean of zero and scaled to a unit variance. The same scaling factors (mean and standard deviation) derived from the training data were then applied to the validation data to avoid any bias that could occur if validation data influenced the scaling process. A fully connected neural network was implemented to classify the target variable. The network architecture consisted of an input layer matching the number of features in the dataset, followed by two hidden layers with ReLU activation. A grid search over hyperparameters was conducted for the number of units in each layer (32, 64; 16, 32), dropout rates (0.2 to 0.5), learning rates (0.001 to 0.01), batch size (fixed to 32), and the number of epochs (max = 50). Dropout was incorporated into the architecture to reduce overfitting by randomly setting a fraction of input units to zero at each update during training. The model was compiled using the Adam optimizer with binary cross-entropy as the loss function. Early stopping was implemented to prevent overfitting, with training halted if the validation loss did not improve after five consecutive epochs. The neural network was trained on the scaled training data and validated using the scaled validation data for each fold.Random Forest Model.A Random Forest classifier was trained using default hyperparameters, with 200 estimators, a maximum tree depth of 10, and a minimum of 5 samples required to split an internal node. The model was trained on the original, unscaled features, and predictions were made on the validation set. To further evaluate model performance, we computed the AUC. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was also generated for the Random Forest classifier, and a specific threshold corresponding to a desired false positive rate (FPR = 0.05) was identified. This threshold was used to adjust the classification threshold to control for the false positive rate.Gradient Boosting Model.We also implemented a Gradient Boosting Classifier using the default hyperparameters to assess its performance on the dataset. This model iteratively trains weak learners (decision trees) and improves upon the performance of previously trained models by minimizing the loss function.Support Vector Machine (SVM) model.Finally, a Support Vector Machine (SVM) model with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel was trained on the scaled training data.Model Evaluation.For each model and fold, performance was recorded using AUC. These results were computed for each fold to provide a robust comparison of model performance.Predictions.We trained the Random Forest model on the whole training datasets (the labels and features are as indicated above) and made predictions on the unseen dataset. Tissues for polar metabolomic and lipidomic analysis.Tissue samples stored at −80°C were placed onto dry ice alongside pre-chilled 2 mL reinforced microtubes containing 2.8 mm ceramic beads (Omni Cat#.19-628). Samples were transferred into the microtubes, and 2:2:1 methanol:acetonitrile:water (MAH; LC-MS Grade) at 40 μL per mg of tissue (minus 100 μL) was added to the tube. Samples were immediately homogenized using the Omni Bead Ruptor Elite Homogenizer (speed: 6 m/s; run time: 20 seconds) equipped with a cooling hood attachment and the Omni BR-Cryo Cooling Unit at medium flow. Three cycles of homogenization were performed, with tubes cooled between cycles. Post-homogenization, samples were transferred into labeled 1.7 mL Eppendorf tubes, followed by centrifugation at maximum speed (> 12,800 rcf) for 10 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred carefully to new Eppendorf tubes without disturbing the pellet. Pellets were resuspended in 40 μL isopropanol (IPA; LC-MS Grade) per mg tissue (minus 100 μL), mixed thoroughly, and homogenized as previously described. Following identical incubation and centrifugation steps, the supernatant was transferred into separate Eppendorf tubes. Extracts for lipidomics were prepared by mixing MAH and IPA in a 1:1 ratio. The samples were stored at −80°C.

RNA-sequencing.Total RNA was extracted from the livers harvested as described above from Slc25a45−/− vs. Slc25a45+/−, Slc25a45+/+ mice using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen #74104) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. Library preparation and RNA-sequencing was performed by Genewiz. The analysis of the sequencing data was performed as in Timson et al. with some modifications.52 Quality control of the sequencing data was conducted with Rfastp.53 The reads alignment was performed according to the reference genome (Mus_musculus.GRCm39.cdna.all.fa.gz), and gene models were based on Mus_musculus.GRCm39.107.gtf.gz. Transcript expression was computed using Salmon54, followed by processing with DESeq255, with the interaction model design = ~ genotype + fasting + genotype:fasting. The interaction term (genotype:fasting) specifically tests whether the effect of fasting on gene expression is significantly different in the knockout mice compared to wild-type. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was applied to genes ranked by the interaction statistic to assess pathway-level differential responses to fasting between genotypes. The terms for Gene Set Enrichment (GSEA) and Gene Ontology (GO) analysis were retrieved from Mouse MSigDB Collections Gene Ontology Analysis.55,56 GSEA was performed using the fgsea() package.58
