# Atg7-Atg12 conjugation and autophagy are negatively regulated by the disordered region of Atg12.

The macroautophagy/autophagy machinery has two ubiquitin-like (UBL) conjugation
systems. The Atg8/MAP1LC3/GABARAP (yeast/human) and Atg12/ATG12 proteins are UBL
substrates for Atg7/ATG7, a non-canonical E1 enzyme, that thioesterifies its
substrates; however, autophagy requires a much greater amount of conjugated Atg8
(Atg8-PE) than Atg12 (Atg12-Atg5). Exactly how Atg7/ATG7 distinguishes between
its two substrates to facilitate this differential biogenesis remains elusive.
Here, analyses of recombinant complexes of yeast proteins reveal that the N
termini of Atg8 and Atg12 are structural determinants for conjugation to Atg7,
but play no role in conjugation to Atg3 or Atg10, non-canonical E2 enzymes. The
disordered N terminus of Atg12 is a protector of the Atg12 C terminus and a
negative regulator of Atg7-Atg12 conjugation and autophagy, whereas the
N-terminal helical domain in Atg8 promotes autophagy and has a high avidity to
Atg7. We show that balanced autophagy requires different specific N termini
attached to the UBL domains, which are structural determinants for selective
transfer to the native E2s. These findings deepen our understanding of the two
autophagy UBL conjugation systems that is far from complete.

Introduction

The attachment of the ubiquitin molecule, a process known as ubiquitination, is a fast and reversible posttranslational modification of proteins in eukaryotic cells. Ubiquitination employs E1, E2, and E3 enzymes in a three-step process that marks proteins for degradation or signaling1. One of the signaling pathways that utilizes the E1-E3 cascade is autophagy, an evolutionarily conserved degradative process that removes unnecessary molecules and organelles from the cytoplasm. The autophagy process relies on a transient double-membrane structure, termed a phagophore, that sequentially expands and engulfs cytoplasmic material destined for degradation. Phagophore completion generates an autophagosome that subsequently fuses with the terminal hydrolytic compartment, the lysosome (the vacuole in plants and yeast), which allows for degradation of the autophagososomal content and subsequent reuse of individual components2–4.

The autophagy pathway incorporated ubiquitin and the E1, E2, and E3 enzymes in a few proteins of its core machinery that form the non-canonical ubiquitin-like (UBL) conjugation cascades involving some of the most conserved Atg (autophagy-related) proteins. Many reports are published on the UBL conjugation systems in autophagy5–10. Briefly, Atg8 and Atg12 in yeast and the MAP1LC3/LC3 (microtubule associated protein 1 light chain 3) or GABARAP (GABA type A receptor-associated protein) sub-families (hereafter referred to as ATG8) and ATG12 in more complex eukaryotes are the UBL substrates for the E1-like and E2-like, and for ATG8 the E3-like, enzymes in the two conjugation cascades that are mutually intertwined. In the Atg12/ATG12 conjugation system, Atg12/ATG12 binds to Atg7/ATG7, a non-canonical E1 activating enzyme, that catalyzes via Mg-ATP the adenylation of the C-terminal glycine in the UBL domain (ULD) of Atg12/ATG12. The resulting acyl adenylate is attacked by the Atg7/ATG7 catalytic cysteine to form a thioester intermediate. The Atg10/ATG10 protein, operating as a non-canonical E2, then transfers the C-terminal glycine of Atg12/ATG12 to its catalytic cysteine also via a thioester bond. Finally, the C-terminal glycine of Atg12/ATG12 conjugates in an E3-independent manner to the acceptor lysine in Atg5/ATG5. The resulting Atg12–Atg5/ATG12–ATG5 conjugate noncovalently bound to Atg16/ATG16L1 operates as a non-canonical E3 enzyme in the conjugation system for Atg8/ATG8 proteins. Before entering the conjugation cascade, Atg8/ATG8 is processed by the Atg4/ATG4 protease, which cleaves an amino acid residue or sequence downstream of the C-terminal glycine. In analogy to Atg12/ATG12, this glycine is then adenylated within the adenylation domain in the C terminal half of Atg7/ATG7, an enzyme that both conjugation systems share. Atg8/ATG8 subsequently forms a thioester intermediate with Atg7/ATG7, and then transfers to Atg3/ATG3, a non-canonical E2, also via a thioesterification reaction. Subsequently, the Atg3/ATG3–Atg8/ATG8 conjugates translocate to the membrane of the autophagosomal precursor, the phagophore, where the E3 resides. The interaction of Atg3/ATG3 with Atg12/ATG12 navigates Atg8/ATG8 to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), a phospholipid embedded in the phagophore bilayer. Conjugation of Atg8/ATG8 to PE decorates the phagophore with many molecules of Atg8/ATG8, which facilitates phagophore expansion and ultimately leads to engulfment of the cargo within a closed autophagosome. The autophagosome then fuses with the vacuole/lysosome to degrade the cargo; the resulting breakdown products are then released for cellular reuse.

The autophagy pathway operates with a constitutive level of the Atg12–Atg5 conjugate11, whereas Atg8/ATG8 is strongly upregulated to enter the conjugation cascade in support of phagophore expansion12. Thus, the E1 faces a differential requirement for activation of its UBL substrates, and needs to distinguish between Atg12/ATG12 and Atg8/ATG8. The architectures of the substrates are unique (Supplementary Fig. 1A,B). The ULD of Atg8/ATG8 is preceded by a short N-terminal α-helical domain (NHD) of less than 30 amino acid residues. The NMR and crystal structures13,14 show that the NHD and C-terminal tail of Atg8/ATG8 are distant, that is, on the opposite sides of the molecule. In comparison, the ULD of Atg12/ATG12 is preceded at the N terminus by an intrinsically disordered region (IDR)11,15 that is 100/52 amino acid residues long in the yeast/human protein. In yeast, the Atg12 IDR has intra-molecular contacts with the ULD that ensure intactness of the C terminus15, suggesting that a part of the IDR is in close proximity to the C-terminal tail of Atg12. How Atg7/ATG7 differentially activates its UBL substrates and what roles the N-terminal domains (NTDs) of Atg8/ATG8 and Atg12/ATG12 play in this process remain elusive, in part due to the inability to visualize the Atg12 IDR by X-ray crystallography. Owing to a high homology of the autophagy UBL conjugation cascades in yeast and humans16 (Supplementary Fig. 1C-G) and the presence of single isoforms of these proteins in yeast, we used recombinant proteins from the yeast system to answer these questions. Because recombinant Atg12 alone is not feasible to purify6,17, we applied a recombinant approach relying on the formation of thioesters during protein expression in bacterial cells that supply Mg-ATP. In-cell covalent conjugation of autophagy proteins has been used successfully in previous studies17,18. We isolated thioester-forming recombinant complexes containing Atg8 or Atg12 with either Atg7 alone or Atg7-E2 dimers. This approach made it possible, for the first time to the best of our knowledge, to structurally compare Atg8 and Atg12 in thioesterification reactions.

The data presented here reveal that the autophagy machinery uniquely acquired and modified the function of ubiquitin by attaching distinctive N termini to the UBL molecule to regulate its activation by the E1 and, ultimately, to balance autophagy.

To further confirm our results, we employed affinity isolation with recombinant His6-Atg7 wild type or mutant (C507S) co-expressed with Atg12. The C507S mutation ensures a more stable ester bond, compared to a fragile thioester linkage. The immunoblots did not detect any His6-Atg7–Atg12 conjugate of about 93 kDa above the free full-length His6-Atg7 protein of 71 kDa or Atg12 of 22 kDa (Fig. 1C). Only N-terminal degradation products of His6-Atg7 were detected below 35 kDa, suggesting that Atg7 is susceptible to a proteolytic cleavage approximately in the middle of the protein, where a disordered linker between the N-terminal and C-terminal domains resides. To further probe conjugation between recombinant Atg7 and Atg12, we turned to a custom expression system in BL21 Gold(DE3) cells15. This system relies on a modification of the commercial MCSG7 vector by removing the TEV sequence between the N-terminal His tag and ATG12 ORF, to keep the extension of the Atg12 IDR as short as possible. This modified MCSG7 vector (mMCSG7) expressing His6-Atg12 when used together with the COLADuet-1 vector co-expressing Atg7 and Atg10 in BL21 Gold(DE3) cells yields a well-detectable Atg10–His6-Atg12 thioester15, whereas the commercial MCSG7 vector containing the TEV sequence does not (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Therefore, in this study, we co-expressed His6-Atg12 from mMCSG7 and Atg7 from COLADuet-1. The immunoblot after affinity isolation detected only a very faint and diffuse signal above free His6-Atg12 (Fig. 1D), suggesting inefficient formation of the Atg7–His6-Atg12 conjugate. A negative control in this experiment was co-expression of Atg10 and His6-Atg12, as no covalent conjugate of 42 kDa can be formed between Atg10 and Atg12 in the absence of the activating enzyme Atg7 (Fig. 1D).

To compare Atg12 with Atg8, we co-expressed His6-Atg8[∆R] from mMCSG7 and Atg7 from COLADuet-1. In contrast to His6-Atg12, we detected a clear signal corresponding to a protein species of ~30 kDa above free His6-Atg8[∆R] (Fig. 1E). This signal was sensitive to reducing conditions, and was detected, along with free His6-Atg8[∆R], with both anti-His and anti-Atg8 antibodies, suggesting an isoform of Atg7–His6-Atg8[∆R]. This 30-kDa isoform was abundant relative to the bacterial protein background on Coomassie Brilliant Blue-stained gels, and is presumably a result of a proteolytic cleavage, as the full-length Atg7–His6-Atg8[∆R] covalent complex has an expected molecular mass of 85 kDa (Fig. 1F). Mass spectrometry analysis of the 30 kDa band positively confirmed the presence of Atg7 with 11 unique peptides and Atg8 with 22 unique peptides (Supplementary Fig. 2B); Atg8 was detected with 90% sequence coverage, whereas Atg7 with 20% coverage.

Collectively, these results (Fig. 1A–E) suggest that Atg7 forms a thioester bond efficiently with His6-Atg8[∆R], but not with His6-Atg12. This finding supports the notion that Atg7 differentiates between its UBL substrates and that the biogenesis of abundant Atg8–PE, relative to Atg12–Atg5, can be a result of more efficient conjugation of Atg8 to Atg7 than Atg12 to Atg7. Specifically, strong signals of ~30 kDa for His6-Atg8[∆R], ~60 kDa for His6-Atg12N-8ULD[∆R], and ~25 kDa for His6-Atg8N-12ULD appeared. His6-Atg12 was the only polypeptide where no additional band above the full-length protein was detected (Fig. 2C, D), not even on overexposed anti-His immunoblots (Supplementary Fig. 2C). These additional bands for His6-Atg8[∆R], His6-Atg12N-8ULD[∆R], and His6-Atg8N-12ULD match in molecular mass to the major isoforms of the Atg7 conjugates with these three proteins (compare Fig. 2C, D with Fig. 2E, F). Given the absence of Atg7 during overexpression of the individual full-length His6-Atg8[∆R], His6-Atg12N-8ULD[∆R], and His6-Atg8N-12ULD, we hypothesized that these three polypeptides may conjugate with a bacterial protein. To probe this assumption, we employed mass spectrometry on one representative, the 30 kDa band detected above His6-Atg8[∆R].

The LC-MS/MS analysis of this band (Supplementary Fig. 3A) revealed the presence of ThiF and MoeB, prokaryotic antecedents of the E1 enzyme22,23. These two enzymes were not detected by LC-MS/MS analysis of the 30-kDa isoform of the Atg7–His6-Atg8[∆R] thioester formed in the presence of Atg7 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). ThiF and MoeB function as adenylyltransferases that adenylate prokaryotic homologs of ubiquitin, ThiS and MoaD, respectively24,25. Both enzymes have a strong homology to the Atg7 CTD20,26 (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Titration of the His6-Atg8[∆R]-containing bacterial conjugate of ~30 kDa with DTT showed sensitivity to reducing conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3C); as did the Atg7–His6-Atg8[∆R] thioester. To further confirm that the aforementioned proteins were conjugated to prokaryotic E1s, we employed affinity isolation with the His tag from BL21 Gold(DE3) cells expressing His6-Atg12N-8ULD[∆R] from mMCSG7 and FLAG-MoeB from COLADuet-1 (Supplementary Fig. 3D). We opted for this chimera because its bacterial conjugate of ~60 kDa was well distinguishable from free FLAG-MoeB of ~30 kDa. Probing the Ni-NTA eluate by anti-His and anti-FLAG antibody showed the DTT-sensitive signal for the FLAG-MoeB–His6-Atg12N-8ULD[∆R] conjugate, consistent with the LC-MS/MS analysis of MoeB/ThiF–His6-Atg8[∆R]. These results suggest that the prokaryotic E1 antecedents can replace Atg7 in thioesterification reactions and that the autophagy ULDs from yeast can conjugate to an evolutionarily less complex E1. Because these prokaryotic E1s are homologous to Atg7CTD, our finding that Atg12 did not conjugate to the Atg7 CTD (Fig. 1A, B) explains why Atg12 did not conjugate to bacterial MoeB or ThiF (Fig. 2D). Based on these results, the Atg12 IDR emerges as the structural element that prevents the efficient conjugation of Atg12 to Atg7, Atg7CTD, and prokaryotic E1s (Figs. 1, 2). However, an efficient conjugation between Atg12 and Atg7 is needed to generate the Atg12–Atg5 subunit of the E3. Therefore, a mechanism eliminating the obstruction by the Atg12 IDR should exist. A previous study revealed that Atg12 has a higher affinity for Atg10 than Atg715, which presented a clear question: Is Atg10 the factor that releases the blockage by the Atg12 IDR?

The functionally uncharacterized Atg12 IDR emerges here as an important negative regulator and structural protector of the C terminus in the indispensable Atg12 protein. Through negative regulation of the Atg7–Atg12 conjugation reaction, the Atg12 IDR downregulates, in an unknown manner, the endogenous level of Atg8 controlling phagophore expansion and autophagy. In this sense, the Atg12 IDR resembles Ume6, a negative regulator of Atg8 protein levels, autophagosome size36, and autophagy12. Via control of the Atg8 protein level entering the conjugation cascade, the Atg12 IDR manifests as an executor of a crosstalk between the two autophagy UBL conjugation systems in yeast cells. This finding deserves further investigation in future studies because it reveals the need to balance the amount of Atg8 and Atg12 going through the conjugation cascades. Apparently, the tight control of the E3 biogenesis on the phagophore ensures that this enzyme does not exceed the supply of Atg8 destined for lipidation. Exactly how the Atg12 IDR directly or indirectly controls this balance and whether the transcriptional control of Atg8 is involved remain to be examined. Importantly, our data show that it is not the Atg12IDR sequence but its unique folding near the Atg12 ULD that is the key for its function. The far-reaching effect on both autophagy conjugation systems makes the unique Atg12IDR folding indispensable for autophagy regulation. Our understanding of this carefully orchestrated plasticity is vital, as the human ATG12 protein, with functions far beyond autophagy37–39, also carries an IDR at its N terminus.

To strengthen this hypothesis, we carried out additional experiments where we produced three types of thioesters with Atg7CTD and then probed them by DTT titrations. The Atg7CTD–His6-Atg8[∆R] conjugate was used as a control to show that a high DTT sensitivity of this thioester corresponds to a high solvent accessibility of the catalytic cysteine, C507 in yeast Atg7, revealed by the crystal structure (PDB ID: 3VH4) (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). The other two thioester complexes, Atg7CTD–His6-Atg8[∆C-12C] and Atg7CTD–His6-Atg8N-12ULD, were probed by DTT titration to reveal their strikingly different sensitivity to reducing conditions, reflecting very different solvent accessibility of their thioester bonds (Supplementary Fig. 5C, D). Consistently with our expectations, we observed that the Atg7CTD–His6-Atg8[∆C-12C] thioester started dissolving at 0.5 mM DTT whereas Atg7CTD–His6-Atg8N-12ULD needed 450 mM DTT for breakage (Supplementary Fig. 5C, D). This distinction apparently arises from the substrate ULDs because the His6-Atg8[∆C-12C] and His6-Atg8N-12ULD molecules share the same amino acid sequences in the N-terminal and C-terminal tails, but not the ULDs. The former molecule has the ULD of Atg8 and the latter carries the ULD of Atg12. We propose that the evolutionarily divergent amino acid sequences in the substrate ULDs (Fig. 2A) modulate Atg7CTD differently. Such a modulation places the C507(Atg7CTD)–G116(Atg8ULD[∆R]) and C507(Atg7CTD)–G186(His6-Atg12) thioesters into distinct positions in Atg7CTD, where the C507–G116 position aligns with C234 of Atg3 and C507–G186 aligns with C133 of Atg10. This mechanism would allow for selective transfer only to the native E2s. Such a selectivity requires two distinct orientations of Atg3 and Atg10 relative to Atg7CTD. Indeed, these distinct positions are visible in the Atg3-Atg7 and Atg10-Atg7 crystal structures (PDB IDs: 4GSL and 4GSK) aligned by Atg7 (Supplementary Fig. 5E). Together, these data (Supplementary Fig. 5) support the notion that the process of the selective transfer from Atg7 to the native E2s relies on the ULDs of Atg8 and Atg12 that ensure direct juxtapositions of the catalytic cysteines.

In summary (Fig. 6C), we reveal that the specific N termini in Atg8 and Atg12 are structural determinants of conjugation to Atg7, and, ultimately, a balanced autophagy process. The functionally uncharacterized Atg12 IDR emerges here as an important negative regulator and structural protector of the C terminus in the indispensable Atg12 protein. Through negative regulation of the Atg7–Atg12 conjugation reaction, the Atg12 IDR downregulates, in an unknown manner, the endogenous level of Atg8 controlling phagophore expansion and autophagy. In this sense, the Atg12 IDR resembles Ume6, a negative regulator of Atg8 protein levels, autophagosome size36, and autophagy12. Via control of the Atg8 protein level entering the conjugation cascade, the Atg12 IDR manifests as an executor of a crosstalk between the two autophagy UBL conjugation systems in yeast cells. This finding deserves further investigation in future studies because it reveals the need to balance the amount of Atg8 and Atg12 going through the conjugation cascades. Apparently, the tight control of the E3 biogenesis on the phagophore ensures that this enzyme does not exceed the supply of Atg8 destined for lipidation. Exactly how the Atg12 IDR directly or indirectly controls this balance and whether the transcriptional control of Atg8 is involved remain to be examined. Importantly, our data show that it is not the Atg12IDR sequence but its unique folding near the Atg12 ULD that is the key for its function. The far-reaching effect on both autophagy conjugation systems makes the unique Atg12IDR folding indispensable for autophagy regulation. Our understanding of this carefully orchestrated plasticity is vital, as the human ATG12 protein, with functions far beyond autophagy37–39, also carries an IDR at its N terminus.

MS1 scans were acquired at 60 K resolution (AGC target = 3 × 106; max IT = 50 ms). Data-dependent collision-induced dissociation MS/MS spectra were acquired on 20 of the most abundant ions following each MS1 scan (NCE ~ 28%; AGC target 1 × 105; max IT 45 ms).

For the database search (FixedPSM validation), proteins were identified by searching the data against the S. cerevisiae S288C protein database using Proteome Discoverer (v3.0, Thermo Scientific). Search parameters included MS1 mass tolerance of 10 ppm and fragment tolerance of 0.05 Da; two missed cleavages were allowed; carbamidimethylation of cysteine was considered a fixed modification and oxidation of methionine, and deamidation of asparagine and glutamine were considered as potential modifications. FixedPSM validator was used to retain peptides/psms with a deltaCn = 0.05 and proteins/peptides with a FDR of ≤ 1% were retained for further analysis.
