The androgen receptor (AR) is a type I nuclear receptor and master transcription
factor responsible for development and maintenance of male secondary sex
characteristics. Aberrant AR activity is associated with numerous diseases,
including prostate cancer, androgen insensitivity syndrome, spinal and bulbar
muscular atrophy, and androgenic alopecia. Recent studies have shown that AR
adopts numerous conformations that can modulate its ability to bind and
transcribe its target DNA substrates, a feature that can be hijacked in the
context of cancer. Here, we summarize a series of structural observations
describing how this elusive shape-shifter binds to multiple partners, including
self-interactions, DNA, and steroid and non-steroidal ligands. We present
evidence that AR's pervasive structural plasticity confers an ability to broadly
bind and transcribe numerous ligands in the normal and disease state, and
explain the structural basis for adaptive resistance mutations to antiandrogen
treatment. These evolutionary features are integral to receptor function, and
are commonly lost in androgen insensitivity syndrome, or reinforced in cancer.

Introduction

Androgen receptor (AR) is a nuclear receptor that forms the steroid hormone receptor family along with the estrogen, progesterone, glucocorticoid, and mineralocorticoid receptors.1,2 AR is a transcription factor that regulates numerous genes, including those required for male sexual development and differentiation. Testosterone is an endogenous ligand for AR, and its metabolite 5-alpha dihydrotestosterone (DHT) is more metabolically potent and has a stronger affinity for the receptor.3–5 DHT binding to AR initiates the receptor’s release from heat shock proteins, promotes interdomain interactions, and induces AR’s nuclear import.6–8 As a homodimer, AR binds DNA sequences called androgen response elements within the promoter regions of its target genes.9–12 Transcription of these genes is associated with typical outcomes like male development and atypical outcomes such as the formation of prostate cancer (PCa).

PCa is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in males in the United States.13 The progression of PCa requires androgenic signaling, so the first lines of treatment are depleting androgen levels or competitively blocking their binding to AR. Unfortunately, patients do not maintain response to these treatments and adaptive mutations or increased AR expression enable disease recurrence. Robust efforts to characterize AR’s ordered domains via high-resolution techniques have deepened our understanding of the receptor and its actions. However, there remains a need for more structural and biochemical experiments performed with multidomain variants of the receptor in order to capture and more fully understand the complex allosteric regulation of AR. In this review, we summarize structure-function relationships of individual domains, investigate the mechanism of AR antagonism, and discuss intramolecular allostery and higher-order complex formation.

Recent studies have demonstrated that the NTD is the predominant domain that facilitates liquid-liquid phase separation, and it is thought that low-complexity regions (i.e., amino acid repeat sequences) specifically mediate these multivalent, hydrophobic interactions.14–17 Therefore, complete structural characterization of the NTD via x-ray crystallography and other high resolution structural methodologies has been untenable due to its intrinsic disorder and propensity to aggregate, but NMR experiments have shown that various regions can adopt transient secondary structures.18 Despite the difficulty with structural characterization, strong efforts have been made to map the functional units of the NTD. There are both steroidal (e.g. cyproterone acetate, megestrol acetate) and nonsteroidal (e.g. bicalutamide, flutamide, enzalutamide) antiandrogens (Table 1; Fig. 4). Despite the first antiandrogen being discovered in the 1960’s, the structural basis of AR antagonism remains unknown.60 Structures of other nuclear receptor LBDs including PPAR-alpha and ER-alpha show H12 displacement when bound by an antagonist (PDBs: 1KKQ and 3ERT).61,62 Molecular dynamics simulations predict that antiandrogens, including the standard of care enzalutamide, exert antagonist effects by similarly restricting the movement of the H11-H12 loop and H12, preventing formation of the agonist conformation.63,64 However, there are currently no published experimentally-obtained structures of antagonist-bound wildtype AR or enzalutamide-bound AR to confirm this proposed mechanism of action.Long-term treatment with antiandrogens can induce mutations within the AR ligand binding domain in prostate cancer tumors. Published structures of the AR ligand binding domain containing adaptive prostate cancer mutations bound to their respective antagonists (i.e. functional agonists) show how these mutations confer drug resistance by remodeling the hydrogen bond network within the ligand binding pocket. Generally, these structures show that the substitution of large amino acids for smaller ones allows the ligand binding domain to simultaneously accommodate antiandrogens and permit movement of H12. For example, the T878A mutation commonly found in prostate cancer has been found to result in a weaker selectivity filter, widening the AR ligand binding pocket to permit binding of androgens, antiandrogens, and other steroid hormone receptor ligands while remaining transcriptionally active.3,51,54,59,65 Interestingly, a structure of T878A LBD bound by hydroxyflutamide shows an additional water molecule within the ligand binding pocket (PDB: 2AX6; Fig. 4B). This water can hydrogen bond with the main chain carbonyl of L875 and carbonyl of the ligand. Notably, a structure of the W742L mutant bound by bicalutamide also shows an additional water molecule in the ligand binding pocket (PDB: 1Z95).53 This water can form hydrogen bonds with the side chain of H875 and the fluorine in the fluorophenyl group of the ligand (Fig. 4C). Therefore, it is possible that antiandrogen resistance is caused by mutations in the ligand binding pocket enabling the formation of a water-based hydrogen bonding network that stabilizes the agonist conformation. There are multiple theories for how SARMs exert tissue-specific anabolic effects. Some propose that tissue-specific cofactor expression influences the biological effects of AR signaling. Others propose that the partial agonism is due to the fact that nonsteroidal SARMs cannot be converted into DHT nor estrogens, unlike testosterone. Similar to the nonsteroidal antiandrogen ligands, ostarine interacts with two water molecules in the ligand binding pocket (PDB: 3RLJ; Fig. 5A).72 In all, Asp706, Gln712, Arg753, and His875 engage in hydrogen bonding. Additionally, Phe765 and Trp742 participate in pi stacking with the ligand. Another structure with S-24 [(2S)-N-(4-cyano-3-iodophenyl)-3-(4-cyanophenoxy)-2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanamide] shows an almost identical interaction between the same residues observed in the ostarine structure (PDB: 3B65; Fig. 5B).74 Lastly, a SARM with the developmental code “(+)-13a” [(5R)-3-(3,4-dichlorophenyl)-5-(4-hydroxyphenyl)-1,5-dimethyl-2-thioxoimidazolidin-4-one] shows a bonding network with unique features (PDB: 3V4A).73 The aromatic interactions observed in the other SARM structures and hydrogen bonding with Arg753 and His875 are conserved with (+)-13a (Fig 5C).Overall, the architecture of the ligand binding pocket is dynamic and its remodeling is associated with promiscuous activation, acquired drug resistance, and tissue-specific agonism. Endogenous androgens interact with the ligand binding pocket primarily through hydrophobic interactions with minimal hydrogen bonds. Notably, although steroidal ligands contain four carbon rings, these ligands lack aromaticity and therefore cannot participate in pi stacking interactions. Nonsteroidal antiandrogens contain aromatic substituents, but the presence of adaptive mutations results in an expanded hydrogen bonding network and, possibly, the disruption of π-π stacking between ligand and receptor. The tissue-specific agonism effects of SARMs may be due to the combination of wildtype receptor and pi stacking.Structural insight to intramolecular allostery and complex assemblyUntil recently, structures of AR have been limited to isolated domains, shedding light on the molecular details of how AR binds its DNA or steroid or steroid-like ligands. However, structural insight into interdomain cooperativity and how this process impacts DNA binding and transactivation have been less clear due to several challenges. These include technical difficulties in isolating pure, soluble and active multi-domain protein amenable for structural studies, as well as the AR’s propensity and the rest of the 3-ketosteroid receptor family to form monomers rather than dimerize. This latter observation is in contrast to the better structurally characterized estrogen receptor family and type II nuclear receptors, which form obligate LBD dimers.38,75 As a result, the vast majority of AR LBD structures published on the Protein Data Bank are monomeric; however, Estébanez-Perpiña and colleagues isolated a dimeric form of the AR LBD and resolved its structure by x-ray crystallography (PDB: 5JJM).76 The asymmetric unit in the model contains four copies of the AR LBD and depicts the core dimerization interface to be head-to-head between H5, loop H9-H10, and β1-β2. Wasmuth and colleagues later resolved a similar LBD dimeric interface in a structure of ΔNTD AR bound to palindromic ARE DNA by single particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), suggesting that this dimeric interface is utilized to allosterically promote DNA binding. Importantly, both groups demonstrated that residues lining the LBD dimer interface are mutated in partial AIS and prostate cancer, with the latter mutations either predicted or experimentally shown to interfere with allosteric interactions or protein stability, attenuating AR transactivation, and the former predicted to increase stability of the AR.38,76,77Intriguingly, Estébanez-Perpiña and colleagues also resolved two additional AR LBDs attached to the core dimer via their BF3 sites – however, the biological significance of the LBD-LBD interaction at BF3, if any, is currently unclear.76Additionally, two recent structural studies of multi-domain AR complexes using cryo-EM have been reported. In the first, O’Malley and colleagues generated full-length recombinant AR complexes sourced from insect cells and found a critical role of the AR NTD in recruitment of SRC-3 and p300 cofactors to form transcriptionally active complexes.78 Although lower resolution (13–20 angstrom), these models are supported by positional antibody labeling to place the NTD. Another study from Wasmuth and Sawyers leveraged cryo-EM coupled with cross-linking mass spectrometry to resolve three moderate resolution (9-11 angstrom) models of ΔNTD AR (sourced from bacteria) bound to ARE DNA.38 These structures reveal a dynamic range of motion about the previously identified LBD dimer interface76, where LBD dimers resemble entrenched, splayed or divorced states. The DBD dimer interface was observed to pivot as well, depending on the extent of allosteric engagement between the LBD and DBD. These newly identified allosteric contacts between the LBD and DBD are highly conserved, important for DNA binding, and mutated in partial AIS (PAIS), resulting in partial loss-of-function of DNA binding and transactivation within the receptor.38 Strikingly, the plastic LBD dimerization interface is “inside out” relative to other nuclear receptors, including the estrogen receptor.The authors invoke that AR’s structural plasticity may have functional implications underlying the shift in AR’s cistrome in cancer by oncoprotein cofactors, permitting AR to bind a larger repertoire of non-canonical DNA sequences. As proof-of-concept, the authors demonstrated that partially activated AR – modeled by low androgen or non-canonical ARE sequences – is more susceptible to direct modulation by the ETS factor and prostate cancer oncoprotein, ERG, than AR fully primed with high androgen levels or presented with classical palindromic ARE binding sequences. These findings provide a structural basis for previous work demonstrating how ERG cooperates with AR to promote a pro-oncogenic gene expression program.32,79,80

DNA binding domain (DBD) and hinge

All steroid receptors bind to two hexameric half-sites separated by a 3-nucleotide spacer. It has been found that AR and its close family members of the 3-ketosteroid receptor family which include the glucocorticoid receptor, mineralocorticoid receptor, and progesterone receptor bind a consensus hexamer (5’-AGAACA-3’) and a variable inverted repeat hexamer. This direct repeat-inverted repeat palindromic motif is unique to steroid hormone response elements (HREs). The variable inverted repeat allows steroid receptors to discriminate between otherwise identical motifs, and there are unique sequences that permit selective activation by AR, also known as androgen response elements (AREs). Interestingly, the AR DNA binding domain possesses a unique ability to bind HREs with a more degenerate inverted repeat than other members of the SR family. The redistribution of AR from canonical palindromes to more degenerate AREs is associated with prostate cancer.33–35

The first crystal structure of the AR DBD bound to ARE DNA was published in 2004 (PDB: 1R4I).36 As observed in other steroid receptors, the AR DBD contains two zinc fingers, with each finger coordinating a Zn2+ cation via conserved, solvent-sequestered cysteine residues (Fig 2A).13 The N-terminal zinc finger contains a proximal (P) box (578GSCKV582) that makes direct contacts with the hexameric steroid HRE. The C-terminal zinc finger contains a distal (D) box (597ASRND601) that creates the dimer interface mediated by hydrogen bonding and electrostatic forces between monomers.13,36,37 The structure further shows two monomers dimerized in a “head-to-head” arrangement on a synthetic direct (non-inverted) repeat ARE that is seldom bound by AR in vivo.36 Interestingly, two recent structural studies using X-ray crystallography of the AR DBD and single particle cryo-electron microscopy of an AR construct lacking its N-terminus (ΔNTD) have demonstrated that dimeric AR binds canonical inverted repeat AREs in the same head-to-head arrangement, indicating that this conformation is likely the canonical mode of AR DBD dimerization.37,38 There are sequence-specific contacts between the DBDs and AREs, and it has been suggested that these differing contacts could have functional consequences.37

Androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS) is an inherited condition wherein XY individuals exhibit resistance to androgen signaling (i.e. hormone resistance) and do not develop normal male phenotypes. AIS exists on a spectrum and in mild or moderate cases, partial insensitivity can result in typical male genital development with infertility or gynecomastia whereas complete androgen insensitivity results in individuals presenting as phenotypically female, developing external female genitalia but retaining undescended testicles.39,40 Mutations in the AR DBD are causative of AIS. Antiandrogens

Antiandrogens function as antagonists by competitively binding the ligand binding pocket. There are both steroidal (e.g. cyproterone acetate, megestrol acetate) and nonsteroidal (e.g. bicalutamide, flutamide, enzalutamide) antiandrogens (Table 1; Fig. 4). Despite the first antiandrogen being discovered in the 1960’s, the structural basis of AR antagonism remains unknown.60 Structures of other nuclear receptor LBDs including PPAR-alpha and ER-alpha show H12 displacement when bound by an antagonist (PDBs: 1KKQ and 3ERT).61,62 Molecular dynamics simulations predict that antiandrogens, including the standard of care enzalutamide, exert antagonist effects by similarly restricting the movement of the H11-H12 loop and H12, preventing formation of the agonist conformation.63,64 However, there are currently no published experimentally-obtained structures of antagonist-bound wildtype AR or enzalutamide-bound AR to confirm this proposed mechanism of action.

Long-term treatment with antiandrogens can induce mutations within the AR ligand binding domain in prostate cancer tumors. Published structures of the AR ligand binding domain containing adaptive prostate cancer mutations bound to their respective antagonists (i.e. functional agonists) show how these mutations confer drug resistance by remodeling the hydrogen bond network within the ligand binding pocket. Generally, these structures show that the substitution of large amino acids for smaller ones allows the ligand binding domain to simultaneously accommodate antiandrogens and permit movement of H12. For example, the T878A mutation commonly found in prostate cancer has been found to result in a weaker selectivity filter, widening the AR ligand binding pocket to permit binding of androgens, antiandrogens, and other steroid hormone receptor ligands while remaining transcriptionally active.3,51,54,59,65 Interestingly, a structure of T878A LBD bound by hydroxyflutamide shows an additional water molecule within the ligand binding pocket (PDB: 2AX6; Fig. 4B). This water can hydrogen bond with the main chain carbonyl of L875 and carbonyl of the ligand. Notably, a structure of the W742L mutant bound by bicalutamide also shows an additional water molecule in the ligand binding pocket (PDB: 1Z95).53 This water can form hydrogen bonds with the side chain of H875 and the fluorine in the fluorophenyl group of the ligand (Fig. 4C). Therefore, it is possible that antiandrogen resistance is caused by mutations in the ligand binding pocket enabling the formation of a water-based hydrogen bonding network that stabilizes the agonist conformation. Finally, the enzalutamide resistance mutation, F877L, has been proposed by molecular dynamics to render enzalutamide an agonist by widening the ligand binding pocket and repositioning H12.63,64

A structure of the LBD containing four common prostate cancer driver mutations bound to DHT showcases the extent to which these adaptive mutations remodel the hydrogen bond network within the ligand binding pocket (PDB: 8FGY; Fig. 4D).55,66–71 The combined L702H, H875Y, F877L, and T878A mutations result in the creation of three new hydrogen bonds involving: a water with the guanidino group of Arg753; a water with the carbonyl oxygen of Gln712’s side chain; and the amide in Asn706 with the backbone of H702. This structure, along with the antagonist-bound structures and molecular dynamics simulations, further suggests that the key to antiandrogen resistance lies in the ability of an adaptive mutant to favorably alter the hydrogen bonding pattern within the ligand binding pocket.

Selective Androgen Receptor ModulatorsSelective androgen receptor modulators (SARMs) are classes of mostly nonsteroidal small molecules that enable tissue-specific activation of AR. Although androgenic signaling in the prostate can lead to prostate cancer, androgenic signaling in other tissues such as bone or muscle can have therapeutic benefits. There are multiple theories for how SARMs exert tissue-specific anabolic effects. Some propose that tissue-specific cofactor expression influences the biological effects of AR signaling. Others propose that the partial agonism is due to the fact that nonsteroidal SARMs cannot be converted into DHT nor estrogens, unlike testosterone. Although divergent in their chemical structures from androgens, several crystal structures of the AR ligand binding domain bound to SARMs demonstrate the small molecules’ on-target specificity for AR.72–74 These structures reveal that SARMs engage in π-π stacking interactions that are absent in the androgen-bound and adaptive mutant structures.Ostarine, also known as enobosarm, is the most well-studied SARM. Similar to the nonsteroidal antiandrogen ligands, ostarine interacts with two water molecules in the ligand binding pocket (PDB: 3RLJ; Fig. 5A).72 In all, Asp706, Gln712, Arg753, and His875 engage in hydrogen bonding. Additionally, Phe765 and Trp742 participate in pi stacking with the ligand. Another structure with S-24 [(2S)-N-(4-cyano-3-iodophenyl)-3-(4-cyanophenoxy)-2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanamide] shows an almost identical interaction between the same residues observed in the ostarine structure (PDB: 3B65; Fig. 5B).74 Lastly, a SARM with the developmental code “(+)-13a” [(5R)-3-(3,4-dichlorophenyl)-5-(4-hydroxyphenyl)-1,5-dimethyl-2-thioxoimidazolidin-4-one] shows a bonding network with unique features (PDB: 3V4A).73 The aromatic interactions observed in the other SARM structures and hydrogen bonding with Arg753 and His875 are conserved with (+)-13a (Fig 5C).Overall, the architecture of the ligand binding pocket is dynamic and its remodeling is associated with promiscuous activation, acquired drug resistance, and tissue-specific agonism. Endogenous androgens interact with the ligand binding pocket primarily through hydrophobic interactions with minimal hydrogen bonds. Notably, although steroidal ligands contain four carbon rings, these ligands lack aromaticity and therefore cannot participate in pi stacking interactions. Nonsteroidal antiandrogens contain aromatic substituents, but the presence of adaptive mutations results in an expanded hydrogen bonding network and, possibly, the disruption of π-π stacking between ligand and receptor. While antiandrogens and adaptive mutations result in more hydrogen bonds, nonsteroidal SARMs remodel the ligand binding pocket architecture by engaging in unique binding patterns. The tissue-specific agonism effects of SARMs may be due to the combination of wildtype receptor and pi stacking.

Structural insight to intramolecular allostery and complex assemblyUntil recently, structures of AR have been limited to isolated domains, shedding light on the molecular details of how AR binds its DNA or steroid or steroid-like ligands. However, structural insight into interdomain cooperativity and how this process impacts DNA binding and transactivation have been less clear due to several challenges. These include technical difficulties in isolating pure, soluble and active multi-domain protein amenable for structural studies, as well as the AR’s propensity and the rest of the 3-ketosteroid receptor family to form monomers rather than dimerize. This latter observation is in contrast to the better structurally characterized estrogen receptor family and type II nuclear receptors, which form obligate LBD dimers.38,75 As a result, the vast majority of AR LBD structures published on the Protein Data Bank are monomeric; however, Estébanez-Perpiña and colleagues isolated a dimeric form of the AR LBD and resolved its structure by x-ray crystallography (PDB: 5JJM).76 The asymmetric unit in the model contains four copies of the AR LBD and depicts the core dimerization interface to be head-to-head between H5, loop H9-H10, and β1-β2. Wasmuth and colleagues later resolved a similar LBD dimeric interface in a structure of ΔNTD AR bound to palindromic ARE DNA by single particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), suggesting that this dimeric interface is utilized to allosterically promote DNA binding. Importantly, both groups demonstrated that residues lining the LBD dimer interface are mutated in partial AIS and prostate cancer, with the latter mutations either predicted or experimentally shown to interfere with allosteric interactions or protein stability, attenuating AR transactivation, and the former predicted to increase stability of the AR.38,76,77Intriguingly, Estébanez-Perpiña and colleagues also resolved two additional AR LBDs attached to the core dimer via their BF3 sites – however, the biological significance of the LBD-LBD interaction at BF3, if any, is currently unclear.76Additionally, two recent structural studies of multi-domain AR complexes using cryo-EM have been reported. In the first, O’Malley and colleagues generated full-length recombinant AR complexes sourced from insect cells and found a critical role of the AR NTD in recruitment of SRC-3 and p300 cofactors to form transcriptionally active complexes.78 Although lower resolution (13–20 angstrom), these models are supported by positional antibody labeling to place the NTD. Another study from Wasmuth and Sawyers leveraged cryo-EM coupled with cross-linking mass spectrometry to resolve three moderate resolution (9-11 angstrom) models of ΔNTD AR (sourced from bacteria) bound to ARE DNA.38 These structures reveal a dynamic range of motion about the previously identified LBD dimer interface76, where LBD dimers resemble entrenched, splayed or divorced states. The DBD dimer interface was observed to pivot as well, depending on the extent of allosteric engagement between the LBD and DBD. These newly identified allosteric contacts between the LBD and DBD are highly conserved, important for DNA binding, and mutated in partial AIS (PAIS), resulting in partial loss-of-function of DNA binding and transactivation within the receptor.38 Strikingly, the plastic LBD dimerization interface is “inside out” relative to other nuclear receptors, including the estrogen receptor.The authors invoke that AR’s structural plasticity may have functional implications underlying the shift in AR’s cistrome in cancer by oncoprotein cofactors, permitting AR to bind a larger repertoire of non-canonical DNA sequences. As proof-of-concept, the authors demonstrated that partially activated AR – modeled by low androgen or non-canonical ARE sequences – is more susceptible to direct modulation by the ETS factor and prostate cancer oncoprotein, ERG, than AR fully primed with high androgen levels or presented with classical palindromic ARE binding sequences. These findings provide a structural basis for previous work demonstrating how ERG cooperates with AR to promote a pro-oncogenic gene expression program.32,79,80

PerspectivesAR is remarkably adaptive in its ability to recognize a wide range of ligands and protein binding partners, owing to flexibility within and proximal to its ordered domains. These include a permissive ligand binding pocket that is capable of binding androgens, as well as non-steroidal ligands including SARMs and antiandrogens, a DNA binding domain that is hard-wired to bind palindromic and non-repeat AREs in a head-to-head configuration, and a hydrophobic cofactor binding patch (AF2) on its ligand binding domain that is endowed to recognize a broader range of LXXLL-like motifs compared to other nuclear receptors, including within AR’s own N-terminus. Moreover, AR adopts a non-constitutive and dynamic mode of dimerization that can be fine-tuned through DNA and cofactor binding. Recent work has invoked this structural plasticity as an underlying factor that drives the shift in AR’s cistrome from a pro-differentiation to pro-proliferative master regulator in the context of cancer, allowing AR to bind primarily half sites and other degenerate sequences. Additional questions pertaining to how AR conformations are altered by post-translational modifications, or domain alternation induced by the N-terminus in the presence or absence of DNA require higher resolution structural models and thus remain elusive given AR’s “shape-shifting” abilities, posing challenges for current structural methodologies, including x-ray crystallography and cryo-EM. In conclusion, as resistance mechanisms to the best AR targeted therapies continue to emerge81, structural biologists will undoubtedly continue to discover new modes of AR activation and inhibition for decades to come.
