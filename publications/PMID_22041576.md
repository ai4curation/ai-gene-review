## Full Text

Abstract

The ebi gene of Drosophila melanogaster has been implicated in diverse signaling pathways, cellular functions and developmental processes. However, a thorough genetic analysis of this gene has been lacking and the true extent of its biological roles is unclear. Here, we characterize eleven ebi mutations and find that ebi has a novel role in promoting growth of the wing imaginal disc: viable combinations of mutant alleles give rise to adults with small wings. Wing discs with reduced EBI levels are correspondingly small and exhibit downregulation of Notch target genes. Furthermore, we show that EBI colocalizes on polytene chromosomes with Smrter (SMR), a transcriptional corepressor, and Suppressor of Hairless (SU(H)), the primary transcription factor involved in Notch signaling. Interestingly, the mammalian orthologs of ebi , transducin β-like 1 (TBL1) and TBL-related 1 (TBLR1), function as corepressor/coactivator exchange factors and are required for transcriptional activation of Notch target genes. We hypothesize that EBI acts to activate (de-repress) transcription of Notch target genes important for Drosophila wing growth by functioning as a corepressor/coactivator exchange factor for SU(H).

Keywords: ebi , wing, growth regulation, Notch pathway, TBL1, TBLR1, corepressor/coactivator exchange factor

Introduction

Drosophila melanogaster EBI functions in diverse signaling pathways and cellular processes. It was originally characterized as a positive regulator of Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling during embryogenesis and adult eye development. 1 Subsequent studies have demonstrated a role for EBI in cell cycle regulation, 2 Notch pathway activation, 3 , 4 and Wingless (WG) signal transduction, 5 , 6 while genetic interactions implicate EBI in basolateral junction signalling, 7 Ret signal transduction, 8 and embryonic crystal cell development. 9 Nonetheless, a thorough genetic characterization of ebi has not hitherto been performed and the full extent of its biological influence remains unclear.

EBI is a nuclear-localized protein of 700 amino acids comprising a LisH domain 10 and an F box-like motif 1 , 2 at the amino terminus, and a series of WD repeats 11 in the carboxy-terminal half ( Fig. 1A and B ). Several reports describe a transcriptional role for EBI, although the nature of this role is unresolved. Some studies cast EBI as a positive regulator of gene transcription. For example, EBI associates physically with the 88 kDa isoform of the Tramtrack (TTK88) transcriptional repressor and promotes its proteasome-dependent degradation in response to an EGFR signal. 1 , 2 EBI also forms a complex with the Suppressor of Hairless (SU(H)) transcription factor and the Smrter (SMR) corepressor. 3 Here, similar to above, EGFR signaling acts via EBI in a process requiring the proteasome to alleviate SMR-SU(H)-mediated repression of target genes. 3 , 12 In a third example, EBI is required for transcriptional activation mediated by Armadillo in response to WG stimulation. 5 , 6

Figure 1.

In contrast, other reports implicate EBI in transcriptional repression. Two studies have found that EBI is in a complex with Histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3) and either the Snail or SMYD4 transcriptional repressor. 13 , 14 In both cases, EBI is thought to function with HDAC3 to repress target genes. In a third example, and in contrast to the earlier report in reference 3 , EBI is proposed to mediate EGFR-dependent transcriptional repression as an integral part of an EBI-SMR-SU(H) corepressor complex. 4

Further insights into the mechanism of action of EBI come from studies of the mammalian orthologs, TBL1 and TBLR1. The two mammalian proteins share >90% amino acid identity with each other and >75% identity with Drosophila EBI within the functional domains. TBL1 and TBLR1 are core components of the HDAC3-containing n uclear receptor cor epressor (NCoR) and s ilencing m ediator of r etinoic acid and t hyroid hormone receptor (SMRT) transcriptional corepressor complexes. 15 These associations mirror those found in flies as NCoR and SMRT are the mammalian counterparts of Drosophila SMR, and both SMR and HDAC3 associate with EBI. 3 , 13 , 14 Interestingly, while TBL1 and TBLR1 are required to mediate transcriptional repression by the NCoR/SMRT complex, they are also necessary for ubiquitylation-dependent dissociation of the corepressors upon receipt of an activating signal. 16 In this way, TBL1 and TBLR1 serve as transcriptional corepressor/coactivator exchange factors. Several transcription factors have been shown to require the exchange function of TBL1/TBLR1 in order to activate transcription, including NFκB, CBF1 (the mammalian ortholog of Drosophila SU(H)) and most nuclear receptors. 17 The possibility that Drosophila EBI has bifunctionality similar to mammalian TBL1/TBLR1 is attractive but has not been explored to date.

We originally identified two ebi mutations as enhancers of a small-wing phenotype resulting from inhibition of Insulin Receptor/Phosphoinositide-3-Kinase (InR/PI3K) signalling. 18 Here, we confirm that EBI has a critical role in promoting growth of the wing. We also find that Notch target genes are downregulated in ebi mutant wing discs and that an EBI-SMR-SU(H) complex exists at discrete sites on polytene chromosomes. We hypothesize that EBI facilitates the conversion of SU(H) from a transcriptional repressor to an activator, thereby promoting expression of Notch target genes important for wing growth.

Results

ebi mutations dominantly enhance small-wing phenotypes.

The E-2g 1 and E-2g 2 mutations were isolated as dominant enhancers of a small-wing phenotype induced by inhibition of the InR/PI3K pathway. 18 Both mutations also dominantly enhance the small-wing and wing venation phenotypes caused by antagonizing EGFR signaling in the wing. 18 Subsequent mapping and sequencing (see Materials and Methods) revealed that E-2g 1 and E-2g 2 disrupt the ebi gene and we therefore refer to these mutations as ebi 2g1 and ebi 2g2 . Both mutations result in amino acid substitutions at conserved positions within the WD repeat consensus ( Table 1 and Fig. 1 ). Interestingly, the residues affected by ebi 2g1 and ebi 2g2 are predicted to be hydrogen-bonded together within the fifth β-propeller blade of the EBI protein ( Fig. 1C ). As such, these mutations are expected to have similar deleterious effects on the protein, which is borne out in the similar phenotypes displayed by the mutant animals (see below).

Molecular nature of ebi alleles

We obtained and characterized several additional ebi mutations ( Table 1 and Fig. 1 ; see also Materials and Methods) and tested them in our wing assays. All mutations dominantly enhance the InR/PI3K-sensitized phenotype and most dominantly enhance the EGFR-sensitized phenotypes ( Table 2 ). Importantly, all nine additional ebi alleles fail to complement ebi 2g1 and ebi 2g2 . These data confirm that mutations at the ebi locus are responsible for the observed genetic interactions and suggest that EBI normally acts to promote wing growth.

ebi interacts genetically with vestigial and scalloped .

Both the InR/PI3K-and EGFR-sensitized wing phenotypes alluded to above are the result of GAL4-mediated expression of a UAS-containing transgene. 19 Thus, a trivial explanation of the genetic interactions between ebi and the InR/PI3K and EGFR pathways might be that reduced ebi dosage results in increased expression or stability of the GAL4 protein, leading to hyper-activation of the UAS transgenes and enhancement of the phenotypes. To test this possibility and to further explore the role of ebi in wing development, we tested for genetic interactions between ebi and vestigial ( vg ) or scalloped ( sd ), two genes which are critical for wing growth and patterning. 20

Flies heterozygous for both the vg 1 mutation and an ebi mutation ( ebi 2g1 , ebi 2g2 or ebi E4 ) display wing notching and wing venation phenotypes that are not observed in any single heterozygote (data not shown). More dramatic effects are observed in sd ETX4 hemizygous male flies that are also heterozygous for an ebi mutation ( Fig. 2A–F and Table 2 ). In the most severe cases, the wing is reduced to little more than a stump ( Fig. 2F ). These data provide additional evidence that ebi has an important function during wing growth/development and indicate that ebi was identified in our original screen for this reason rather than indirect effects on GAL4.

Figure 2.

Characterization of ebi mutations using ebi P7 .

We used the weak ebi P7 allele 1 , 3 to better characterize the developmental role of ebi and to assess the relative strength of the eleven ebi mutant alleles we had collected ( Table 2 ). No or very few trans-heterozygous progeny are observed when the following mutations were combined with ebi P7 : ebi 2g2 , ebi E90 , ebi S602F , ebi 2g1 , ebi CCS-8 . These are therefore the strongest alleles. Four other alleles— ebi E4 , ebi delta1 , ebi W418X and ebi k16213 —are semi-viable in trans with ebi P7 and are therefore classed as intermediate strength alleles. Finally, ebi WKS-24 and ebi P7 are classed as the weakest alleles as ebi WKS-24 /ebi P7 flies and ebi P7 homozygotes are fully viable. This ranking of ebi alleles based on viability in trans with ebi P7 closely mirrors the strengths of their genetic interactions with sd ETX4 ( Table 2 ). Taken together, these phenotypes and genetic interactions order the eleven ebi mutations into a robust allelic series.

The phenotypic severity of the mutations also correlates with their molecular nature (compare Tables 1 and 2 ). For example, ebi E90 , ebi 2g2 , ebi 2g1 and ebi S602F result in amino acid substitutions at the interfaces between propeller blades that likely disrupt propeller structure and/or stability ( Fig. 1 ). Given that EBI is likely to function as a homo-tetramer, 21 the proteins encoded by these four mutant alleles probably act as dominant negatives when present in the heterozygous condition. All of these alleles are classed as ‘strong’ in the phenotypic analyses. The medium strength alleles include ebi E4 , which was characterized as a null mutation, 1 and two mutations ( ebi delta1 and ebi W418X ) that truncate the protein before or early within the WD repeat domain and are therefore also likely to be null alleles. Finally, the weakest alleles are ebi P7 , which does not affect the coding region, and ebi WKS-24 , which causes an amino acid substitution in the eighth WD repeat that is not predicted to form part of the tertiary structure ( Fig. 1C ).

ebi mutants have disproportionately small wings.

The most obvious phenotype shown by ebi P7 trans-heterozygous escapers is a reduction in wing size ( Fig. 3A–C and Table 2 ). For example, the mean wing area of flies bearing ebi P7 in combination with an intermediate strength ebi allele is 82–86% that of ebi P7 /+ control wings. Cell density measurements reveal that the size of individual wing cells is not significantly reduced in most of these cases, meaning that such modest reductions in wing area are primarily the result of reduced cell number ( Table 2 ). A more severe allelic combination reduces wing size further: the mean area of ebi P7 /ebi 2g1 wings is 72% that of control wings and, in this case, a reduction in cell size makes a significant contribution to the small wing phenotype ( Table 2 ). Gross defects in wing patterning are not apparent in any ebi trans-heterozygous combination.

Figure 3.

Interestingly, the significant reduction in wing size in transheterozygous combinations of weak or intermediate ebi alleles is never accompanied by a significant decrease in the size of other organs or the whole organism. Male trans-heterozygous escapers harboring the strongest ebi alleles occasionally show an overall reduction in body size ( Fig. 3A shows a rare example), but their wings are still disproportionately small.

Other, less penetrant and more variable morphological defects observed in ebi trans-heterozygotes include stunted and ectopic macrochaetae in the notum, notching of the wing margin, slight roughening of the eye, malformed legs, and abnormal growths in the antennal region ( Fig. 3D and E and data not shown).

The results above establish, for the first time, an important and peculiarly specific role for EBI in the growth of the wing. We therefore decided to characterize this requirement earlier in development by examining ebi mutant imaginal wing discs. (Other ebi phenotypes were not examined further).

ebi mutant wing discs are small and show reduced expression of Notch/SU (H) target genes.

First, we examined ebi trans-heterozygous wing discs from wandering stage third instar larvae immunostained for WG ( Fig. 4 ). WG is expressed in two domains in the developing wing pouch ( Fig. 4A ): the ring of expression around the perimeter provides a useful read-out of wing pouch size, while the prominent stripe of WG expression across the middle of the pouch marks the dorsal-ventral (d-v) boundary and is dependent on Notch signalling. 22 , 23 Larvae trans-heterozygous for ebi P7 and an intermediate strength ebi allele ( ebi W418X or ebi E4 ) have visibly smaller wing pouches, though the WG pattern appears normal in most examples ( Fig. 4B and C ). Around 50% of flies with these genotypes survive to adulthood and display the small wing phenotype ( Table 2 ). As expected, larvae that are trans-heterozygous for ebi P7 and a strong ebi allele ( ebi 2g2 , ebi S602F or ebi 2g1 ) show a more severe phenotype: wing pouches are reduced to about a quarter of their normal size and the WG staining is generally weaker with breaks in the d-v stripe of expression ( Fig. 4D–F ). Flies with these genotypes rarely survive to adulthood ( Table 2 ).

Figure 4.

We initially sought to confirm and extend these results by inducing homozygous clones of strong/intermediate strength ebi alleles in wing imaginal discs. However, no or very few ebi E4 , ebi E90 , ebi 2g2 or ebi W418X clones survive to late third instar stage when induced at 48 hours after egg deposition (AED), and recovery could not be improved by inducing clones in a Minute /+ background. These data indicate that ebi is required for cell viability in the wing disc at this stage. Clones induced at 72 hours AED do survive to late third instar stage but were too small to be useful in our experiments.

As an alternative approach, we expressed dsRNA targeted against ebi mRNA to knock-down EBI expression in specific areas of the wing. Several independent UAS-ebi IR transgenic lines were generated and all constructs gave similar results. Whereas expression of UAS-ebi IR using the ubiquitous da-GAL4 driver results in pupal lethality (data not shown), expression with either engrailed (en)-GAL4 or MS1096-GAL4 results in viable flies that exhibit a reduction of overall wing size ( Fig. 5A–D ). en-GAL4 UAS-ebi IR wings are reduced to 76–81% of the size of controls, with a greater reduction in the posterior compartment where en-GAL4 is expressed ( Fig. 5A and C ). Similarly, MS1096-GAL4 UAS-ebi IR wings are 69–78% the size of control wings, though these wings curve upwards owing to the predominant expression of MS1096-GAL4 in the dorsal compartment of the wing disc ( Fig. 5B and D ). These significant reductions in wing size occur in the absence of gross patterning defects, consistent with the results obtained with ebi P7 trans-heterozygotes.

Figure 5.

We then examined third instar wing discs from these animals to assess: (i) EBI expression levels, (ii) the size of the prospective wing pouch and (iii) activation of the Notch pathway ( Fig. 5E–G ). As expected, EBI levels are specifically reduced in the posterior of en-GAL4 UAS-ebi IR discs ( Fig. 5F ) and are reduced to lower levels in the dorsal half of the wing pouch compared to the ventral half in MS1096-GAL4 UAS-ebi IR discs ( Fig. 5G ). Thus, in both cases, we could assess the effect of knock-down of EBI in one half of the wing pouch with the other half acting as a convenient internal control.

We used the vg quadrant enhancer reporter ( vg QE -lacZ ) in these experiments as a measure of wing pouch size. 24 Expression of UAS-ebi IR using either GAL4 driver reduces the overall size of the wing pouch compared to controls, with the half of the pouch targeted for RNAi being more severely affected than the internal control ( Fig. 5E′–G′ ). This is consistent with the adult phenotypes ( Fig. 5A–D ) and the trans-heterozygous mutant discs ( Fig. 4 ).

The interruptions observed in the d-v stripe of WG expression in ebi trans-heterozygotes ( Fig. 4 ) suggested that a reduction in functional EBI compromises Notch signaling in the wing disc. 22 , 23 Indeed, this idea is supported by the strong genetic interactions observed between ebi and genes encoding Notch pathway components in wing assays ( Fig. 2G and H and data not shown). We wished to examine this hypothesis further using an alternative approach and used the m β 1.5-lacZ reporter 25 to examine the status of Notch/SU(H)-mediated transcription in UAS-ebi IR discs. m β 1.5-lacZ is normally expressed in a stripe at the d-v margin of the wing disc and in broad regions on either side that correspond to the intervein regions of the adult wing 25 ( Fig. 5E″ ). Significantly, m β 1.5-lacZ is reduced when EBI levels are knocked down by RNAi ( Fig. 5F″ and G″ ). The effect is relatively weak using en-GAL4 , where the reduction in m β 1.5-lacZ expression is restricted to the prospective wing blade regions in the posterior half of the disc ( Fig. 5F″ ), while a clear reduction in reporter activity is observed throughout the dorsal half of the wing pouch when UAS-ebi IR is expressed using the stronger MS1096-GAL4 driver ( Fig. 5G″ ).

Our observations in ebi mutant larvae demonstrate that EBI is required to promote growth and Notch/SU(H) signaling in the developing wing pouch, and lead us to infer that EBI may play a role in upregulating Notch/SU(H) target genes required for wing growth. This conclusion is consistent with two previous observations. First, ebi mutant wing discs were found to have reduced expression of the Notch ligand Delta at the d-v boundary, 3 which supports the idea of EBI having a positive influence on Notch pathway activation. Second, the wing pouch of discs mutant for Notch or Su(H) was found to be abnormally small, 22 , 26 which demonstrates that reduced Notch signaling (in our case via mutation of ebi ) does indeed retard wing growth.

EBI colocalizes with SU(H) and SMR on polytene chromosomes.

Previous work has shown that EBI and SU(H), together with the transcriptional corepressor SMR, can be co-immunoprecipitated when transiently expressed in S2 cells, 3 suggesting that the three proteins exist in a complex. However, these associations have not previously been examined in conditions where the proteins are expressed at their normal endogenous levels in vivo. We sought to gain further insight into the relationship between EBI and SU(H) by immunostaining polytene chromosomes from larval salivary glands.

We find that EBI is localized to many discrete sites on polytene chromosomes ( Fig. 6A ), demonstrating that EBI is an abundant chromosome-associated protein in vivo. SMR is also detected at specific sites ( Fig. 6B ), as demonstrated previously. 27 Strikingly, EBI and SMR appear to completely colocalize ( Fig. 6D ). SU(H) could also be detected at several distinct chromosomal sites ( Fig. 6C ), many of which are also positive for EBI/SMR ( Fig. 6E and F , open arrowheads). Nevertheless, SU(H) is also clearly detected at sites that lack EBI/SMR (arrows in Fig. 6 ), and conversely, EBI/SMR is detected at sites that apparently lack SU(H) (solid arrowheads in Fig. 6 ).

Figure 6.

The complete colocalization of EBI and SMR suggests they are obligate partners on DNA, which would mean that EBI is a constitutive component of the SMR corepressor complex. The many cases where the EBI-SMR complex also colocalizes with SU(H) probably represent the complex repressing transcription of SU(H) target genes. The existence of SU(H)-negative, EBI-SMR-positive sites indicates that EBI-SMR can be recruited to DNA via factors other than SU(H), which is consistent with previous reports in references 27 and 28 (also see Introduction). Sites that are SU(H)-positive but EBI-SMR-negative could be examples of SU(H)-mediated transcriptional activation where EBI-SMR has been lost from the complex. Alternatively, they may be cases of SU(H) repressing transcription independently of EBI-SMR; for example, sites where SU(H) is bound to other corepressors such as Hairless, CtBP or Groucho. 29

Discussion

EBI promotes wing growth.

The ebi gene has been implicated in several different developmental processes but a function in promoting wing growth has not been described. We originally identified ebi mutations as dominant enhancers of a small-wing phenotype, 18 suggesting a growth-promoting role for the wild type gene product. In this report, we have confirmed through genetic analyses that ebi is indeed required by the developing wing to attain its normal size. We found that ebi mutant wings comprise fewer cells than controls, though whether this is the result of decreased cell proliferation and/or increased apoptosis during wing development remains to be determined. The size of ebi mutant wing cells is also reduced in the most severe cases, but again, the root cause of this effect is unclear at this time.

The most striking phenotype of viable ebi mutants is the disproportionate reduction in wing size—the size of other organs is not similarly decreased if affected at all. It is possible that the two-dimensional nature of the adult wing exaggerates the size reduction, while reductions in the size of other structures such as the eye, head, legs or body are less apparent. However, the strong genetic interactions between ebi and the vg and sd genes, which are specifically required for wing and haltere growth and development, 20 support a prominent role for EBI in wing growth. This specificity is not the result of wing-specific expression as EBI is widely expressed in larvae, 1 but it could be explained if EBI is a target of signals predominantly active in the wing and/or if EBI promotes expression of genes that are particularly important for wing development. Alternatively, the fact that Su(H) expression is relatively high in the wing pouch region of wing imaginal discs 26 could explain the sensitivity of this organ to ebi dosage, given the involvement of EBI in Notch-SU(H) signaling (discussed below).

While our data reveal a growth-promoting role of EBI, Boulton et al. 2 reported that EBI inhibits cell cycle progression. These contradictory observations may be explained by tissue/stage-specific roles of EBI: its role in preventing S phase entry was characterized in the embryonic nervous system and the developing eye, whereas our results describe a positive role for EBI in the growth of the wing.

EBI potentiates Notch signaling in vivo.

Several lines of evidence now indicate that EBI influences Notch signal transduction. First, ebi interacts genetically with genes encoding core components of the Notch pathway 3 (this work). Second, ebi mutants exhibit supernumerary macrochaetae in the notum, occasional notching of the wing margin and a reduction in the size of the pouch region of the wing imaginal disc (this work), phenotypes that are reminiscent of Notch pathway mutants. 22 , 26 , 30 , 31 Third, mutation of ebi results in downregulation of the Notch target genes wg and E(spl)m β in wing imaginal discs (this work) and cut in eye imaginal discs. 3 Fourth, EBI interacts physically with SU(H), 3 which is the key transcription factor downstream of Notch, and colocalizes with it on polytene chromosomes (this work). Fifth, EBI orthologs are required for Notch-dependent gene activation in mammalian cells. 17 These observations indicate that EBI potentiates Notch signaling in vivo.

Admittedly, ebi mutants do not display canonical Notch loss-of-function phenotypes, such as consistent notching of the wing margin, additional wing vein material, an increased density of notal microchaetae, or an embryonic neurogenic phenotype. Neither has ebi been identified in whole genome screens for Notch pathway regulators. 32 – 34 This suggests that EBI may only be relevant to Notch signal transduction in particular contexts. For example, SU(H) may form distinct corepressor complexes of which only a subset involve the EBI-SMR complex. 29 In this case, genetic manipulation of ebi will affect only Notch target genes regulated by SMR, and will therefore result in a correspondingly narrow subset of phenotypes.

Is EBI a corepressor/coactivator exchange factor for SU(H)?

Work by Tsuda and colleagues has shown that EBI can form a tri-partite complex with SU(H) and the transcriptional corepressor SMR. 3 They suggest that, in the presence of an EGFR signal, an EBI-SMR corepressor complex out-competes the coactivation complex containing the Notch intra-cellular domain (Notch ICD ), and thereby represses Notch/SU(H) target genes such as charlatan . 4 Our polytene data are consistent with this model: EBI appears to be a constitutive component of the DNA-bound SMR corepressor complex, and EBI-SMR frequently colocalizes with SU(H) where the three proteins presumably act together to repress transcription of target genes.

However, a model in which EBI is an obligate transcriptional corepressor of SU(H) does not fit easily with the evidence that EBI has a positive role within the Notch pathway, or the observations that EBI promotes gene expression downstream of other signaling pathways. 1 , 2 , 6 Moreover, such a model does not take account of the evidence from the mammalian system.

CBF1 is the mammalian ortholog of SU(H), and NCoR and SMRT are the mammalian counterparts of Drosophila SMR. As in Drosophila, the NCoR and SMRT corepressors associate with CBF1 to mediate repression of Notch target genes. 35 CBF1/SU(H) is a bifunctional transcription factor: 29 it represses genes in the absence of Notch ICD , and activates them in the presence of Notch ICD . Significantly, TBL1 and TBLR1, the mammalian orthologs of EBI, are core components of the NCoR and SMRT corepressor complexes, but are also necessary to convert CBF1 from a repressor to an activator upon stimulation by Notch. 17 TBL1 and TBLR1 recruit the proteosome to the promoter where they mediate ubiquitylation-dependent dismissal of the NCoR or SMRT corepressor complex.

Taking account of all available data, we propose the following model. In the absence of an activating signal, EBI is an integral part of a complex containing the SMR corepressor and SU(H), and this complex represses target genes. Upon activation, EBI participates in the dissociation or degradation of the corepressor(s) via the proteosome, and facilitates the recruitment of coactivators, including Notch ICD , resulting in transcription of Notch/SU(H) target genes—in our case, genes required to promote growth of the Drosophila wing. The identity of the signal(s) that activate the exchange function of EBI in the context of the developing wing is not known, but based on our work and that of others, EGFR or InR/PI3K or Notch itself are all excellent candidates.

In summary, we hypothesize that EBI has both a negative and positive function in transcriptional regulation. Such bifunctionality is unlikely to be specific to regulation of SU(H), and we suspect it is also applicable to the other transcription factors with which EBI has been shown to associate, such as TTK88, Armadillo, Snail and SMYD4. 1 , 2 , 13 , 14 This view could explain the apparently contradictory functions ascribed to EBI in the literature as, depending on the biological context, either the positive or negative transcriptional role of EBI will be evident.

Materials and Methods

ebi mutations.

E-2g 1 (FBal0190862) and E-2g 2 (FBal0191945) were initially identified as dominant enhancers of a small-wing phenotype and defined a lethal complementation group on chromosome 2. 18 The E-2g 1 -bearing chromosome was used to map the locus as described in reference 18 . Meiotic recombination mapping using visible markers and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) delimited the E-2g -containing region to approximately 2 Mb at the tip of 2L (computed cytology 21A1-22B8). We then used deficiency chromosomes affecting this region to map E-2g 1 and E-2g 2 by lethal complementation analysis. (The enhancer mutation and the lethal locus cosegregated in all recombinants examined, so we were reasonably confident that these traits mapped to the same gene). Df(2L)al (FBab0001692), a well characterized deficiency with breakpoints 21C1;21C7 and which uncovers 25 genes, failed to complement the lethality of both mutations. Available stocks containing mutations in candidate genes were obtained and ebi k16213 (FBal0064059) was found to be lethal in trans to both E-2g 1 and E-2g 2 . Both E-2g mutations failed to complement all other ebi mutations obtained, confirming allelism. Finally, we sequenced the ebi gene (located at 21C2) in both mutants and found point mutations resulting in amino acid substitutions in each case ( Table 1 and Fig. 1 ). Thus E-2g 1 and E-2g 2 were renamed ebi 2g1 and ebi 2g2 , respectively.

The homozygous lethal P{lacW}ebi k16213 line 36 was obtained from the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center (FBst0011166). Crosses with the weak ebi P7 mutation (FBal0249412) revealed that the P{lacW}ebi k16213 chromosome contained a second-site lethal mutation. This was removed by recombination, although the ‘cleaned’ P{lacW}ebi k16213 chromosome remained homozygous lethal.

ebi E4 (FBal0096973), ebi E9 0 (FBal0096972) and ebi P7 have been described in reference 1 ; ebi E4 and ebi E90 were obtained from Larry Zipursky, and ebi P7 from Leo Tsuda. ebi P7 is an imprecise excision of the P{lacW}ebi k16213 insertion, 1 although its molecular characterization has not previously been reported. Through PCR and DNA sequencing, we found that the ebi P7 chromosome retains an approximately 2.5 kb remnant of the original ∼10.7 kb P{lacW} element with both the 5′ and 3′ P{lacW} sequences intact, suggesting that the ebi P7 excision is an internal deletion of P{lacW}ebi k16213 of approximately 8.2 kb. The original ebi P7 chromosome we obtained contained a second-site lethal (presumably the same one present in the P{lacW}ebi k16213 parental chromosome) and was homozygous lethal. We removed this second-site lethal by recombination, after which the ebi P7 chromosome was homozygous viable.

ebi S602F , ebi W418X and ebi delta1 were obtained from Pascal Heitzler. ebi S602F is identical to the ebi CC3 allele (FBal0119260, 2 ). ebi delta1 is an 8 bp deletion starting in codon 294 which changes the reading frame and introduces a new stop codon at codon 334, which would truncate the protein before the WD repeat region ( Table 1 and Fig. 1 ). ( ebi delta1 is similar, but not identical to the ebi CC4 mutation 2 ). ebi W418X is a previously undescribed mutation that converts codon 418 to a stop codon, thereby truncating the protein within the 2 nd WD repeat. (We were unable to obtain a stock harboring the ebi CC1 mutation). 2

ebi CCS-6 (FBal0155358), ebi CCS-8 (FBal0155357) and ebi WKS-24 (FBal0155351) were obtained from the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center and were originally generated by Exelixis Inc. Their molecular characterization has not previously been reported. The ebi CCS-6 stock contained viable homozygotes, and ebi CCS-6 /ebi E90 and ebi CCS-6 /ebi 2g1 trans-heterozygotes are completely viable and aphenotypic. We concluded that the ebi mutation in this stock is either extremely weak or has been lost, and ebi CCS-6 was not studied further. In contrast, ebi WKS-24 and ebi CCS-8 are each lethal in combination with ebi 2g1 or ebi E90 , and DNA sequencing found missense or nonsense mutations in the ebi coding region ( Table 1 and Fig. 1 ).

All ebi mutations were recombined with FRT40A and balanced with In(2LR)Gla or CyO . The homozygous lethality of ebi k16213 , ebi WKS-24 , ebi E4 , ebi E90 , ebi 2g2 and ebi W418X FRT40A recombinants could be rescued with an ebi genomic rescue construct (FBtp0010759 1 ), thus demonstrating that these chromosomes lacked secondary lethal loci. These chromosomes were used to make ebi mutant clones in the wing imaginal disc.

Seven independent UAS-ebi IR lines were established with insertions on chromosomes X, 2 and 3. Based on the relative strengths of phenotypes observed with the MS1096-GAL4 (FBti0002374) driver line, insertions f6 and f28 were classified as strong expressers, insertions m17 and f5 as medium expressers and m7, m8 and m12 as weak expressers. Insertion f5 on chromosome X was recombined with MS1096-GAL4 , and insertion f6 on chromosome 2 was recombined with en-GAL4 (FBtp0055689) for the experiments shown in Figure 6 .

Phenotypic analyses.

Flies were maintained and crossed as described in reference 18 . Wing preparations, wing size measurements and wing cell density measurements were performed as described in reference 37 .

Molecular biology.

Isolation of genomic DNA from flies, primer design, PCRs, SNP identification and genotyping, DNA sequencing, and sequence analysis were performed as described in references 18 and 37 . For the UAS-ebi IR transgenics, a 553 bp region within the 2 nd exon of ebi was amplified and inserted in the pWIZ vector 38 as a head-to-head inverted repeat in a two-step cloning procedure. The insert was verified by DNA sequencing and used to transform flies using standard methods. Primers and SNPs are available upon request.

Immunofluorescence.

Imaginal disc and polytene chromosome dissection and immunolabeling were performed as described in reference 39 . Primary antibodies used were: mouse anti-WG 1:500 (Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank), mouse anti-EBI 1:1,000, 2 rabbit anti-βgal 1:10,000 (Cappell), rabbit anti-SMR 1:100, 27 and rat anti-SU(H) 1:100. 40 Alexa Fluor secondary antibodies and TO-PRO-3 were obtained from Invitrogen. Imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 510 confocal microscope. Images were processed in ImageJ and assembled using Adobe Illustrator.

Bioinformatics.

The EBI protein sequence was obtained from FlyBase 41 and functional domains were predicted using Pfam, 42 Prosite 43 and SMART. 44 Results from SMART are presented herein.
