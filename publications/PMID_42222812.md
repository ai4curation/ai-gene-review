# Bridging Manual and Computational Approaches: The Pseudophosphatase Scanner for Genome-Wide Fungal Pseudophosphatome Analysis.
**Authors:** Pongpom M, Wattanasombat S, Aphiwongcharoen C, Limsamutpet K, Wangsanut T

Pseudophosphatases are proteins with mutations in their catalytic motifs,
resulting in the predicted loss of enzymatic activity. Although
pseudophosphatases are established regulators of various signaling pathways in
humans and other metazoans, their biological roles in fungi remain largely
unexplored. Identifying fungal-specific pseudophosphatases is particularly
important because they control fungal growth, development, and virulence through
noncatalytic mechanisms and could represent selective antifungal targets due to
the absence of close human homologues. Here, we present a comprehensive overview
of fungal pseudophosphatases identified across all major phosphatase families.
To compile a robust data set, we integrated information from literature and
databases as well as systematically scanned the phosphatomes of the human
pathogenic yeast Cryptococcus neoformans, the plant pathogen Fusarium
graminearum, and the neglected pathogenic fungus Talaromyces marneffei. We
identified candidate pseudophosphatases and assessed their conservation through
BLAST analysis across humans, true yeasts, filamentous ascomycetes, and
basidiomycetes. We classified pseudophosphatase candidates into 10 distinct
groups, including three groups (CC1-type Oca, CC1-type Yvh1, and HAD-type NIF)
that appear to be fungal-specific with no human homologues and exhibit
lineage-specific features. Available evidence indicates that fungal
pseudophosphatases contribute to signaling pathways that regulate development,
metabolism, stress responses, and virulence. In addition, we developed the
Pseudophosphatase Scanner tool as a post-HMM (Hidden Markov Model) analysis
pipeline to enable genome-wide pseudophosphatase detection. By combining HMM
scan-based fold assignment with motif-level pattern matching, the
Pseudophosphatase Scanner distinguishes canonical motifs, relaxed variants, and
degenerated fold remnants, facilitating functional interpretation of phosphatase
active sites at the sequence level. The Pseudophosphatase Scanner is accessible
both as a Python script for local execution and as a Google Colab notebook,
offering a point-and-click interface for cloud-based analysis. This study
provides a catalog of fungal pseudophosphatases and a bioinformatics platform
for efficient large-scale pseudophosphatase discovery.

Pseudophosphatase Scanner PipelineThe Pseudophosphatase
Scanner pipeline was implemented as a Python script operating in four
main modules, as follows. First, the tool collects the necessary input
files, including the hmmscan --tblout output from Phosphatase Scanner
pipeline, the original FASTA file containing the predicted proteome,
and designated output files in FASTA and TSV formats. Inputs can be
provided either interactively or via upload through the Google Colab
interface. Second, the script parses the HMM scan results; filtering
hits based on a defined E-value cutoff and annotating
each sequence with its predicted phosphatase fold family. For each
sequence, only the top scoring HMM hit, determined by the lowest E-value and highest score, is retained, and fold family
information is added to the corresponding FASTA header. Third, for
all sequences with assigned fold families, motif-specific regular
expression patterns are applied to identify canonical motifs, relaxed
variants, and degenerate or remnant motifs, allowing precise localization
of conserved residues. Finally, all resultsincluding sequence
ID, sequence, fold family assignment with HMM confidence metrics,
motif pattern, match type, and positional dataare summarized
in a comprehensive output table that records all motif matches as
well as explicit cases where no motifs are detected.Also, the
pipeline additionally generates results in HTML format, allowing users
to easily visualize and explore the data through a web browser using
an intuitive point-and-click interface. The source code for the Pseudophosphatase
Scanner is openly available at Github (see data availability).

To provide an overview
of the fungal pseudophosphatase landscape identified in this study,
we summarized the major findings in Figure
, including their overall prevalence across
species, fold-group classification, evolutionary distribution, and
reported biological functions. In the selected fungi, pseudophosphatases
were identified within the CC1–2, PPPL, PPM, HAD, and HP phosphatase

belonged to the CC1–2 folds (4 out of 10 groups). BLASTP searches

Pseudophosphatase and Phosphatase ActivityMutations
in conserved phosphatase signature motifs are often used to identify
pseudophosphatases; however, available experimental evidence indicates
that motif degeneration does not uniformly predict loss of enzymatic
function. In fungi, several proteins with active-site substitutions
lack detectable phosphatase activity. In S. cerevisiae, the phosphofructokinase PFK26 is inactive due to a histidine to
serine substitution in the RH motif, and restoring this His residue
rescues phosphatase activity (Supporting Figure S2A). Likewise, YLR345W is predicted
to be catalytically inactive due to a histidine to serine at the RH
motif, although this has not yet been experimentally confirmed (Supporting Figure S2B). In addition, S. cerevisiae Oca1 lacks activity against the generic
substrate pNPP.
Conversely, some
evolutionarily conserved “pseudophosphatase” families
retain measurable phosphatase activity despite noncanonical motifs,
indicating functional repurposing rather than simple inactivation.
Vip1/Asp1 homologues display substitutions in RHGXRXP and HD motifs
(RHAXRXP and HI) (Supporting Figure S2C), yet S. pombe Asp1 retains 1-phosphatase activity toward inositol pyrophosphates.
,
 Similarly, the phosphatase activity has been reported in S. cerevisiae Cyr1, the homologue of human PHLPP-family
proteins (PPM-type), despite containing degenerated metal-binding
motifs (Supporting Figure S3;). Tim50 further illustrates lineage-dependent
divergence: fungal Tim50 lacks canonical DXDX­(T/V) residues and shows
no phosphatase activity in S. cerevisiae, whereas the human homologue has been reported to exhibit phosphatase
activity under acidic conditions, and kinetoplastid homologues retain
a fully conserved motif and are catalytically active (Supporting Figure S4).
−

 Finally, Ppn2
is a fungal-specific pseudophosphatase that retains enzymatic activity,
with an aspartate residue in its altered motif required for endopolyphosphatase
function (Figure
). Together, these examples show that active-site
mutations can be associated with loss of activity, retained activity,
or lineage-specific functional divergence.

Concluding RemarksAdvancements in bioinformatics tools,
combined with multiomics
approaches, have deepened our understanding of pseudophosphatases.
Despite these advances, significant challenges persist in studying
pseudophosphatases. For example, the catalytic motifs of pseudophosphatases
are often highly divergent, complicating both evolutionary analyses
and the identification of novel members. Importantly, degeneration
of these motifs does not always equate to a loss of catalytic activity;
in some cases, it enables the evolution of new molecular functions.
As highlighted in this study, pseudophosphatases exhibit a range of
catalytic capacities, from being entirely inactive to retaining full
enzymatic function. Regardless of their catalytic status, pseudophosphatases
are far from being functionally “dead”; they play essential
roles in various cellular processes. In fungi, pseudophosphatases
are commonly involved in regulating signaling pathways, growth and
development, metabolism, environmental stress responses, and virulence.
Here, we present the first comprehensive catalog of fungal pseudophosphatases,
identified through literature mining, manual curation, and bioinformatic
tools such as the Pseudophosphatase Predictor Server and BLAST-based
analyses. While our study focuses on fungi, it is likely that many
pseudophosphatases remain undiscovered across other kingdoms of life.
Importantly, functional characterization of fungal pseudophosphatases
could facilitate the development of pharmacological strategies targeting
these proteins. The Oca family, for instance, represents a particularly
promising drug target due to its fungal lineage-specific distribution
and lack of conservation in humans. These features make Oca pseudophosphatases
attractive candidates for antifungal therapeutics targeting yeast
infections, with minimal off-target effects in humans.In summary,
our findings highlight the biological importance of
fungal pseudophosphatases and underscore the need for computational
approaches that can systematically map pseudophosphatomes across species.
Pseudophosphatase Scanner developed in our study is powerful because

Concluding Remarks

Advancements in bioinformatics tools,
combined with multiomics
approaches, have deepened our understanding of pseudophosphatases.
Despite these advances, significant challenges persist in studying
pseudophosphatases. For example, the catalytic motifs of pseudophosphatases
are often highly divergent, complicating both evolutionary analyses
and the identification of novel members. Importantly, degeneration
of these motifs does not always equate to a loss of catalytic activity;
in some cases, it enables the evolution of new molecular functions.
As highlighted in this study, pseudophosphatases exhibit a range of
catalytic capacities, from being entirely inactive to retaining full
enzymatic function. Regardless of their catalytic status, pseudophosphatases
are far from being functionally “dead”; they play essential
roles in various cellular processes. In fungi, pseudophosphatases
are commonly involved in regulating signaling pathways, growth and
development, metabolism, environmental stress responses, and virulence.
Here, we present the first comprehensive catalog of fungal pseudophosphatases,
identified through literature mining, manual curation, and bioinformatic
tools such as the Pseudophosphatase Predictor Server and BLAST-based
analyses. While our study focuses on fungi, it is likely that many
pseudophosphatases remain undiscovered across other kingdoms of life.
Importantly, functional characterization of fungal pseudophosphatases
could facilitate the development of pharmacological strategies targeting
these proteins. The Oca family, for instance, represents a particularly
promising drug target due to its fungal lineage-specific distribution
and lack of conservation in humans. These features make Oca pseudophosphatases
attractive candidates for antifungal therapeutics targeting yeast
infections, with minimal off-target effects in humans.

our findings highlight the biological importance of
fungal pseudophosphatases and underscore the need for computational
approaches that can systematically map pseudophosphatomes across species.
