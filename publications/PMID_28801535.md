## Full Text

Abstract

ISWI chromatin remodelers mobilize nucleosomes to control DNA accessibility. Complexes isolated to date pair one of six regulatory subunits with one of two highly similar ATPases. However, we find that each endogenously expressed ATPase co‐purifies with every regulatory subunit, substantially increasing the diversity of ISWI complexes, and we additionally identify BAZ2B as a novel, seventh regulatory subunit. Through reconstitution of catalytically active human ISWI complexes, we demonstrate that the new interactions described here are stable and direct. Finally, we profile the nucleosome remodeling functions of the now expanded family of ISWI chromatin remodelers. By revealing the combinatorial nature of ISWI complexes, we provide a basis for better understanding ISWI function in normal settings and disease.

Keywords: ACF, BAZ, chromatin remodelers, ISWI ATPase, SMARCA

Subject Categories: Chromatin, Epigenetics, Genomics & Functional Genomics

Introduction

In eukaryotic nuclei, DNA wraps around octamers of the core histone proteins H2A, H2B, H3, and H4 to form nucleosomes, the repetitive unit of chromatin. DNA‐templated processes such as DNA replication, transcription, and repair require remodeling of chromatin to access DNA. In concert with the post‐translational modification of histones, protein complexes known as chromatin remodelers couple ATP hydrolysis to changes in histone–DNA contacts to mobilize, assemble, or evict nucleosomes, thus modulating DNA accessibility 1 , 2 .

Human chromatin remodelers of the ISWI family pair one of two ATPase subunits, SMARCA1 (SNF2L) or SMARCA5 (SNF2H), to one of six regulatory subunits: BAZ1A (ACF1), BAZ1B (WSTF), BAZ2A (TIP5), BPTF, CECR2, or RSF1, to form the six core ISWI complexes characterized to date 3 (Table 1 ). In addition, some ISWI heterodimeric core complexes can co‐purify with auxiliary subunits, including the histone‐fold proteins CHRAC‐15/17 and histone‐binding protein RBBP4 that stably bind to the BAZ1A–SMARCA5 and BPTF–SMARCA1 complexes, respectively 4 , 5 .

Each ISWI‐type ATPase has been described to bind only a subset of the available regulatory subunits. The association of SMARCA1 and BPTF forms the NURF (nucleosome remodeling factor) complex, which is the founding member of the ISWI family of chromatin remodelers 5 , 6 , 7 . NURF plays a critical role in transcriptional regulation downstream of multiple prominent signaling pathways and is required for proper development 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , as reviewed 12 . Intriguingly, BPTF has been shown to bind stably to a splice variant of SMARCA1, termed SMARCA1.13, which is devoid of enzymatic activity 13 , yet the role of this inactive complex remains unclear. More recently, SMARCA1 was found to also associate with CECR2, a protein required for proper neurulation in mice, to form a heterodimeric complex named CERF (CECR2‐containing remodeling factor) 14 .

SMARCA5, the other ISWI‐type ATPase found in humans, is present in a broader range of characterized complexes. SMARCA5 binds to either BAZ1A or BAZ1B in a mutually exclusive manner to form the complexes known as ACF (ATP‐utilizing chromatin assembly and remodeling factor) or WICH (WSTF‐ISWI chromatin remodeling complex), respectively 4 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 . Both ACF and WICH appear to facilitate DNA replication 21 , 22 and promote DNA repair 23 , 24 , 25 . Similarly, the association of RSF1 and SMARCA5 forms RSF (remodeling and spacing factor), which, while initially implicated in transcriptional regulation 19 , 26 , has emerged more recently as a factor in responding to DNA damage 27 , 28 , 29 . Finally, SMARCA5 can bind BAZ2A to form a complex named NoRC (nucleolar remodeling complex) 30 . NoRC promotes genome stability 31 and plays a critical role in repressing ribosomal gene transcription 32 , 33 , 34 .

ISWI complexes continue to serve a paradigmatic role in understanding the biochemical properties and regulation of chromatin remodelers 2 . Uncomplexed ISWI catalytic subunits from multiple species, including human SMARCA5, can reposition nucleosomes without the need of a regulatory subunit, an activity dependent on an intact histone H4 N‐terminal tail and coupled to a transient deformation of the histone octamer 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 . However, the addition of regulatory subunits significantly increases the rate and directs the product distribution of the remodeling reaction 36 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 . The current understanding of the consequences of regulatory subunit incorporation to ISWI‐dependent chromatin remodeling is due largely to study of the ACF complex. When bound to BAZ1A, the ATPase and remodeling activities of SMARCA5 become tightly regulated by the length of extranucleosomal DNA present in the nucleosome substrate (also referred to as “linker” DNA) 48 , 49 . This regulation can be particularly appreciated in the context of mononucleosome substrates. As a mononucleosome is engaged on opposite sides by two ACF complexes 48 , 50 , the histone octamer slides preferentially toward the longer linker DNA and the mononucleosomes assume a “center” configuration at equilibrium, with equivalent lengths of extranucleosomal DNA protruding from each side 46 . In contrast to the known role of BAZ1A in the ACF complex, the effect that other regulatory subunits may have on the regulation of ISWI‐type ATPases remains largely unknown.

Here, we reveal that both human ATPases, SMARCA1 and SMARCA5, form stable complexes with all ISWI regulatory subunits when purified from cells endogenously expressing the components, and we discover that BAZ2B is a novel ISWI regulatory subunit. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the new ISWI complexes described here readily mobilize nucleosomes, and we profile the biochemical functions of the now expanded family of ISWI chromatin remodelers.

Results and Discussion

The discovery of the six specific regulatory subunit–ATPase pairings described above has led to the belief that each regulatory subunit binds preferentially to only one of the two human ISWI ATPases. However, evidence exists that the non‐catalytic subunits BAZ1A, CECR2, and RSF1 may interact with the “non‐preferred” SMARCA family member in a cell type‐dependent fashion 4 , 14 , 20 , 51 . Therefore, we reassessed over the entire family whether there is indeed a preferred ATPase in the various ISWI complexes. To determine which regulatory subunits bound to each human ISWI ATPase, we took an unbiased approach by immunoprecipitating endogenous SMARCA1 or SMARCA5 from HeLa cells and identifying co‐purifying proteins by mass spectrometry (Fig 1 A). To serve as negative controls, we used CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated genome editing to abrogate expression of SMARCA1 or SMARCA5 in HeLa cells (Fig EV1 A and B). Expression of SMARCA1 was completely lost (knockout), while SMARCA5 levels were reduced to trace amounts (knockdown; only detectable by high‐sensitivity mass spectrometry but not Western blotting) (Fig EV1 ). Parallel immunoprecipitation (IP) studies in SMARCA1 ‐knockout and SMARCA5 ‐knockdown cells were used to exclude any proteins that were enriched despite the absence of the relevant ATPase ( Dataset EV1 ). In the remaining sets of interactors, negligible co‐enrichment of SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 was observed, indicating that these two ATPases are not present simultaneously in the same complexes (Fig EV1 C and D). However, with the exception of CECR2 which is not expressed in HeLa cells 52 , we found every known ISWI regulatory subunit and the novel interactor BAZ2B among the most abundant proteins (top 30) co‐immunoprecipitating with SMARCA1 or SMARCA5 (Fig 1 A and Datasets EV1 and EV2 ). We note that despite nuclease treatment, IP experiments are prone to non‐specific interactions through residual contaminating nucleic acid and that trace amounts of the alternative ATPase (< 1% of the targeted ATPase and undetectable by immunoblotting, see Fig EV2 A) were found in IPs of both SMARCA1 and SMARCA5. The abundance of contaminating ATPase was at least one order of magnitude lower than that of the least abundant regulatory subunit (Fig 1 A and Dataset EV1 ). To demonstrate further that the trace amounts of non‐targeted ATPase were not responsible for co‐purifying ISWI regulatory subunits, we immunoprecipitated SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 from engineered cells that do not express or express extremely low levels of the alternative ATPase. Again, we found all ISWI regulatory subunits, with the exception of CECR2, co‐purifying with both SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 (Fig EV1 E and Dataset EV3 ). A complementary experiment from HEK293 cells showed CECR2 present in both SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 immune complexes (Fig EV2 A). Consistent with these observations, precipitation with antibodies against the regulatory subunits BAZ1A, BPTF, or CECR2 enriched both ATPases, as detected by Western blotting (Fig EV2 B). Moreover, both SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 co‐purified with BAZ1A and BAZ1B in immune complexes from HeLa and NTERA‐2 cells, as analyzed by mass spectrometry (Fig EV2 C and Dataset EV4 ), indicating that incorporation of the non‐canonical ATPase is observed across multiple cell types.

One might expect that previously described regulatory subunit–ATPase pairings would be preferred over the novel interactions described here. However, we found that the relative recovery of regulatory subunits from the IPs largely, but not perfectly, tracked the mRNA level for that subunit 52 (Fig 1 B). This suggests that abundance is an important factor determining the incorporation of regulatory subunits into ISWI complexes, but these data do not exclude subtle preferences that might be revealed by quantitative K D determinations. The idea that all regulatory subunits can bind to both SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 is sensible given the high identity (~75%) between these two ISWI‐type ATPases. Previously described ISWI complexes were often uncovered by purifying factors responsible for specific chromatin assembly and remodeling activities 7 , 15 , 16 . However, alternative active complexes might have been lost during such purification procedures, and uncovering the full spectrum of possible regulatory subunit–ATPase pairings requires instead the use of an unbiased approach, such as the one employed here.

To test whether the novel regulatory–ATPase interactions discovered by co‐immunoprecipitation were direct, we expressed and purified recombinant human ISWI complexes from insect cells (Fig 2 A). Complexes containing non‐catalytic subunits (with the exception of the 340‐kDa BPTF) and either SMARCA1 or SMARCA5 were obtained by double‐affinity purification (Fig 2 A and B). An earlier study hypothesized that the protein BAZ2B might associate with ISWI‐type ATPases 53 . We found that BAZ2B forms stable complexes with both SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 (which we named BRF‐1 and BRF‐5; see Table 1 ). Additionally, we readily purified the uncomplexed ATPases from this expression system: SMARCA5, SMARCA1, and the catalytic‐dead isoform SMARCA1.13 13 (Fig 2 B). Low yields of pure ISWI complexes hindered stoichiometry quantification, yet band intensity in the denaturing SDS–PAGE appears consistent with 1:1 assemblies for most complexes, as suggested previously 3 . Importantly, complex purification demonstrates that both the previously isolated and novel ISWI complexes described here are formed by direct interactions.

While uncomplexed SMARCA5 is capable of remodeling nucleosomes without directional bias, the BAZ1A–SMARCA5 holocomplex (ACF) has the remarkable ability to slide mononucleosomes to the center of a DNA template 45 , 46 , 49 . The nucleosome remodeling activity of other ISWI complexes is only partially understood, and moreover, the equilibrium product distribution of ISWI complexes containing the alternative ATPase remains entirely unexplored (e.g., BAZ1A–SMARCA1, ACF‐1; Table 1 ). To survey the biochemical activities of the newly expanded family of ISWI complexes, we reconstituted fluorescently labeled, positioned nucleosomes to serve as substrates for sliding reactions (Fig 3 A and Appendix Fig S1 ). Edge‐positioned and center‐positioned nucleosomes were mixed in equimolar ratio and subjected to remodeling in the presence of purified ISWI complexes or uncomplexed catalytic subunits. Nucleosome remodeling (i.e., sliding of the histone octamer on the DNA template) can be followed by monitoring the re‐distribution of nucleosomes to discrete species detected by native gel electrophoresis (Fig 3 A). Consistent with previous observations using similar substrates 46 , 54 , ISWI‐mediated remodeling can produce up to four distinct bands, including those containing edge‐ and center‐positioned nucleosomes. The intensities of these bands can be quantified, providing a direct way to compare individual sliding reactions and product distributions (Figs 3 A–C and EV3 ). As expected 13 , SMARCA1.13 showed no remodeling activity. In contrast, SMARCA5 efficiently slid both edge‐ and center‐positioned nucleosomes to multiple positions, and ACF‐centered nucleosomes as reported previously 46 . We discovered that SMARCA1, like SMARCA5, slid both edge‐ and center‐positioned nucleosomes away from their original location (Fig 3 B and C). For the ISWI holocomplexes, we observed robust nucleosome remodeling with all complexes tested (Fig 3 B and C). This demonstrates that the novel regulatory subunit–ATPase pairings described here produce functional chromatin remodelers (Table 1 ). Band distribution after remodeling was independent of the nucleosome starting position (edge or center). In addition, a time‐course experiment indicated that, at least in the case of BAZ1A‐containing complexes, these product distributions are reached in about 10 min, and extending the reaction time from 30 to 60 min produced equivalent results across all complexes (Figs 3 B and EV4 A and B). Taken together, these observations support the conclusion that the band patterns of Fig 3 B represent equilibrium for each complex. We discovered that, like ACF, the alternative complex ACF‐1 and those complexes pairing BAZ1B, BAZ2A, or BAZ2B to either ATPase have the propensity to center nucleosomes. In contrast, CECR2‐ and RSF1‐containing complexes did not (Fig 3 B and C). Thus, it appears that, in a highly purified system, the regulatory subunits largely dictate the output of the sliding reaction. However, the activities of SMARCA1‐ and SMARCA5‐containing complexes likely are not fully redundant in a cellular context. The spatial and temporal expression patterns of SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 are distinct throughout murine development and in the adult animals, suggesting that these ISWI ATPase paralogs may have non‐overlapping functions 55 . Supporting this notion, a recent publication showed that while both SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 contribute to nucleosome phasing adjacent to several transcription factor binding sites, only SMARCA5 depletion significantly altered chromatin structure in proximity to the key architectural insulator CTCF 56 . Cell‐specific expression of SMARCA5 and SMARCA1, in combination with differential expression of regulatory subunits, may determine which ISWI complex, or subset of complexes, is present in a specific cell population. Additional forms of regulation, such as post‐translational modification or cellular compartmentalization, may fine‐tune the assembly and function of ISWI complexes. Combinatorial, context‐dependent assembly may be a shared trait among chromatin remodelers. For instance, SWI/SNF (BAF) complexes undergo changes in subunit composition during development, so that different cell types carry particular subunit assemblies 57 . It is likely that the assembly of different ISWI chromatin remodelers is coordinated in a similar fashion.

A previous study found that the ISWI regulatory subunit BPTF can stably bind to an isoform of SMARCA1, called SMARCA1.13, which is devoid of ATPase activity 13 . Similarly, using co‐expression in insect cells followed by double‐affinity purification, we discovered that the ISWI regulatory proteins BAZ1A and BAZ1B can form stable complexes with the catalytic‐dead SMARCA1.13 isoform and that these complexes remain inactive in nucleosome remodeling assays (Fig EV4 C and D). Complex formation is expected given the high degree of identity (98.9%) between canonical SMARCA1 and the SMARCA1.13 isoform, yet the biological significance of these (and presumably other) inactive SMARCA1.13 ISWI complexes remains to be explored.

Given the pronounced differences in product distributions for sliding reactions carried out by different ISWI complexes (Fig 3 ), we decided to measure ATP hydrolysis rates for the full set of possible regulatory subunit–ATPase pairs purified for this study. We measured ATP hydrolysis as a function of time in the absence or presence of purified polynucleosomes (Figs 4 and EV5 ). Expanding on initial observations made with a limited number of ISWI family members 5 , 14 , 19 , the ATP hydrolysis rate of all human ISWI complexes evaluated was stimulated by the presence of chromatinized templates. The degree of stimulation seemed to vary across different complexes. In agreement with an earlier study 5 , we found that SMARCA1 has a lower polynucleosome‐stimulated ATPase rate compared to SMARCA5 (Figs 4 and EV5 ). Finally, we did not find a correlation between rate of ATP hydrolysis and product distributions in sliding reactions, indicating that the different catalytic outputs observed in Fig 3 do not correspond to differences in specific activity, but rather to fundamental differences in nucleosome remodeling among the multiple purified complexes. Surprisingly, we found that RSF1 (and possibly BAZ2A when bound to SMARCA5) directly stimulates the ATPase activity of SMARCA1 and SMARCA5. Taken together, the differences in product distributions in sliding reactions and ATP hydrolysis rates reported here (Figs 3 and 4 , and EV3 , EV4 , EV5 ) are consistent with a model where different ISWI complexes are functionally segregated and remodel chromatin in distinct ways to play specific biological roles 3 .

While the dysregulation of ISWI chromatin remodelers has been implicated in numerous human disorders, including several types of cancer 58 , 59 , 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 and mental illness 64 , their cellular functions remain only partially understood. One contributing factor is that previous functional studies only contemplated a portion of the ISWI complexes that can form in mammalian cells (Table 1 ). In addition to uncovering novel regulatory subunit–ATPase pairings, the protein–protein interaction study presented here provides a list of auxiliary factors expected to take part in endogenous complexes of SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 (refer to Dataset EV1 ). Surprisingly, we found a significant number of histone proteins, beyond what could be expected from trace amounts of contaminating nucleic acid. However, the biological significance of these and all novel interactions remains to be established. Satisfactorily, we co‐enriched several known SMARCA5‐interacting proteins, including the poly(ADP‐ribosyl)ation factor PARP1 65 and the structural nuclear protein NuMA 66 , further validating our results. The incorporation of auxiliary subunits into ISWI complexes has been observed before 4 , 5 and is likely to further define the biological function of ISWI remodelers.

Despite the determination of a low‐resolution model 67 , we still lack high‐resolution structural information for a complete ISWI complex. Here we present a simple method to produce multiple well‐defined ISWI complexes that may enable the discovery of a regulatory–catalytic pair optimally suited to solve the high‐resolution structure of an intact chromatin remodeler. Future work aimed at uncovering the molecular determinants for heterodimeric ISWI complex assembly might shed light on the regulation of more complex multi‐protein remodeling machines, such as the SWI/SNF (BAF) complexes, powered by the ATPases SMARCA2 (BRM) and SMARCA4 (BRG1), that play a critical role in development and are mutated in numerous human cancers 57 .

In conclusion, this work significantly expands the human family of ISWI chromatin remodelers, from six to fourteen members, and provides the basis for a better understanding of ISWI function and chromatin remodeling in normal settings and disease.

Materials and Methods

Generation of SMARCA1 ‐KO and SMARCA5 ‐KD cell lines

SMARCA1‐ knockout (KO) and SMARCA5 ‐knockdown (KD) cell lines were generated by CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated genome editing 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 , as reviewed 74 . A human codon‐optimized cDNA encoding S. pyogenes Cas9 was cloned downstream of the human cytomegalovirus immediate‐early promoter in a pRK vector backbone. Individual small guide RNAs (sgRNAs; see Fig EV1 A for the sequences of each sgRNA) for human SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 were cloned downstream of the human U6 promoter of the pLKO.5 vector (Sigma). Cas9 and sgRNA constructs were co‐transfected in HeLa cells with the Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Three days post‐transfection, cells were harvested and plated in 96‐well plates at a dilution of 0.5 cell/well. Clonal wells were expanded and tested by Western blotting with antibodies against SMARCA1 (#12483, Cell Signaling Technology), SMARCA5 (NB100‐55310, Novus Biologicals), or tubulin (T9028, Sigma). Clones where SMARCA1 or SMARCA5 expression was abrogated were used to perform immunoprecipitation experiments.

Immunoprecipitation, Western blotting, and mass spectrometry

HeLa cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 2 mM l ‐glutamine at 37°C and 5% CO 2 . NTERA‐2 cells were maintained in McCoy's 5A medium, supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum and 2 mM l ‐glutamine at 37°C and 5% CO 2 . For each immunoprecipitation, ~8 × 10 6 cells were washed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and lysed for 30 min on ice in 50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 300 mM KCl, 10% glycerol, 1% Triton X‐100, freshly supplemented with 50 U/ml Benzonase (Sigma), 1 mM tris(2‐carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), and protease inhibitors (cOmplete, EDTA‐free, Roche), with three 10 s vortex cycles every 10 min. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C and supplemented with 5 μg of antibody against SMARCA1 (#12483, Cell Signaling Technology), SMARCA5 (NB100‐55310, Novus Biologicals), BAZ1A (NB100‐61042, Novus Biologicals), BAZ1B (#2152, Cell Signaling Technology), BPTF (NB100‐41418, Novus Biologicals), or CECR2 (#730031, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were incubated with rotation for 2 h at 4°C before addition of 50 μl slurry of Dynabeads Protein G (#10003D, Thermo Fisher Scientific) pre‐washed with PBS supplemented with 0.05% Tween‐20 (PBS‐T). After 1 h at 4°C with gentle rotation, the beads were washed twice with PBS‐T and twice with PBS, resuspended in NuPAGE LDS sample buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and placed for 5 min at 95°C for elution. Mock controls were processed in parallel without the addition of antibody. Denatured samples were subjected to Western blotting or mass spectrometry studies. For mass spectrometry analysis (for immunoprecipitations of SMARCA1, SMARCA5, BAZ1A, and BAZ1B only), eluted proteins were alkylated with N ‐isopropyliodoacetamide (NIPIA) and separated on a denaturing SDS–PAGE gel. Each lane was cut into ten slices, and each gel slice was subjected to in‐gel digestion with trypsin in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate pH 8.0 overnight at 37°C. Peptides were extracted with a solution containing 50% acetonitrile and 1% formic acid. Extracted samples from the same gel lane were combined, dried under vacuum, and reconstituted in 2% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. Samples were loaded onto a 0.1 × 100 mm analytical column packed with 1.7 μm BEH‐130 C18 using a NanoAcquity UPLC (Waters). Peptides were eluted with a 45‐min gradient from 2 to 90% acetonitrile at flow rate of 1 μl/min and directly introduced into a LTQ‐Orbitrap XL mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) through an ADVANCE electrospray ionization source (Michrom BioResources/Bruker). Full mass spectrometry (MS) data were acquired at 60,000 resolution. The top eight most abundant precursors from the full MS spectra were further selected for collision‐induced dissociation and MS/MS data acquisition. MS/MS data were searched using the Mascot algorithm (version 2.4.1, Matrix Sciences). Search criteria included a full MS tolerance of 50 ppm, MS/MS tolerance of 0.8 Da with oxidation (+15.9949 Da) of methionine as a variable modification, and NIPIA (+99.0684 Da) of cysteine as a static modification, with up to three missed cleavages. Resulting peptide sequences were searched against the Uniprot database (version 2011_12). Linear discriminant analysis was used to filter peptide spectral matches to a 1% peptide false discovery rate. Slightly different mass spectrometry parameters were used for SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 immunoprecipitations from cells engineered to strongly reduce (KD) or eliminate (KO) expression of the alternative ATPase. In this case, peptides were eluted with a 60‐min acetonitrile gradient and introduced in a LTQ‐Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The top fifteen most abundant precursors from the full MS spectra were selected for MS/MS data acquisition, and peptide sequences were searched against the Uniprot database (version 2016_06). The raw list of proteins identified from parental cells was filtered based on data from genome‐edited cells to increase confidence in hits highlighted in Fig 1 A. See Datasets EV1 , EV3 and EV4 for a comprehensive list of all recovered proteins and the criteria used for filtering. Mass spectrometry proteomics data were deposited in the MassIVE database ( https://massive.ucsd.edu/ ) under accession code MSV000081134. The abundance of each protein is reported as the collective area under the curve (AUC) for all peptides detected. To generate Fig 1 B, the AUC value for a given regulatory subunit was expressed as percentage of the combined AUC for all regulatory subunits recovered. Expression levels are reported as reads per kilobase of transcript per million total reads normalized by gene length (nRPKM), and are only qualitative as gene‐specific technical variation cannot be excluded. For Western blotting, eluted proteins or lysates were separated on a denaturing SDS–PAGE gel, processed with iBlot Gel Transfer Device (P1, 8 min; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and visualized with primary antibodies against SMARCA1 (#12483, Cell Signaling Technology), SMARCA5 (NB100‐55310, Novus Biologicals), tubulin (T9028, Sigma), BAZ1A (A301‐318A, Bethyl Laboratories), BAZ1B (NB600‐279, Novus Biologicals), BPTF (NB100‐41418, Novus Biologicals), or CECR2, (sc‐86101, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies against mouse (NA931V, GE Healthcare) or rabbit (NA934V, GE Healthcare).

Purification of ISWI complexes by co‐expression in insect cells

cDNAs for human BAZ1A (accession number Q9NRL2 ‐1), BAZ1B ( Q9UIG0 ‐1), BAZ2A ( Q9UIF9 ‐1), BAZ2B ( Q9UIF8 ‐1), CECR2 ( Q9BXF3 ‐1), RSF1 ( Q96T23 ‐1), SMARCA1 ( P28370 ‐2), SMARCA1.13 ( P28370 ‐1), and SMARCA5 ( O60264 ‐1) were codon‐optimized for expression in insect cells, synthesized (GenScript), and cloned into the pAcGP67A vector. The BAZ1A construct included a N‐terminal His 6 ‐tag, while constructs for BAZ1B, BAZ2A, BAZ2B, CECR2, and RSF1 included a C‐terminal His 6 ‐tag. The constructs for SMARCA1, SMARCA1.13, and SMARCA5 included a C‐terminal Strep‐tag; the constructs for SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 also included a N‐terminal FLAG‐tag. Alternative SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 constructs that included only the N‐terminal FLAG‐tag or only the C‐terminal Strep‐tag produced equivalent results. cDNA‐containing vectors were co‐transfected with BestBac 1.0 linearized viral DNA (Expression Systems) into Sf9 cells using the TransIT transfection reagent (Mirus Bio). Resulting recombinant baculoviruses were amplified twice to produce a final viral stock suitable for protein expression. Virus quality was confirmed by gp‐64 staining. For protein production, 2 l of Sf9 cells was diluted to 2 × 10 6 cells/ml in protein‐free ESF 921 media (Expression Systems) and infected with a total of 5 ml virus solution. Cultures were maintained at 27°C and cells harvested 3 d post‐infection by centrifugation. To purify the ATPases alone (SMARCA1, SMARCA1.13, and SMARCA5), cell pellets were resuspended in 100 mM Tris pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, 0.2% Tween‐20, and 1 mM TCEP in the presence of protease inhibitors (cOmplete, EDTA‐free, Roche) and 5 U/ml Benzonase (Sigma), cells lysed by sonication, and lysates cleared by centrifugation at 30,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The cleared lysates were applied onto Strep‐Tactin Superflow high capacity resin (IBA) and incubated with rotation for 2 h at 4°C. The resin was moved to a gravity column and washed extensively with 100 mM Tris pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, 0.2% Tween‐20, and 1 mM TCEP. Proteins were eluted with 100 mM Tris pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 2.5 mM D‐desthiobiotin. Eluted protein was subjected to size‐exclusion chromatography on a Superdex S200 preparative column (HiLoad 16/60, GE Healthcare), pre‐equilibrated with 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP. All protein samples were pooled from a single peak of the expected retention volume and concentrated using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter units with 30‐kDa cutoff (Millipore). To purify the ISWI complexes containing BAZ1A, BAZ1B, or CECR2, cell pellets were resuspended in 50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, 10% glycerol, 10 mM imidazole, and 0.2% Tween‐20, in the presence of protease inhibitors and 5 U/ml Benzonase. Cells were lysed and cleared as described for the ATPases alone. The cleared lysates were applied onto Ni‐NTA Superflow resin (Qiagen) in batch mode for 1 h at 4°C. The resin was moved to a gravity column and washed extensively with 50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM TCEP, and 10 mM imidazole. Proteins were then eluted with 50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, 10% glycerol, and 300 mM imidazole. Eluted protein was diluted 10‐fold in 50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.2% Tween‐20 supplemented with protease inhibitors. The samples were immediately applied to anti‐FLAG M2 Affinity Gel (Sigma) for 1 h at 4°C. After extensive washes with 50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM TCEP, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.2% Tween‐20, complexes were eluted with 150 μg/ml FLAG peptide (Sigma) in 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP. A SMARCA1.13 construct bearing a C‐terminal Strep‐tag and a N‐terminal FLAG‐tag was used to purify the BAZ1A–SMARCA1.13 complex, following the procedure described above. To purify the BAZ1B–SMARCA1.13 complex, sequential Ni‐NTA and Strep‐Tactin purifications were used. Complexes containing BAZ2A, BAZ2B, or RSF1 required a different purification protocol to ensure removal of nucleic acids that were empirically determined to co‐purify in larger amounts with these complexes. Increased pH and decreased NaCl concentration were used to increase the nuclease activity of Benzonase. Accordingly, cell pellets were resuspended in 20 mM Tris pH 8.5, 100 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl 2 , 10% glycerol, 1 mM TCEP, 10 mM imidazole, and 0.2% Tween‐20, in the presence of protease inhibitors and 50 U/ml Benzonase. Protein complexes were purified by Ni‐NTA chromatography as described above but including extensive washes with 50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 600 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM TCEP, and 10 mM imidazole. Complexes were then subjected to FLAG‐tag affinity purification as described above. Proteins were concentrated as described for the ATPases alone, and concentration was determined by absorbance at 280 nm with extinction coefficients calculated using ProtParam (ExPASy, http://web.expasy.org/protparam/ ). The extinction coefficients used posit a 1:1 stoichiometry (regulatory: ATPase) for all complexes analyzed, and therefore, concentrations may not be exact in a case where one subunit is present sub‐stoichiometrically. Aliquots were flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

Nucleosome reconstitution and remodeling assay

For nucleosome reconstitution, DNA templates labeled at the 5′ position with Cy3 or Cy5 dyes were generated by PCR and contained 80 bp of arbitrary sequence followed by the Widom‐601 nucleosome positioning sequence 75 , or the Widom‐601 sequence flanked by 40 bp of arbitrary, non‐positioning sequence on each side. Nucleosomes were reconstituted with the EpiMark Nucleosome Assembly Kit (New England Biolabs), to produce homogeneous edge‐ or center‐positioned nucleosomes (see Appendix Fig S1 ). Edge‐ and center‐positioned nucleosomes were then mixed in 1:1 ratio (10 nM each final concentration) for assays. Sliding reactions were carried out for 30 min at 30°C in 20 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 4.8 mM Tris, 3 mM MgCl 2 , 12% glycerol, 0.024% IGEPAL CA‐630, 30 μM FLAG peptide, 80 mM NaCl, and 2 mM ATP. Reaction mixtures contained 300 nM of ATPase alone or 60 nM of the indicated ISWI complex. Extending the reaction time to 60 min produced equivalent results. Reactions were stopped by adding salmon sperm DNA to a final concentration of 0.5 μg/μl (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to compete with nucleosomes for ISWI proteins. Samples were supplemented with Novex Hi‐Density TBE sample buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated on 6% DNA retardation gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific) run at a constant 150 V for 1 h. Nucleosome bands were visualized on a Typhoon Trio (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) using Cy3 and Cy5 filters. Quantification was performed using ImageJ 76 .

Quantitation of ATP hydrolysis rates

ATP hydrolysis reactions were carried out at 30°C in 20.1 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 2.9 mM Tris, 3 mM MgCl 2 , 11.4% glycerol, 0.02% IGEPAL CA‐630, 37 mM NaCl, 0.06 mM EDTA, 20 μM FLAG peptide, and 2 mM ATP (saturating). Reaction mixtures contained 200 nM ISWI protein or complex and, when indicated, 200 nM (mononucleosomal concentration) of a mixture of mono‐ and polynucleosomes purified from HeLa cells (EpiCypher; 16‐0003). Reactions were started by addition of ATP and stopped at the indicated time points by adding 5 μl of sample to 100 μl of the malachite green reagent solution from the ATPase/GTPase Activity Assay Kit (Sigma; MAK113) in a white 96‐well microplate, non‐binding (Greiner Bio One). After 1‐h incubation at room temperature, absorbance at 620 nm was measured on a SpectraMax M5e multi‐mode reader (Molecular Devices). Standard curves were used to calculate the amount of free phosphate present in each sample.
