ABSTRACT

Ergosterol, a fungus-specific sterol enriched in cell plasma membranes, is an effective antifungal drug target. However, current knowledge of the ergosterol biosynthesis process in the saprophytic human fungal pathogen Aspergillus fumigatus remains limited. In this study, we found that two endoplasmic reticulum-localized sterol C-24 reductases encoded by both erg4A and erg4B homologs are required to catalyze the reaction during the final step of ergosterol biosynthesis. Loss of one homolog of Erg4 induces the overexpression of the other one, accompanied by almost normal ergosterol synthesis and wild-type colony growth. However, double deletions of erg4A and erg4B completely block the last step of ergosterol synthesis, resulting in the accumulation of ergosta-5,7,22,24(28)-tetraenol, a precursor compound of ergosterol. Further studies indicate that erg4A and erg4B are required for conidiation but not for hyphal growth. Importantly, the Δ erg4A Δ erg4B mutant still demonstrates wild-type virulence in a compromised mouse model but displays remarkable increased susceptibility to antifungal azoles. Our data suggest that inhibitors of Erg4A and Erg4B may serve as effective candidates for adjunct antifungal agents with azoles.

IMPORTANCE Knowledge of the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway in the human opportunistic pathogen A. fumigatus is useful for designing and finding new antifungal drugs. In this study, we demonstrated that the endoplasmic reticulum-localized sterol C-24 reductases Erg4A and Erg4B are required for conidiation via regulation of ergosterol biosynthesis. Moreover, inactivation of both Erg4A and Erg4B results in hypersensitivity to the clinical guideline-recommended antifungal drugs itraconazole and voriconazole. Therefore, our finding indicates that inhibition of Erg4A and Erg4B might be an effective approach for alleviating A. fumigatus infection.

KEYWORDS: ergosterol biosynthesis, Aspergillus fumigatus , sterol C-24 reductase, azoles

INTRODUCTION

Aspergillus fumigatus is a saprophytic fungus with a large number of small airborne spores that can survive under various environmental conditions. Due to its strong adaptability to the environment, A. fumigatus has become the most prevalent opportunistic pathogen which might cause invasive aspergillosis (IA) ( 1 ). Unfortunately, in recent years, immunosuppressed populations have increased, and the incidence of IA has risen simultaneously ( 2 ). Although the utilization of antifungal drugs clearly improves the health condition of patients with IA, the continued use of antifungal drugs has also increased the number of drug-resistant strains over the years ( 3 , 4 ). To date, the most widely used antifungal drugs are azoles, which mainly target ergosterol synthesis, since ergosterol is a fungus-specific sterol and is primarily distributed in plasma membranes. Moreover, many previous studies have found that ergosterol is also involved in many biological processes, including membrane fluidity, permeability, signal transduction, and others ( 5 – 8 ). For example, in Aspergillus nidulans , it has been reported that ergosterol-enriched sterol-rich membrane domains (SRDs), which are distributed at plasma membranes in hyphal tips, play important roles during the process of polarized growth ( 9 ). In addition, oxysterol-binding protein homologs OshA to OshE, which are involved in nonvesicular sterol transport for dispatching ergosterol to the distinct site, have also been implicated in regulating fungal cell growth ( 10 ).

In the fungal kingdom, the biosynthesis pathway of ergosterol is highly conserved, and approximately 20 enzymes are involved ( 11 ). Although the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway in Saccharomyces cerevisiae has been characterized ( 12 ), little is known about this pathway in A. fumigatus . To date, Erg11, Erg25, and Erg3 are components that have been identified in the ergosterol synthesis pathway in A. fumigatus ( 13 , 14 ). Erg11 contains two homologs, Erg11A (Cyp51A) and Erg11B (Cyp51B), which encode two distinct 14α sterol demethylases. Deletion of erg11A has no effect on ergosterol levels, whereas the erg11B deletion mutant produces a prominent decrease in ergosterol compared to that of the parental wild-type strain ( 13 ). However, double deletions of erg11A and erg11B in A. fumigatus are lethal ( 15 ). Likewise, Erg25, an ergosterol synthesis enzyme that is downstream of Erg11, also contains two homologs, referred to as Erg25A and Erg25B. Single deletion of erg25A or erg25B leads to no significant differences in production of ergosterol compared to that of the parental wild-type strain, while double deletions of erg25A and erg25B are lethal ( 16 ). Another verified component of the ergosterol synthesis enzyme in A. fumigatus is Erg3, which contains 3 copies termed Erg3A, Erg3B, and Erg3C ( 17 ). Notably, single deletion of erg3A or erg3C does not result in an obvious difference in total ergosterol production compared to that of the parental wild-type strain. Nevertheless, deletion of erg3B results in dramatically decreased ergosterol production ( 13 , 17 ).

Correspondingly, cholesterol present in mammalian cell membranes serves a role similar to that of ergosterol in fungi. Cholesterol is known to be an essential component of plasma membrane functions, such as membrane permeability, fluidity, and so on ( 18 , 19 ). However, most enzyme homologs in the early steps of ergosterol and cholesterol biosynthesis pathways share common functions, which limits the possibility for the design of antifungal drugs. Interestingly, Erg4, Erg5, and Erg6 are specific components in the ergosterol synthetic pathway, suggesting that these enzymes might be used as promising targets for antifungal drugs ( 12 ). It has been reported that the enzyme involved in the last step catalyzing ergosta-5,7,22,24(28)-tetraenol to ergosterol is encoded by erg4 in S. cerevisiae ( 20 ). The Scerg4 deletion mutant fails to biosynthesize ergosterol and significantly increases susceptibilities to divalent cations and to several antifungal drugs, miconazole, fluconazole, and other azoles ( 20 ). Similarly, in the plant pathogen Fusarium graminearum , deletion of Fgerg4 causes absolute deficiency of ergosterol and increases sensitivities to metal cations and osmotic and oxidative stresses ( 21 ). Importantly, the Fgerg4 -null mutant showed abnormal vegetative differentiation and attenuated virulence in the plant host ( 21 ).

Given the difference between the biosynthesis processes of ergosterol and cholesterol, the present study examines whether blocking the final step of ergosterol biosynthesis would affect hyphal growth, drug resistance, and virulence in A. fumigatus . We identified two predicted homologs of Erg4 in A. fumigatus , here referred to as Erg4A and Erg4B, and indicated that these homologs are required to catalyze the reaction in the final step of ergosterol biosynthesis. Deletion of one homolog induces the overexpression of the other such that either deletion of erg4A or erg4B has almost no effect on ergosterol biosynthesis or conidiation. However, double deletions of erg4A and erg4B completely block the conversion of ergosta-5,7,22,24(28)-tetraenol, a precursor compound of ergosterol, to ergosterol. Further, concurrent inactivation of erg4A and erg4B results in a severe defect for conidiation but not for hyphal growth. Accordingly, the Δ erg4A Δ erg4B mutant displayed wild-type virulence in A. fumigatus . Importantly, Δ erg4A Δ erg4B showed hypersensitivities to the azole drugs itraconazole and voriconazole, suggesting that inhibitors of Erg4A and Erg4B may serve as effective candidates for use as adjunct antifungal agents with azoles.

RESULTS

A. fumigatus includes two homologs of S. cerevisiae Erg4.

To identify homologs of Erg4 of S. cerevisiae in A. fumigatus , the amino acid sequence of ScErg4 was used as a query to perform BLASTP analysis in the genome database of A. fumigatus . The result showed that AFUB_062080 ( EDP52170.1 , identity 53%, E value 7e−166) and AFUB_007490 ( EDP56047.1 , identity 50%, E value 1e−152) were possible homologs of ScErg4 in A. fumigatus . Subsequent BLASTP analyses using AFUB_062080 and AFUB_007490 as queries were performed in the S. cerevisiae database, and results showed that ScErg4 was the best match, suggesting that AFUB_062080 and AFUB_007490, here referred to as Erg4A and Erg4B, might be potential homologs of ScErg4 in A. fumigatus . Based on the fact that A. fumigatus contains two predicted Erg4 homologs, while S. cerevisiae has only one, we were interested in exploring whether other fungi also contain two Erg4 homologs. To this end, Erg4 family members of more than 10 fungi from the NCBI GenBank were identified using the BLAST algorithm with a cutoff value of e−55. Interestingly, the selected yeast-form fungi, including S. cerevisiae , Candida albicans , Schizosaccharomyces pombe , and Cryptococcus gattii , only have a single homolog of Erg4, whereas many selected filamentous fungi contain two Erg4 homologs, suggesting that the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway in filamentous fungi may be more complicated than that in single-cell yeasts ( Fig. 1A ).

FIG 1.

The deduced DNA sequence of erg4A is 1,510 bp in length and encodes 471 amino acids, while the genomic DNA of erg4B is 2,066 bp in length and encodes 568 amino acids. Sequence analysis showed that Erg4A and Erg4B share 62% identity with each other. Intriguingly, compared to Erg4A or ScErg4, Erg4B displays an extended N terminus with approximately 95 amino acids. The transmembrane domain analysis predicted by the TMHMM v2.0 program ( http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0 ) or the SMART protein search ( http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/ ) revealed that both Erg4A and Erg4B contain nine transmembrane structures. In comparison, ScErg4 only contains seven predicted transmembrane structures. This suggests that the function of ScErg4 may be different from that of Erg4A and Erg4B in A. fumigatus ( Fig. 1B ).

Double deletions of erg4A and erg4B result in fluffy colonies with severely impaired conidiation.

To investigate the function of Erg4A and Erg4B, we constructed erg4A -, erg4B -, and erg4A - and erg4B -null mutants in the background strain A1160 (Δ ku80 , pyrG ). Diagnostic PCR showed that the selection marker hph or pyr4 completely replaced the open reading frame (ORF) of erg4A or erg4B , respectively, suggesting that the ORF of erg4A or erg4B was fully deleted (see Fig. S1 in the supplemental material). As shown in Fig. 2A , the erg4B single-deletion mutant showed a phenotype similar to that of the parental wild type with normal colony growth and conidiation in YUU medium (yeast extract-agar-glucose [YAG] medium supplemented with 5 mM uridine and 10 mM uracil) (see Fig. S2A and B in the supplemental material). In contrast, the erg4A single-deletion mutant displayed a slightly attenuated colony diameter of approximately 20% that of the parental wild type, while conidia numbers per unit area in the Δ erg4A mutant were not affected compared to those of its parental wild type ( Fig. 2A , Fig. S2A and B). However, the Δ erg4A Δ erg4B double mutant exhibited a nearly white and fluffy colony plus a decreased colony diameter, approximately 26% of that of its parental wild type. The severely impaired conidiation phenomenon suggests that Erg4A and Erg4B are essential for conidiation of A. fumigatus ( Fig. 2A , Fig. S2A and B). To elucidate how the defect of conidiation occurred in the aforementioned double-deletion mutant, we compared the conidiophores of Δ erg4A , Δ erg4B , Δ erg4A Δ erg4B , and the parental wild type. As shown in Fig. 2A , for Δ erg4A and Δ erg4B single mutants, the conidiophore structure was normal and similar to that of the parental wild type with numerous conidia in it, whereas in Δ erg4A Δ erg4B there were no detectable conidiophore structures. Instead, there were several abnormal septa at the stalk of the foot cell in Δ erg4A Δ erg4B , while Δ erg4A and Δ erg4B single mutants or the parental wild-type strain did not show any detectable septa at the same site ( Fig. 2A ). To address whether the impaired conidiation of Δ erg4A Δ erg4B was due to the absence of ergosterol, we tested its colony morphology and conidiation structure with the extra ergosterol addition in solid medium. As expected, in the presence of ergosterol, the formation of conidia and conidiophores of Δ erg4A Δ erg4B recovered significantly ( Fig. 2B ), which suggests that the defect in conidiation may be partially caused by ergosterol deficiency. To further determine whether the defective phenotype in Δ erg4A Δ erg4B was really due to deletions of these two genes, we carried out the complementation experiment. The result in Fig. 2C clearly showed that introducing either the erg4A or erg4B gene or both into the Δ erg4A Δ erg4B receipt strain was able to completely rescue mutant defects in conidiation. Further, to exclude the side effects of ku80 for defect phenotypes, the wild-type ku80 cassette was also introduced into the Δ erg4A Δ erg4B mutant under the control of AngpdA . Semiquantitative reverse transcription-PCR (RT-PCR) analysis showed that ku80 was expressed successfully in the transformants tested (Fig. S2C). Phenotypic analysis indicates that wild-type ku80 transformants displayed phenotypes similar to Δ erg4A Δ erg4B , with colony size reduction and impaired conidial formation ( Fig. 2C ). It demonstrates that the defect phenotype of Δ erg4A Δ erg4B was specifically caused by deletions of erg4A and erg4B rather than being the result from any second mutations. Next, we asked if deletion of erg4A and/or erg4B would affect the hyphal growth. We compared the hyphal morphology features of the Δ erg4A , Δ erg4B , Δ erg4A Δ erg4B , and parental wild-type strains under liquid culture conditions. First, through the microscopy study for examining the hyphal tips of erg4A - and/or erg4B -null mutants, we found that all mutants tested had hyphal phenotypes similar to that of the parental wild type, suggesting that deletions of erg4A and/or erg4B have no detectable effect on polarized growth under liquid conditions ( Fig. 2D ). Due to the abnormal septa displayed in the conidiophore structure of Δ erg4A Δ erg4B , we then stained the hyphal septa with the chitin dye, calcofluor white. The result showed that no significant difference exists in the septum formation between mutants and the parental wild-type strain ( Fig. 2D ). As the ergosterol-enriched SRDs are mainly located in hyphal tip membranes, we then stained the SRDs of the indicated strains with the sterol dye filipin to visualize the sterol distribution. As shown in Fig. 2D , there was no detectable difference between mutants and the parental wild-type strain, which suggests that deletion of erg4A and/or erg4B has no significant effect on the SRD formation and sterol distribution. Taken together, these data demonstrate that erg4A and erg4B are required for conidiation but not for hyphal growth in A. fumigatus .

FIG 2.

The Δ erg4A Δ erg4B mutant demonstrates wild-type virulence.

Because Δ erg4A Δ erg4B displayed impaired colony growth and blocked conidiation, we compared its virulence with that of the parental wild-type strain in an immunosuppressed mouse model of invasive aspergillosis. Mice infected with the parental wild-type strain or Δ erg4A Δ erg4B began to die at day 2 or 3, respectively. Survival of mice was monitored during 14 days after infection. As shown in the survival curves of Fig. 3A , the parental wild-type and the Δ erg4A Δ erg4B strains caused similar mortalities (about 87%) with no significant difference by log-rank analysis ( P = 0.874) ( Fig. 3A ). To address whether the death of mice was caused by the A. fumigatus strain used for infection, we cultured lung tissues isolated from the infected dead mice on YAG medium. The results in Fig. 3B show that each plate inoculated with lung tissue isolated from infected mice displayed live colonies of A. fumigatus . Next, histopathological examinations of lung sections were performed with periodic acid-Schiff staining. As shown in Fig. 3C , large amounts of growing hyphae appeared around the lung airways of mice which were infected by both the parental wild-type and the Δ erg4A Δ erg4B strains. Therefore, data from the survival curve combined with the results of the histopathological analysis strongly suggest that despite having defects in colony size and conidiation, the Δ erg4A Δ erg4B mutant exhibits virulence similar to that of the parental wild-type strain.

FIG 3.

Erg4A and Erg4B are required for the biosynthesis of ergosterol.

In S. cerevisiae , it has been demonstrated that ScErg4 is the enzyme which catalyzes the final step of ergosterol biosynthesis, and absolute deficiency of ergosterol can be observed without this enzyme ( 20 ). To investigate whether A. fumigatus Erg4A and Erg4B are involved in the last step of ergosterol biosynthesis ( Fig. 4A ), we analyzed the content of ergosterol extracted from Δ erg4A , Δ erg4B , and Δ erg4A Δ erg4B mutants and the parental wild type by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). In this assay, a commercial purified ergosterol was used as a standard to determine the retention time of ergosterol. As shown in Fig. 4B , there is a single absorption peak, which suggests that the specific absorption peak of ergosterol was at the retention time of ∼10.6 min ( Fig. 4B ). Next, a similar approach was used to detect the content of ergosterol in extracts from the above-mentioned strains. Intriguingly, both Δ erg4A and Δ erg4B mutants showed no detectable differences from the parental wild type in the HPLC profile for the ergosterol synthesis analysis ( Fig. 4B and C ). However, in contrast to the single mutants, Δ erg4A Δ erg4B had no ergosterol-specific absorption peak at the time of ∼10.6 min. Instead, at the retention time of ∼8.7 min, an absorption peak that probably belonged to the sterol intermediate was detected ( Fig. 4B ). To determine whether this sterol intermediate was the precursor of ergosterol, we then authenticated it by liquid chromatography-mass spectroscopy (LC-MS). As expected, this sterol intermediate was identified as ergosta-5,7,22,24(28)-tetraenol (see Fig. S3 in the supplemental material). Taken together, our data suggest that the combination of Erg4A and Erg4B but neither one individually is required for the biosynthesis of ergosterol in A. fumigatus .

FIG 4.

Loss of ER-localized Erg4A induces the overexpression of Erg4B and vice versa .

To further explore the function of Erg4A and Erg4B in A. fumigatus , we studied their subcellular localization. Through homologous recombination, we successfully labeled Erg4B with green fluorescent protein (GFP) in the C terminus under the control of its native promoter. Unfortunately, using this strategy, we were unable to obtain the functional Erg4A-GFP strain. Instead, we labeled Erg4A in the N terminus under the control of AngpdA promoter. As shown in Fig. 5A and B , both GFP-Erg4A and Erg4B-GFP fusion proteins had the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-like localization pattern, with a network of strands around the nucleus, which were predicted and consistent with the patterns of most of the proteins involved in ergosterol biosynthesis ( 22 ). Further, to investigate whether tagging Erg4A or Erg4B would affect its function, we transformed the Δ erg4A and Δ erg4B mutants with gfp-erg4A and erg4B-gfp cassettes, respectively. As shown in Fig. S4 in the supplemental material, the localization and colony characteristics of the Erg4A and Erg4B GFP-tagged strains are both similar to those of the parental wild type, which suggests that the GFP-tagged Erg4A and Erg4B proteins are functional. To directly demonstrate whether Erg4A and Erg4B are localized in ER, we performed an experiment labeling the ER marker Erg11A with red fluorescent protein (RFP) in its C terminus in the background strains of GFP-Erg4A and Erg4B-GFP. Microscopic observations showed that both GFP-Erg4A and Erg4B-GFP were colocalized with Erg11A-RFP, which directly demonstrates that the Erg4A and Erg4B proteins both have ER localization ( Fig. 5A and B ).

FIG 5.

As mentioned above, in the Δ erg4A and Δ erg4B single mutants, the ergosterol content was similar to that of the parental wild type, while in Δ erg4A Δ erg4B , the ergosterol content was nearly undetectable. Therefore, we hypothesized that Erg4A and Erg4B may have redundant functions during ergosterol biosynthesis. To verify this hypothesis, we first detected the mRNA levels of erg4A and erg4B in the parental wild-type strain. As shown in Fig. 5C , relative to the transcription of tubA , the mRNA level of erg4B was approximately 2.5 times higher than that of erg4A in the parental wild-type strain. Next, we examined the mRNA level of erg4A in the Δ erg4B mutant and of erg4B in the Δ erg4A mutant compared to those in the parental wild type. Remarkably, the mRNA level of erg4A in the absence of erg4B was increased to 3.4 times that of the parental wild type ( Fig. 5D ). Similarly, under the condition of erg4A deficiency, the mRNA level of erg4B was increased up to 1.6 times compared to that of the parental wild type ( Fig. 5D ). Therefore, our data verified that ER-located Erg4A and Erg4B have redundant functions during ergosterol biosynthesis and loss of one paralog would induce the increased expression of the other one.

Sensitivities of the erg4 mutant to osmotic and oxidative stresses and cell wall-perturbing agents.

Ergosterol is an important sterol constituent in cellular plasma membranes, and it is involved in cell membrane integrity and fluidity and permeability of fungal cells ( 5 , 23 , 24 ). Given the predominant function of ergosterol, we speculated that deficiency of ergosterol might change the susceptibility of A. fumigatus to some plasma stresses or cell wall-perturbing agents. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed the phenotypes of the Δ erg4A , Δ erg4B , Δ erg4A Δ erg4B , and parental wild-type strains in YUU solid medium supplemented by different reagents. Interestingly, under the osmotic stress condition generated by d -sorbitol, all mutants tested showed colony growth similar to that of the parental wild type, while under the stress condition induced by NaCl, the Δ erg4A and Δ erg4A Δ erg4B strains showed slight sensitivities compared to that of the reference strain ( Fig. 6A and B ). In a similar manner, we tested and compared the phenotypes of those mutants to that of the parental wild-type strain under oxidative stresses induced by H 2 O 2 and menadione. Under the oxidative stress condition with H 2 O 2 , both the Δ erg4A and Δ erg4A Δ erg4B mutants exhibited increased susceptibilities. However, compared to the results with the parental wild-type strain, Δ erg4A Δ erg4B displayed a slight resistance in the presence of menadione, whereas Δ erg4A showed an increased sensitivity under the same condition ( Fig. 6A and B ). Erg4 is involved in cell wall assembly in S. cerevisiae ( 25 ). Thus, we examined a potential role for Erg4A and Erg4B in cell wall integrity. To this end, we tested the sensitivity of erg4A and/or erg4B deletions to cell wall-perturbing agents such as Congo red and calcofluor white. Notably, in the presence of Congo red, compared to the results with the parental wild-type strain, both the Δ erg4A single mutant and the Δ erg4A Δ erg4B double mutant showed hypersusceptibility, while Δ erg4B displayed a phenotype similar to that of the parental wild type. Likewise, results similar to those with Congo red were obtained with calcofluor white, which suggests that erg4A may play a more important role than that of erg4B in the cell wall integrity in A. fumigatus ( Fig. 6A and B ). Collectively, the above data demonstrate that Erg4A and/or Erg4B is critical for protecting fungal cells against plasma and cell wall stresses.

FIG 6.

Susceptibilities of the erg4 mutant to antifungal drugs.

Because the widely used antifungal azole drugs mainly inhibit the biosynthesis of ergosterol, we then tested the sensitivity of those mutants to some azole drugs. As depicted in Fig. 7A , under treatment with itraconazole or voriconazole (inhibitors of the lanosterol 14α-demethylase enzyme), Δ erg4A Δ erg4B showed increased susceptibility to the azoles tested compared to that of single mutants, the erg4A - and/or erg4B -complemented strain, or the parental wild-type strain. Because Δ erg4A Δ erg4B showed hypersensitivity to itraconazole and voriconazole, we subsequently tested its MICs using commercial Etest strips. As shown in Fig. 7B , the MIC value of itraconazole for the Δ erg4A Δ erg4B mutant (1.2 μg/ml) was significantly lower than that of the parental wild-type strain (3 μg/ml) under the same condition. Similarly, as observed with the voriconazole Etest strip, the MIC value for Δ erg4A Δ erg4B (0.032 μg/ml) was dramatically lower than that for the parental wild-type strain (0.125 μg/ml). This MIC test further demonstrates that Δ erg4A Δ erg4B is much more sensitive to antifungal azoles than the parental wild-type strain. Interestingly, all mutants showed slight resistance to terbinafine, which interferes with the biosynthesis of ergosterol by inhibition of squalene epoxidase ( Fig. 7C ). Moreover, because the polyene drug amphotericin B was known to kill fungal cells by binding ergosterol to form a pore to disrupt the integrity of membranes, we then tested the drug susceptibility for the indicated strains. As shown in Fig. 7C , we found that compared to the result for the parental wild-type strain, Δ erg4A Δ erg4B showed significant resistance to amphotericin B. As mentioned above, because the Δ erg4A and Δ erg4A Δ erg4B mutants displayed increased susceptibility to cell wall-perturbing agents, such as Congo red and calcofluor white, we then examined the sensitivity of the erg4A and/or erg4B mutants to caspofungin, an echinocandin drug which inhibits β-1,3-glucan synthase. Unexpectedly, the susceptibility of both the single and double erg4A and erg4B mutants did not show any difference from that of the parental wild-type strain ( Fig. 7C ).

FIG 7.

Given the aforementioned fact that addition of ergosterol can partly rescue the conidiation defect of Δ erg4A Δ erg4B , we carried out another experiment to test whether ergosterol supplementation might affect drug susceptibilities in mutants. Unexpectedly, adding ergosterol did not significantly change the susceptibilities of erg4A and/or erg4B mutants, as shown in Fig. S5 in the supplemental material. Collectively, the data suggest that inactivation of erg4A and/or erg4B obviously alters the susceptibility of A. fumigatus to itraconazole, voriconazole, terbinafine, and amphotericin B but not to caspofungin.

DISCUSSION

Knowledge of the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway in the human opportunistic pathogen A. fumigatus is useful for novel antifungal drug design. In this study, through bioinformatics analysis, we characterized two homologs of yeast Erg4 in A. fumigatus . In the absence of one homolog of A. fumigatus Erg4, its function might be complemented by the increasing expression of the other one. Thus, deletion of neither erg4A nor erg4B would affect the biosynthesis of ergosterol. However, concurrent deletions of erg4A and erg4B completely block the biosynthesis of ergosterol and result in severely impaired conidiation. Moreover, hypersensitivity to the clinically used first-line antifungal azoles in Δ erg4A Δ erg4B suggests that Erg4A and Erg4B might be promising targets for antifungal drugs.

Erg4A and Erg4B are required for conidiation but not for hyphal growth.

As shown in Fig. 2A , when the function of ergosterol in biosynthesis was lost, Δ erg4A Δ erg4B was still viable, which demonstrates that ergosterol is not necessary for fungal survival. It also indicates that other intermediate products in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway may play a substitute role for the function of ergosterol. Moreover, it is possible that the filipin staining could not distinguish between ergosterol and ergosta-5,7,22,24(28)-tetraenol so that the wild-type and the double mutant, which lacks ergosterol but profoundly accumulates the intermediate precursor of ergosterol, showed no detectable difference ( Fig. 2D ). In contrast, previous studies have reported that genes involved in the early steps in ergosterol biosynthesis, such as erg11 ( cyp51A and cyp51B ) and erg25 ( erg25A and erg25B ), are essential. These data suggest that enzymes which are involved in different steps of the biosynthesis pathway of ergosterol have unique functions for fungal survival.

Interestingly, our findings indicate that inactivation of both erg4A and erg4B results in a white and fluffy colony with a severe conidiation defect ( Fig. 2A , Fig. S2), demonstrating that ergosterol is required for conidiation. Indeed, in the presence of extra ergosterol added to the culture medium, the conidiation of Δ erg4A Δ erg4B was partially rescued ( Fig. 2B ). However, to the best of our knowledge, how ergosterol might affect conidiation has not been reported until now. In yeasts, previous studies have verified that ergosterol was able to interact with the long acyl chain of sphingolipids to form microdomains within membranes to participate in pheromone signaling and plasma membrane fusion ( 7 , 8 ). In Aspergillus , many lines of evidence have indicated that the signal to control asexual development or vegetative growth is tightly regulated in a sequence ( 26 ). To date, a large number of proteins related to asexual development regulation, such as FluG, BrlA, and FlbB, have been reported ( 27 ). Interestingly, the conidiation-defective phenotype in Δ erg4A Δ erg4B was similar to that of the Δ fluG , Δ brlA , and Δ flbB strains, with a fluffy appearance, which implies that loss of both erg4A and erg4B may cause dysfunction of asexual development-related proteins ( 26 ). In addition, trimeric G-protein signaling has been reported to be involved in the regulation of vegetative growth and asexual development ( 28 – 30 ). Therefore, we speculate that ergosterol-enriched in plasma membranes may be necessary for the transduction of the extracellular signal into cells. If this is true, under the condition of ergosterol deficiency, trimeric G-protein or other relevant molecules might be unable to be localized to the appropriate functioning sites so that the signaling for asexual development is blocked. Of course, it is also possible that there may be other mechanisms such that Erg4A and Erg4B might interact with some unknown proteins that are required for conidiation.

Previous research has indicated that ScErg4 is able to interact with Ste20 (the p21-activated kinase) to regulate cell polarity, without which the apical growth is significantly affected ( 25 ). Different from results for the yeast Scerg4 mutant, our data indicate that Δ erg4A Δ erg4B showed morphology similar to that of the parental wild-type strain in hyphal polarized growth ( Fig. 2D ), which demonstrates that Erg4A and Erg4B are not essential for hyphal growth. This may also explain why Δ erg4A Δ erg4B still has wild-type virulence in a compromised mouse model ( Fig. 3 ). Compared to Erg4 in F. graminearum , erg4A and erg4B of A. fumigatus also encode sterol C-24 reductase, so they may share functions during fungal development ( 21 ). However, Fgerg4 is required for full virulence of F. graminearum , whereas Erg4A and Erg4B are not essential for virulence of A. fumigatus , suggesting that they may have different functions in vivo ( 21 ).

Erg4A and Erg4B mediate susceptibility to azole drugs.

In fungi, the widely used azoles, such as itraconazole and voriconazole, mainly target lanosterol 14α-demethylase, which is encoded by Erg11. The inhibition of lanosterol 14α-demethylase caused by azoles results in accumulation of toxic sterol and thus leads to cell death ( 31 ). In our study, we found that inactivation of erg4A and erg4B resulted in hypersensitivity to itraconazole and voriconazole ( Fig. 7A and B ), suggesting that Erg4A and Erg4B are required for drug resistance. To explain the reason for the increased sensitivity of Δ erg4A Δ erg4B to itraconazole and voriconazole, we first may exclude the mechanism mediated by ergosterol deficiency since a previous study in erg3 mutants with decreased ergosterol content has demonstrated that there was no significant difference in voriconazole susceptibility ( 17 ). Second, the possibility ofergosta-5,7,22,24(28)-tetraenol accumulation might also be excluded as the Δ erg5 mutant with blocked production of ergosta-5,7,22,24(28)-tetraenol in Neurospora crassa or Fusarium verticillioides also exhibited increased susceptibility to azoles ( 32 ). Therefore, the reason for the increased susceptibility is probably the changed fluidity of membranes or the varied activities of membrane-located azole pumps, which may be affected in the absence of Erg4A and Erg4B. Under this defect condition, azoles might be easy to be taken up or difficult to be drained off, causing hypersensitivity to them. Moreover, it has been reported that ketoconazole can directly bind Erg5 with an affinity similar to that for Erg11 in S. cerevisiae ( 33 ). Thus, we hypothesize that itraconazole and voriconazole may also have the ability to bind to Erg4. If this is true, the absence of Erg4 would increase the probability of targeting drugs to Erg11, resulting in the increased sensitivity to these antifungals. Another possible reason for the increased susceptibility of Δ erg4A Δ erg4B to itraconazole and voriconazole is the altered cell wall integrity compared to that of the parental wild-type strain ( 34 ). Additionally, previous studies in fungi have demonstrated that when azoles were used, the drug target, lanosterol 14α-demethylase enzyme activity encoded by erg11 , was inhibited, resulting in an abnormal accumulation of a toxic sterol [14α-methylergosta-8,24(28)-dien-3β,6α-diol]. This suggests that toxic sterol accumulation might be a reason for the arresting of fungal cell growth ( 31 , 35 ). In yeasts, inactivation of Δ 5,6 desaturase encoded by erg3 was able to suppress the growth defect under the treatment of azoles and losing Δ 5,6 desaturase might decrease accumulation of a toxic sterol [14α-methylergosta-8,24(28)-dien-3β,6α-diol] ( 36 , 37 ). Based on this information, we hypothesize that hypersensitivity to azoles in Δ erg4A Δ erg4B may be due to accumulation of toxic sterols induced by these drugs. However, further investigation is necessary to verify this hypothesis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Strains, media, and culture conditions.

All strains of A. fumigatus used in this study are given in Table 1 . Generally, A. fumigatus strains were grown on YAG- or YUU (YAG supplemented with 5 mM uridine and 10 mM uracil)-rich medium, according to Jiang et al., which contained 2% glucose, 0.5% yeast extract, and 1 ml/liter 1,000× trace elements ( 38 ). To test the sensitivity of A. fumigatus to stresses, NaCl, d -sorbitol, H 2 O 2 , menadione, Congo red, calcofluor white, itraconazole, voriconazole, terbinafine, amphotericin B, and caspofungin were supplemented in the YAG or YUU medium. For the plate-point assay, a 2-μl slurry of the indicated spores from the stock suspensions (10 7 , 10 6 , and 10 5 /ml) was spotted onto the YAG or YUU medium. All plates were incubated at 37°C for 1.5 to 2 days. For medium to screen transformants, generally 0.1 μg/ml pyrithiamine (Sigma) or 200 μg/ml hygromycin B (Sangon) was added to the related medium ( 39 ).

Deletion and complementation of erg4A and/or erg4B .

All primers used in this study are shown in Table 2 . For the construction of the erg4A deletion cassette, the fusion PCR was used as previously described ( 40 ). Microscopic observation.

To visualize the subcellular localization of GFP-Erg4A and Erg4B-GFP, the indicated strains were incubated in 3 ml of liquid YUU medium on coverslips for 18 h. For nucleus staining, 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma) that dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used at a final concentration of 0.8 μg/ml and incubated for 5 min at room temperature after fixing with 4% paraformaldehyde (Polysciences, Warrington, PA). For chitin and sterol-rich membrane domain staining, calcofluor white (Sigma) and filipin (Sigma) were used at a final concentration of 2 μg/ml. For observation of the conidiophore structure, coverslips were stuck to the plate on which each strain was spread and incubated at 37°C for 1.5 to 2 days. Images were captured using a Zeiss Axio imager A1 microscope (Zeiss, Jena, Germany), and the photos were managed with Adobe Photoshop.

RNA extraction for qRT-PCR.

Total RNA of the indicated strains was isolated from the fresh mycelia using TRIzol (Roche) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR), methods were used as previously described ( 41 ).

Ergosterol extraction and analysis.

The extraction and analysis of ergosterol were performed as described previously ( 13 , 42 ). Briefly, 10 8 spores of each strain were incubated in 100 ml of YG medium (YAG medium without agar) or YUU medium at the speed of 220 rpm at 37°C for 24 h. Mycelia were obtained via filtration with gauze, washed 3 times with distilled water, and lyophilized. Approximately 100 to 200 mg of dry mycelia was treated with 3 ml of 25% alcoholic potassium hydroxide (3:2, methanol to ethanol) and incubated at 85°C for 1 h. Thereafter, 1 ml of distilled water and 3 ml of pentane were added and vortexed for 3 min. The solution was then set aside for 10 min, and the upper layer was transferred to a clear tube and evaporated in a fume hood at room temperature. Before analysis, all samples were dissolved in 1 ml of methanol and filtered with a 0.2-μm pore-size filter. Total ergosterol was analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatograph (HPLC) (Agilent Technologies) and detected at 282 nm on an AQ-C18 column (250 mm by 4.6 mm, 5 μm) with a flow rate of 1 ml/min.

Virulence assay.

Virulence assays used in this study were performed according to the method developed by Li et al. and Zhang et al. ( 43 , 44 ). Briefly, 6- to 8-week-old male mice (ICR) were immunosuppressed on days −3 and −1 with cyclophosphamide (150 mg/kg) and on day −1 with hydrocortisone acetate (40 mg/kg). On day 0, after anesthetization with pentobarbital sodium, mice were infected intratracheally with a 50-μl slurry that contained 10 6 conidia or 50 μl of PBS as the control. After infection, cyclophosphamide (75 mg/kg) was injected every 3 days to maintain immunosuppression. For mortality statistics, mice were monitored for 14 days after inoculation. For histopathological analysis, lungs were isolated from the sacrificed mice and fixed in 4% formaldehyde (vol/vol) before periodic acid-Schiff staining. All animal experiments in this study were performed according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the U.S. National Institutes of Health ( 45 ). The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Normal University, China (permit no. 2090658), according to the governmental guidelines for animal care.
