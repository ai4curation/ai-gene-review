Plants can sense temperature changes and adjust their growth accordingly. In
Arabidopsis, high ambient temperatures stimulate stem elongation by activating a
key thermoresponsive regulator, PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PIF4). Here,
we show that warmth promotes the nighttime transcription of GI, which is
necessary for the high temperature-induced transcription of TOC1. Genetic
analyses suggest that GI prevents excessive thermoresponsive growth by
inhibiting PIF4, with this regulatory mechanism being partially reliant on TOC1.
GI transcription is repressed by ELF3 and HY5, which concurrently inhibit PIF4
expression and activity. Temperature elevation causes the deactivation or
degradation of ELF3 and HY5, leading to PIF4 activation and relief of GI
transcriptional repression at high temperatures. This allows PIF4 to further
activate GI transcription in response to elevated temperatures. GI, in turn,
inhibits PIF4, establishing a negative feedback loop that fine-tunes PIF4
activity. In addition, we demonstrate that ELF3, HY5, and PIF4 regulate GI
transcription by modulating the enrichment of histone variant H2A.Z at the GI
locus. Together, our findings suggest that thermal release of a negative
feedback loop finely adjusts plant thermomorphogenesis.

Introduction

Understanding how plants adapt to high temperatures is becoming increasingly important as global warming threatens plant growth and crop production. Even a mild increase in temperature can trigger a series of architectural and developmental changes in plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana. These include enhanced hypocotyl and petiole elongation and the formation of thinner leaves (Quint et al., 2016; Ibanez et al., 2017; Casal and Balasubramanian, 2019). Collectively, these adjustments are termed thermomorphogenesis and enable plants to dissipate excess heat under conditions of high ambient temperature (Crawford et al., 2012; Quint et al., 2016). Recent studies have uncovered complex mechanisms that regulate plant thermal adaptation, which are intimately linked to light response and circadian rhythm (Li et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021).

The basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) transcription factor PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PIF4) plays a central role in regulating thermomorphogenesis (Perrella et al., 2022). High temperatures activate PIF4, which in turn stimulates cell elongation and thermomorphogenic growth by upregulating auxin pathway genes (Koini et al., 2009; Franklin et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2012). Besides PIF4, other PIF family proteins also contribute to environmentally induced plant elongation growth (Leivar and Quail, 2011). Multiple integrated pathways regulate the transcription of PIF4 and its protein stability and activity. A core circadian clock component, EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3), acts as a thermosensor and repressor of PIF4 transcription (Mizuno et al., 2014; Box et al., 2015; Raschke et al., 2015; Jung et al., 2020). Moreover, ELF3 directly interacts with PIF4 protein and inhibits its activity (Nieto et al., 2015). Elevated temperature inactivates ELF3 and releases thermoresponsive growth, primarily at night (Mizuno et al., 2014; Box et al., 2015; Jung et al., 2020). The red-light photoreceptor and thermosensor phytochrome B (phyB) interacts with PIFs, causing their phosphorylation and subsequent degradation (Lorrain et al., 2008). High temperature induces the conversion of phyB from the active form Pfr to the inactive form Pr (Jung et al., 2016; Legris et al., 2016). LONG HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5) binds to the PIF4 promoter and suppresses PIF4 transcription at cool temperatures (Lee et al., 2007; Delker et al., 2014). HY5 and PIF4 also compete with each other for target binding, and they exhibit opposite effects on the transcription of target genes (Toledo-Ortiz et al., 2014). HY5 protein is degraded at high temperatures (Toledo-Ortiz et al., 2014; Kim et al., 2017), coinciding with the increase in PIF4 abundance and activity.

Although thermomorphogenesis is critical for plant adaptation to high temperature conditions, uncontrolled stem elongation can cause spindliness and can be potentially detrimental to plant survival. FLOWERING TIME CONTROL PROTEIN A (FCA) mediates removal of the permissive histone modification H3K4me2 at the PIF4 target YUCCA8 (YUC8), restricting PIF4 binding to YUC8 and attenuating thermomorphogenesis (Lee et al., 2014). The blue-light receptor CRYPTOCHROME 1 (CRY1) physically interacts with PIF4 in a blue light-dependent manner and inhibits PIF4 activity to repress thermomorphogenesis (Ma et al., 2016). Ultraviolet-B light (UV-B) suppresses thermomorphogenesis via its receptor UV RESISTANCE LOCUS 8 (UVR8), which works together with CONSTITUTIVELY PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1) to repress PIF4 transcript accumulation (Hayes et al., 2017). In blue light or UV-B, CRY1 or UVR8 also helps to stabilize the bHLH protein LONG HYPOCOTYL IN FAR RED (HFR1), which inhibits PIF4 activity at high temperatures (Ma et al., 2016; Hayes et al., 2017). A key circadian clock regulator, TIMING OF CAB EXPRESSION1 (TOC1), represses both the transcription of PIF4 and the transcriptional activation activity of its protein (Zhu et al., 2016; Li et al., 2020). TOC1 expression peaks at the end of the day and in early evening. Therefore, TOC1 has been suggested to suppress PIF4 and thermomorphogenesis, particularly during this period, and to mediate the circadian gating of thermoresponsive growth (Zhu et al., 2016).

The plant-specific protein GIGANTEA (GI) regulates the protein stability of TOC1 by interacting with the F-box protein ZEITLUPE (ZTL) (Kim et al., 2007). Loss of GI results in increased TOC1 protein levels during the day but reduced levels during the night (Kim et al., 2007). In addition, GI is involved in regulating the transcription of TOC1 (Pokhilko et al., 2010; Anwer et al., 2020). Like that of TOC1, expression of GI is controlled by the circadian clock and serves to sustain the circadian oscillator (Park et al., 1999; Mizoguchi et al., 2005; David et al., 2006; Kim et al., 2013a). In addition, GI has diverse functions in plant development and responses to environmental stimuli, including flowering-time regulation, light signaling, and stress tolerance (Huq et al., 2000; Suarez-Lopez et al., 2001; Martin-Tryon et al., 2007; Oliverio et al., 2007; Sawa et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2013b; Riboni et al., 2013). Recent studies have shown that GI interacts with PIF1, PIF3, PIF4, and PIF5 and inhibits their transcriptional activity by directly modulating their stability and target-binding ability (Nohales et al., 2019). GI has been proposed to accumulate during the day and early evening, suppressing PIF activity during this period, whereas a decrease in GI expression at night enables PIF release (Nohales et al., 2019). Moreover, GI stabilizes the DELLA protein REPRESSOR OF ga1-3 (RGA), a suppressor of PIF4 (Nohales and Kay, 2019; Park et al., 2020). GI abundance is higher under long-day (LD) conditions than under short-day (SD) conditions, contributing to the photoperiodic control of thermomorphogenesis (Park et al., 2020).

Although expression of TOC1 and GI usually declines at night, coinciding with the release of PIF4 activity during this period (Zhu et al., 2016; Nohales et al., 2019), a previous study suggested that elevated temperature at night stimulated TOC1 and GI expression (Mizuno et al., 2014), hinting at the influence of temperature on expression of these genes. In this study, we corroborate the thermal induction of both TOC1 and GI, especially at night, and investigate the regulatory mechanism and the significance of this regulation for plant thermoresponsive growth. Our results demonstrate that the increase in TOC1 expression at high temperatures requires GI, which suppresses thermomorphogenesis by inhibiting PIF4, partially via TOC1. GI transcription is repressed by ELF3 and HY5 but promoted by PIF4. As ELF3 and HY5 are deactivated or degraded under high temperature conditions, this relieves the repression of GI transcription and promotes PIF4 expression and activity, leading to further activation of GI by PIF4. These findings suggest the formation of a negative feedback loop involving GI, TOC1, and PIF4 at warm temperatures, which helps to adjust thermomorphogenic growth.

We next analyzed GI transcript levels in mutants of ELF3, phyB, or HY5 to investigate their roles in regulating GI transcription. Loss of ELF3, phyB, or HY5 resulted in increased GI transcript levels, especially at night; ELF3 mutation had the strongest effect and phyB mutation the weakest (Figure 4C and 4D; Supplemental Figure 7B). These results suggest that ELF3, phyB, and HY5 repress GI transcription. Along with the induction of GI, TOC1 transcript levels were also increased in elf3-7 and hy5-2 mutants (Supplemental Figure 7C). To determine whether ELF3 functions in the EC to repress GI transcription, we measured GI transcript levels in elf4-209 and lux-6 mutants and found that ELF4 and LUX were required for GI repression as well (Supplemental Figure 7D).

Because ELF3 and HY5 showed a stronger regulatory effect on GI transcript levels than phyB, we focused on ELF3 and HY5 and tested their direct effect on GI transcription using a transient expression assay in Arabidopsis protoplasts. Because the LUC reporter driven by the GI promoter alone was expressed at low levels in Arabidopsis protoplasts, hindering further analyses, a minimal 35S promoter was fused with the GI promoter to drive LUC expression in Arabidopsis protoplasts, which increased LUC signals. The bioluminescence signals of pGI::LUC were reduced when it was co-expressed with ELF3 or HY5 in Arabidopsis protoplasts (Figure 4E–4G). However, deletion of the ELF3-binding motif (AAGATTTT: −817 to −809) or the HY5-binding motif (CACGTCATCA: −872 to −862) from the GI promoter abolished the repressive effect of ELF3 or HY5, respectively, on the GI promoter (Figure 4B and 4E–4G), suggesting that ELF3 and HY5 repress GI transcription by recognizing their binding motifs.

We next performed a complementation test with gi-2 using 3×Myc (Myc)-fused GI genomic sequences driven by the GI promoter or lacking the ELF3- or HY5-binding motif (Figure 4H). For each transgenic event, two independent, single-copy transgenic lines were randomly selected, and T3 homozygous were obtained. Compared with plants complemented with the intact GI promoter, transgenic lines in which the ELF3- or HY5-binding motif was deleted from the GI promoter displayed reduced hypocotyl elongation and increased exogenous GI transcript levels at high temperatures (Figure 4I and 4J), confirming the significance of ELF3- and HY5-binding motifs in GI transcriptional control. In Arabidopsis, the activity of ELF3 decreases at high temperatures (Jung et al., 2016, 2020; Legris et al., 2016), consistent with the reduced binding of ELF3 at the GI promoter (Figure 4A). In addition, high temperature triggers the degradation of HY5 (Toledo-Ortiz et al., 2014; Kim et al., 2017). Therefore, it is likely that high temperature relieves the suppression of GI transcription by weakening ELF3 and HY5.
