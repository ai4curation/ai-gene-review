SLIT2 is a secreted protein that repels axons from the CNS midline. Full-length
SLIT2 (SLIT2-FL) is proteolytically cleaved into two fragments, SLIT2-N and
SLIT2-C. SLIT2-FL and SLIT2-N have opposing biological effects on cultured
dorsal root ganglion (DRG) axons. This study identified SLIT2 cleavage
mechanisms and functional significance for DRG axon guidance. The
Tolloid-related protease TLL1 cleaved SLIT2 in cultured cells, with TLL1
requiring activation by furin/prohormone convertases. We used CRISPR editing in
mice to produce a Slit2ΔTLS allele lacking the TLL1 cleavage site. Slit2ΔTLS
embryos retained dorsal repulsion of DRG axons, in contrast to DRG midline
invasion in Slit2 knockouts. However, DRG axons in Slit2 knockouts and Slit2ΔTLS
mutants showed reduced fasciculation of rootlets and longitudinal DRG
projections. In vitro, SLIT2-N promoted fasciculation of DRG axons. These
results suggest that proteolytic cleavage generates additional SLIT2 biological
functions for organizing DRG central axon projections.

Introduction

Despite the complexity of connectivity in the nervous system, only a small number of axon guidance cues have been identified as responsible for establishing neuronal connectivity. Navigating axons respond to guidance cues within their tissue substrate via their growth cones. Growth cones react to cues with directional responses such as repulsion or attraction, and related responses such as growth rates and adhesion to other axons. One solution to the small number of cues is to have multiple receptors for a single cue protein that can mediate different responses (Cortés et al., 2023). Alternatively, a cue protein can be modified by proteolysis to generate fragments with distinct functions via other receptors (Zang et al., 2022). These modifications of cue signals and receptors potentially expand diversity in signal output from the small number of axon cues.

SLITs are large, secreted glycoproteins extensively studied as repellents in the central nervous system (CNS) midline. SLITs are evolutionarily conserved, for example, with one SLIT in Drosophila, and three in vertebrates, including the main focus of this study, mouse SLIT2. SLIT repulsive activity is mediated by the Robo receptors, specifically ROBO1 and 2 in vertebrates (Blockus and Chédotal, 2016; Brose et al., 1999; Kidd et al., 1998; Kidd et al., 1999). However, SLITs are cleaved into fragments with potentially diverse functions, including positive signals in specific contexts. SLIT2-FL (~180 kDa) is proteolytically processed into N-terminal (SLIT2-N; ~150 kDa) and C-terminal (SLIT2-C; ~55 kDa) fragments. In previous studies using cultured DRG axons, SLIT2-FL inhibited growth, while SLIT2-N promoted outgrowth and branching (Nguyen-Ba-Charvet et al., 2001a; Wang et al., 1999), while SLIT2-N can also promote the fasciculation of cultured motor axons (Jaworski and Tessier-Lavigne, 2012). In Drosophila, SLIT-N functions via distinct receptors, as the SLIT-N fragment binds to Drosophila ROBO1 and DSCAM1 to promote longitudinal axon guidance and sensory axon branching. The role of SLIT2-C is less well understood, but appears to repel commissural axons and influence thermogenesis (Delloye-Bourgeois et al., 2015; Svensson et al., 2016). While proteolytic cleavage has been assumed as necessary for SLIT repulsive activity, Drosophila Slit transgenes that rescue Slit null alleles showed that only full-length SLIT is repulsive and that the SLIT fragments have different functions (Coleman et al., 2010; Kellermeyer et al., 2020). In contrast, in the developing vertebrate nervous system, the potential in vivo functions of SLIT cleavage and fragments remain largely unknown.

While the initial discovery of SLITs revealed the existence of the N and C fragments, the identification of the proteolytic mechanism producing SLIT fragments has progressed only in recent years. The first candidate SLIT protease was Drosophila amontilado/amon, a homolog to vertebrate proprotein convertase 2 (PC2) (Ordan and Volk, 2016). Tests of PC2 function as a SLIT2 protease left unresolved whether this was a direct or indirect interaction (Ducuing et al., 2020). We have recently identified the protease responsible for cleaving SLIT in Drosophila as the BMP-1/Tolloid family metalloprotease, Tolkin (Tlr/Tok (Kellermeyer et al., 2020)). This implicates Tolloid family members as candidate vertebrate SLIT proteases. Previously identified functions of Tolloid family genes include regulation of extracellular matrix assembly and processing of developmental signaling proteins (Troilo et al., 2016). Intriguingly, Tolloid family metalloproteases require activation by members of the Prohormone convertase/Furin families of proteases (Leighton and Kadler, 2003; Raeeszadeh-Sarmazdeh et al., 2020), suggesting a possible indirect role for Furins in SLIT processing.

The overall goal of our study was to gain insights into the potentially distinct roles of full-length SLIT2 and SLIT2 fragments in axon guidance in the vertebrate nervous system. One axon population with potentially diverse responses to SLIT2 fragment signals are the sensory neurons in dorsal root ganglia (DRGs). Each ganglion initially projects axons in bundles to enter the spinal cord. There, each axon bifurcates in the dorsal root entry zone (DREZ), with daughter branches projecting longitudinally both anteriorly and posteriorly to form the tightly fasciculated dorsolateral funiculus (DLF) tract. In Slit1/2 knockout mice, which lack both full length and fragments for both genes, SLIT signals are required to repel DRG axons from the roof plate, with axons in double mutant embryos overshooting the DLF to extend toward the dorsal midline (Ma and Tessier-Lavigne, 2007). These in vivo SLIT guidance functions indicate midline repulsive signals but may involve fragment signals with diverse functions. In explant cultures, a plasmid expressing a modified SLIT2 with a deletion of the cleavage site (SLIT2-U) repels DRG axons, while SLIT2-N promotes axon growth and branching (Nguyen-Ba-Charvet et al., 2001a; Wang et al., 1999). SLIT2-N can also initiate growth cone collapse at high concentrations (Ma and Tessier-Lavigne, 2007; McConnell et al., 2016) but the role of SLIT2-N on DRG axons in vivo is unclear. These in vivo mutant phenotypes and cultured axon responses to SLIT2 suggest that DRG central axon branches may be responsive to SLIT2 fragment signals in vivo, but a strategy to selectively test full length from fragment functions would be needed.

In this study, we sought to identify the distinct functions of SLIT2-FL and SLIT2-N in developing mouse embryos. To study the function of SLIT2-FL and SLIT2-N, we first created a novel Slit2 mouse allele which disrupted the canonical cleavage site. To develop tools to study cleavage mechanisms and fragment functions, we identified that BMP1/Tolloid metalloprotease family members were sufficient to cleave SLIT2 in vitro, and that TLL1 was the primary protease in vivo. We discovered novel axon errors in Slit2 knockout mutants that disrupt key fasciculation events which organize DRG axons, and these fasciculation errors were also found in SLIT2 cleavage and TLL1 protease mutants. Together these results reveal positive SLIT2 functions for DRG axons that implicate proteolytic processing mechanisms in modulating SLIT2 guidance.

ResultsTolloid family metalloproteases TLL1 and BMP-1 cleave Slit2SLIT2 is an evolutionarily conserved protein with similar domains and a highly conserved cleavage site compared to Drosophila SLIT (Fig. 1A). In Drosophila, the sole SLIT protease is tolkin (tok) (Kellermeyer et al., 2020). The closest vertebrate homolog to Tok is Tolloid-like1 (TLL1) (Clark et al., 1999; Scott et al., 1999a). TLL1 potentially has expression in small numbers of cells in the dorsal spinal cord of embryonic mice. We analyzed the single cell RNA seq data of (Delile et al., 2019) to determine the expression pattern of Tll1 in the embryonic spinal cord. Querying the datasets revealed that Tll1 is expressed at a low level in a very sparse pattern in cells of the developing spinal cord, likely neuroepithelial cells. As Tolloid proteases are highly diffusible, this makes establishing the site of cleavage challenging. However, previous work in Drosophila suggests that Slit cleavage occurs extracellularly upon binding to Robo receptors (Ordan and Volk, 2015).To test if TLL1 cleaves SLIT2, we co-expressed human TLL1 and human SLIT2 in Drosophila S2 cells. SLIT2 was not endogenously cleaved in S2 cells, as previously noted (Brose et al., 1999). Similarly, SLIT2 was not endogenously cleaved in mouse Neuro2a (N2a) cells. Co-transfection with hTLL1 generated SLIT2 N- and C- fragments (Fig. 1B). These findings indicate that TLL1 protease activity is sufficient to induce SLIT2 cleavage. It should be noted that many mammalian cell lines, such as COS7, express endogenous SLIT protease activity (Fig 1C,F).Previous work proposed PC2, a proprotein convertase with known targets that include most if not all neuropeptide precursors (Zhang et al., 2010), as a vertebrate SLIT protease (Ducuing et al., 2020). To test whether TLL1 acts directly on full-length SLIT2, we transfected COS7 cells with human SLIT2, human TLL1, or an activated TLL1 lacking its inhibitory prodomain (Scott et al., 1999b). The COS7 cells were then treated with chloromethylketone (CMK), a PC2 inhibitor. CMK treatment of cells expressing wildtype TLL1 inhibited SLIT2 cleavage (Fig 1C), as previously reported (Ducuing et al., 2020). However, in CMK-treated cells co-transfected with SLIT2 and activated TLL1, we found significant levels of SLIT2 cleavage (Fig. 1C), evidence that direct SLIT2 cleavage by TLL1 is resistant to CMK inhibition. From these results, we propose a model in which PC2 activates TLL1 by cleavage of the regulatory prodomain, thus enabling TLL1 cleavage of SLIT2. To test if SLIT2 is a direct target of the TLL1 protease, we next assayed for a physical interaction of TLL1 and SLIT2. We performed a co-IP with human TLL1-FLAG and a SLIT2-U expression plasmid carrying a mutation that deletes the known cleavage site (Nguyen-Ba-Charvet et al., 2001b). We found by pull down with a FLAG antibody that TLL1 and SLIT2-U physically interacted (Fig. 1D). The potent SLIT cleavage activity and evidence for direct physical interaction suggest that SLIT2 is a direct TLL1 substrate.The bone morphogenic protein (BMP)-1/Tolloid family of proteases is a family of metalloproteases that plays many roles in embryo patterning, from heart development to axon morphogenesis (Clark et al., 1999; Scott et al., 1999a). To examine the potential redundancy within this family, we tested three additional related proteases in SLIT cleavage. We co-transfected N2a cells with hSLIT2 and the following candidate proteases: hTLL1, hTLL2, mTolloid (mTld), and hBMP-1. In addition to hTLL1, hBMP-1 was also sufficient to cleave hSLIT2, while hTLL2 was not (Fig 1E,F). Interestingly, we found that the ratio of protease to SLIT2 expression (by transfecting with different concentrations of expression plasmids) was critically important in these experiments. If protease levels were too high, we saw a complete loss of SLIT2 (FL and fragments) from cell media and cell lysates (not shown). This was observed for all of the tested proteases, in several different cell lines. We did observe cleavage in mTld-expressing cells (Fig 1E,F), although the quantification of this cleavage did not reach statistical significance due to the loss of SLIT2 expression in many of these samples. These results show that TLL1 is a SLIT2 protease, and suggest that BMP-1 and Tld may also participate in SLIT2 cleavage (Scott et al., 1999a).Development of a mouse SLIT2 cleavage site alleleTo study the function of full-length SLIT2 in the absence of TLL1-generated SLIT2 fragments in vivo, we designed an allele of mouse Slit2 with a cleavage site mutation, Slit2 ΔTLS (deletion of Tolloid-like site). The mutation removed nine amino acids upstream of and including the cleavage site (Fig. 1A), similar to the previously reported Slit2-U construct which prevents SLIT2 cleavage in cultured cells (Fig S1; Nguyen-Ba-Charvet et al., 2001b). Our allele was first tested as a Slit2 ΔTLS plasmid in COS7 cells, which express an endogenous activity that cleaves transfected SLIT2, but were unable to cleave the SLIT2 full length protein produced by the plasmid, lacking detectable SLIT2-N or –C bands (data not shown). None of the tested BMP-1/Tolloid proteases cleaved the Slit2ΔTLS mutant allele in co-transfections, validating the allele design (Fig. 1E,F). Like SLIT2-FL, we saw loss of Slit2ΔTLS in cells overexpressing the proteases at too high of a level. This indicates that the loss of SLIT2 due to protease over-expression does not require cleavage at the known cleavage site.We generated a Slit2ΔTLS mouse allele via CRISPR, with two independent mouse lines generated and verified by sequencing of the genomic locus, and one chosen for further analysis. The Slit2ΔTLS mouse allele was homozygous lethal, likely prior to E9.5. Heterozygous crosses failed to produce homozygous mutant embryos (0 of 39 embryos), suggesting that this cleavage site mutation is detrimental to early embryo survival. Surprisingly, the Slit2ΔTLS allele as a trans-heterozygote with a Slit2 knockout allele produced Slit2ΔTLS/− embryos and adult mice, showing that a single Slit2ΔTLS/− cleavage site allele is sufficient for embryo viability in the absence of wildtype Slit2 alleles.To test processing of the Slit2ΔTLS cleavage site allele in vivo, we assessed SLIT2 fragments in samples from whole embryo lysates by western blotting. Lysates from E12.5 mouse embryos were probed with antibodies for both SLIT2-N and -C epitopes (Fig. 1G). Lysates were first probed with the SLIT2-N antibody. This showed the presence of SLIT2-FL at ~200 kDa in Slit2ΔTLS/− and Tll1 knockout embryos, contrasting with the loss of SLIT2-FL in Slit2−/− embryos, verifying that our Slit2− (Δ8) allele is null (Gibson et al., 2014) (Fig. 1G). Unexpectedly, both full length and a cleaved fragment of the expected molecular weight for SLIT2-N were observed in the Slit2ΔTLS/− and Tll1 knockout lysates with the SLIT2-N antibody, but not in the Slit2−/− embryos, verifying that this is a fragment of SLIT2, and not a cross-reacting band (as observed with the SLIT2-C antibody in embryos and in cell lysates). Based on our results with expression of (BMP)-1/Tolloid family proteases in transfected tissue culture cells, which showed no cleavage of SLIT2 ΔTLS protein, we suggest that this N-reacting band in the Slit2ΔTLS/− and Tll1 knockout lysates arises from cleavage by other proteases at a non-canonical site.Evidence that the Slit2ΔTLS allele disrupted SLIT2 fragment production in vivo was obtained from probing the lysates with the C-fragment antibody. Using this antibody, the full length 200 kDa fragment was observed in all samples except Slit2−/−, consistent with the prediction that our Slit2− (Δ8) allele is null (Gibson et al., 2014). However, there was a prominent non-specific band in all samples obscuring the detection of the C fragment at 55–60 kDa, including in the Slit2−/− null mutants. We observed this very prominent off-target band in tissue culture cell lysates, as well as in lysates of adult mouse tissues (data not shown), but not in tissue culture conditioned media samples (Fig. 1B). To resolve SLIT2-C from the off-target band, we developed a strategy to assess SLIT2 cleavage with 2D gel electrophoresis and western blotting. Embryo lysates were run first in one dimension to separate proteins by isoelectric point, then in a second dimension to separate the proteins by molecular weight. Blots were then labeled with the SLIT2-C antibody (Fig. 1H, I). At the expected isoelectric point for SLIT2, two bands were present in the wild-type samples but were absent in Slit2−/− mutants, indicating these bands are SLIT2-C products. We evaluated our novel Slit2ΔTLS/− allele and Tll1 knockout for SLIT2 cleavage and found the SLIT2-C bands absent, consistent with the expected disruption of cleavage (Fig. 1I). Based on the evidence that C-fragment production is disrupted, as well as the subsequent axon phenotypes observed in Slit2ΔTLS/− and TLL1−/− mutant mice, the Slit2ΔTLS mutant allele appears to disrupt SLIT2 cleavage at the canonical known cleavage site, while also providing evidence that TLL1 is necessary for SLIT2 cleavage at this site in vivo.Slit2ΔTLS allele retains dorsal repellent activity for DRG axons entering the spinal cordDRG sensory neurons extend their central axons into the dorsal spinal cord. These axons bifurcate inside the dorsal root entry zone (DREZ) and extend along the spinal cord to form the compact and highly fasciculated bundle of the dorsolateral funiculus (DLF) (Fig 2A, B, F) (Holmes et al., 1998). DRG axons are confined within the DLF due to the repulsive signaling of SLITs expressed in the roof plate (SLIT1 and SLIT2). Previously, Slit1/2 mutant embryos were shown to have DRG axons which overshoot toward the midline (Ma and Tessier-Lavigne, 2007) due to a lack of SLIT-mediated dorsal repulsion. Slit2 mRNA expression has prominent roof plate expression, in contrast to Slit1 (Brose et al., 1999). To resolve which SLIT was responsible for dorsal repulsion, DRG axon projections were examined by neuronal antibody labeling in spinal cord sections, and axon tracing in whole mounts of spinal cords. Slit2−/− and Robo1−/−; Robo2−/− mutants, but not Slit1−/− mutants, had overshooting DRG axons (Figs. 2C, G, S2A, and data not shown). This extends the previous study by showing that SLIT2, but not SLIT1, acts via Robo1/2 receptors and is essential to repel DRG axons from the roof plate.We then evaluated DRG guidance in the dorsal spinal cord in Slit2ΔTLS and TLL1 genotypes. Neither Slit2ΔTLS or Tll1−/− had stray axons extending towards the roof plate (Fig. 2D, D’, I; E, E’, J). This indicates that the SLIT2 signals produced by Slit2ΔTLS and Tll1−/− embryos retain function that is sufficient to repel DRG axons from the roof plate. These results are consistent with previous findings demonstrating that a plasmid expressing a modified SLIT2 with a deletion of the cleavage site (SLIT2-U) produced inhibitory signals for cultured DRG axons (Nguyen-Ba-Charvet et al., 2001a; Nguyen-Ba-Charvet et al., 2001b; Wang et al., 1999).Slit2ΔTLS disrupts fasciculation of DRG axons entering the spinal cordAlthough Slit2ΔTLS and Tll1−/− mutants retained DRG axon midline repulsion, these mutations led to dis-organization of the projection of DRG axons toward and within the spinal cord. In wildtype embryos, DRG axons initially project toward the spinal cord in a small number of distinct, fasciculated bundles (rootlets) that remain segregated from neighboring DRGs. These bundles enter the spinal cord at the dorsal root entry zone (DREZ) (Fig. 3A). Disruption of SLIT2 caused changes in the DRG axon bundles. Slit2−/− mutants had wider DREZs caused by spreading of defasciculated DRG rootlets (Fig. 3B,E). Additionally, axons occasionally crossed to merge with rootlets from adjacent ganglia (Fig. 3F,H). The Slit2ΔTLS allele caused a similar increased width and crossing of DRG axon bundles, indicating that the SLIT2 signals produced by this allele were not sufficient to promote fasciculation (Fig. 3G,H). The Tll1−/− mutants also had wider entry zones, although not significant crossing axons (Fig. 3D, E, H). This fasciculation function was further investigated by analysis of Robo1−/−; Robo2−/− embryos. Removal of Robo1 and Robo2 showed a similar phenotype of defasciculated DRG axons that resulted in wider DRG axon bundles and crossing (Fig. S2B). The DRG axon responses to Slit2−/−, Slit2ΔTLS, Tll1−/− , and Robo1/2 alleles suggests that SLIT2-ROBO normally produces signals with positive activities to organize condensed and segregated axon bundles as they project into the spinal cord. Because the roof plate is the closest prominent source of SLIT2 as assayed by in situ hybridization (Brose et al., 1999; Ma and Tessier-Lavigne, 2007), and evidently can repel DRG axons immediately after their entry into the spinal cord (Ma and Tessier-Lavigne, 2007), we speculate that this same SLIT2 signal may also reach just outside of the spinal cord to influence the approaching DRG axons before their entry.Slit2 knockout and Slit2ΔTLS alleles disrupt longitudinal fasciculation within the spinal cordDRG axons normally bifurcate after entering the spinal cord to project axons longitudinally to form the narrow DLF tract that carries DRG signals anteriorly and posteriorly to other spinal segments. DiI tracer labeling that targeted the DLF showed that this tract consisted almost entirely of axons projecting from several DRGs anterior and posterior to the label site, while few neuronal cell bodies in the spinal cord itself were detected (Supplementary Figure S3). This allowed the use of βIII-tubulin antibody labeling to evaluate the role of SLIT2 fragments in DLF organization.On E12.5, we examined E12.5 embryos from dorsal views with confocal microscopy. In wildtype embryos, DRG axons stayed tightly fasciculated within the DLF (Fig. 4A,F). In contrast, the DLF was noticeably depleted in Slit2−/− mutants, consistent with the frequent mis-projections into the medial spinal cord (Fig. 4B,G). There were also longitudinally projecting axons medial to the DLF. In Slit2ΔTLS mutants, the DLF had more prominent errant defasciculation, with longitudinal projections shifted medially towards the midline, although projections toward the midline were not present (Fig. 4C,H). In Robo1−/−; Robo2−/− mutants, similar phenotypes to Slit2−/− mutants were seen, with DRG axons projecting to the roof plate, but not defasciculation from the DLF (Fig. S2C). Tll1−/− mutants had similar defasciculation errors, with DRG axons outside the boundaries of the DLF (Fig. 4D,I). Upon examination of DLF width/depth from a transverse, cross-section view, the DLF in Tll1−/− mutants appears thinner than wildtype DLF (Fig. 4I, J). The observed thinning of the DLF is likely due poor visualization of depleted neurons from the DLF. The disrupted DLF organization is apparent two days earlier, on E10.5, as we also observed a wider DLF tract in side views of the DLF in E10.5 embryos (Fig. 4K–N). These observations extend the SLIT2 role in organizing DRG axons into the spinal cord, indicating a continuing positive function of SLIT2 signals, dependent on normal proteolytic processing, to organize the DRG axons into the condensed longitudinal DLF tract.SLIT2-N promotes fasciculation of cultured DRG neuronsThe fasciculation phenotypes of DRG axons in mutant embryos suggests that SLIT2 produces signals with positive functions to promote DRG fasciculation. To test if SLIT2-N can directly promote DRG axon fasciculation, we cultured dissociated E11.5 DRG neurons for 24 hours in the presence of SLIT2-N. We found DRG axons cultured in control conditions had a low base level of fasciculation with many single axons (Figure 5A). When DRG neurons were cultured with recombinant SLIT2-N protein, axon fasciculation increased as SLIT2 concentration was increased (Figure 5B, C, D). Importantly, the concentrations of SLIT2-N that were effective at increasing fasciculation did not cause a significant inhibition of DRG axon growth. When DRG neurons were cultured with recombinant SLIT2-C protein or SLIT2-U produced in COS7 cells, DRG axon growth was inhibited (Fig. 5E, F). Due to the lack of axon growth compared to mock or SLIT2-N treatments, we were unable to compare the effects of SLIT2-C or SLIT2-U on fasciculation. Tracing of DRG axons with diIThe lipophilic axon tracer diI was used to trace DRG axon projections within the spinal cord by insertion of small crystals of diI into DRGs in fixed embryos, followed by incubation in 0.1M phosphate buffer at 37C overnight for tracer diffusion. Several DRGs were labeled in each embryo. The spinal cords with attached DRGs were dissected out, clarified by incubation in 50% glycerol; 50% 0.1M phosphate buffer, and examined and imaged by fluorescence microscopy. To trace the DLF, E11.75 embryo spinal cords were dissected to remove single DRGs at two or three locations on each side, then small diI crystals were placed into the entry zones to label the superficial DLF.
