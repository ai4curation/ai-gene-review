The dynamic balance between formation and disaggregation of amyloid fibrils is
associated with many neurodegenerative diseases. Multiple chaperones interact
with and disaggregate amyloid fibrils, which impacts amyloid propagation and
cellular phenotypes. However, it remains poorly understood whether and how
site-specific binding of chaperones to amyloids facilitates the concerted
disaggregation process and modulates physiological consequences in vivo. Here,
we identified binding sites of Ssa1, Sis1, and Hsp104 chaperones for Sup35, the
protein determinant of yeast prion [PSI+] yeast. Our biophysical and genetic
analyses with various Sup35 deletion mutants and amyloid conformations revealed
that the Ssa1-binding to the region outside amyloid core plays a key role in
facilitating disaggregation and propagation of yeast prions both in vitro and in
vivo. Furthermore, we developed a reconstitution system, including the
Ssa1-binding tag and the HAP/Caseinolytic protease P (ClpP) hybrid chaperones,
and found that this reconstitution system successfully degraded distinct prion
strain conformations. Together, these results show that the properly positioned,
exposed Ssa1-binding region in amyloid fibrils influences the efficiency of
amyloid disaggregation and propagation, and eventually prion strain phenotypes.
More broadly, our findings provide molecular foundations for previous, puzzling
observations of prion propagation in vivo, and offer insights into elimination
of amyloid deposits in cells.

## Full Text

Formation of β-sheet-rich fibrillar aggregates, amyloid fibrils, is associated with many neurodegenerative disorders including Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and prion diseases (1). Amyloid fibrils show cell-to-cell propagation, and this phenomenon is universally observed in various neurodegenerative diseases, highlighting the importance of amyloid propagation in the onset and progression of neurodegenerative disorders (2–4). The efficiency of amyloid propagation is likely to be regulated by the tendency of amyloid to undergo disaggregation, which includes both fibril fragmentation and monomer depolymerization (5–8), since the disaggregation efficiency could determine the number and the size of short, fragmented fibrils which can propagate more efficiently than long, matured fibrils. Importantly, short fiber fragments yielded by disaggregation recruit not only the same monomeric protein for amyloid growth but also other functional proteins into fibrils, which potentially elicits cellular dysfunctions (9). Our previous study revealed that the rate of amyloid fragmentation has greater effects on strain phenotypes than that of amyloid extension (7). Together, the efficiency of amyloid disaggregation will be one of the key determinants of both amyloid propagation and cellular phenotypes, suggesting that the activity of cellular disaggregation machinery provides substantial impacts on the pathogenesis of amyloid-associated diseases.

Disaggregation of amyloid fibrils is achieved by the actions of multiple chaperone proteins. Previous studies on interactions between amyloid fibrils and yeast/human chaperones have provided important insights into the complex process of amyloid disaggregation (10–14). Nonetheless, due to the lack of suitable experimental platforms for structural analysis, our mechanistic understanding is still highly limited as to whether and how site-specific interactions of a chaperone or a chaperone complex with amyloid fibrils initiates and facilitates the process of ATP-dependent, concerted amyloid disaggregation by multiple chaperones. More importantly, it remains unclear whether such interactions of each chaperone with amyloid fibrils can modulate physiological consequences in vivo. Furthermore, it has been challenging for a long time to develop an engineered chaperone system which efficiently degrades amyloid fibrils.

To address these key questions in amyloid biology, we combined biophysical techniques including NMR and total intensity reflection fluorescence (TIRF) spectroscopies with yeast genetics, and investigated interactions of Sup35NM, an aggregation-prone fragment [an N-terminal prion domain and a highly charged middle (M) domain] of yeast prion protein Sup35, which is a protein determinant of yeast prion [PSI+] state (15–17), with Ssa1(Hsp70), Sis1(Hsp40), or Hsp104 chaperone that are essential to disaggregation and propagation of Sup35NM fibrils (14, 18–21). Here, we identified a binding site of each chaperone in Sup35NM, and revealed roles of site-specific Ssa1-binding to fibrils in Sup35 prion propagation in vivo. Furthermore, our structural analysis allowed us to design an in vitro reconstitution system including the identified Ssa1-bindng tag and the HAP/ClpP hybrid chaperones, which were found to degrade distinct prion strain conformations. The present findings revealed that the properly positioned, exposed Ssa1-binding site can initiate the whole cascade of amyloid disaggregation, providing physical foundations for previous, otherwise puzzling observations in vivo (22–24). More broadly, our results suggest that the chaperone-binding to outside amyloid core region potentially modulates amyloid propagation and its physiological consequences in neurodegenerative diseases associated with amyloid deposition.

Here, we designed three Sup35NM mutants, termed M1, M2, and M3 (Fig. 4A). M1 contains an additional Ssa1-binding site (residues 143 to 164) at the C-terminus of Sup35NM, while M2 has an additional whole M domain. M3 contains a new Ssa1-binding site at the C-terminus but the endogenous Ssa1-binding site was eliminated, resulting in a positional shift of the Ssa1-binding site within Sup35NM. We polymerized the M1, M2, and M3 monomers in the presence of 5% (mol/mol) Sc4 WT amyloid seeds, and prepared the seeded fibrils denoted as M1[WT], M2[WT] and M3[WT], respectively, which harbor a WT Sc4-type amyloid core but contain a distinct Ssa1-binding region (33) (Fig. 4B). The above findings suggest a hypothesis that distinct amyloid conformations may undergo disaggregation with different efficiency, depending on whether the Ssa1-binding site in Sup35NM amyloid is exposed in a proper location or buried into amyloid core. Interestingly, contrary to the N-terminal amyloid core (residues 1 to 42) of WT Sc4 amyloid, the S17R mutant amyloid, which elicits characteristically weak and sectoring [PSI+] strain phenotypes (38), has a core region of the C-terminal prion domain (PrD-C; residues 81 to 148) (33). Notably, this PrD-C amyloid core partially overlaps with the Ssa1-binding site (residues 143 to 164) (Fig. 1B), indicating that disaggregation of S17R amyloid may be less efficient due to the buried Ssa1-binding region into the PrD-C amyloid core and thus potentially weakened recognition by Ssa1/Sis1 (Fig. 4B). Indeed, we revealed by the ThT assay that the S17R mutant amyloid shows resistance to disaggregation by Ssa1/Sis1/Hsp104 (Fig. 4D).

In order to gain mechanistic insights, we exploited TIRF microscopy and compared the disaggregation process between differentially fluorescence-labeled WT (Sc4) and S17R fibrils under the same microscopic field. DiscussionIn this study, we identified the binding sites of Ssa1, Sis1, and Hsp104 chaperones in Sup35NM. Since Ssa1/Sis1 first bind to Sup35NM amyloid and then Hsp104 is recruited the site where Ssa1/Sis1 have already bound (14), our results indicated that the Ssa1/Sis1-binding to the residues 143 to 164 can initiate the whole cascade of amyloid disaggregation. The deletion of the Ssa1/Sis1-binding region in Sup35 disrupted the propagation of [PSI+] yeast. Therefore, an efficient binding of Ssa1/Sis1 to Sup35 amyloids will be required for the following Hsp104-binding and amyloid fragmentation, which is critical to maintain [PSI+] propagation (Fig. 7). Furthermore, we found that the addition of an extra Ssa1-binding region to the C-terminus of Sup35NM improved the efficiency of disaggregation for the S17R mutant amyloid, although the S17R amyloid was resistant to disaggregation due to the buried Ssa1-binding region inside the PrD-C amyloid core (Fig. 7). Finally, we developed the engineered reconstitution system including the Ssa1-binding tag and the HAP/ClpP hybrid chaperones, which efficiently degraded both WT and the S17R mutant fibrils. Together, we presented the in vitro and in vivo data showing that the proper Ssa1-binding to the 143 to 164 AA region of Sup35 fibrils impacts amyloid disaggregation and maintenance of [PSI+] states. However, it should be noted that the previous studies suggest that the residues 1 to 113 or 1 to 137 of Sup35 are the minimum domain required for [PSI+] propagation (22, 24). The discrepancy might be caused by the different experimental systems between ours and the previous studies.Fig. 7.Efficiency of amyloid disaggregation and prion strain phenotypes depend on how Ssa1/Sis1-binding site is exposed in the fibrils. In Sup35NM WT Sc4 amyloid that has the core region of residues 2 to 42, the Ssa1/Sis1-binding site (residues 143 to 164) is exposed. Therefore, Ssa1/Sis1 can efficiently bind to the Sc4 amyloid and initiate the whole cascade of amyloid disaggregation. This situation results in efficient amyloid fragmentation and thereby a large number of fragmented fibers (seeds), leading to strong [PSI+] strain phenotypes. By contrast, the S17R amyloid harbors the amyloid core of residues 81 to 148, which partially masks the Ssa1/Sis1-binding site, preventing Ssa1/Sis1 from interacting with the S17R amyloid. This situation results in less efficient amyloid fragmentation and thereby a small number of fragmented fibers (seeds), leading to weak and sectoring [PSI+] strain phenotypes.Our findings provide insights into the perplexing [PSI+] curing mechanism by Hsp104 overexpression. Previous studies indicated that the 129 to 148 AA region is a binding site of “overexpressed” Hsp104 toward Sup35 (34). We suggest that the binding of overexpressed Hsp104 to the residues 129 to 148 partially mask the Ssa1/Sis1-binding region, which potentially prevents Ssa1/Sis1 from interacting with Sup35NM amyloid and impairs the following disaggregation process. Consistently, the size of in vivo prions is not decreased but increased by Hsp104 overexpression (40). Moreover, our results might explain why the overexpression of ΔNTD Hsp104 mutant cannot cure [PSI+] states (31), as ΔNTD Hsp104 mutant lost the regional specificity of Hsp104-binding to Sup35NM (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B) and thus the overexpression of ΔNTD Hsp104 mutant would not selectively mask the Ssa1/Sis1-binding site. These results revealed how concerted, dose-dependent interactions of the chaperones with Sup35NM impact yeast prion propagation.Furthermore, our findings help to explain enigmatic prion strain phenomena in vivo. Although the very weak [PSI+(S17R)] yeast strains that show sectoring, dark pink color phenotypes contained a larger size of prion particles than [PSI+(Sc4)] yeast (33), its underlying mechanism had remained unclear. We revealed that the S17R amyloid is resistant to chaperone-mediated disaggregation and that the Ssa1/Sis1-binding site (143 to 164 AA) was partly buried into the PrD-C amyloid core (81 to 148 AA). Therefore, the impaired disaggregation of S17R amyloid is likely to be caused by the weakened recognition by Ssa1/Sis1. Remarkably, the M1[S17R] amyloid containing an exposed Ssa1-binding tag (143 to 164 AA) showed an enhanced susceptibility to disaggregation (Fig. 4D). Together, the properly positioned, exposed Ssa1/Sis1-binding site in Sup35NM amyloid impacts amyloid disaggregation thereby yeast prion propagation, and strain phenotypes. Importantly, the previous studies suggest that a mutation or amino acid deletion in the M domain can affect strain phenotypes and mitotic stability of [PSI+] yeast albeit through unknown mechanisms (23, 41). Our result that the residues 143 to 164 in the M domain are recognized by Ssa1/Sis1 suggests that a mutation or deletion around this region influences disaggregation efficiency of Sup35 prions, resulting in alterations of not only [PSI+] strain phenotypes but also the rate at which Sup35 newly adopts the prion conformation, as the disaggregation (fragmentation) step is also involved in the early phase of Sup35 fibrillation (14). Our finding that Ssa1/Sis1-binding outside the amyloid core plays key roles in maintenance of [PSI+] states may also be extended to other yeast prion states such as [RNQ+] or [URE3], as the same chaperones are involved in their propagation (19, 42). This system allows to degrade even the fibrils intrinsically resistant to disaggregation, providing a strategy to promote not only disaggregation but also degradation for distinct strain conformations of amyloid fibrils. Notably, Hsp104-mediated fragmentation, but not monomerization, of amyloid fibrils may exert adverse effects on cells due to the potentially enhanced cell-to-cell propagation by an increased number of fragmented fibers (8, 44). In contrast to Hsp104, the HAP/ClpP system can continuously degrade fibrils without such confound effects caused by the seeding reaction between remaining seeds and monomers derived from disaggregation. Therefore, a future design of the engineered HAP/ClpP/chaperone-tag system and its further improvements by enhanced chaperone activity and substrate specificity will provide insights into the means to tackle neurodegenerative disorders associated with amyloid deposition.

In this study, we identified the binding sites of Ssa1, Sis1, and Hsp104 chaperones in Sup35NM. Since Ssa1/Sis1 first bind to Sup35NM amyloid and then Hsp104 is recruited the site where Ssa1/Sis1 have already bound (14), our results indicated that the Ssa1/Sis1-binding to the residues 143 to 164 can initiate the whole cascade of amyloid disaggregation. The deletion of the Ssa1/Sis1-binding region in Sup35 disrupted the propagation of [PSI+] yeast. Therefore, an efficient binding of Ssa1/Sis1 to Sup35 amyloids will be required for the following Hsp104-binding and amyloid fragmentation, which is critical to maintain [PSI+] propagation (Fig. 7). Furthermore, we found that the addition of an extra Ssa1-binding region to the C-terminus of Sup35NM improved the efficiency of disaggregation for the S17R mutant amyloid, although the S17R amyloid was resistant to disaggregation due to the buried Ssa1-binding region inside the PrD-C amyloid core (Fig. 7). Finally, we developed the engineered reconstitution system including the Ssa1-binding tag and the HAP/ClpP hybrid chaperones, which efficiently degraded both WT and the S17R mutant fibrils. Together, we presented the in vitro and in vivo data showing that the proper Ssa1-binding to the 143 to 164 AA region of Sup35 fibrils impacts amyloid disaggregation and maintenance of [PSI+] states. However, it should be noted that the previous studies suggest that the residues 1 to 113 or 1 to 137 of Sup35 are the minimum domain required for [PSI+] propagation (22, 24). The discrepancy might be caused by the different experimental systems between ours and the previous studies.

Efficiency of amyloid disaggregation and prion strain phenotypes depend on how Ssa1/Sis1-binding site is exposed in the fibrils. In Sup35NM WT Sc4 amyloid that has the core region of residues 2 to 42, the Ssa1/Sis1-binding site (residues 143 to 164) is exposed. Therefore, Ssa1/Sis1 can efficiently bind to the Sc4 amyloid and initiate the whole cascade of amyloid disaggregation. This situation results in efficient amyloid fragmentation and thereby a large number of fragmented fibers (seeds), leading to strong [PSI+] strain phenotypes. By contrast, the S17R amyloid harbors the amyloid core of residues 81 to 148, which partially masks the Ssa1/Sis1-binding site, preventing Ssa1/Sis1 from interacting with the S17R amyloid. This situation results in less efficient amyloid fragmentation and thereby a small number of fragmented fibers (seeds), leading to weak and sectoring [PSI+] strain phenotypes.

Our findings provide insights into the perplexing [PSI+] curing mechanism by Hsp104 overexpression. Previous studies indicated that the 129 to 148 AA region is a binding site of “overexpressed” Hsp104 toward Sup35 (34). We suggest that the binding of overexpressed Hsp104 to the residues 129 to 148 partially mask the Ssa1/Sis1-binding region, which potentially prevents Ssa1/Sis1 from interacting with Sup35NM amyloid and impairs the following disaggregation process. Consistently, the size of in vivo prions is not decreased but increased by Hsp104 overexpression (40). Moreover, our results might explain why the overexpression of ΔNTD Hsp104 mutant cannot cure [PSI+] states (31), as ΔNTD Hsp104 mutant lost the regional specificity of Hsp104-binding to Sup35NM (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B) and thus the overexpression of ΔNTD Hsp104 mutant would not selectively mask the Ssa1/Sis1-binding site. These results revealed how concerted, dose-dependent interactions of the chaperones with Sup35NM impact yeast prion propagation.

Furthermore, our findings help to explain enigmatic prion strain phenomena in vivo.
