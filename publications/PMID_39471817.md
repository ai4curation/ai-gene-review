Hepatic de novo lipogenesis (DNL) is a fundamental physiologic process that is 
often pathogenically elevated in metabolic disease. Treatment is limited by 
incomplete understanding of the metabolic pathways supplying cytosolic 
acetyl-CoA, the obligate precursor to DNL, including their interactions and 
proportional contributions. Here, we combined extensive 13C tracing with 
liver-specific knockout of key mitochondrial and cytosolic proteins mediating 
cytosolic acetyl-CoA production. We show that the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier 
(MPC) and ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY) gate the major hepatic lipogenic acetyl-CoA 
production pathway, operating in parallel with acetyl-CoA synthetase 2 (ACSS2). 
Given persistent DNL after mitochondrial citrate carrier (CiC) and ACSS2 double 
knockout, we tested the contribution of exogenous and leucine-derived 
acetoacetate to acetoacetyl-CoA synthetase (AACS)-dependent DNL. CiC knockout 
increased acetoacetate-supplied hepatic acetyl-CoA production and DNL, 
indicating that ketones function as mitochondrial-citrate reciprocal DNL 
precursors. By delineating a mitochondrial-cytosolic DNL substrate supply 
network, these findings may inform strategies to therapeutically modulate DNL.

INTRODUCTION

De novo lipogenesis (DNL) is an essential mammalian physiologic process that produces lipids required for membrane structures, signaling, and fuel storage. While most whole-body DNL occurs in adipose tissue and the liver, the liver has less capacity to store fat healthily. Elevated hepatic DNL and excess hepatic lipid accumulation are independent risk factors for insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)1–4. Consequently, substantial effort has been applied to develop small molecules that decrease hepatic DNL by inhibiting acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and fatty acid synthase (FAS), obligate enzymes in the central DNL pathway5–8. However, because some DNL is necessary for normal liver function, near-complete inhibition of ACC and FAS has caused therapeutically unacceptable side effects6,9,10.

Cytosolic acetyl-CoA is the common precursor feeding the central DNL pathway, beginning with carboxylation to malonyl-CoA by ACC. Malonyl-CoA is then utilized by the FAS complex to build fatty acids with successive, two-carbon additions. Multiple mitochondrial and cytosolic metabolic pathways are known or hypothesized to converge at cytosolic acetyl-CoA. Therefore, by more precisely disrupting pathological but not physiologically essential metabolism, targeting discrete mitochondrial or cytosolic pathways supplying cytosolic acetyl-CoA may be a therapeutically viable alternative to inhibiting DNL at ACC or FAS. However, how the enzymes and substrates of these pathways metabolically interact to control cytosolic acetyl-CoA availability and contribute to hepatic DNL remains unclear.

Although dietary carbohydrate provides the major carbon source and hormonal signals for DNL11–13, circulating lactate/pyruvate (pyruvate) and acetate are considered to be the primary substrates supplying cytosolic acetyl-CoA for hepatic DNL14,15. Because pyruvate is converted to acetyl-CoA by pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) in mitochondria, the presumed pathway for pyruvate to supply DNL is through a mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC)-mitochondrial citrate carrier (CiC) axis. By this model, pyruvate is imported into mitochondria through the MPC, converted into citrate, and exported out of mitochondria through the CiC. Cytosolic citrate is cleaved to acetyl-CoA by ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY) for shunting into DNL through ACC. Importantly, acetyl-CoA produced in mitochondria cannot be transported across the mitochondrial inner membrane, which necessitates the transport of citrate as an acetyl-CoA equivalent for pyruvate fed DNL.

Acetate fed DNL is thought to be mediated by the cytosolic enzyme acetyl-CoA synthetase 2 (ACSS2). Significant sources of acetate include hepatic metabolism of ingested ethanol and gut microbial fermentation of dietary carbohydrate16,17. Compared to pyruvate fed DNL, acetate fed DNL is a more direct pathway by carbon routing, bypassing mitochondria transport and metabolism steps through cytosolic ligation of acetate to CoA by ACSS2. Nonetheless, mitochondrial metabolism is necessary for normal hepatic substrate and redox metabolism and may therefore influence DNL regardless of source18. Thus, the organization of the DNL substrate supply network is complex, and a basic a priori challenge for selective therapeutic modulation is experimentally delineating its nutritional-genetic structure.

Here, we present a systematic investigation of the hepatic DNL substrate supply network. We began by measuring hepatic DNL in CiC liver-specific knockout (LivKO) mice. After observing surprisingly persistent DNL in CiC LivKO mice, we used a series of 13C stable isotope tracing experiments to disentangle pathways of DNL. To achieve metabolic uniformity across experiments, we employed a parallel 13C tracing approach, co-administering labeled and unlabeled substrates with label swapping in paired experiments. We traced DNL in CiC, MPC, ACSS2, and ACLY single and selective double LivKO mice. Our results led us to consider ketone fed DNL as a mitochondria-originating, reciprocal pathway to CiC-mediated DNL. We demonstrate that hepatic ketone-fed DNL depends upon acetoacetyl-CoA synthetase (AACS). We discovered that CiC disruption leads to strikingly elevated hepatic DNL from acetoacetate supplied both exogenously and from ketogenic leucine metabolism. Overall, our results show that the MPC and ACLY gate the major lipogenic acetyl-CoA production pathway. They provide a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic and substrate interactions comprising the hepatic DNL network and may inform strategies for selective therapeutic modulation.

RESULTSThe mitochondrial citrate carrier (CiC) controls hepatic de novo lipogenesis (DNL).Mitochondrial citrate export has long been considered a key contributor to hepatic DNL (Figure 1A), but this has not been tested genetically in vivo. To address the role of mitochondrial citrate transport in hepatic DNL, we generated liver-specific CiC knockout (CiC LivKO) mice (Figure S1A). Western blots of liver tissue confirmed CiC knockout (Figure 1B). Respirometry experiments functionally corroborated this result by demonstrating isolated CiC LivKO mitochondria have markedly decreased citrate but not pyruvate oxidation, consistent with impaired citrate transport rather than a global respiratory defect (Figure 1C, 1D).We aimed to measure hepatic DNL in both a baseline and an upregulated state modeling metabolic disease. Given the potentially confounding effects of obesity on tests of metabolism, we also aimed to increase hepatic DNL without changing body weight. We selected short-term high sucrose diet (HSD) feeding, which is well-demonstrated to increase DNL without altering body weight16,19. We divided WT and CiC LivKO mice into normal chow diet (NCD) and HSD feeding groups for 9 days. Throughout the dietary intervention, no significant body weight differences were observed between groups, suggesting the absence of body weight effects on DNL outcome differences (Figure 1E).To measure the contribution of DNL to hepatic lipids, NCD- and HSD-fed WT and CiC LivKO mice were injected with deuterated water (2H2O) followed by overnight administration in drinking water to reach uniform deuterium enrichment across all groups of ~3% (Figures S1B, S1C)20. Deuterium was traced into hepatic lipids using 1H and 2H NMR spectroscopy to determine the content of new lipid20. In NCD-fed mice, CiC LivKO did not change the amount of lipid derived from DNL (Figure 1F). Conversely, CiC LivKO prevented the stimulatory effect of HSD on hepatic DNL (Figure 1F). These results demonstrate that CiC-independent DNL pathways completely and partially compensate for CiC disruption under NCD- and HSD-fed conditions, respectively. They also show that the elevated hepatic DNL induced by HSD feeding requires the CiC.CiC LivKO decreases DNL from lactate/pyruvate but not acetate.The partial persistence of DNL after CiC LivKO evoked questions about what pathways sustain it independently of CiC function. Tracing deuterium from 2H2O measures DNL from all sources and thus would not detect off-setting changes in substrate utilization. Therefore, we used 13C-labeling to specifically test the use of pyruvate and acetate as substrates for DNL (Figure 2A). Because pyruvate and acetate have different metabolic fates and regulatory roles, we devised substrate competition tests with 13C-label swapping, administered by intraperitoneal bolus injection [U13C-lactate/-pyruvate (10:1 to approximate endogenous redox state) + unlabeled acetate] versus [unlabeled lactate/pyruvate (10:1) + U13C-acetate]. This strategy provides episodic substrate delivery like feeding and controls for substrate competition by changing only the location of 13C and not the mixture of substrate. Furthermore, because the unlabeled substrate competes with the labeled substrate, we scheduled two injections, spaced 30 minutes apart, to provide additional time for 13C incorporation into newly synthesized hepatic lipid. Substrate dosages per injection were based on those we and others have previously used to trace hepatic pyruvate and acetate metabolism in vivo (2.2 g/kg lactate/pyruvate, 0.5 g/kg acetate)21–23.Given that HSD feeding increased CiC-dependent DNL from the endogenous substrate milieu (Figure 1F), mice were again fed a short-term HSD to induce conditions where CiC function controls total DNL. Importantly, no differences in body weight were observed between genotypes before or after HSD (Figure 2B). Mice were 4h food restricted to normalize metabolism, injected with tracers with 13C-label swapping, as described above, and livers were harvested 75 minutes after the first injection under freeze-clamp conditions. Polar and non-polar metabolites were extracted and analyzed for 13C enrichments by targeted-high resolution liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)24 (Figure 2C).Because of the expected role of the CiC in mediating mitochondrial routed DNL, we first examined TCA cycle pool sizes. Since total substrate administration was the same, we combined metabolite abundances from each of the paired 13C-labeling schemes. We observed an increased citrate concentration in CiC LivKO livers, suggesting mitochondrial entrapment (Figure 2D, Table S1 containing all abundances). Conversely, downstream intermediates decreased, consistent with decreased production or increased cytosolic consumption as compensation for disrupted mitochondrial citrate export. Although hepatic 13C-pyruvate enrichment was lower than 13C-acetate enrichment, TCA cycle enrichments from injections containing 13C-lactate/-pyruvate were higher than from injections containing 13C-acetate (Figure 2E, Table S2 containing mass isotopologue distribution (MID) analysis of all non-fatty acid metabolites discussed). This observation is consistent with pyruvate carbon entering the TCA cycle by high rates of anaplerosis through pyruvate carboxylase in addition to citrate synthase, while acetate entry is restricted to citrate synthase25. Nevertheless, CiC disruption had very small, though statistically significant, effects on the 13C enrichment of TCA cycle intermediates from either 13C-lactate/-pyruvate or 13C-acetate (Figure 2E). Acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA levels did not significantly change (Figure 2F). Conversely, 13C-enrichment from lactate/pyruvate but not acetate into acetyl-CoA was mildly decreased, consistent with its decreased flux into cytosolic acetyl-CoA, increased influx of substrate other than pyruvate or acetate, or both (Figure 2G, Table S2). Free fatty acid levels were unchanged, which was expected due to their large pool size relative to their production rate (Table S1).To investigate the impact of CiC disruption on hepatic pyruvate and acetate fed DNL, we measured 13C enrichments into the fatty acids laurate (12:0), myristate (14:0), and palmitate (16:0). Because fatty chains are synthesized by successive two-carbon additions from precursor acetyl-CoA (Figure 2A), we expected to see mass (M) + 2 incrementing. Consistent with this pattern, the major mass isotopologues in order of abundance were M+2, M+4, and M+6 (Table S3 containing all fatty acid MID analysis). When lactate/pyruvate was labeled with 13C, CiC LivKO caused a substantial decrease in these isotopologues (Figure 2H). As a measure of total substrate-specific DNL, we summed the M+2, M+4, and M+6 isotopologues. This analysis indicates that CiC LivKO decreased DNL from pyruvate (Figure 2I). Surprisingly, increased, compensatory DNL from acetate was not observed.Nonetheless, we noticed that CiC LivKO led to different proportional distributions between lighter (M+2) and heavier (M+4, M+6) fatty acid isotopologues, from both labeled lactate/pyruvate and acetate. This is notable because proportional fatty acid isotopologue distributions signify the fractional contribution of labeled acetyl-CoA to DNL26. To quantify these proportional distribution differences, we normalized the summed M+4 and M+6 isotopologues to the M+2 isotopologue (Table S3). Fold M+2-normalized fatty acid enrichments from lactate/pyruvate and acetate were increased and decreased, respectively, by CiC LivKO (Figure 2J). Thus, compared to direct measurements of total acetyl-CoA enrichments, M+2-normalized fatty acid enrichments reflect the lipogenically active acetyl-CoA pool. It indicates that CiC LivKO led to pyruvate contributing less and acetate contributing more to the acetyl-CoA pool functioning as DNL precursor.Finally, we conducted control experiments to evaluate the generalizability of the short-term HSD feeding, dual substrate, and 13C-label swapping approach. NCD-fed mice received a single injection of labeled lactate/pyruvate (2.2 g/kg) or acetate (0.75 g/kg) without co-administration of the other substrate, which produced similar results (Figure S2A-R, Tables S1, S2, S3). Overall, these data show that CiC LivKO decreases DNL from pyruvate but not acetate and changes their proportional contributions to the DNL acetyl-CoA precursor pool.Acetyl-CoA Synthetase 2 (ACSS2) LivKO decreases DNL from acetate but not lactate/pyruvate.Although acetate and pyruvate are broadly functionally separate substrate pools, in some systems pyruvate may be directly converted to acetate in the cytosol at biologically meaningful levels27. Thus, because DNL from pyruvate was partially maintained in CiC LivKO mice, we performed experiments to evaluate whether pyruvate to acetate conversions contribute to CiC-independent, ACSS2-dependent hepatic DNL. We generated liver-specific ACSS2 knockout mice (ACSS2 LivKO, Figures 3A, S3A). Mice were fed a short-term HSD, and no body weight differences were observed between genotypes before or after the HSD (Figure 3B). As described above (Figure 2C), parallel 13C-labelling substrate competition experiments were performed to test the role of ACSS2 in pyruvate and acetate utilization for DNL. In contrast to CiC LivKO, ACSS2 LivKO minimally altered TCA cycle intermediate abundances and enrichments and did not change acetyl- or malonyl-CoA levels (Figures 3C-E). However, consistent with the role of ACSS2 in producing acetyl-CoA directly from acetate, 13C-acetate enrichment into acetyl-CoA was nearly halved (Figure 3F). Similarly, total and M+2-normalized fatty acid 13C enrichments from acetate were markedly decreased (Figure 3G, 3H, Tables S1 and S2). Conversely, ACSS2 LivKO did not change 13C-lactate/-pyruvate enrichment into acetyl-CoA, malonyl-CoA, or fatty acids, but led to near statistically significant increases in M+2-normalized fatty acid enrichments (Figures 3F-H, Table S3). As with CiC LivKO mice, control experiments with NCD-fed WT and ACSS2 LivKO mice administered single injections of either labeled acetate or labeled lactate/pyruvate produced concordant results (Figure S3B-G, Tables S1, S2, S3). Overall, these data show that ACSS2 LivKO decreases DNL from acetate but not pyruvate and decreases the proportional contribution of acetate to the DNL acetyl-CoA precursor pool.Because we observed persistent pyruvate fed DNL in both CiC and ACSS2 single LivKO mice, with evidence of mild adaptive substrate utilization in response to both interventions, we generated CiC + ACSS2 double liver-specific knockout mice (CiC + ACSS2 DLivKO). We expected this DLivKO model would more convincingly test for a compensatory pyruvate-acetate shunt during CiC disruption. No body weight effects of genotype after short-term HSD feeding were observed (Figure 3I). As with CiC LivKO mice, CiC + ACSS2 DLivKO mice displayed increased citrate abundance, again consistent with mitochondrial citrate entrapment, with slightly decreased 13C enrichments for some TCA cycle intermediates (Figure 3J, 3K, Tables S1, S2). Total acetyl-CoA was increased, and total malonyl-CoA was decreased (Figures 3L). Interestingly, 13C enrichments into acetyl-CoA from both lactate/pyruvate and acetate were decreased, with markedly decreased 13C enrichment from acetate into malonyl-CoA (Figure 3M). The latter was not seen in either CiC or ACSS2 LivKO mice. It is consistent with a genetic-metabolic interaction, where loss of CiC function decreases cytosolic citrate, that normally activates ACC, to generate malonyl-CoA from the limited acetate-derived acetyl-CoA produced after ACSS2 function is lost. Consistent with results from both CiC and ACSS2 single LivKO mice, DLivKO mice showed decreased total and M+2-normalized fatty acid 13C enrichments from both lactate/pyruvate and acetate (Figures 3N, 3O, Tables S3). Together, these results show that ACSS2-mediated DNL does not compensate for CiC LivKO.ATP Citrate Lyase (ACLY) LivKO decreases DNL from lactate/pyruvate.After ruling out an ACSS2-mediated acetate shunt as a bypass sustaining pyruvate fed DNL after CiC disruption, we considered CiC-independent sources of cytosolic citrate. ACLY converts mitochondria-exported citrate to acetyl-CoA for DNL but can also use other sources of citrate (Figure 4A). We fed WT and ACLY LivKO mice a short-term HSD and performed parallel 13C-labelling experiments as described above (Figure 2C). No effects of genotype or HSD feeding were observed on body weight (Figure 4B). Compared to CiC LivKO, which increased citrate abundance with small decreases in TCA cycle enrichments (Figures 2D, 2E), ACLY LivKO increased α-ketoglutarate (αKG) abundance and 13C-enrichment from lactate/pyruvate (Figures 4C, 4D, Tables S1, S2). ACLY LivKO, versus CiC LivKO, also had opposite effects on acetyl-CoA abundance, which decreased by about half (Figure 4E). Acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA enrichments from 13C-lactate/-pyruvate were unchanged, but acetyl-CoA enrichment from 13C-acetate was increased (Figure 4F). As expected, based on the prior literature, ACLY LivKO led to decreased pyruvate fed DNL (Figure 4G, Table S3). Moreover, M+2-normalized fatty acid enrichments indicated that pyruvate contributed less to the DNL precursor acetyl-CoA pool (Figure 4H). Although the loss of ACLY did not stimulate DNL from acetate (Figure 4G), maintenance of the acetyl-CoA DNL precursor pool was proportionally more dependent on acetate (Figure 4H). Notably, for both total and M+2-normalized 13C fatty acid enrichments, the deficit vs WT appeared mildly greater than that observed with CiC LivKO. This is consistent with the CiC being the major but not sole source of cytosolic citrate. In control experiments, comparable results were obtained with NCD-fed WT and ACLY LivKO mice administered single injections of labeled acetate or labeled lactate/pyruvate (Figure S3H-M, Tables S1, S2, S3).Because of the clearly distinctive metabolic effects of CiC vs ACLY LivKO, and the possibility of a CiC-independent pathway to generate cytosolic citrate from pyruvate, we considered whether combined knockout would additively decrease DNL. To test this possibility, we generated CiC + ACLY DLivKO mice and performed parallel 13C-label swapping experiments after short-term HSD feeding. No effects of genotype or HSD feeding were observed on body weight (Figure 4I). TCA cycle intermediate abundances, including citrate and αKG, were increased, but 13C enrichments were unchanged except for mildly increased αKG enrichment when 13C-lactate/-pyruvate was provided (Figure 4J, 4K, Table S1, S2). Thus, the combined effect of CiC and ACLY DLivKO recapitulated the respective increased citrate and αKG levels seen with single CiC and ACLY LivKO. Acetyl- and malonyl-CoA abundances and 13C enrichments closely phenocopied those observed with ACLY LivKO (Figures 4L, 4M). In accord, overall patterns of decreased total and M+2-normalized fatty acid 13C enrichments from lactate/pyruvate in CiC + ACLY DLivKO were similar to those observed with ACLY LivKO (Figure 4N, 4O, Table S3). Together, these results show that the CiC and ACLY play distinct metabolic roles, that ACLY is metabolically epistatic to the CiC for control of total cellular acetyl-CoA, and that the CiC and ACLY function in tandem to control DNL from pyruvate.Mitochondrial Pyruvate Carrier (MPC) LivKO decreases DNL from both lactate/pyruvate- and acetate.Given the similar effects of ACLY LivKO and CiC LivKO to impair pyruvate fed DNL, we next considered the role of mitochondrial pyruvate import through the MPC as upstream of both (Figure 5A)28–30. Neither MPC LivKO nor short-term HSD feeding led to body weight differences (Figure 5B). As expected with MPC disruption, lactate and pyruvate abundances were increased and TCA cycle intermediates except αKG were decreased (Figure 5C, Table S1). TCA cycle enrichments from 13C-lactate/-pyruvate were also decreased, with minimal to no effect on enrichments from 13C-acetate, consistent with a specific defect in pyruvate transport (Figure 5D, Table S2). Similar to CiC LivKO, MPC LivKO increased acetyl-CoA and decreased malonyl-CoA abundances, which was accompanied by decreased labeling of acetyl-CoA from lactate/pyruvate (Figures 5E, 5F). MPC LivKO also decreased total 13C fatty acid enrichments from labeled lactate/pyruvate, with an apparently greater effect than loss of CiC or ACLY (Figure 5G, Table S3). This observation was matched by lower M+2-normalized fatty acid enrichments from labeled lactate/pyruvate (Figure 5H). Together these results indicate that loss of the MPC markedly decreased pyruvate flux through the CiC and ACLY into DNL and the acetyl-CoA DNL precursor pool.In contrast to CiC and ACLY LivKO, MPC LivKO also strongly decreased DNL from acetate (Figure 5G). However, M+2-normalized fatty acid 13C-enrichments from acetate were minimally changed (Figure 5H). These results indicate that although total acetate from DNL was decreased, the proportion of the acetyl-CoA DNL precursor pool supplied by acetate was unchanged, likely because the loss of acetate fed DNL was small compared to the loss of pyruvate fed DNL. These data are consistent with MPC disruption directly constraining generation of DNL precursor acetyl-CoA from lactate/pyruvate but not acetate (Figure 5H). Corroborating results were obtained in NCD-fed WT and MPC LivKO mice administered single injections of 13C-lactate/-pyruvate sampled 30 minutes later (Figure S4A-C, Tables S1, S2, S3). In contrast, fatty acid enrichments were not different between NCD-fed WT and MPC LivKO mice administered a single injection of 13C-acetate without co-administration of lactate/pyruvate, consistent with anMPC-dependent interaction between exogenously administered lactate/pyruvate and acetate fed DNL (Figure S4D-F).Because the MPC and CiC are presumed to regulate sequential steps in the pyruvate fed DNL pathway, we generated CiC + MPC DLivKO mice to test for unanticipated interactions. The same battery of tests and analyses led to results broadly phenocopying results from MPC LivKO mice, consistent with CiC-mediated DNL being downstream of the MPC (Figures S4G-M, Tables S1, S2, S3). Because MPC LivKO impaired DNL more than either CiC LivKO or CiC + ACLY DLivKO, we measured protein content of DNL enzymes by Western blot. This analysis showed a >15% decrease in ACLY, ACC, and FASN levels in MPC LivKO mice (Figure 5I). In contrast, they were upregulated >15% in CiC LivKO mice (Figure 5J). Given these opposite changes in protein expression, we considered the role of master transcription factors for DNL, including carbohydrate response element binding protein (CHREBP) and the sterol response element binding proteins 1 (SREBP1) and 2 (SREBP2). We employedshort-term feeding-entrainment to better match the circadian regulation of the DNL program31–33. CiC and MPC LivKO mice were food-restricted during the light cycle (ZT0 through ZT12) for three consecutive days, which did not affect body weights (Figure 5K). On the fourth day, mice were food-restricted three hours before the beginning of the light cycle (ZT21). CiC LivKO increases utilization of acetoacetate and leucine for DNL.Given the redundancy in DNL substrate supply observed throughout this investigation that was not explained by pyruvate or acetate metabolism, a lingering question was what other pathways could supply hepatic acetyl-CoA for DNL? The literature from several decades prior demonstrates that ketones may serve as DNL substrate in vivo and in ex vivo perfused tissue (Figure 6A)34–36. Based on early biochemical studies, the putative pathway for ketone fed cytosolic acetyl-CoA production is cytosolic thioesterification of acetoacetate to acetoacetyl-CoA by the enzyme acetoacetyl-CoA synthetase (AACS), followed by cleavage and generation of two acetyl-CoA molecules by the enzyme acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase 2 (ACAT2). Interestingly, fasted CiC LivKO livers showed increased transcript levels for Aacs but not Acat2 (Figure 6B), consistent with acetoacetate-sourced cytosolic acetyl-CoA production being upregulated in the absence of the CiC.To test the role of AACS and ACAT2 in DNL, we utilized AAV8 delivery of Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) Cas9 driven by the hepatocyte-specific hybrid liver promoter and empty vector or targeting guide RNAs driven by the U6 promoter37,38. Three guide RNAs for each gene were screened in vivo and the most potent for each was selected for DNL experiments (Figures 6C, S5A, S5B). To test the hypothesis that AACS and ACAT2 mediate acetoacetate fed hepatic DNL in vivo, we traced [U13C]-acetoacetate (1.0 g/kg) in NCD-fed AACS and ACAT2 LivKO mice. Disruption of neither gene affected mouse body weights (Figure 6D). Because this was an initial test of principle, to elicit a physiologic insulin response to stimulate DNL, we co-administered glucose (1.75 g/kg). AACS LivKO substantially decreased 13C-enrichment into acetyl-CoA and fatty acids (Figures 6E, 6F, S5C, Tables S1, S2, S3). Conversely, the effect of ACAT2 LivKO was minimal, suggesting a yet to be identified mechanism for converting cytosolic acetoacetyl-CoA into acetyl-CoA, likely through the activity of other thiolase enzymes. Importantly, as a control, AACS LivKO mice administered a single injection of 13C-lactate/-pyruvate showed no significant decrement in 13C enrichment into fatty acids, validating the specific effect of AACS LivKO to decrease acetoacetate fed DNL (Figure S5D).Because high levels of hepatic ketogenesis versus net citrate synthesis occur under polar physiologic states, we hypothesized that ketone fed DNL could function as mitochondria-originating, CiC-independent DNL pathway. CiC LivKO mice were fed a short-term HSD, and once again neither genotype nor diet affected mouse body weights (Figure 6G). Similar to experiments with 13C-acetate tracing, we co-injected 13C-acetoacetate (1.0 g/kg) with lactate/pyruvate (2.2 g/kg). 13C-acetoacetate generated and equilibrated with 13C-β-hydroxybutyrate, confirming penetrance into endogenous ketogenic metabolism. CiC LivKO led to increased citrate abundance and slightly decreased citrate enrichment from 13C-acetoacetate (Figures 6H, 6I, Tables S1, S2). Acetyl-CoA but not malonyl-CoA abundance was increased (Figure 6J). In striking contrast to results from tracing either 13C-lactate/pyruvate or 13C-acetate, 13C-enrichment from acetoacetate into both acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA was increased nearly two-fold by CiC LivKO (Figure 6K). Increased 13C-enrichments continued into DNL, with a ~3-fold increase in M+2-normalized fatty acid 13C enrichments (Figures 6L, 6M, Table S3). Thus, CiC LivKO leads to marked upregulation of acetoacetate flux into DNL and precursor acetyl-CoA.We then considered the potential for ketogenic DNL fed by the branched chain amino acid leucine. Leucine is abundant in dietary protein and plasma, with circulating concentrations ranging from 50–200 μM39. Leucine is metabolized to the branched chain keto acid α-ketoisocaproate, in both the periphery and liver. In the liver, α-ketoisocaproate is further metabolized in mitochondria to acetoacetate and acetyl-CoA, with the latter available for flux into further ketogenesis or into citrate when sufficient anaplerotic substrate is available. Importantly, because leucine cannot generate mitochondrial oxaloacetate in net, it is not an anaplerotic substrate, and, therefore, cannot supply net citrate synthesis. Thus, leucine is a consistently available, physiologic source of acetoacetate and mitochondrial acetyl-CoA that could supply DNL in net through AACS pathway, yet label but not make a net contribution to fatty acids produced from mitochondrially exported citrate (Figure 6N). To test the role of leucine as a precursor for ketone fed DNL, we generated AACS LivKO mice using AAV8 delivery of S. aureus Cas9 and the most efficient of the guide RNAs targeting Aacs (Figure 6C, S5B). In a proof-of-concept experiment, we first traced [U13C]-leucine (0.5 g/kg) + lactate/pyruvate (2.2 g/kg) in NCD-fed AACS LivKO mice. Leucine was readily used as a DNL substrate, and AACS LivKO markedly decreased 13C-leucine enrichment into acetyl-CoA and fatty acids (Figures 6O, 6P, Tables S1, S2, S3).After finding that leucine supplies hepatic DNL in an AACS-dependent manner, we tested how CiC LivKO impacts hepatic DNL from leucine in short-term HSD fed mice. Once again, neither CiC LivKO nor HSD feeding affected mouse body weights (Figure 6Q). As in the pilot experiment above, 13C-leucine was co-injected with lactate/pyruvate and traced into downstream metabolites. 13C enrichments largely phenocopied those obtained when tracing acetoacetate. Citrate was increased in CiC LivKO mice, and α-ketoglutarate was decreased (Figure 6R, Table S1). Importantly, 13C-leucine and 13C-α-ketoisocaproate equilibrated and propagated to > 60% 13C-enrichment into β-hydroxybutyrate, demonstrating that leucine deamidation to α-ketoisocaproate was not limiting in mediating robust leucine-driven ketogenesis (Figure 6S, Table S2). β-hydroxybutyrate 13C enrichment and abundance were decreased by CiC LivKO, possibly because of adaptively increased mitochondrial acetoacetate export (Figure 6R, 6S).Because [U13C]-leucine metabolism into mitochondrial acetoacetate, acetyl-CoA, and citrate produces both M+1 and M+2 cytosolic acetyl-CoA (Figure 6N), channeling of [U13C]-leucine into fatty acid synthesis generates even and odd 13C-enriched species (Figure 6V). CiC LivKO increased 13C-leucine enrichment into combined M+1 and M+2 acetyl-CoA isotopologues, alongside a near significant increase in acetyl-CoA abundance (Figure 6T, 6U). Remarkably, CiC LivKO mice showed a ~2-fold increase in total fatty acid 13C enrichments (Figure 6W, Table S3). As a control experiment, we also administered 13C-leucine as a single substrate to WT and CiC LivKO mice. Results largely matched those obtained when 13C-Leucine and lactate/pyruvate were co-administered (Figure S5E-S5J, Tables S1, S2, S3). Finally, given the apparent reciprocity between CiC- and AACS-routed DNL, and to rule out involvement of a leucine-to-acetate, ACSS2-dependent mechanism, we traced 13C-leucine with unlabeled lactate/pyruvate in WT and CiC + ACSS2 DLivKO mice. These data phenocopied the increased DNL and other 13C-enrichment patterns we observed when tracing both 13C-acetoacetate and 13C-leucine in CiC LivKO mice (Figure S5K-O, Tables S1, S2, S3). Thus, ACSS2 is dispensable for the upregulated ketone-fed DNL induced by CiC LivKO (Figure 6X). Overall, these results show that exogenously supplied and leucine-generated ketones feed hepatic DNL in an AACS-dependent manner and that ketone fed DNL is upregulated by disrupting mitochondrial citrate transport.

Interestingly, fasted CiC LivKO livers showed increased transcript levels for Aacs but not Acat2 (Figure 6B), consistent with acetoacetate-sourced cytosolic acetyl-CoA production being upregulated in the absence of the CiC.

To test the role of AACS and ACAT2 in DNL, we utilized AAV8 delivery of Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) Cas9 driven by the hepatocyte-specific hybrid liver promoter and empty vector or targeting guide RNAs driven by the U6 promoter37,38. Three guide RNAs for each gene were screened in vivo and the most potent for each was selected for DNL experiments (Figures 6C, S5A, S5B). To test the hypothesis that AACS and ACAT2 mediate acetoacetate fed hepatic DNL in vivo, we traced [U13C]-acetoacetate (1.0 g/kg) in NCD-fed AACS and ACAT2 LivKO mice. Disruption of neither gene affected mouse body weights (Figure 6D). Because this was an initial test of principle, to elicit a physiologic insulin response to stimulate DNL, we co-administered glucose (1.75 g/kg). AACS LivKO substantially decreased 13C-enrichment into acetyl-CoA and fatty acids (Figures 6E, 6F, S5C, Tables S1, S2, S3). Conversely, the effect of ACAT2 LivKO was minimal, suggesting a yet to be identified mechanism for converting cytosolic acetoacetyl-CoA into acetyl-CoA, likely through the activity of other thiolase enzymes. Importantly, as a control, AACS LivKO mice administered a single injection of 13C-lactate/-pyruvate showed no significant decrement in 13C enrichment into fatty acids, validating the specific effect of AACS LivKO to decrease acetoacetate fed DNL (Figure S5D).

Because high levels of hepatic ketogenesis versus net citrate synthesis occur under polar physiologic states, we hypothesized that ketone fed DNL could function as mitochondria-originating, CiC-independent DNL pathway. CiC LivKO mice were fed a short-term HSD, and once again neither genotype nor diet affected mouse body weights (Figure 6G). Similar to experiments with 13C-acetate tracing, we co-injected 13C-acetoacetate (1.0 g/kg) with lactate/pyruvate (2.2 g/kg). 13C-acetoacetate generated and equilibrated with 13C-β-hydroxybutyrate, confirming penetrance into endogenous ketogenic metabolism. CiC LivKO led to increased citrate abundance and slightly decreased citrate enrichment from 13C-acetoacetate (Figures 6H, 6I, Tables S1, S2). Acetyl-CoA but not malonyl-CoA abundance was increased (Figure 6J). In striking contrast to results from tracing either 13C-lactate/pyruvate or 13C-acetate, 13C-enrichment from acetoacetate into both acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA was increased nearly two-fold by CiC LivKO (Figure 6K). Increased 13C-enrichments continued into DNL, with a ~3-fold increase in M+2-normalized fatty acid 13C enrichments (Figures 6L, 6M, Table S3). Thus, CiC LivKO leads to marked upregulation of acetoacetate flux into DNL and precursor acetyl-CoA.

We then considered the potential for ketogenic DNL fed by the branched chain amino acid leucine. Leucine is abundant in dietary protein and plasma, with circulating concentrations ranging from 50–200 μM39. Leucine is metabolized to the branched chain keto acid α-ketoisocaproate, in both the periphery and liver. In the liver, α-ketoisocaproate is further metabolized in mitochondria to acetoacetate and acetyl-CoA, with the latter available for flux into further ketogenesis or into citrate when sufficient anaplerotic substrate is available. Importantly, because leucine cannot generate mitochondrial oxaloacetate in net, it is not an anaplerotic substrate, and, therefore, cannot supply net citrate synthesis. Thus, leucine is a consistently available, physiologic source of acetoacetate and mitochondrial acetyl-CoA that could supply DNL in net through AACS pathway, yet label but not make a net contribution to fatty acids produced from mitochondrially exported citrate (Figure 6N). To test the role of leucine as a precursor for ketone fed DNL, we generated AACS LivKO mice using AAV8 delivery of S. aureus Cas9 and the most efficient of the guide RNAs targeting Aacs (Figure 6C, S5B). In a proof-of-concept experiment, we first traced [U13C]-leucine (0.5 g/kg) + lactate/pyruvate (2.2 g/kg) in NCD-fed AACS LivKO mice. Leucine was readily used as a DNL substrate, and AACS LivKO markedly decreased 13C-leucine enrichment into acetyl-CoA and fatty acids (Figures 6O, 6P, Tables S1, S2, S3).

After finding that leucine supplies hepatic DNL in an AACS-dependent manner, we tested how CiC LivKO impacts hepatic DNL from leucine in short-term HSD fed mice. Once again, neither CiC LivKO nor HSD feeding affected mouse body weights (Figure 6Q). As in the pilot experiment above, 13C-leucine was co-injected with lactate/pyruvate and traced into downstream metabolites. 13C enrichments largely phenocopied those obtained when tracing acetoacetate. Citrate was increased in CiC LivKO mice, and α-ketoglutarate was decreased (Figure 6R, Table S1). Importantly, 13C-leucine and 13C-α-ketoisocaproate equilibrated and propagated to > 60% 13C-enrichment into β-hydroxybutyrate, demonstrating that leucine deamidation to α-ketoisocaproate was not limiting in mediating robust leucine-driven ketogenesis (Figure 6S, Table S2). β-hydroxybutyrate 13C enrichment and abundance were decreased by CiC LivKO, possibly because of adaptively increased mitochondrial acetoacetate export (Figure 6R, 6S).

Because [U13C]-leucine metabolism into mitochondrial acetoacetate, acetyl-CoA, and citrate produces both M+1 and M+2 cytosolic acetyl-CoA (Figure 6N), channeling of [U13C]-leucine into fatty acid synthesis generates even and odd 13C-enriched species (Figure 6V). CiC LivKO increased 13C-leucine enrichment into combined M+1 and M+2 acetyl-CoA isotopologues, alongside a near significant increase in acetyl-CoA abundance (Figure 6T, 6U). Remarkably, CiC LivKO mice showed a ~2-fold increase in total fatty acid 13C enrichments (Figure 6W, Table S3). As a control experiment, we also administered 13C-leucine as a single substrate to WT and CiC LivKO mice. Results largely matched those obtained when 13C-Leucine and lactate/pyruvate were co-administered (Figure S5E-S5J, Tables S1, S2, S3). Finally, given the apparent reciprocity between CiC- and AACS-routed DNL, and to rule out involvement of a leucine-to-acetate, ACSS2-dependent mechanism, we traced 13C-leucine with unlabeled lactate/pyruvate in WT and CiC + ACSS2 DLivKO mice. These data phenocopied the increased DNL and other 13C-enrichment patterns we observed when tracing both 13C-acetoacetate and 13C-leucine in CiC LivKO mice (Figure S5K-O, Tables S1, S2, S3). Thus, ACSS2 is dispensable for the upregulated ketone-fed DNL induced by CiC LivKO (Figure 6X). Overall, these results show that exogenously supplied and leucine-generated ketones feed hepatic DNL in an AACS-dependent manner and that ketone fed DNL is upregulated by disrupting mitochondrial citrate transport.

DISCUSSIONHere, we employed parallel isotope tracing across several liver-specific knockout mouse models to delineate a structure of the hepatic de novo lipogenesis (DNL) substrate supply network. While the carbon routing steps through the central DNL pathway beginning at acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) are well understood, a remaining challenge has been delineating the proportional contributions of substrates and enzymes supplying acetyl-CoA for ACC. Our results show that pyruvate fed DNL, through a mitochondrial-cytosolic MPC-CiC-ACLY pathway, is the greatest contributor. The acetate fed ACSS2 pathway operates largely in parallel to the MPC-ACLY pathway, while the ketone fed AACS pathway operates reciprocally to it, being upregulated when mitochondrial citrate transport is disrupted.Given the hypothetical path of pyruvate fed hepatic DNL through the MPC and then the CiC to ACLY, the distinctive control each enzyme exerts over DNL and precursor acetyl-CoA has remained unclear. For pyruvate fed DNL, we observed that the degree of control exerted by the MPC ≥ ACLY > CiC. Our results show that MPC LivKO and CiC LivKO increase total acetyl-CoA levels and that CiC LivKO also leads to citrate accumulation consistent with mitochondrial entrapment. The mechanisms for increased acetyl-CoA abundance with MPC disruption are uncertain, but may result from anaplerotic insufficiency, where the regeneration of mitochondrial oxaloacetate required to clear mitochondrial acetyl-CoA is limited. This is consistent with the decreased citrate enrichment seen with lactate/pyruvate tracing in MPC LivKO mice. Thus, a model consistent with our data is that the MPC controls movement of mitochondrial acetyl-CoA into citrate, the CiC controls clearance of mitochondrial acetyl-CoA through citrate export, and ACLY controls cytosolic acetyl-CoA production from mitochondrially exported and other sources of citrate.Given the mitochondrial routing of pyruvate fed DNL, a key initial question was whether the cytosolic acetate-ACSS2 pathway would be upregulated to compensate for its disruption. In mammals, most acetate originates independently of pyruvate metabolism, episodically from ethanol or high fructose consumption, and more continuously from the gut microbiota. Yet, in some systems pyruvate is directly converted to acetate by reactive oxygen species or off target dehydrogenase activity27. However, we observed that ACSS2 LivKO alone or in combination with CiC LivKO did not impair pyruvate fed DNL and that 13C from lactate/pyruvate resulted in only miniscule labeling of acetate. These results suggest that direct pyruvate to acetate conversions do not occur at meaningful rates in the liver. Given the apparent separation of pyruvate and acetate metabolism in the liver, acetate could reasonably be expected to function reciprocally to pyruvate in supplying DNL. However, because DNL from acetate did not increase, and in some cases decreased, when the MPC-CiC-ACLY pathway was disrupted, our results show that acetate fed DNL does not compensate for disrupted pyruvate fed DNL.Our discovery that CiC disruption leads to upregulated DNL from acetoacetate and ketogenic leucine metabolism suggests that ketone fed DNL, in contrast to acetate fed DNL, operates reciprocally to pyruvate fed DNL. While less addressed in the present literature, many studies since the 1950s have shown that ketones are utilized for sterol and fatty acid synthesis in the liver and other tissues (recently reviewed by Bergstrom34). Given that like CiC-mediated hepatic DNL, ketone fed hepatic DNL is obligately routed through hepatic mitochondria, we speculate they are evolutionary complements. Indeed, both the CiC and AACS are highly conserved in eukaryotes, and acetoacetate has been posited to be an alternative to citrate as a mitochondrial acetyl-CoA carrying metabolite, capable of partially supplying hepatic lipogenic carbon needs35,36. Moreover, the conversion of acetoacetate to acetyl-CoA is kinetically favorable, especially under conditions of decreased cytosolic acetyl-CoA production such as when CiC or ACLY are disrupted40. Given the metazoan need to transition between fed and fasted states to survive, the reciprocity of CiC-versus AACS-mediated DNL may facilitate an efficient transition from a fasted to a fed state, storing ketones again as lipids in preparation for the next fasting cycle.In contrast to DNL from acetoacetate, DNL from leucine, through either peripherally or hepatically generated α-ketoisocaproate, may utilize ketones as acute intermediates in a linear, intrahepatocyte pathway versus a systemic cycle. In the liver, α-ketoisocaproate is further metabolized into mitochondrial HMG-CoA, which can generate acetoacetate directly and secondarily through product acetyl-CoA condensation (Figure 6N for diagram). While the physiologic role of leucine as a hepatic DNL substrate is uncertain, it could contribute to hepatic energy storage after a high protein meal, during starvation when circulating BCAAs increase, or constitutively. The latter is noteworthy in that both elevated hepatic DNL and circulating BCAAs, including leucine, are strongly associated with metabolic disease1–3,41–43. This raises the intriguing possibility that high leucine levels could drive pathogenic DNL. Nonetheless, given the much lower endogenous contributions to DNL we observed compared to pyruvate and acetate, future studies will be required to determine if and under what conditions leucine functions as a physiologically meaningful endogenous DNL substrate.Although the primary goal of this study was a basic investigation of how mitochondria-transiting fluxes contribute to DNL, our findings provide context for translational efforts. To the degree that high rates of hepatic DNL is a risk factor for metabolic disease, our data suggest that targeting the MPC-CiC-ACLY pathway could be therapeutically beneficial. Moreover, although hepatic and plasma lipids were not a primary investigative endpoint of our study, MPC, CiC, and ACLY disruption, each as single knockouts or contributors in a double knockout model, decreased either liver or plasma lipids. This aligns with results from preclinical mouse studies and newer and prospective treatment approaches in humans. In mice, in vivo MPC inhibition, liver-specific MPC knockout, in vivo CiC inhibition, whole-body CiC heterozygous knockout, liver-specific ACLY knockout, and pharmacologic targeting of ACLY with bempedoic acid have all be reported to partially protect against MASLD and MASH23,44–46. Notably, several studies suggest that the MPC may be a target for some of the beneficial metabolic effects of TZD and like molecules, such as pioglitazone and MSDC-0602K47. In humans, targeting ACLY with bempedoic acid has emerged as a treatment for statin-intolerant dyslipidemic patients, decreasing LDL cholesterol levels and lowering the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events48–50. Nonetheless, bempedoic acid may also confer ACLY-independent therapeutic benefits46. The co-published manuscript by Wellen and Metallo and colleagues carefully evaluates this, showing markedly different effects on responses to a Western diet, including divergent effects on PPARα activation.The major goal of this study was to delineate a nutritional-genetic structure for the DNL substrate supply network. By systematically employing parallel, 13C label swapping, substrate competition isotope tracing experiments across multiple genetic models of disrupted hepatic metabolism, we generated comparative context not possible with more constrained studies. We show that the MPC and ACLY gate the major acetyl-CoA production pathway that operates in parallel to the ACSS2 pathway. Our results also suggest that ketones, through the AACS pathway, function as mitochondrial-citrate reciprocal DNL substrates, albeit at much lower endogenous rates under the fed conditions used here. These findings may inform strategies to therapeutically modulate DNL by elucidating a structure for the DNL substrate supply network.Limitations of the studyOur approach has advantages and limitations. We utilized bolus injections to deliver labeled substrates for tracing DNL. An advantage of this approach is that compared to continuous tracer infusions, it leads to measurable fatty acid labeling over short time periods and was efficiently scalable to test the many genetic models investigated here. In addition, because of their perturbative nature, bolus injections partially simulate the phasic nature of eating. A disadvantage is that tracer fluxes exceeded post-prandial fluxes by several fold, which is not ideal for quantitative metabolic modeling. However, the substrate competition design we employed here helps counter the specific perturbative effect of the traced substrate on metabolism, because the non-labeled substrate is competing for flux into DNL and its precursors. Across all substrate competition assays, the average acetyl-CoA 13C enrichment weighted by the labeling percentage of the administered tracer, was about 26%, which is observed for metabolite tracer enrichments with continuous infusions15. We also utilized fractional contribution analysis to isolate endogenous contributions, which provided results aligning with known in vivo substrate turnover rates. Nonetheless, future studies will be required for finely resolved quantitative modeling. Lastly, in this basic research investigation, we utilized short-term high sucrose diet feeding to stimulate DNL, to amplify the constraining effects of genetic disruptions, and to partially model the upregulated DNL often accompanying metabolic disease. Future studies using a variety of diet paradigms, over different time courses, will be required to evaluate interactions between the DNL substrate supply network we delineate here and discrete pathophysiologic features of hepatic and whole-body metabolic dysfunction.

These results suggest that direct pyruvate to acetate conversions do not occur at meaningful rates in the liver. Given the apparent separation of pyruvate and acetate metabolism in the liver, acetate could reasonably be expected to function reciprocally to pyruvate in supplying DNL. However, because DNL from acetate did not increase, and in some cases decreased, when the MPC-CiC-ACLY pathway was disrupted, our results show that acetate fed DNL does not compensate for disrupted pyruvate fed DNL.

Our discovery that CiC disruption leads to upregulated DNL from acetoacetate and ketogenic leucine metabolism suggests that ketone fed DNL, in contrast to acetate fed DNL, operates reciprocally to pyruvate fed DNL. While less addressed in the present literature, many studies since the 1950s have shown that ketones are utilized for sterol and fatty acid synthesis in the liver and other tissues (recently reviewed by Bergstrom34). Given that like CiC-mediated hepatic DNL, ketone fed hepatic DNL is obligately routed through hepatic mitochondria, we speculate they are evolutionary complements. Indeed, both the CiC and AACS are highly conserved in eukaryotes, and acetoacetate has been posited to be an alternative to citrate as a mitochondrial acetyl-CoA carrying metabolite, capable of partially supplying hepatic lipogenic carbon needs35,36. Moreover, the conversion of acetoacetate to acetyl-CoA is kinetically favorable, especially under conditions of decreased cytosolic acetyl-CoA production such as when CiC or ACLY are disrupted40. Given the metazoan need to transition between fed and fasted states to survive, the reciprocity of CiC-versus AACS-mediated DNL may facilitate an efficient transition from a fasted to a fed state, storing ketones again as lipids in preparation for the next fasting cycle.

In contrast to DNL from acetoacetate, DNL from leucine, through either peripherally or hepatically generated α-ketoisocaproate, may utilize ketones as acute intermediates in a linear, intrahepatocyte pathway versus a systemic cycle. In the liver, α-ketoisocaproate is further metabolized into mitochondrial HMG-CoA, which can generate acetoacetate directly and secondarily through product acetyl-CoA condensation (Figure 6N for diagram). While the physiologic role of leucine as a hepatic DNL substrate is uncertain, it could contribute to hepatic energy storage after a high protein meal, during starvation when circulating BCAAs increase, or constitutively. The latter is noteworthy in that both elevated hepatic DNL and circulating BCAAs, including leucine, are strongly associated with metabolic disease1–3,41–43. This raises the intriguing possibility that high leucine levels could drive pathogenic DNL. Nonetheless, given the much lower endogenous contributions to DNL we observed compared to pyruvate and acetate, future studies will be required to determine if and under what conditions leucine functions as a physiologically meaningful endogenous DNL substrate.

Although the primary goal of this study was a basic investigation of how mitochondria-transiting fluxes contribute to DNL, our findings provide context for translational efforts. To the degree that high rates of hepatic DNL is a risk factor for metabolic disease, our data suggest that targeting the MPC-CiC-ACLY pathway could be therapeutically beneficial. Moreover, although hepatic and plasma lipids were not a primary investigative endpoint of our study, MPC, CiC, and ACLY disruption, each as single knockouts or contributors in a double knockout model, decreased either liver or plasma lipids. This aligns with results from preclinical mouse studies and newer and prospective treatment approaches in humans. In mice, in vivo MPC inhibition, liver-specific MPC knockout, in vivo CiC inhibition, whole-body CiC heterozygous knockout, liver-specific ACLY knockout, and pharmacologic targeting of ACLY with bempedoic acid have all be reported to partially protect against MASLD and MASH23,44–46. Notably, several studies suggest that the MPC may be a target for some of the beneficial metabolic effects of TZD and like molecules, such as pioglitazone and MSDC-0602K47. In humans, targeting ACLY with bempedoic acid has emerged as a treatment for statin-intolerant dyslipidemic patients, decreasing LDL cholesterol levels and lowering the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events48–50. Nonetheless, bempedoic acid may also confer ACLY-independent therapeutic benefits46. The co-published manuscript by Wellen and Metallo and colleagues carefully evaluates this, showing markedly different effects on responses to a Western diet, including divergent effects on PPARα activation.

The major goal of this study was to delineate a nutritional-genetic structure for the DNL substrate supply network. By systematically employing parallel, 13C label swapping, substrate competition isotope tracing experiments across multiple genetic models of disrupted hepatic metabolism, we generated comparative context not possible with more constrained studies. We show that the MPC and ACLY gate the major acetyl-CoA production pathway that operates in parallel to the ACSS2 pathway. Our results also suggest that ketones, through the AACS pathway, function as mitochondrial-citrate reciprocal DNL substrates, albeit at much lower endogenous rates under the fed conditions used here. These findings may inform strategies to therapeutically modulate DNL by elucidating a structure for the DNL substrate supply network.

Limitations of the studyOur approach has advantages and limitations. We utilized bolus injections to deliver labeled substrates for tracing DNL. An advantage of this approach is that compared to continuous tracer infusions, it leads to measurable fatty acid labeling over short time periods and was efficiently scalable to test the many genetic models investigated here. In addition, because of their perturbative nature, bolus injections partially simulate the phasic nature of eating. A disadvantage is that tracer fluxes exceeded post-prandial fluxes by several fold, which is not ideal for quantitative metabolic modeling. However, the substrate competition design we employed here helps counter the specific perturbative effect of the traced substrate on metabolism, because the non-labeled substrate is competing for flux into DNL and its precursors. Across all substrate competition assays, the average acetyl-CoA 13C enrichment weighted by the labeling percentage of the administered tracer, was about 26%, which is observed for metabolite tracer enrichments with continuous infusions15. We also utilized fractional contribution analysis to isolate endogenous contributions, which provided results aligning with known in vivo substrate turnover rates. Nonetheless, future studies will be required for finely resolved quantitative modeling. Lastly, in this basic research investigation, we utilized short-term high sucrose diet feeding to stimulate DNL, to amplify the constraining effects of genetic disruptions, and to partially model the upregulated DNL often accompanying metabolic disease. Future studies using a variety of diet paradigms, over different time courses, will be required to evaluate interactions between the DNL substrate supply network we delineate here and discrete pathophysiologic features of hepatic and whole-body metabolic dysfunction.

Folch extraction and liver and plasma triglyceride and total cholesterol quantification.10–20 mg of liver tissue was homogenized in 4 ml of chloroform:methanol (2:1) and stored at room temperature for 30 minutes. 1 ml of 50 mM NaCl was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 20 seconds to form an emulsion. Emulsions were centrifuged at 1000 x g for 10 minutes and the lower organic phase containing the lipids was collected and dried under a stream of N2. Dried lipids were resuspended in 500 μl of chloroform and 10 μl or 100 μl of this suspension was transferred to a new tube for triglyceride and total cholesterol determination, respectively. The transferred lipid resuspension was mixed with 10 μl of triton-x100:chloroform (1:1) and allowed to dry under a stream of N2. Dried lipid:triton-x100 mixtures were resuspended in 500 μl of the Infinity Triglyceride and Total Cholesterol reagents, vigorously vortexed for 15 seconds, and allowed to incubate for 20 minutes with intermittent vertexing. Samples were transferred to 96-well plates and the difference in absorbance 500 nm – 660 nm was measured. For plasma samples, 3 μl of plasma collected as described above, was pipetted into duplicate wells of a 96-well plate and 300 μl of either Infinity Triglyceride or Total Cholesterol reagents was added, and plates were incubated at 37°C for 15 minutes before the difference in absorbance 500 nm – 660 nm was measured. The Verichem Laboratories Inc Matrix Plus Chemistry and Cholesterol Reference kits were used to generate standard curves for quantitative determinations.
