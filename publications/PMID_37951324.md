The innate immune response relies on the ability of host cells to rapidly detect
and respond to microbial nucleic acids. Toll-like receptors (TLRs), a class of
pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), play a fundamental role in distinguishing
self from non-self at the molecular level. In this study, we focused on TLR21,
an avian TLR that recognizes DNA motifs commonly found in bacterial genomic DNA,
specifically unmethylated CpG motifs. TLR21 is believed to act as a functional
homologue to mammalian TLR9. By analysing TLR21 signalling in chickens, we
sought to elucidate avian TLR21 activation outputs in parallel to that of other
nucleic acid species. Our analyses revealed that chicken TLR21 (chTLR21)
triggers the activation of NF-κB and induces a potent type-I interferon response
in chicken macrophages, similar to the signalling cascades observed in mammalian
TLR9 activation. Notably, the transcription of interferon beta (IFNB) by chTLR21
was found to be dependent on both NF-κB and IRF7 signalling, but independent of
the TBK1 kinase, a distinctive feature of mammalian TLR9 signalling. These
findings highlight the conservation of critical signalling components and
downstream responses between avian TLR21 and mammalian TLR9, despite their
divergent evolutionary origins. These insights into the evolutionarily conserved
mechanisms of nucleic acid sensing contribute to the broader understanding of
host-pathogen interactions across species.
