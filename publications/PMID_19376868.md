Abstract

Proteasomes are energy-dependent proteolytic machines. We elaborate here on the previously observed N α acetylation of the initiator methionine of the α1 protein of 20S core particles (CPs) of Haloferax volcanii proteasomes. Quantitative mass spectrometry revealed this was the dominant N-terminal form of α1 in H. volcanii cells. To further examine this, α1 proteins with substitutions in the N-terminal penultimate residue as well as deletion of the CP “gate” formed by the α1 N terminus were examined for their N α acetylation. Both the “gate” deletion and Q2A substitution completely altered the N α -acetylation pattern of α1, with the deletion rendering α1 unavailable for N α acetylation and the Q2A modification apparently enhancing cleavage of α1 by methionine aminopeptidase (MAP), resulting in acetylation of the N-terminal alanine. Cells expressing these two α1 variants were less tolerant of hypoosmotic stress than the wild type and produced CPs with enhanced peptidase activity. Although α1 proteins with Q2D, Q2P, and Q2T substitutions were N α acetylated in CPs similar to the wild type, cells expressing these variants accumulated unusually high levels of α1 as rings in N α -acetylated, unmodified, and/or MAP-cleaved forms. More detailed examination of this group revealed that while CP peptidase activity was not impaired, cells expressing these α1 variants displayed higher growth rates and were more tolerant of hypoosmotic and high-temperature stress than the wild type. Overall, these results suggest that N α acetylation of α1 is important in CP assembly and activity, high levels of α1 rings enhance cell proliferation and stress tolerance, and unregulated opening of the CP “gate” impairs the ability of cells to overcome salt stress.

Proteolysis is important in regulation and protein quality control. Energy-dependent proteases are crucial to early stages of these proteolytic events and include proteasomes, multicatalytic proteases present in all eukaryotes and archaea and in some bacteria. The catalytic component of proteasomes, the 20S core particle (CP), consists of four heptameric rings of α- and β-type subunits stacked as a barrel in an α7β7β7α7 configuration and is essential for growth of archaeal and eukaryotic cells ( 39 , 54 ). The active sites responsible for peptide bond hydrolysis are formed by N-terminal Thr residues of β-type subunits and are sequestered within the central chamber of the barrel-like structure. Energy-dependent triple-A ATPases, including regulatory particle triple-A ATPases (Rpt) in eukaryotes and proteasome-activating nucleotidases (PAN) in archaea, mediate the unfolding and translocation of substrate proteins through the α-rings for degradation within the CP ( 39 , 40 ).

One major difference between eukaryotic and prokaryotic proteasomal CPs is in the crystal structure of the channel opening formed by the α-rings. Due to partial disorder of the α-subunit N termini, the site of substrate entry appears open at the ends of the cylinders of archaeal and bacterial CPs (e.g., CPs of Thermoplasma acidophilum , Archaeoglobus fulgidus , and Mycobacterium tuberculosis ) ( 13 , 15 , 27 ). In contrast, X-ray structures of the CPs of yeast ( 14 ) and bovine ( 45 ) do not contain this opening. Instead the extreme N termini of the α2, α3, and α4 subunits and the loop structure of α5 fill the central pore in a gate-like structure.

Evidence suggests that all CPs are gated, and the major differences observed in the state of the α-ring gate in crystal structures are not physiological. For example, the N-terminal 11 amino acids of the A. fulgidus α subunit, which are not defined by electron density in the CP structure, are more ordered in the 16S “half” proteasome precursor ( 13 ). Furthermore, cryoelectron microscopy of the M. tuberculosis CP reveals closed ends that are dependent on the first eight residues of the α-subunit and which diminish peptidolytic activity. Consistent with this, deletion of the N-terminal α-helix (Δ2-12) of the T. acidophilum CP α-subunit abolishes the need for an ATPase (i.e., PAN) in the proteasome-mediated degradation of acid-denatured green fluorescent protein-SsrA or casein ( 4 ). In addition, the conserved YDR motif thought to be important in the sterics of α-ring gating is present in all archaeal α-type subunits to date ( 13 ). Thus, prokaryotes are thought to gate the α-ring aperture of their proteasomes; however, the physiological consequences of unregulated opening of this gate have not been examined.

A gated CP channel formed by the N termini of α-rings may be a general mechanism for regulating the activity of proteasomes. The rate-limiting step in proteasome-mediated protein degradation is translocation of substrates through the α-rings to the active sites contained within the β-rings of the CP ( 24 ). Gating is supported by the finding that eukaryotic CPs have no peptidolytic activity in the absence of Rpt proteins or mild chaotropic agents such as sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) or heat treatment ( 9 ). Furthermore, peptidase activity of the yeast CP is blocked by the N-terminal regions of the α3 subunit. Deletion (Δ2-9) or single substitution (D9A) of N-terminal residues of α3 derepresses this peptidase activity ( 12 ).

An additional gating mechanism could be employed by posttranslational modifications of the N termini of the α-type subunits. The α-type subunits of CPs are modified by N α acetylation in several eukaryotes and haloarchaea, including Haloferax volcanii ( 10 , 16 , 20 , 21 , 44 ). In yeast, N -acetyltransferase 1 (NAT1), the catalytic component of NatA, is responsible for the N α acetylation of five of the α-type subunits (α1, α2, α3, α4, and α7). Proteasomes purified from a nat1 mutant have twofold-higher chymotrypsin-like peptidase activity in the absence of SDS compared to the wild type, suggesting that N α acetylation enhances closure of the α-gate ( 21 ). In H. volcanii , both α1 and α2 are N α acetylated on their initiator methionine residue with a subset of α1 not acetylated and instead cleaved by an apparent methionine aminopeptidase ( 16 ). A large-scale proteomic survey reveals N α acetylation is common to other proteasomal α-type proteins of the haloarchaea ( 10 ). In this previous survey, the ratios of N α -acetylated and cleaved forms of the α-type proteins were quantified by spectral counting and estimated to be around 3:1 and 4:3 for Halobacterium salinarum and Natronomonas pharaonis , respectively ( 10 ). So far, this existence of these two unique forms of α subunit N termini in the cell simultaneously (initiator methionine N α acetylated and methionine aminopeptidase [MAP] cleaved) has only been observed in the haloarchaea.

In the present study, quantitative tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) was used to precisely determine the ratio of the N α -acetylated to MAP-cleaved forms of the proteasomal α1 protein in H. volcanii . In addition, site-directed mutagenesis was used to examine how the N-terminal penultimate (second) residue and N-terminal α-helix of α1 influence its N α -acetylated state, CP activity, and cell physiology. Alterations that either fully abolished N α acetylation or enhanced MAP cleavage of α1 (i) resulted in an increase in CP peptidase activity and (ii) rendered the cells more sensitive to hypoosmotic stress than wild type. In contrast, site-directed changes that generated a mixed population of α1 proteins in various N α -acetylated states, yet similar N α -acetylation profiles in CPs to wild type, had profound consequences, including (i) a substantial increase in the levels of α1 protein as heptameric rings, (ii) higher growth rate and cell yield, and (iii) enhanced tolerance of cells to thermal and hypoosmotic stress.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials.

Biochemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Other organic and inorganic analytical-grade chemicals were from Fisher Scientific (Atlanta, GA) and Bio-Rad (Hercules, CA). Radioactive chemicals were purchased from Perkin-Elmer (Waltham, MA) and StrepTactin Superflow resin was purchased from Qiagen (Valencia, CA). Desalted oligonucleotide primers were from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA). Site-directed mutagenesis materials were from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA).

Strains, plasmids, media, and growth conditions.

The strains, plasmids, and oligonucleotide primers used are summarized in Tables S1 and S2 in the supplemental material. H. volcanii strains included DS70 ( 50 ), H26 ( 2 ), and GZ130 ( 54 ). H. volcanii GZ130 (Δ psmA ) was used as a host for protein production and purification of CPs. Escherichia coli strain DH5α was used for routine cloning, and GM2163 was used for purification of plasmid DNA for transformation of H. volcanii as previously described ( 8 ). H. volcanii strains were grown in ATCC 974 medium at 42°C and 200 rpm. E. coli strains were grown in Luria-Bertani broth at 37°C. Media were supplemented with 100 μg·ml −1 of ampicillin or 0.1 μg·ml −1 of novobiocin as needed. All E. coli-H. volcanii shuttle plasmid constructs were derived from pBAP5010 ( 17 ) and pJAM202 ( 18 ). The fidelity of all PCR-amplified products was confirmed by DNA sequencing using the dideoxy termination method with capillary array sequencers (Genomics Core, Interdisiplinary Center for Biotechnology Research, University of Florida).

Protein techniques and purification.

For α1 and proteasomal CP purification, H. volcanii GZ130 (Δ psmA ) strains expressing synthetic operons of psmA -His 6 (encoding either N-terminal variants or a wild-type form of α1 with a C-terminal hexahistidine, or His 6 , tag) preceded by psmB-strepII (encoding wild-type β with a C-terminal StrepII tag) from a pHV2 ori -based plasmid were used. For each recombinant H. volcanii strain, three 1-liter cultures were grown in 2.8-liter Fernbach flasks at 42°C and 200 rpm to stationary phase (optical density at 600 nm [OD 600 ] of ∼2.5) in ATCC 974 medium with novobiocin. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (10,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C) and resuspended in 60 ml of 20 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.2) supplemented with 2 M NaCl (buffer A) and 5 mM imidazole. Cells were passed through a chilled French pressure cell at 20,000 lb/in 2 . Cell debris was removed by centrifugation (16,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C). The filtrate obtained with a 0.45-μm-pore-size filter (Nalge Nunc International, Rochester, NY) was applied to a 5-ml Ni 2+ -Sepharose column (HiTrap chelating; Amersham Biosciences) equilibrated with buffer A and washed with a step gradient consisting of 5 and 60 mM imidazole in buffer A. Fractions containing His 6 -tagged proteins were eluted in buffer A with 500 mM imidazole. Fractions containing proteasomal CPs were purified further by application onto a 0.2-ml Streptactin column (Qiagen) equilibrated with 20 mM Tris (pH 7.5) with 2 M NaCl (buffer L). The column was washed with 10 ml of buffer L, and proteins were eluted in 20 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.5) with 2 M NaCl and 1.5 mM desthiobiotin (buffer E). Protein fractions with peptide-hydrolyzing activity were pooled and dialyzed against buffer A using a D-Tube Dialyzer (Novagen). Fractions were determined to be homogeneous by Coomassie blue R-250-stained SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gels and MS. Molecular mass standards for SDS-PAGE included phosphorylase b (97.4 kDa), serum albumin (66.2 kDa), ovalbumin (45 kDa), carbonic anhydrase (31 kDa), trypsin inhibitor (21.5 kDa), and lysozyme (14.4 kDa) (Bio-Rad).

Gel filtration.

Samples (0.5 ml per run) were applied to a Superose-6 HR 10/30 gel filtration column (Amersham Biosciences) equilibrated in 20 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.2) with 2 M NaCl at a flow rate of 0.1 ml·min −1 . Molecular mass standards for gel filtration included cytochrome c (12 kDa), carbonic anhydrase (29 kDa), serum albumin (66 kDa), alcohol dehydrogenase (150 kDa), β-amylase (200 kDa), apoferritin (443 kDa), thyroglobulin (669 kDa), and blue dextran (2,000 kDa) (Sigma).

Peptide-hydrolyzing activity.

Chymotrypsin-like peptide-hydrolyzing activity was assayed using the fluorogenic peptide N -Suc-Leu-Leu-Val-Tyr-7-amido-4-methylcoumarin (LLVY-AMC). Peptide substrate was solubilized in 100% (vol/vol) dimethyl sulfoxide at 5 mM prior to dilution for assay (20 μM final) in buffer A. Release of 7-amino-4-methylcoumarin was monitored by determining the increase in fluorescence over time (excitation λ of 350 nm and emission λ of 460 nm) with an Aminco fluorocolorimeter (Spectronic Instruments). Assays were performed at 37°C and 65°C. Protein concentrations were determined by Bradford ( 5 ) with bovine serum albumin as the standard (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA).

Real-time PCR.

Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy minikit for bacteria (Qiagen) as previously reported ( 37 ), with the following modifications. One milliliter of cells (OD 600 of 0.8) was harvested by centrifugation and lysed in 100 μl of nuclease-free distilled water. Lysed cells were treated with DNase I according to manufacturer's protocol (Sigma). The RNeasy mini RNA isolation column was spun an additional 2 min before elution to remove residual ethanol. Quality of RNA was determined by 0.8% (wt/vol) agarose gel electrophoresis in 1× TAE buffer (40 mM Tris acetate, 2 mM EDTA [pH 8.0]) after heating of the RNA samples to 50°C for 15 min. Total RNA concentration was measured by A 260 . Total RNA was used to generate cDNA using the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad). Quantitative real-time-PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the iQ SYBR green Supermix and iCycler MyiQ real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad) with primers specific for psmA (α1) ( 37 ) and ribL10 (ribosomal protein) ( 6 ), the latter of which served as a normalization control. qRT-PCR samples that omitted incubation for reverse transcriptase were included as negative controls. The qRT-PCR analysis was performed as both a dilution series of the cDNA library and as triplicate samples of a dilution in the linear range of detection. A standard curve with PCR product specific for psmA was used to measure the linear range of detection of the iCycler. The ratio of ribL10 -specific transcript from the two samples was calculated, and the resulting scaler was used to correct for the psmA -specific transcript levels of the different samples.

Qualitative and quantitative MS.

Proteasomal fractions (3 to 6 μg protein) from Ni 2+ -affinity chromatography and sequential affinity chromatography were separated by reducing 12% SDS-PAGE or provided as in-solution samples. Gels were stained with Bio-Safe Coomassie (Bio-Rad). Protein bands corresponding to the molecular masses of the α- and β-type subunits of CPs (20 to 30 kDa) were excised from the gel and destained with 100 mM NH 4 HCO 3 in 50% (vol/vol) acetonitrile (4°C, overnight). Protein samples were reduced, alkylated in gel or in solution, and digested with trypsin (Promega). Capillary reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography separation of the protein digests was performed using a PepMap C 18 column (75-μm inside diameter, 15-cm length; LC Packings, San Francisco, CA) in combination with an Ultimate capillary high-performance liquid chromatography system (LC Packings, San Francisco, CA) operated at a flow rate of 200 nl·min −1 . A gradient (60 min) from 5 to 50% (vol/vol) acetonitrile in 0.1% (vol/vol) acetic acid was used. MS/MS analysis was performed using a hybrid quadrupole time-of-flight (QTOF) instrument (QSTAR) equipped with a nanoelectrospray source (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) and operated with Analyst QS 1.1 data acquisition software.

Quantitative MS techniques were performed using a triple-quadrupole MS/MS analyzer (QTRAP) equipped with a nanoelectrospray source (Applied Biosystems). A control peptide (amino acids 13 to 22 of the α1 protein [GITIFSPDGR], which is unmodified in all samples) was included to minimize variability. Peptides were targeted for precursor ion scanning using a multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) program developed in the MIDAS workflow software system. Relative quantities of different peptides were measured by integration of the respective peaks on the initial MS scan using the integration function on Analyst QS 1.2.

Database searching.

MS/MS data generated by information-dependent acquisition via QSTAR were searched against the deduced proteome of the H. volcanii DS2 genome sequence (communicated by J. Eisen, TIGR; http://archaea.ucsc.edu/ , April 2007 version) using the Mascot database search engine (Matrix Science, Boston, MA). Probability-based Mascot scores were determined by comparison of search results against estimated random match population and are reported as ∼10 × log 10 ( p ), where p is the absolute probability. Individual Mascot ion scores greater than 32 were considered to indicate identity or extensive homology ( P < 0.05). Scores above this default significant value were considered for protein identification in addition to validation by manual interpretation of MS/MS spectra. Carbamido-methylation of cysteine was used as a fixed modification based on sample preparation. Variable modifications implemented in the database search included deamidation of asparagine and glutamine; N-terminal acetylation; oxidation (single and double) of methionine; and phosphorylation of serine, threonine, and tyrosine.

2D gel electrophoresis.

Two-dimensional (2D) gel electrophoresis was preformed as previously described ( 19 , 22 ) with the following modifications. Two milliliters of cells (OD 600 of 1) was pelleted by centrifugation (10,000 × g at 4°C) and lysed in distilled water. The lysed cells were treated with DNase I for 30 min at room temperature or 21°C. Proteins were precipitated from cell lysate by mixing 100% cold acetone with the cell pellet. The resulting protein pellet was washed 5 times with 100% cold acetone and resuspended in 20 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.2) with 10% (vol/vol) glycerol. Protein (250 μg) was loaded onto 11 cm ReadyStrip IPG strips with a pI range of 3.9 to 5.1 (Bio-Rad). Isoelectric focusing (IEF) for 40,000 Vh was performed using a Protean IEF cell (Bio-Rad). The second dimension was run on Criterion precast 11-cm 12.5% SDS-PAGE gels with an IPG + 1 well (Bio-Rad) at 16°C for 90 min. Gels were stained with SYPRO ruby according to supplier (Invitrogen). Gels were imaged on a Typhoon 9400 (Amersham), and spots were picked manually and identified by MS.

RESULTS

20S proteasomes rapidly isolated by two-step affinity purification from H. volcanii .

An efficient, two-step affinity purification protocol was developed to rapidly isolate 20S proteasomal CPs from H. volcanii . It involved fusing a StrepII and His 6 tag to the C terminus of the β and α1 subunits, respectively. Both affinity tags are compatible with high-salt chromatography (M. Pohlschröder, personal communication). The tagged proteins were synthesized from plasmid pJAM2545, which carried a synthetic operon of psmB -StrepII (encoding β-StrepII) followed by psmA -His 6 (encoding α1-His 6 ) positioned downstream of the strong Halobacterium cutirubrum rRNA P2 promoter and upstream of a T7 phage transcriptional terminator. Cell lysate of H. volcanii DS70 expressing this synthetic operon was applied to an Ni 2+ -affinity column to isolate α1-His 6 -containing proteins. To purify β-StrepII in complex with α1-His 6 and remove the nonspecific histidine-rich PitA protein of ∼60 kDa encoded by the genome ( 3 ), fractions were applied to a second column, which contained StrepTactin (a streptavidin variant).

The purity of fractions isolated by the two-step affinity method was determined by MS/MS, SDS-PAGE, and Superose 6 gel filtration. Based on MS/MS (see Table S3 in the supplemental material), only α1, α2, and β protein fragments of proteasomal CPs were detected. Likewise, Coomassie blue staining of fractions separated by reducing SDS-PAGE revealed only proteasomal CP-specific protein bands, including a doublet of β and β-StrepII at ∼20 kDa, a doublet of α1 and α1-His 6 at ∼30 kDa, and α2 at 26 kDa (Fig. 1A ). The appearance of only one major peak in the gel filtration chromatogram at the molecular mass of proteasomal CPs (600 kDa) further supports the conclusion that the fractions were homogeneous for 20S proteasomes including tagged α1 and β proteins and untagged α2 proteins (Fig. 1B ).

This purification strategy proved sufficient for rapid and effective purification of proteasomal CPs from H. volcanii cells under native conditions, and the types of α and β proteins isolated in the 28 subunit CP assemblies were consistent with the genotype of the strains used for protein synthesis. 26S proteasomes have been purified from human cells by a similar approach, which included fusing the non-ATPase subunit Rpn11 to a C-terminal HTBH tag and directly binding these complexes to streptavidin resin followed by tobacco etch virus protease-based elution under native conditions ( 48 ).

Acetylated form of α1 is dominant in the cell.

As previously reported, the proteasomal α1 protein of H. volcanii appears as a mixture of proteins that either are acetylated on the initiating methionine (Ac1) or are unacetylated with the methionine removed (N2) ( 16 ). In order to better understand the relative ratios of these forms, quantitative MS of α1-His 6 proteins purified by single-step Ni 2+ -affinity chromatography from H. volcanii DS70(pJAM204) cells was performed; thus, this initial analysis was of α1 proteins either unassembled or assembled into CPs. Spectral counting was combined with a comparative integration of peak area of each of the isotopic envelopes of the two N-terminal peptides. The ratio of the Ac1 to N2 form of α1 was 103:1 (Table 1 ), heavily favoring the acetylated form (Ac1) over the unacetylated methionine-removed form (N2). To confirm the integration intensity of the MS scan from the QTOF instrument, different instrumentation, a triple quadrupole instrument, was used with a multiple reaction monitoring (MRM)-initiated detection and sequencing workflow (MIDAS). Using this software, the mass spectrometer only detects ions of a user-defined set of parameters. The ions defined were for the two forms of the N-terminal α1 peptide (733.79, 2+ for Ac1 and 647.17, 2+ for N2), and, for additional selectivity, product ions from the second MS scan (MS/MS) were used for identification. The resulting filtering identified two peptides that matched those of defined mass/charge ratios and product ions. The calculated ratio of these two ions was 100:1 (Fig. 2 ). Thus, the spectral counting and MRM results are in good agreement and reveal that the Ac1 form of α1 far exceeds its N2 form in the cell.

In good agreement with our observations, the proteasomal α-type proteins of both H. salinarum and N. pharaonis also appear incompletely acetylated ( 1 , 10 ). However, the levels of the unacetylated fractions of these latter α proteins are much higher, accounting for 25 to 43% of the total forms compared to less than 1% for H. volcanii α1. Since spectral counting is semiquantitative and known to overreport low-abundance peptides ( 34 ), it is typically reserved for determining whether one peptide is in excess of another. Estimation of the ratio of unacetylated to acetylted forms of the α-type proteins of H. salinarum and N. pharaonis was solely derived by spectral counting of a proteomic survey of N-terminal peptides without additional validation methods or controls. This, in part, could explain the differences in the relative levels of the acetylated and unacetylated forms of the α-type subunits from species to species. Alternatively, H. salinarum and N. pharaonis , which encode only a single α-type subunit, may be more prone to differential N α acetylation of their α-type subunit. This would be in contrast to H. volcanii , which produces two different CP α-type subunits (α1 and α2) and modulates the levels of these proteins in response to growth ( 37 ). It should be noted that in addition to H. volcanii , other archaea ( 28 ) and eukaryotes ( 31 ) modulate CP composition, and this can have a profound impact on cell physiology.

Amino acid substitutions in the N-terminal penultimate position of α1 affect acetylation and cleavage of the initiator methionine.

As a result of N-terminal processing and protein maturation by MAP and N α -acetyltransferase (NAT) enzymes, at least four different forms of the N terminus of a protein are possible. These include proteins that retain their initiator methionine in either an N α -acetylated (Ac1) or unmodified form (N1) as well as those which undergo MAP cleavage resulting in a cleaved methionine (N2) which can also be N α acetylated (Ac2). Unlike bacteria, formyl-methionine has not been identified as an initiating residue in archaea ( 36 ). These final N-terminal states of a protein are thought to be governed by the nature of the first three amino acids: the initiating methionine and the penultimate and antepenultimate amino acids ( 10 , 23 , 26 , 29 , 35 , 49 ). Taking this into consideration, a series of amino acid substitutions were introduced into the α1 protein through site-directed mutagenesis of the psmA gene. The resulting substitutions in α1 were Q2A, Q2D, Q2P, Q2S, Q2T, Q2V, and a deletion of the N-terminal α-helix (ΔQ2-R12) resulting in fusion of the initiating methinione to G13. All of the α1 protein variants were expressed in an H. volcanii strain with the wild-type psmA gene deleted from the chromosome (GZ130), and similar to the “wild type,” all of these α1 variants had C-terminal His 6 tags. For analysis of the acetylated state of total α1 protein, variant α1 proteins were purified from whole-cell lysates by Ni 2+ -affinity chromatography alone. Chromatography fractions enriched for α1 variants were subjected to tryptic digest and MS/MS by electrospray ionization QTOF (ABI QSTAR) (Table 2 ). The results revealed variant α1 Q2A was acetylated on the N-terminal penultimate (second) amino acid, alanine, after removal of the initiator methionine (Ac2) 100% of the time. Variant α1 ΔQ2-R12 (G13) was not modified by either acetylation or methionine removal (N1). Variants α1 Q2S and Q2V were acetylated on the initiating methionine (Ac1) similar to the wild type. Variants α1 Q2D, Q2P and Q2T were a mixture of cleaved and uncleaved, acetylated, and unacetylated forms of the protein (Ac1, N2, and/or N1), with the ratios of these different forms varying among different chromatography preparations (Table 2 ).

The wild-type and variant α1-His 6 fractions were further purified by StrepTactin chromatography to isolate the 28-subunit proteasomal CPs using the coproduced β-StrepII as the affinity bait. The relative level of α1 N α acetylation in these purified CPs was measured. For the vast majority of CPs, including those with wild-type or Q2S, Q2V, Q2P, Q2T, and Q2D α1 subunits, nearly 100% of the α1 proteins that were assembled into CPs were in the Ac1 form (acetylated on the initiator methionine residue) (Table 2 ). The exceptions included CPs with the α1 ΔQ2 minus R12 and Q2A variants, which similar to their population of α1 proteins, were, respectively, unacetylated or cleaved and N α acetylated on the second residue (Ac2) of the α1 protein. Thus, in contrast to the total population of α1 protein for the α1 variants Q2D, Q2T, and Q2P, which was a mixture of unmodified forms (N1), acetylated forms (Ac1), and unacetylated forms with the initiator methionine removed (N2), when CPs were isolated containing these variants, only the Ac1 form of the α1 protein was detected by MS/MS. The other forms (N1 and N2) were found either as α1 heptamers or associated in non-CP complexes. Thus, although modification of the N-terminal penultimate residue perturbed the acetylation pattern of these α1 variants, the dominant N-terminal form of α1 in these CPs was Ac1. These results suggest that either the Ac1 form of α1 is preferentially selected for incorporation into CPs or CP structural elements are required for proper acetylation of the N-terminal initiator methionine of α1.

It should also be noted that N α acetylation of an initiator methionine preceding a glutamine residue (Met-Gln-), as in α1, is not common among proteins of eukaryotes ( 35 ) or haloarchaea ( 1 , 10 , 23 ). This motif is somewhat related to substrates of the eukaryotic protein NatB, which acetylates initiator methionine residues preceding glutamate, aspartate, asparagine, or methionine residues (Met-Glu-, Met-Asp-, Met-Asn-, or Met-Met-, respectively). However, N α acetylation of α1 was not abolished by the various single-amino-acid substitutions examined, including acidic (Asp), neutral nonpolar (Ala, Val, and Pro), and neutral polar (Ser and Thr) residues. Only deletion of the N-terminal α-helix abolished the N α acetylation of α1, suggesting this N-terminal secondary structure plays an important role in recognizing α1 for acetylation. Whether this structure is recognized in a nacent polypeptide emerging from the ribosome during translation (cotranslationally) or posttranslationally is unclear.

Interestingly, while a number of penultimate residues with a small radius of gyration known to be substrates of methionine aminopeptidases (i.e., glycine, alanine, serine, threonine, proline, valine, and cysteine) were included in this study, only the Q2A substitution rendered α1 completely susceptible to removal of the initiator methionine. The Q2T, Q2D, and Q2P alterations resulted in only a slight (10 to 20%) increase in the cleaved (N2) form of α1. Furthermore, Q2S and Q2V had no influence on α1 cleavage. Thus, only substitution for glutamine with alanine, one of the smallest amino acids, appears to render α1 completely susceptible to methionine aminopeptidase cleavage.

CP peptidase activity was enhanced by the α1 Q2A and Δ2-12 modifications.

The influence of the N-terminal modifications of α1 on the chymotrypsin-like activity of the various CPs was examined under conditions previously found optimal for proteasomes of α1β composition purified from H. volcanii DS2 (i.e., Tris-HCl buffer at pH 7.5 with 2 M NaCl at 65°C) ( 51 ). Assays were also performed at 37°C to assess the effect of temperature. Not surprisingly, CPs containing the α1 Δ2-12 variant showed a three- to fivefold increase in activity compared to the wild type at both temperatures examined (Table 2 ). The N-terminal α-helix thought to make up the regulatory gate of the CP was deleted in this α1 variant (Δ2-12), and this change is likely to allow the passive diffusion of cleavable peptides into the proteolytic core. Interestingly, the Q2A variant, which has only modest changes in its N terminus, including the removal of the initiator methionine and acetylation of the exposed N-terminal alanine, was also enhanced (nearly twofold at 65°C) in its CP peptidase activity, suggesting it too has a more “open” gate conformation than the wild type. CPs containing the α1 Q2V, Q2S, and Q2T variants displayed activity comparable to that of the wild type at both temperatures examined, consistent with the N α -acetylated state of these CPs, which is similar to that of the wild type.

α1 variants (Q2D, Q2P, and Q2T) accumulate as heptameric rings in H. volcanii .

We also noted during purification that α1 variant proteins Q2D, Q2T and Q2P, which were detected in multiple N-terminal forms (N1, Ac1, and/or N2), accumulated to much higher levels than the α1 wild type or other α1 variants when expressed in H. volcanii (Fig. 3A ). Analysis of cell lysate by 2D gels revealed that a 30-kDa isoform chain predominated in the H. volcanii GZ130 cells (grown at 37°C) expressing α1 Q2T compared to wild-type α1 (Fig. 3B ). This isoform chain was identified by MS/MS to be solely composed of α1 protein (see Table S4 in the supplemental material), highlighting the extreme excess of α1 protein in these α1 Q2T-expressing cells. Additional differences in cell lysate separated by 1D gels were also observed, including an ∼70-kDa protein that was more abundant and a 37-kDa protein that was less abundant in the α1 variant strains than the wild type (Fig. 3A ). However, these differences appeared modest compared to the α1 protein excess of the 2D gels (Fig. 3B ).

To investigate the quaternary structure of the excess α1, the variant and wild-type α1 proteins enriched by Ni 2+ -Sepharose chromatography were compared by Superose 6 gel filtration chromatography (Fig. 3C ). For cells expressing wild-type α1, the majority of this α1 protein was associated in CPs. In contrast, the dominant quaternary form of the α1 variant proteins that appeared in excess (Q2D, Q2T, and Q2P) was of a molecular mass consistent with heptameric rings (170 kDa), with the levels of the 600-kDa CPs similar to those of the wild type (Fig. 3C ). Since β subunits are required for proteasome assembly, the highly abundant α1 protein variants appear to be trapped in an assembly or degradation intermediate of heptameric rings. qRT-PCR analysis revealed no dramatic difference in the levels of psmA -specific transcript among the various strains (e.g., cells expressing the α1 wild type versus α1 Q2D) (Fig. 3D ), suggesting the enhanced levels of α1 protein in the variant strains are a result of a posttranscriptional event such as protein half-life or translation efficiency.

Complementation of the hypoosmotic stress phenotype of psmA mutants by expression of α1 variants.

H. volcanii requires high concentrations of salt for optimal growth (1.7 to 2.5 M NaCl) ( 30 ). Recently characterized strains of H. volcanii that are lacking in proteasomal subunit genes, most notably psmA (α1), display an increased sensitivity to hypoosmotic environments, low-salt-containing media ( 54 ). For the psmA mutants, this increased sensitivity to low-salt media can be rescued by the addition of a psmA -His 6 gene in trans (e.g., plasmid pJAM204 encoding α1-His 6 ) ( 54 ). In order to understand how the individual substitutions in the penultimate residue of the α1 protein were affecting the cell, strains expressing α1 variant proteins were grown in rich medium with various concentrations of NaCl (Fig. 4 ). As previously reported, while the Δ psmA strain (GZ130) is fully complemented to wild type by providing psmA -His 6 (α1) in trans , the negative control (pJAM202c vector alone) failed to rescue the hypoosmotically sensitive phenotype caused by deletion of the psmA gene. Interestingly, both genes encoding the α1 Q2A and α1 ΔQ2 − R12 variants, that had enhanced CP peptidase activity, had negative phenotypes with even greater growth inhibition when expressed in trans than the psmA knockout (GZ130) alone (Fig. 4 ). In contrast, GZ130 strains expressing α1 Q2D and α1 Q2T were less sensitive to osmotic stress than the wild type. Effectively, strains with α1 Q2D or α1 Q2T grew at lower concentrations of NaCl than wild-type cells (Fig. 4 ).

Cells expressing α1 Q2T display a higher growth rate and are more thermotolerant than those expressing wild-type α1.

Curiosity about the possibility of a temperature-sensitive phenotype of cells producing proteasomal CPs with an N-terminally truncated α1 (i.e., ΔQ2 − R12 and Q2A) led us to measure the temperature sensitivity of H. volcanii GZ130 (Δ psmA ) cells expressing the various α1 proteins in trans . Strains expressing the N-terminally truncated α1 variants did not display a significant temperature-sensitive phenotype compared to strains expressing wild-type α1. However, to our surprise, strains expressing α1 Q2D and α1 Q2T variants grew to a higher cell density at 50°C than those expressing wild-type α1 in both H26 parent and GZ130 Δ psmA mutant strain backgrounds, revealing that this growth phenotype at 50°C associated with the α1 variants was dominant over the genomically encoded wild-type α1 protein. To further analyze this phenomenon, a growth curve was determined for cells which synthesized α1 Q2T versus the wild type at 50°C (Fig. 5A ). Cells with α1 Q2T grew to 6 times the final density of those with wild-type α1 (Fig. 5A ). The growth rate of the Δ psmA cells expressing wild-type α1 and α1 Q2T from plasmids was further analyzed at temperatures ranging from 37 to 50°C (Fig. 5B ). At all temperatures measured, cells expressing the variant α1 Q2T had a significantly higher growth rate than cells expressing wild-type α1. It should also be noted that growth at 50°C of the Δ psmA strain expressing psmA -His 6 (α1) in trans [GZ130(pJAM204)] was comparable to that of H26, the parent strain of this study (data not shown). Likewise, the Arrhenius plot for cells expressing wild-type α1 in this study is in good agreement with that previously published for growth of H. volcanii DS70 ( 38 ).

FIG. 5.

It is surprising that such a dramatic increase in overall growth rate, cell yield, and tolerance of stress (low salt and high temperature) was observed for the α1 Q2T variant compared to the wild type, since the difference between the two strains expressing these variants at the genetic level is only two nucleotide substitutions in the penultimate codon of the 5′ end of the psmA gene resulting in a single-amino-acid substitution. While the thermostability of extremophilic proteins is relatively common, including optimal activity of the H. volcanii CP at 65 to 75°C ( 51 ), increasing the growth rate, growth yield, and stress tolerance of the entire organism by making a subtle change to a structural gene is less common. There are a few examples of accomplishing this by overexpressing heat shock proteins, such as Hsp70 ( 32 , 33 ) and ShsP ( 42 ), or by uncoupling the regulation of heat shock-inducible transcription factors (hsf) ( 47 ) in various organisms.

Oddly, the connection between proteasomal activity and acquired stress tolerance appears inversely proportional with the more active, potentially ungated and unregulated, proteasomal CPs (i.e., Q2A and Δ2-12 variants) rendering the H. volcanii cells less tolerant of stress than those with gated CPs. Interestingly, this type of inverse relationship correlates with previous studies in eukaryotic cells, including the observed increase in thermotolerance of yeast after inhibition of proteasomal CP activity, apparently due to an induced heat shock response ( 7 , 25 ). In our study, the increased stress tolerance of H. volcanii α1 Q2T cells does not appear to be a product of enhanced CP peptidase activity. Both wild-type and Q2T α1-containing CPs had similar peptidase activities at both moderate and high temperatures (37 and 65°C, respectively) (Table 2 ), and their thermostabilities were comparable, based on preincubation at 75 to 80°C for 30 min and an activity assay at 65°C (data not shown). Although 2D gel electrophoresis did not indicate cells expressing the α1 Q2T variant were locked into a constitutive heat shock phenotype when grown at 37°C, it did reveal unusually high levels of α1 protein that were also detected throughout α1 purification (Fig. 3 ). Based on these findings, we propose that the high levels of the α1 Q2T rings may interact with and stimulate the protein folding activity of chaperones such as those of the triple-A ATPase family or more directly act as a chaperone to stabilize proteins needed for cell proliferation and/or stress response. Chaperone activities have been found to be closely associated with energy-dependent protease pathways such as the proteasome, Clp, and FtsH systems ( 11 , 41 , 53 ). It is also possible that the Q2T strain is in an induced heat-shock state based on the observed increase in the levels of a 70-kDa protein and decrease in the levels of a 37-kDa protein compared to the wild type by 1D PAGE (Fig. 3A ); however, these differences could not be resolved by 2D PAGE to facilitate rapid protein identification.

DISCUSSION

This study elaborates on the N α acetylation of the α1 protein of proteasomal CPs of the haloarchaeon H. volcanii . The acetylated (Ac1) and unacetylated (N1) forms of α1 protein were quantified, and the acetylated form was shown to be in 100-fold excess of the unacetylated form in whole cells and purified 20S proteasomes or CPs. Amino acid substitutions in the N-terminal penultimate position and removal of the N-terminal α-helix of the α1 protein were used to probe N α -acetyltransferase and MAP activities of H. volcanii . Deletion of the N-terminal α-helix (ΔQ2-R12) and substitution of the penultimate glutamine for an alanine residue (Q2A) had the most profound influence on these activities. The N-terminal deletion (ΔQ2-R12) rendered α1 unavailable for modification by N α -acetyltransferase based on the detection of α1 only in the N1 form. Likewise, although the Q2A modification did not impair N α acetylation of α1, it did alter the site of this modification to the N2 position based on detection of α1 only in the Ac2 form. This latter result suggests that the M-A bond of the Q2A variant is a much better substrate for MAP cleavage than the M-Q bond of wild-type α1, consistent with the preferences of purified MAP enzymes ( 52 ). In contrast to ΔQ2-R12 and Q2A, the Q2D, Q2P, and Q2T variants did not alter the prevalence of α1 proteins in the Ac1 form in the CPs; however, these latter site-directed mutations did result in an increase in the population of unacetylated N1 and/or N2 forms of α1 as rings in whole cells. Thus, although the population of N α -acetylated α1 proteins was impacted by these latter amino acid substitutions, the acetylated state of the α1 proteins within the CP did not change.

Cells expressing the different α1 N-terminal variants had a collection of phenotypes and CP activities that correlated with the ratios of α1 in the N α -acetylated or unacetylated state. For example, CPs purified from strains producing α1 variants completely devoid (i.e., α1 ΔQ2-R12) or impaired in the position of N α acetylation (i.e., Q2A in the Ac2 versus Ac1 form) had enhanced levels of peptidase activity suggesting the gate of the CP was in a more open state than the wild type, allowing better access of substrate to the proteolytic active sites harbored within the central chamber. Interestingly, the H. volcanii strains producing these “open” CPs displayed reduced growth in low-salt medium, suggesting that gating of the CP (likely to protect the cell from uncontrolled proteolysis) is important for cell viability under these stressful conditions. All of the other site-directed mutations examined did not impact the level or residue position of the N α acetylation of α1 in CPs, nor did they enhance (some actually reduced) the CP peptidase activity, suggesting the CP gate is not artificially opened by these mutations. Although these latter strains produced apparently “gated” CPs, a number of them (i.e., those producing α1 Q2D, Q2T, and Q2P) accumulated unusually high levels of α1 rings in various unacetylated states (N1 and N2 forms). Of these, two strains were further examined (i.e., α1 Q2D and Q2T) and found to display higher growth rates and improved tolerance to heat and hypoosmotic stress compared to the wild type. These phenotypes were dominant even when the α1 variant was coproduced with wild-type α1, suggesting the accumulation of α1 rings can significantly alter the proliferation and survival of cells.

Whether N α acetylation occurs co- and/or posttranslationally in H. volcanii or other archaea remains to be determined. Although cotranslational N α acetylation is one of the most common forms of protein modification in eukaryotic cells, N α acetylation is rare in bacteria and has only been demonstrated to occur posttranslationally ( 35 ). In yeast, all seven of the CP α subunits are N α acetylated by NatA (NAT1 catalytic subunit) or NatC (MAK3 catalytic subunit) during translation ( 21 ). Thus, similar to eukaryotic cells, it is possible that H. volcanii uses a cotranslational mechanism to acetylate the N terminus of α1 and requires the N-terminal α-helix for this modification. If so, incomplete N α acetylation during translation of the Q2T, Q2D, and Q2P variants could explain why some of the α1 proteins in the whole population are acetylated and some are not. If the acetylated forms of α1 have a higher affinity for incorporation into CPs, this would explain the observed difference in the acetylated state of the assembled and unassembled populations of α1 protein. Alternatively, the observed unacetylated N1 and/or N2 forms that accumulated as α1 rings in these strains (expressing the α1 Q2T, Q2D, and Q2P variants) may be degradation intermediates in which the cotranslationally added N α -acetyl group was removed and the resulting α1 protein has yet to be degraded. If so, modification of the N-terminal penultimate glutamine to an aspartate, threonine, or proline residue may enhance the stability of the α1 protein. Although an archaeal N-end rule has yet to be described, N-terminal residues, including those in the penultimate position, are known to substantially influence protein stability in bacteria ( 43 ) and eukaryotes ( 46 ). Alternatively, the N α acetylation of α1 is posttranslational and requires CP structure including the N-terminal α-helix but not the initiator methionine. If so, it would be interesting to understand how the NAT enzyme differentiates between α1 rings alone compared to α1 rings in the proteasomal CP complex, since the site of N-terminal modification of α1 surrounds the openings on each end of the CP cylinder.
