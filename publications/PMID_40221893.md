PURPOSE: GPKOW, a gene on the X-chromosome, encodes a nuclear RNA-binding
protein important in messenger RNA (mRNA) processing as a spliceosome subunit.
This work aims to establish GPKOW as a disease-associated gene.
METHODS: We describe 3 males from 2 unrelated families with hemizygous
frameshift variants affecting the last exon of GPKOW p.(Arg441SerfsTer30) and
p.(Ser444GlufsTer28). The effect of p.(Ser444GlufsTer28) on gene expression was
evaluated in patient's fibroblasts. In vivo studies in Drosophila melanogaster
targeting the sole GPKOW fly ortholog, CG10324 (Gpkow) were performed.
RESULTS: Clinical presentations included intrauterine growth restriction,
microcephaly/microencephaly, and eye, brain, skin, and skeletal abnormalities.
Heterozygote females presented with short stature, microcephaly, and vision
problems. Sequencing of fibroblasts' mRNA confirmed that GPKOW mRNA escapes
nonsense-mediated decay. Yet, reduced protein levels suggested protein
instability. Studies in Drosophila showed that Gpkow is essential and broadly
expressed. It is enriched in neurons and glia in eyes and head of developing and
adult flies. Knockdown and overexpression of Gpkow in the fly eye cause
eyeless/headless phenotype, suggesting that the gene is dosage sensitive.
Importantly, overexpression of the p.(Ser444GlufsTer28) variant caused milder
defects than the reference allele, indicating that the truncated protein behaves
as a partial loss-of-function allele.
CONCLUSION: Rare variants in GPKOW cause a multisystemic X-linked syndrome.

Introduction

Pre-mRNA splicing is catalyzed by the spliceosome, a ribonucleoprotein complex that functions in a dynamic manner. Multiple proteins are associated with the various stages of the spliceosome activity1. Among these proteins, GPKOW was identified as a core protein in the spliceosome interacting with the DEAH-type RNA helicase proteins hPRP2 (encoded by DHX16, OMIM*603405) and hPRP16 (encoded by DHX38, OMIM*605584)2,3. The ortholog of GPKOW in C.elegans (gkow-1) has been suggested to play a role in embryonic development based on RNAi experiments that resulted in embryonic lethality4; however, little is known about the involvement of GPKOW in metazoan development and human disease. GPKOW is encoded by a gene on the X-chromosome (OMIM*301003), and there is only one report in the literature so far describing a family with a male-lethal microcephaly and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) segregating with a donor splice site variant in GPKOW (c.331+5G>A)5.

GPKOW has a pLI (probability of loss-of-function intolerance) score of 1 in gnomAD (v.2.1.1). There are only two frameshift loss-of-function variants in gnomAD (v4.1.0), none in a hemizygous state, suggesting this gene is highly intolerant for loss of function variants. Interestingly, there are two males reported in DECIPHER6 with multiple congenital anomalies and deletions that include the GPKOW locus. However, since both individuals carry additional copy number variants, the extent in which the reported complete loss of GPKOW contributes to their phenotypes is uncertain. These data suggest loss-of-function or dosage sensitivity as a possible mechanism for pathogenicity.

Here we present three males from two unrelated families with hemizygous frameshift variants in the last exon of GPKOW (NM_015698.5), c.1323_1324del:p.(Arg441SerfsTer30) and c.1329dupG:p.(Ser444GlufsTer28). In the first family, two maternal half-brothers died in the first year of life and in the second family we report, the male pregnancy was terminated. Clinical presentation included IUGR, severe brain and eye phenotype, and skeletal abnormalities documented in all three males. Our work aims to characterize GPKOW as a novel disease-associated gene by investigating the effect of a C-terminal frameshift variant p.(Ser444GlufsTer28) on GPKOW expression in patient’s fibroblasts and showing the importance of Gpkow to survival and to the development of brain and eye in the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster.

ResultsCase report – Family IFamily 1 presented with a history of two maternal half-brothers who died in the first year of life from respiratory failure with similar clinical findings and course (see Table 1).Individual I was born at 36 weeks gestation to a 31-year-old mother via spontaneous vaginal delivery. He was born small for gestational age at 1.4 kg which is −2.6 standard deviations from the mean (SD) on Fenton preterm boys growth charts. Birth length was 39.8cm (−3.0 SD) and head circumference was 29.1cm (−2.4 SD). Apgar scores of 9 at one and at five minutes suggested no acute complications after birth. During the neonatal period, he had recurrent aspirations and feeding difficulties, and failure to thrive. He was intubated for acute respiratory distress at 7 weeks. On physical exam he was found to have relative macrocephaly, low-set ears, depressed nasal bridge with anteverted nares, bilateral microphthalmia, narrow palpebral fissures, sparse eyelashes and hair, and macroglossia. Additionally, he had hypoplastic nails and generalized hyperkeratotic scaling without involvement of palms or soles (Fig.1A). His weight was 2.38 kg (−4.5 SD), his length was 41 cm (−6.3 SD), and his head circumference was 35 cm (−1.3 SD). He developed anasarca and showed rapid increase in his head circumference. Blood workup was significant for thrombocytopenia to 50,000/μL (normal range 229–597×103/mL), lymphopenia to 1.04×103/μL (normal range 2.14–8.99×103/μL), anemia with hemoglobin to 7.1g/dL (normal range 8.9–12.7g/dL), and hypoalbuminemia (albumin to 2 g/dL, normal range 2.3–4.8g/dL). Echocardiogram showed a small patent foramen ovale. He died at 9 weeks of an acute respiratory decompensation, severe restrictive lung disease, and shock. Blood, urine, and bronchoalveolar lavage cultures were negative.Individual II was born at 36 weeks gestation. His neonatal period was complicated by failure to thrive, neonatal apnea, seizures, and severe gastroesophageal reflux and feeding difficulties necessitating a Nissen fundoplication and G-tube placement. He died at 6 months of age from acute respiratory failure. The clinical course shared between the two affected maternal half-brothers included feeding difficulty, failure to thrive, recurrent apnea, chronic lung disease, and recurrent aspirations with respiratory failure as the terminal event. Shared clinical findings included congenital ichthyosis, microphthalmia, choroidal colobomas, optic nerve hypoplasia, absent septum pellucidum, ventriculomegaly, platyspondyly, short fingers with metacarpal cupping. Interestingly, both had apparent paucity and/or sclerosis of portal vein branches identified on postmortem examination. While individual II presented persistent microcephaly, individual I presented progressive increase in head size. Postmortem exam confirmed severe aqueductal stenosis and ventriculomegaly with small brain size (Table 1).Individual I had a normal prenatal chromosomal microarray. Trio exome sequencing performed postnatally identified a hemizygous variant of uncertain significance in the last exon (11/11) of GPKOW (c.1329dupG:p.Ser444GlufsTer28). His mother, Individual II, and three maternal sisters were all found to carry the same variant. The mother presented with short stature, history of thrombocytopenia, and has a diagnosis of multiple sclerosis. One of the sisters presented with a history of progressive vision loss, short stature (−2.4 SD) and a head circumference in the 5th percentile. The other two maternal half-sisters carrying the variant were asymptomatic. p.(Ser444GlufsTer28) results in an exchange of the last 33 amino acids with 28 other amino acids and a premature protein termination. The mutant mRNA is predicted to escape non-sense mediated decay (NMDEscPredictor12). This variant was not reported in gnomAD13 or ClinVar14 and is predicted to be pathogenic on VarSome15. DiscussionGPKOW is located on Xp11.13 and encodes a G-patch domain containing protein, with one G patch domain and two KOW (Kyrpides, Ouzounis, and Woese) motifs (Fig.1B). There is limited information about GPKOW, but it does appear to localize to spliceosomes and is involved in pre-mRNA splicing2. Prior to this study, there is only one family described in the literature with a pathogenic variant in GPKOW. In 2017, Carroll et al described a splicing variant c.331+5G>A in GPKOW in a multi-generational family associated with male perinatally lethal microcephaly and IUGR5. The phenotype also included holoprosencephaly, hydranencephaly, edema, limb contractures, and hypoplastic lungs28. Heterozygote females were suggested to present a milder phenotype of microcephaly and short stature, but X-chromosome inactivation studies were inconclusive. The variant was experimentally shown to result in abnormal splicing. Maternal lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCL) clones expressing only the c.331+5G>A variant showed reduced expression of the full-length GPKOW protein and produced a protein of shorter size that was not found in control samples. Because the shorter protein is predicted to lack part of the G-patch domain (amino acids 59–189) which is essential for GPKOW to interact with DHX16, the authors proposed a dominant negative model of pathogenicity.Both frameshift variants presented here are in the final exon and are not expected to trigger NMD. These variants alter the tail of the protein. p.(Arg441SerfsTer30) disrupts the 3rd barrel structure which is the KOW 2 domain (amino acids 415–442) and both variants disrupt two phosphorylation sites: 471 phosphoserine, and 473 phosphothreonine. We show that mRNA with the variant p.(Ser444GlufsTer28) escapes NMD and mRNA levels in Individual I are comparable to the levels in controls. Yet, the protein levels in fibroblasts are significantly lower than that of controls, similar to what was seen in the LCL clones carrying the c.331+5G>A variant. Inhibition of protease activity with MG132 led to increase in protein levels, suggesting that the mutated protein is not stable. In addition, based on Drosophila experiments in which we forced the expression of reference and variant human GPKOW, we also concluded that the p.(Ser444GlufsTer28) variant behaves as a hypomorphic allele, even when this protein is expressed at the same level as the reference protein. Therefore, the reduced protein stability and reduced biological activity both support partial loss-of-function mechanism of pathogenicity.Based on both human and fly data, GPKOW is a novel crucial player in the development of the eye and brain. Interestingly, pathogenic variants in other genes that physically interact with GPKOW are also associated with rare diseases that affect the eye and brain. GPKOW is part of the Bact complex that contains other proteins such as RNF11329. RNF113, encoded by RNF113A, is an RNA-binding protein which regulates the splicing of multiple candidates involved in cell survival. Pathogenic loss-of-function variants in this gene are associated with X-linked trichothiodystrophy (OMIM #300953) that presents with IUGR, short stature, brittle hair, dry skin, progressive microcephaly, as well as brain and eye abnormalities30. Considering the close molecular link and significant overlap with the neurologic and ophthalmologic phenotype in our patients, the congenital ichthyosis phenotype in our patients may also be related to GPKOW function and presents a phenotypic expansion. PRP2, encoded by DHX16, and PRP16, encoded by DHX38, are two other proteins that have been reported to physically interact with GPKOW21,2. Pathogenic variants in these genes are also associated with Mendelian conditions involving the eye and the brain. DHX16 is associated with neuromuscular disease and ocular or auditory anomalies with or without seizures (NMOAS, OMIM #618733) and DHX38 is associated with the autosomal recessive retinitis pigmentosa 84 (OMIM#618220). As these genes are also evolutionarily conserved, Drosophila could be potentially used to investigate the functional consequences of variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in these and other related genes17 and could be further applied to studying the in vivo function of the minor spliceosome complex, which is significantly understudied compared to the major spliceosome complex31.Overall, our work supports GPKOW as a novel disease-associated gene with C-terminal frameshift variants leading to a multisystemic disorder via partial loss-of-function mechanism.
