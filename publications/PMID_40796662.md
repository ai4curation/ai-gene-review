In vivo CRISPR screens in CD8+ T cells have previously uncovered targets for
cancer immunotherapy; however, a minority of the genome has been individually
annotated, suggesting that additional regulators remain to be discovered. Here
we assessed 899 genes in CD8+ T cells responding to murine melanoma and
identified the E3 ubiquitin ligase STUB1 as a new negative regulator of
anti-tumor CD8+ T cell function. We demonstrated that Stub1 knockout CD8+ T
cells effectively control tumor growth across multiple murine models.
Mechanistically, STUB1 interacts with the adapter protein CHIC2 to regulate
cytokine receptor expression in mouse and human CD8+ T cells. Among the
regulated cytokine receptors, interleukin-27 receptor α is essential for tumor
growth control mediated by Stub1/Chic2 knockout CD8+ T cells. Together, these
findings establish the STUB1-CHIC2 complex as a regulator of cytokine receptor
expression in CD8+ T cells and provide rationale for inhibiting this pathway to
enhance CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity.

Immunotherapies, such as immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), have
revolutionized the treatment of cancer, leading to durable responses in multiple
cancer types1,2. However, over 50% of patients either fail to
respond to ICB or experience relapse3; thus, to extend clinical benefit to more patients, there is a
critical need to identify novel regulators of tumor immunity for use as
monotherapies or in combination with ICB. CD8+ T lymphocytes are a major

functional impairment correlates with cancer progression4,5. This
highlights their importance as a key population to study for discovering new
immunotherapy targets. Anti-tumor CD8+ T cell responses are influenced by
spatial, metabolic, cellular, and cytokine cues, which are difficult to model in
vitro6,7. Consequently, in vivo CRISPR screens have
been employed in murine models of cancer to simultaneously examine thousands of
putative regulators of CD8+ T cell responses to tumors in the complex
tumor microenvironment8–15. Notably, these published screens
have utilized CD8+ T cells at different stages of differentiation
(naive14 or
activated8–13,15).
It is unclear whether the initial differentiation state of the CD8+ T
cells or the timing and conditions under which they receive initial TCR stimulation,

anchoring their genome-wide screen with an established negative regulator, which may
have affected the hits recovered9;
or (3) the unsuccessful recovery of the majority of genome-targeting gRNAs following
cell engraftment8. Thus, a minority
of genes in the genome have been individually annotated by these published screens,
suggesting that many additional regulators of CD8+ T cells remain to be
discovered.

screens, one in naive and the other in activated CD8+ T cells, to
discover new regulators of CD8+ T cell responses in tdLNs and tumors. We
identified the E3 ubiquitin ligase, STUB1, as a novel negative regulator of
CD8+ T cells in both screens. Genetic deletion of
Stub1 improves CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor
immunity in multiple murine cancer models. Mechanistically, we found that STUB1, in

and IL-27Rα on both murine and human CD8+ T cells, and

To identify the substrates of STUB1 in primary CD8+ T cells, we
performed mass spectrometry of ubiquitinated peptides from in vitro-activated
control and Stub1 KO OT-1 CD8+ T cells. A peptide
from CHIC2 was the most differentially ubiquitinated peptide, with greater
ubiquitination in Stub1-expressing (control) vs.
Stub1 KO CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 4). CHIC2 is a
transmembrane protein with no known enzymatic function but has been identified
as a scaffolding partner of STUB1 in targeting CSF2RB16. CHIC2 interacts with STUB1 through its
IFRPD motif (last 5 amino acids in its C-terminus). To confirm the physical
interaction between STUB1 and CHIC2 in T cells, we generated EL4 cells
expressing either V5-tagged wild-type CHIC2 or a CHIC2 mutant lacking the IFRPD
motif (V5-Chic2 ΔIFRPD) (Fig. 4b). V5 immunoprecipitation followed by Western blotting for
STUB1 (Fig. 4c) revealed that wild-type
CHIC2 could co-precipitate STUB1 but CHIC2 ΔIFRPD could not. These
results indicate that (1) STUB1 and CHIC2 interact in T cells and (2) this
interaction requires the IFRPD motif.

Similarly to Stub1, Chic2 was
expressed at comparable levels in both naive CD8+ T cells and
tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Based on our immunoprecipitation data, we
hypothesized that STUB1 and CHIC2 may form a complex in CD8+ T cells
and this complex may regulate CD8+ T cell functions. Thus, we
evaluated whether Chic2 KO CD8+ T cells would
phenocopy Stub1 KO CD8+ T cells in vivo. We examined
the abundance of Chic2 KO CD8+ T cells (Extended Data Fig. 4b) in the tdLN and tumor
relative to control, Stub1 KO, and Stub1/Chic2
double KO (DKO) cells. To study these four populations simultaneously, we used
CD8+ OT-1 T cells expressing unique combinations of CD45.1 and
CD45.2 isoforms as well as THY1.1 and THY1.2 isoforms (Fig. 4d). Analysis of Stub1 KO,
Chic2 KO, and Stub1/Chic2 DKO
CD8+ T cell populations showed an approximately 1.5-fold
expansion in the tdLN and tumor relative to the initial frequency for each cell
type, with a decrease in the relative representation of control CD8+
T cells (Fig. 4e,f). Notably, Stub1 KO,
Chic2 KO, and Stub1/Chic2 DKO cells were
equivalently enriched, suggesting a non-additive effect of STUB1 and CHIC2,
which may be due to their formation of a protein complex or regulation of the
same pathway in CD8+ T cells.

We next evaluated the impact of Chic2 KO on
CD8+ T cell differentiation and function. Chic2
KO phenocopied the effects of Stub1 KO, exhibiting similar
skewing of exhausted subsets (Fig. 4g and
Extended Data Fig. 4c), enhanced
effector functions (Fig. 4h and Extended Data Fig. 4d), and decreased
features of exhaustion (Fig. 4i and Extended Data Fig. 4e–g). To evaluate how these changes impact B16-OVA tumor
growth control, we adoptively transferred control, Chic2 KO,
Stub1 KO, or Chic2/Stub1 DKO OT-1
CD8+ T cells into mice that we subsequently injected with B16-OVA
tumor cells. Chic2 KO, Stub1 KO, and
Chic2/Stub1 DKO CD8+ T cells all significantly
attenuated tumor growth to a comparable extent (Fig. 4j and Extended Data Fig.
4h). Together, these studies demonstrate that STUB1 and CHIC2
physically interact in T cells and that loss of Chic2
phenocopies loss of Stub1 by increasing CD8+ T cell
abundance, effector functions, and tumor growth control. The finding that dual
KO of Stub1 and Chic2 does not further enhance
CD8+ T cell expansion or tumor control compared to individual
gene KO suggests that a STUB1-CHIC2 complex may function to restrain
CD8+ T cell responses to tumors.

DiscussionPublished in vivo CD8+ T cell CRISPR screens have utilized naive
and in vitro-activated CD8+ T cells, but there has not been a direct
comparison between these two approaches8–15. Here we
performed parallel CRISPR-based loss-of-function screens targeting the same set of
genes in both naive and activated CD8+ T cells, to uncover new regulators
of anti-tumor CD8+ T cell responses. We identified STUB1 as a negative
regulator of CD8+ T cell responses to tumors in both screens. We found
that STUB1, in complex with CHIC2, regulates the expression of multiple cytokine
receptors on CD8+ T cells, including IL-27Rα, which is required
for enhanced anti-tumor CD8+ T cell responses. These findings highlight
STUB1 and CHIC2 as promising cancer immunotherapy targets and identify a novel
mechanism by which cytokine sensing is modulated in CD8+ T cells.Although most CRISPR-based screens have utilized in vitro-activated
CD8+ T cells, we hypothesized that this approach may introduce
important biases against T cell priming41,42 by (1) preventing
interrogation of genes essential for priming and early differentiation, (2)
bypassing physiologic activation in the tdLN, and (3) circumventing immunological
synapse formation43. Here, we
directly compared screens in naive and in vitro-activated CD8+ T cells.
Regulators of CD8+ T cell abundance in the tumor were correlated between
the two screens, and top regulators, such as Stub1,
Ptpn2, Pdcd1, Tap1, and
B2m, were recovered from both screens. Given activated
CD8+ T cell screens are easier to perform, if a screener’s
main objective is to identify regulators of CD8+ T cell abundance in the
tumor, an activated CD8+ T cell screen should be the preferred option.
Under which circumstances would a naive CD8+ T cell screen be preferred?
Compared to the activated CD8+ T cell screen, the naive CD8+ T
cell screen identified the known functions of several genes important for T cell
activation, priming, and trafficking (e.g., Lck23, Junb24, Icam125, Tnfaip321, Usp2244, Cxcr326, Cxcr527, and
Cx3cr128)
in the tdLN vs. input comparison. Interestingly, these genes either had no effect, a
weak effect, or the opposite effect in the activated CD8+ T cell screen.
We hypothesize that the naive screen’s ability to recover these genes in the
tdLN vs. input comparison might be due to a more physiologic differentiation
trajectory compared with the activated CD8+ T screening system, which
incorporates an in vitro activation step. Notably, TCR stimulation is sufficient to
initiate multiple cell divisions in the absence of additional antigen
exposure45, suggesting
that the activated screening approach may miss the window for impacting TCR-driven
responses in the tdLN. Thus, naive CD8+ T cell screens are more suited
for discovering regulators of CD8+ T cell priming and
differentiation.E3 ligases modulate several target proteins, making it challenging to
definitively attribute their functional impact to a single target protein. STUB1
targets multiple proteins for degradation16,29,30,36,46, including ATAXIN-1, CSF2RB,
CARMA1, FOXP3, and IFNγR1, suggesting its inhibitory effects may be cell
type- and target-specific. Here we identified IL-27Rα as a STUB1 target and
demonstrated that IL-27Rα is necessary for Stub1 KO and
Chic2 KO CD8+ T cell accumulation within B16-OVA
tumors and resulting tumor growth control. However, IL-27Rα has
CD8+ T cell-intrinsic functions, potentially masking effects of STUB1
on other target proteins. Thus, our findings do not exclude the possibility that
STUB1 may exert important functional effects on additional target proteins in
CD8+ T cells. IL-27 has been shown to both enhance47 and inhibit tumor
immunity48, which may be
influenced by the tumor type and cellular contexts. We previously demonstrated a
CD8+ T cell-intrinsic role for IL-27Rα in enhancing anti-tumor
CD8+ T cell responses14, supporting our current findings that increased IL-27 signaling
promotes CD8+ T cell immunity. Moreover, a recent study also showed that
IL-27 enhances CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity in cancer and that therapeutic
agonism of the IL-27 receptor can promote anti-tumor immunity49. Given that Stub1 KO and
Chic2 KO increase IL-27Rα expression on CD8+
T cells, inhibiting STUB1 or CHIC2 could serve as an alternative approach to
therapeutically enhance IL-27 signaling and improve anti-tumor immunity. Beyond
immune cells, STUB1 also regulates immune signaling pathways in tumor cells.
Previous studies have shown that STUB1 targets IFNγR1 in tumor cells, and
Stub1 KO tumor cells have increased susceptibility to
immune-mediated attack29–31. Collectively, these findings
highlight STUB1 as a particularly potent immunotherapy target with potential to
simultaneously enhance CD8+ T cell function and increase tumor cell
vulnerability to CD8+ T cell killing.CD8+ T cell cytokine signaling is regulated in multiple ways,
including through dynamic expression of cytokines or their receptors, attenuation of
downstream signaling, competition for cytokines, and degradation of cytokine
receptors50. We identified
STUB1 as a novel mediator of cytokine receptor downregulation in CD8+ T
cells. Given STUB1 and CHIC2 have previously been shown to regulate the cytokine
receptor CSF2RB on myeloid cells16, the STUB1-CHIC2 complex may regulate cytokine signaling broadly in
immune cells, thereby influencing the magnitude of cytokine responses in different
cell types in the immune system. Intriguingly, our data indicate that STUB1 does not
regulate all cytokine receptors in CD8+ T cells. It is unclear what
governs the selectivity of the STUB1-CHIC2 complex for some cytokine receptors and
not others. Our findings suggest that spatial proximity at the cell membrane may be
one important mechanism by which cytokine receptors are “selected”.
Since we did not find a direct physical interaction between CHIC2 and either
IL-27Rα or IFNγR1, further work is needed to determine the mechanisms
that govern this selectivity. Furthermore, it is unclear if STUB1-mediated
regulation of cytokine receptors is dynamically controlled. CSF2RB16 and IFNγR130 are both regulated by STUB1 in a
ligand-independent manner, raising the possibility that STUB1 may regulate cytokine
receptors constitutively or, alternatively, that its regulatory effects are
controlled by an as yet unidentified mechanism.In summary, our parallel screens (1) identified multiple novel regulators of
CD8+ T cell responses to cancer and (2) provided the first direct
comparison between naive and activated CD8+ T cell screening approaches.
Our findings indicate that inhibiting STUB1 may enhance anti-tumor immunity and
uncover a previously unrecognized role for STUB1 in controlling cytokine receptor
expression in CD8+ T cells.

Published in vivo CD8+ T cell CRISPR screens have utilized naive
and in vitro-activated CD8+ T cells, but there has not been a direct
comparison between these two approaches8–15. Here we
performed parallel CRISPR-based loss-of-function screens targeting the same set of
genes in both naive and activated CD8+ T cells, to uncover new regulators
of anti-tumor CD8+ T cell responses. We identified STUB1 as a negative
regulator of CD8+ T cell responses to tumors in both screens. We found
that STUB1, in complex with CHIC2, regulates the expression of multiple cytokine
receptors on CD8+ T cells, including IL-27Rα, which is required
for enhanced anti-tumor CD8+ T cell responses. These findings highlight
STUB1 and CHIC2 as promising cancer immunotherapy targets and identify a novel
mechanism by which cytokine sensing is modulated in CD8+ T cells.

Although most CRISPR-based screens have utilized in vitro-activated
CD8+ T cells, we hypothesized that this approach may introduce
important biases against T cell priming41,42 by (1) preventing
interrogation of genes essential for priming and early differentiation, (2)
bypassing physiologic activation in the tdLN, and (3) circumventing immunological
synapse formation43. Here, we
directly compared screens in naive and in vitro-activated CD8+ T cells.
Regulators of CD8+ T cell abundance in the tumor were correlated between
the two screens, and top regulators, such as Stub1,
Ptpn2, Pdcd1, Tap1, and
B2m, were recovered from both screens. Given activated
CD8+ T cell screens are easier to perform, if a screener’s
main objective is to identify regulators of CD8+ T cell abundance in the
tumor, an activated CD8+ T cell screen should be the preferred option.
Under which circumstances would a naive CD8+ T cell screen be preferred?
Compared to the activated CD8+ T cell screen, the naive CD8+ T
cell screen identified the known functions of several genes important for T cell
activation, priming, and trafficking (e.g., Lck23, Junb24, Icam125, Tnfaip321, Usp2244, Cxcr326, Cxcr527, and
Cx3cr128)
in the tdLN vs. input comparison. Interestingly, these genes either had no effect, a
weak effect, or the opposite effect in the activated CD8+ T cell screen.
We hypothesize that the naive screen’s ability to recover these genes in the
tdLN vs. input comparison might be due to a more physiologic differentiation
trajectory compared with the activated CD8+ T screening system, which
incorporates an in vitro activation step. Notably, TCR stimulation is sufficient to
initiate multiple cell divisions in the absence of additional antigen
exposure45, suggesting
that the activated screening approach may miss the window for impacting TCR-driven
responses in the tdLN. Thus, naive CD8+ T cell screens are more suited
for discovering regulators of CD8+ T cell priming and
differentiation.

E3 ligases modulate several target proteins, making it challenging to
definitively attribute their functional impact to a single target protein. STUB1
targets multiple proteins for degradation16,29,30,36,46, including ATAXIN-1, CSF2RB,
CARMA1, FOXP3, and IFNγR1, suggesting its inhibitory effects may be cell
type- and target-specific. Here we identified IL-27Rα as a STUB1 target and
demonstrated that IL-27Rα is necessary for Stub1 KO and
Chic2 KO CD8+ T cell accumulation within B16-OVA
tumors and resulting tumor growth control. However, IL-27Rα has
CD8+ T cell-intrinsic functions, potentially masking effects of STUB1
on other target proteins. Thus, our findings do not exclude the possibility that
STUB1 may exert important functional effects on additional target proteins in
CD8+ T cells. IL-27 has been shown to both enhance47 and inhibit tumor
immunity48, which may be
influenced by the tumor type and cellular contexts. We previously demonstrated a
CD8+ T cell-intrinsic role for IL-27Rα in enhancing anti-tumor
CD8+ T cell responses14, supporting our current findings that increased IL-27 signaling
promotes CD8+ T cell immunity. Moreover, a recent study also showed that
IL-27 enhances CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity in cancer and that therapeutic
agonism of the IL-27 receptor can promote anti-tumor immunity49. Given that Stub1 KO and
Chic2 KO increase IL-27Rα expression on CD8+
T cells, inhibiting STUB1 or CHIC2 could serve as an alternative approach to
therapeutically enhance IL-27 signaling and improve anti-tumor immunity. Beyond
immune cells, STUB1 also regulates immune signaling pathways in tumor cells.
Previous studies have shown that STUB1 targets IFNγR1 in tumor cells, and
Stub1 KO tumor cells have increased susceptibility to
immune-mediated attack29–31. Collectively, these findings
highlight STUB1 as a particularly potent immunotherapy target with potential to
simultaneously enhance CD8+ T cell function and increase tumor cell
vulnerability to CD8+ T cell killing.

CD8+ T cell cytokine signaling is regulated in multiple ways,
including through dynamic expression of cytokines or their receptors, attenuation of
downstream signaling, competition for cytokines, and degradation of cytokine
receptors50. We identified
STUB1 as a novel mediator of cytokine receptor downregulation in CD8+ T
cells. Given STUB1 and CHIC2 have previously been shown to regulate the cytokine
receptor CSF2RB on myeloid cells16, the STUB1-CHIC2 complex may regulate cytokine signaling broadly in
immune cells, thereby influencing the magnitude of cytokine responses in different
cell types in the immune system. Intriguingly, our data indicate that STUB1 does not
regulate all cytokine receptors in CD8+ T cells. It is unclear what
governs the selectivity of the STUB1-CHIC2 complex for some cytokine receptors and
not others. Our findings suggest that spatial proximity at the cell membrane may be
one important mechanism by which cytokine receptors are “selected”.
Since we did not find a direct physical interaction between CHIC2 and either
IL-27Rα or IFNγR1, further work is needed to determine the mechanisms
that govern this selectivity. Furthermore, it is unclear if STUB1-mediated
regulation of cytokine receptors is dynamically controlled. CSF2RB16 and IFNγR130 are both regulated by STUB1 in a
ligand-independent manner, raising the possibility that STUB1 may regulate cytokine
receptors constitutively or, alternatively, that its regulatory effects are
controlled by an as yet unidentified mechanism.

In summary, our parallel screens (1) identified multiple novel regulators of
CD8+ T cell responses to cancer and (2) provided the first direct
comparison between naive and activated CD8+ T cell screening approaches.
Our findings indicate that inhibiting STUB1 may enhance anti-tumor immunity and
uncover a previously unrecognized role for STUB1 in controlling cytokine receptor
expression in CD8+ T cells.
