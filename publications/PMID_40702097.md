Oxidative stress is a major microbicidal mechanism of phagocytes causing
molecular damages, including DNA-protein crosslinks (DPCs), toxic DNA lesions
that can lead to genomic instability. DPC repair depends on Wss1 and Ddi1
proteases, so they may be critical for fungal survival in hosts, yet their roles
have not been fully elucidated. In this study, the roles of Wss1 and Ddi1 under
oxidative stress were examined in the model yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae and
Candida albicans, an important human fungal pathogen. Direct measurements of
DPCs using SDS/KCl precipitation showed that oxidative stress inducers,
including hydrogen peroxide, sodium hypochlorite, menadione and plumbagin,
increased DPC levels in a dose-dependent manner in both species. S. cerevisiae
and C. albicans lacking Wss1 and Ddi1 are hypersensitive to oxidative stress
inducers. Complementation assays demonstrated that catalytic activity of CaWss1
is essential, while the interactions with Cdc48 and SUMO are important but not
absolutely required. Importantly, CaWss1 and CaDdi1 play partially redundant
roles for resistance to macrophage killing, with CaWss1 being more dominant. In
conclusion, Wss1 and Ddi1 in both S. cerevisiae and C. albicans are required for
survival under oxidative stress, and this may be critical for C. albicans to
evade phagocytic killing and establish an infection.

Introduction

Fungal infections have become a major public health threat worldwide, especially among the expanding populations of predisposed or immunocompromised individuals, and with increasing prevalence of antifungal resistance1. The World Health Organization has recently developed the first fungal pathogens priority list to raise awareness and drive research and policies to tackle this important problem2. Candida albicans is among the four species listed in the critical (top) priority, as it causes high burden of mucocutaneous diseases in immunocompromised patients and invasive infections in at-risk populations with high mortality rates1,3. Understanding the mechanisms of fungal pathogenesis in the host is an important step towards developing novel antifungal strategies.

To initiate infections, C. albicans have to survive host immune defense, particularly the microbicidal mechanisms of phagocytes. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced by phagocytes can cause several types of molecular damage, especially DNA damages including base oxidation, DNA breaks, DNA strand crosslinks, and possibly covalent bonds between DNA bases and proteins, leading to DNA-protein crosslinks (DPCs)4,5. ROS, specifically hydroxyl radical (OH•), can induce DPCs through OH• addition or abstraction of hydrogen atom at DNA bases or amino acids, leading to crosslinking of nearby DNA and protein6. DPCs are extremely toxic because they block the passage of DNA interacting enzymes, such as helicases or DNA/RNA polymerases, resulting in stalled replication or transcription, and genomic instability7. Two major classes of DPCs are enzymatic and non-enzymatic DPCs, formed by trapping of covalent DNA-enzyme intermediates (such as DNA topoisomerase I cleavage complex, Top1CC), and by chemical crosslinking of DNA-interacting proteins to DNA (such as those induced by formaldehyde, FA), respectively8.

To survive ROS-induced stress in host phagocytes, C. albicans requires efficient DNA repair mechanisms for various types of damages. Previous studies demonstrated the important roles of Wss1 and Ddi1 in the repair of both enzymatic and non-enzymatic DPCs9–11 in the model yeast, S. cerevisiae. Recently, we have demonstrated similar roles of C. albicans Wss1 in DPC repair12 but the role of Ddi1 in C. albicans has not been investigated. Importantly, while ROS potentially induce DPCs, the roles of DPC repair proteases in oxidative stress inducing conditions have not been formally established in both S. cerevisiae and C. albicans.

In this study, we examined the role of Wss1 and Ddi1 in S. cerevisiae and C. albicans under oxidative stress. We showed that DPCs are induced by oxidative stress inducers and that Wss1 and Ddi1 play partially redundant roles, with Wss1 being more dominant, for cell survival and DPC repair under these conditions. Moreover, our results also showed that Wss1 and Ddi1 are important for C. albicans survival in macrophages, suggesting their involvement in pathogenicity.

Additionally, our results indicate that the catalytic activity of CaWss1 is essential for its function, while the interactions with Cdc48 and SUMO also contribute to CaWss1 function under oxidative stress. Notably, we provided evidence that Wss1 and Ddi1 are required for C. albicans survival from macrophage killing, suggesting that these proteases contribute to the ability of C. albicans to survive within the host and cause infections.Our results showed that treatments with oxidative stress inducers led to elevated levels of DPCs in a dose-dependent manner that associated with the decrease in survival in both fungal species (Fig. 1). This suggests that ROS can indeed induce DPC formation. The mechanisms of DPC formation by ROS, especially hydroxyl radical (OH•), are likely through the generation of radical adducts through OH• addition or abstraction of H atom from DNA bases, sugar backbone, or amino acid sidechains6. Superoxide anion (O2•–) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) may induce DPCs through the generation of OH• via Fenton reaction of transition metal ions4,15,16. Additionally, in the presence of chloride ion (Cl−), H2O2 can also generate a hypochlorous acid (HOCl), which can react with amino acid residues and DNA bases, generating chloramines or protein/nucleobase-derived radicals. These radicals can cause DPCs by crosslinking with other DNA or protein17,18. HOCl is a potent microbicide generated via myeloperoxidase from neutrophils and was shown to induce histone-DNA crosslinks19,20. Among the inducers that were used in this study, H2O2 and NaOCl are strong oxidizing agents that rapidly induce reactive radicals, while MD and PB undergo redox cycling to generate O2•–, H2O2 and OH•, with slower kinetics of effects16. This difference may partly explain the differences in the sensitivities, the doses of the reagents that confer toxicity to the cells, and DPC levels among these inducers as observed in this study. As the prevalence of antifungal drug resistance is rising, novel strategies for antifungal agents are in great need29. Although speculative at this stage, DPC repair pathways may offer a promising area for antifungal target exploration, as fungi could be less likely to develop resistance through target mutation due to its critical role in maintaining genome stability30. Furthermore, the fungal Wss1 is distinct from its human counterpart, SPRTN, particularly in the C-terminal region, suggesting different regulatory mechanisms that could be exploited for fungal-specific drug development31,32. Specifically, PARylation initiates the DPC repair process and influences replication fork stability through ubiquitination signaling, while SUMOylation provides a broader range of DPC targets, including non-replicative regions37. The promoter regions of ScWSS1 and ScDDI1 share common binding sites for Yap1, a key oxidative stress regulator, and Stb5, a regulator of multidrug resistance and oxidative stress response27. While ScWSS1 promoter contains binding sites for Cad1 (Yap2), ScDDI1 promoter includes binding sites for Skn7, Msn2, and Msn426. The presence of binding sites for multiple stress-response regulators within the promoter region of ScDDI1 may allow for a more robust response to oxidative stress. For C. albicans, the highest expression of CaWSS1 and CaDDI1 was found in response to H2O2. This may be due to the presence of binding sites for Skn7, a transcription factor essential for H2O2 resistance in C. albicans, in the promoter regions of these genes26. In contrast, ScWSS1 and ScDDI1 showed higher expression in response to MD and PB. These different expression patterns between S. cerevisiae and C. albicans suggest distinct transcriptional regulatory circuits in the two organisms that remain to be further investigated.

Importantly, the increased sensitivity to oxidative stress inducers of C. albicans lacking Wss1 and Ddi1 (Fig. 3) also translate into an increased susceptibility to macrophage killing (Fig. 6). Macrophages are among the main phagocytes responsible for host defense against C. albicans by releasing a large amount of ROS5,28. The increased susceptibility of the mutants to macrophage killing further supports the important roles of DPC repair pathways for C. albicans survival in host cells and suggests that these DPC repair proteases could represent potential targets for antifungal strategies. As the prevalence of antifungal drug resistance is rising, novel strategies for antifungal agents are in great need29. Although speculative at this stage, DPC repair pathways may offer a promising area for antifungal target exploration, as fungi could be less likely to develop resistance through target mutation due to its critical role in maintaining genome stability30. Furthermore, the fungal Wss1 is distinct from its human counterpart, SPRTN, particularly in the C-terminal region, suggesting different regulatory mechanisms that could be exploited for fungal-specific drug development31,32. Human SPRTN contains a ubiquitin-binding motif, whereas C. albicans Wss1 has SUMO-binding motifs25,33. Nevertheless, while SPRTN and human homologue of Ddi1 both primarily bind to ubiquitin for their functions34,35, SPRTN was recently reported to interact with SUMO-conjugated non-enzymatic DPCs7. Understanding the similarities and differences in the regulation and mechanisms of DPC repair pathways between fungal and human systems will be essential for development of future antifungal strategies.

Furthermore, recent studies have identified additional players in DPC repair. CSA and CSB, proteins involved in the transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair (TC-NER), but not SPRTN, were shown to be the major repair mechanism for stalled RNA polymerase II at DPC sites during transcription36. Another study showed that PARylation by PARP1 at DPC sites triggers ubiquitination and proteasome-dependent DPC repair in CPT-mediated Top1CCs in Xenopus egg37. The preference of TC-NER repair pathway over SPRTN in transcribed genes and the discovery of novel post-translational modification (PTM), PARylation in Top1CC repair suggest additional layers of cross-talk, particularly among the PTMs in the repair of DPCs. Specifically, PARylation initiates the DPC repair process and influences replication fork stability through ubiquitination signaling, while SUMOylation provides a broader range of DPC targets, including non-replicative regions37. Further research into the various post-translational modifications of DPCs and differential recruitment of repair pathways will be key to understand the intricate regulatory mechanisms of DPC repair.

Additionally, it is also important to investigate the responses of phagocytes to fungi, as they are the first line of defense against infections5,28. A recent study using a novel live-cell imaging assay revealed that C. albicans can escape from macrophages through candidalysin and the activation of pyroptosis and ETosis38. C. albicans can also induce NETosis and inhibits neutrophil function39,40. Thus, further examinations from both the fungal and immune cell perspectives will be necessary to better understand Candida infections and devise effective approaches against the pathogen.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated the crucial roles of Wss1 and Ddi1 in both S. cerevisiae and C. albicans in cell survival under oxidative stress. This is also critical for C. albicans survival from macrophage killing. Our results suggest that Wss1 and Ddi1 proteases are likely important for C. albicans pathogenicity.

Cells were then resuspended in spheroplast buffer containing 0.3 U of Zymolyase, 20 T (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) and incubated at 30ºC for 20 min. Spheroplasts were washed with 1 M sorbitol and lysed with M buffer; 5.6 M guanidine thiocyanate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.5, 20 mM EDTA (Affymetrix USB, Ohio, USA), 4% Triton-100 (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), 1% Sarkosyl (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), and 1% dithiothreitol. The lysate was sonicated at 40% duty cycle for 30 s for 10 cycles with the Vibra-Cell™ ultrasonic processor (Sonics and Materials Inc., Newtown, USA), 1 volume of ice-cold absolute ethanol were added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm, 4ºC for 20 min to precipitate DNA. The DNA pellet was washed in washing buffer; 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.5, 150 mM NaCl (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and 50% ice-cold absolute ethanol and samples can be stored at −80ºC or proceeded directly in the next step. The DNA/DPC pellet was dissolved in a pre-warmed SDS buffer; 1% SDS (PanReac AppliChem, Darmstadt, Germany) and 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, and incubated at 42ºC for 6 min. The same volume of KCl buffer; 200 mM KCl (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5 was added, chilled on ice for 6 min, and followed by centrifugation at 14,600 rpm, 4ºC for 5 min to precipitate the SDS-DPCs complex. Supernatant (total DNA) was collected into a new tube for DNA measurement. The DPC pellet was washed in 1.5 ml of KCl buffer; 100 mM KCl, and 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, incubated at 55ºC for 10 min, on ice for 6 min and centrifuged at 14,600 rpm, 4ºC for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and combined with the previous step for DNA measurement. The washing step was repeated again before dissolving the DPC pellet in 1 ml of proteinase K buffer; 100 mM KCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, and 10 mM EDTA, containing 200 µg/ml proteinase K (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) and incubated at 55ºC for 45 min to digest the proteins. The digestion reaction was then chilled on ice for 6 min and centrifuged at 14,600 rpm, 4ºC for 10 min. Supernatant (containing DPC-associated DNA) was collected and DNA was quantified using the Quant-iT™ PicoGreen dsDNA assay kit (Invitrogen by Thermo Fisher Scientific, Eugene, USA). The level of DPCs is expressed as the ratio of total amount of DPC-associated DNA over total DNA in each sample. All experiments were performed at least 3 times.

Macrophage culture growth conditions and killing assayThe RAW264.7 murine macrophage cell line (ATCC TIB-71) (kindly provided by Assoc. Prof. Mayuree Fuangthong, CGI, CRA) was cultured and maintained in RPMI1640 with phenol red (Gibco, Grand Island, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) at 37ºC. Fungal viability was assessed using the modified end-point dilution assay in which the metabolic activity of fungal cells was measured by thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT) (Abcam, Cambridge, UK)47. RAW264.7 cells were seeded at the density of 2.5 × 104 cells/ml in a 96-well plate and incubated overnight at 37ºC in 5% CO2 atmosphere. C. albicans cells were washed twice in PBS, sonicated briefly and resuspended in fresh RPMI1640 without phenol red (Gibco, Grand Island, USA). Approximately 1 × 104
C. albicans cells were added into the wells with or without macrophages and serially diluted at 1:2 ratio for a total of seven times. After 24 h of incubation at 37ºC in 5% CO2 atmosphere, microcolonies in each well were photographed using Olympus inverted microscope with a AM4025X Dino-Eye. Media were then carefully removed and macrophages were lysed with sterile distilled water for 30 min. MTT (0.5 mg/ml) was added in each well and incubated at 37ºC in 5% CO2 atmosphere for 1 h. The formazan crystals in each well were dissolved in DMSO (PanReac AppliChem, Darmstadt, Germany) and appropriate dilutions were performed for reading the absorbance at 570 nm (OD570) in a microplate reader. Fungal survival was presented as the percentage of OD570 of fungal microcolonies with lysed macrophages over fungal microcolonies without macrophages. The experiments were performed six independent times.
