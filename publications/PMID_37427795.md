Abstract

The Drosophila melanogaster protein Glorund (Glo) represses nanos ( nos ) translation and uses its quasi-RNA recognition motifs (qRRMs) to recognize both G-tract and structured UA-rich motifs within the nos translational control element (TCE). We showed previously that each of the three qRRMs is multifunctional, capable of binding to G-tract and UA-rich motifs, yet if and how the qRRMs combine to recognize the nos TCE remained unclear. Here we determined solution structures of a nos TCEI_III RNA containing the G-tract and UA-rich motifs. The RNA structure demonstrated that a single qRRM is physically incapable of recognizing both RNA elements simultaneously. In vivo experiments further indicated that any two qRRMs are sufficient to repress nos translation. We probed interactions of Glo qRRMs with TCEI_III RNA using NMR paramagnetic relaxation experiments. Our in vitro and in vivo data support a model whereby tandem Glo qRRMs are indeed multifunctional and interchangeable for recognition of TCE G-tract or UA-rich motifs. This study illustrates how multiple RNA recognition modules within an RNA-binding protein may combine to diversify the RNAs that are recognized and regulated.

INTRODUCTION

Specific recognition of target RNAs by RNA-binding proteins is essential for gene control and often achieved by modular combination of multiple RNA-binding domains. Tandem RNA-binding domains may arise from duplication to allow recognition of longer linear RNA sequences or more complex structures than a single domain can accomplish. For example, the tandem zinc fingers of Tristetraprolin each recognize a UAUU sequence within the UUAUUUAUU AU-rich element (ARE) ( 1 ). Duplicated domains also may diverge to recognize distinct sequence or structural elements. In the case of HuD protein, its two N-terminal RNA recognition motif (RRM) domains both recognize UU elements in AREs, but RRM1 also interacts with four additional 5′ flanking nucleotides ( 2 ). Although amino acid sequences of RNA-binding domains can indicate whether they retain the characteristic RNA recognition features of that domain family, it remains impossible to predict most domain-specific variations. Hence, the specificity of RNA-binding domains must be determined experimentally. Each study expands our knowledge of sequence features that drive specificity and improves prediction methods to identify target RNAs.

During Drosophila development, translational control of nanos ( nos ) mRNA is essential for the formation of the anterior-posterior body axis of the embryo ( 3 ). This process begins in the ovary when nos mRNA is transferred to oocytes from nurse cells during late oogenesis and translationally repressed until it is localized to the posterior ( 3 , 4 ). The translational repression of unlocalized nos mRNA is mediated by a translational control element (TCE) in the 3′ UTR of nos mRNA (Figure 1A ) ( 3 , 4 ). nos TCE RNA is composed of one stem (TCEI) and two stem loop structures (TCEII and TCEIII). During oogenesis, binding of TCEI and TCEIII by the protein Glorund (Glo) controls Nos expression ( 5 ). This protein:RNA interaction is essential to repress unlocalized nos mRNA during oogenesis, which ultimately ensures the formation of functional anterior structures in the fly embryo ( 4 , 5 ). Repression of nos initiated by Glo during oogenesis is also maintained in the early embryo by another RNA-binding protein, Smaug, which recognizes TCE stem loop II ( 6–8 ).

Glo is an RNA-binding protein belonging to the heterogenous nuclear ribonucleoprotein F/H (hnRNP F/H) family ( 4 , 9 ). Mammalian hnRNP F and H are predominantly nuclear and best known as regulators of alternative splicing ( 10 ). Additionally, hnRNP F and H1 are abundant in regenerating axons, where they bind to mRNAs involved in axonal growth and are thought to regulate their axonal transport and/or translation ( 11 ). Notably, mammalian hnRNP F and H proteins are upregulated in a variety of cancers and are thought to contribute to pathogenicity in part by altering translation ( 12 , 13 ). Like hnRNPs F/H, Glo contains three tandem quasi-RNA recognition motifs (qRRMs). We previously demonstrated that the Glo qRRMs are multifunctional, each capable of binding to G-tract and structured UA-rich features of the nos TCE ( 9 ). The G-tract sequence is found in TCEI and the structured UA-rich motif in TCEIII (Figure 1A ). Both features are essential for TCE function and Glo recognition, yet the affinity for G-tract binding is substantially higher than UA-rich motif binding ( 9 ). Disruption of either RNA motif results in modestly increased Nos protein, but mutation of both RNA motifs dramatically increases Nos protein levels. Moreover, engineered mutations that preferentially disrupt Glo qRRM binding to either the G-tract or UA-rich motif demonstrate that nos repression requires both modes of Glo-TCE recognition in vivo ( 9 ). How the tandem Glo qRRMs combine to recognize the G-tract and UA-rich motifs of TCE RNA remained unanswered.

Here we present a high-resolution solution structure of an RNA containing the elements recognized by Glo: nos TCEI and TCEIII, which we call TCEI_III. We used the divide-and-conquer method, a well-established and effective approach combining structural biology methods, to build a larger macromolecular model from high-resolution structures of its components ( 14–16 ). This divide-and-conquer method allowed us to avoid the technical difficulties of crystallizing structured RNAs without engineered crystal contacts such as tetraloop/tetraloop insertions. We first determined a solution structure of TCEIII RNA by NMR. We then used the TCEIII structure along with NMR data for TCEI_III RNA and overall shape information from small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) to produce the hybrid TCEI_III model. This model suggested that more than one Glo qRRM is necessary for TCE recognition, and in vivo experiments indicated that a minimum of two qRRMs is sufficient for nos repression. Furthermore, we used NMR paramagnetic relaxation experiments (PREs) to probe the interactions between the G-tract and UA-rich motifs of TCE RNA and individually-labeled Glo qRRMs. Our in vitro and in vivo data are consistent with a model where the Glo qRRMs are indeed multifunctional and interchangeable for recognition of nos TCE G-tract or UA-rich motifs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

RNA preparation

TCEIII RNA was transcribed in vitro using T7 RNA polymerase with the following components: 1x transcription reaction buffer [40 mM Tris–HCl, 26 mM MgCl 2 , 1 mM DTT, 2.5 mM spermidine and 0.01% v/v Triton-X-100 (pH 8.0)], 1 mM DTT, 4 mM of each rNTP (ATP, GTP, UTP and CTP) (Sigma), 16 mM GMP (Sigma),1 μM double-stranded DNA template, and 0.001 unit/ml inorganic pyrophosphatase (Fisher). The sequences of oligonucleotides used for in vitro transcription are shown in the Supplementary Methods. A 20-ml transcription reaction mixture was incubated at 37°C for 3 h. The nucleic acids were ethanol precipitated and incubated overnight at −20°C. The RNA was resolved on a 15% denaturing polyacrylamide gel, visualized via UV shadowing, and excised from the gel. The RNA was recovered by crushing and soaking using 0.3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2) and left to nutate overnight at 4°C. The RNA was concentrated to 1 mM and exchanged into NMR buffer [10 mM sodium phosphate (pH 6.8)] using a 3 kDa MWCO Millipore spin concentrator. A uniformly 15 N- and 13 C-labeled TCEIII RNA sample was produced using isotopically-labeled rNTPs (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) using the same protocol as above. The resulting isotopically-labeled RNA was exchanged into NMR buffer and concentrated to 0.7 mM.

TCEI_III template DNA was purchased as gBlocks Gene Fragments from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). The 3′ end of the template contained a self-cleaving hepatitis delta virus ribozyme to ensure homogeneity ( 17 ). The gBlock Gene Fragment DNA was amplified using AccuPrime Pfx SuperMix (Invitrogen). After PCR amplification, the reaction was added to the in vitro transcription reaction as the DNA template. A 10-ml transcription reaction was prepared following the above protocol. The TCEI_III RNA was concentrated to 1 mM and exchanged into NMR buffer. An 15 N- and 13 C-labeled TCEI_III RNA sample was produced using 4 mM each of isotopically-labeled GTP and UTP (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) and 7 mM each of natural abundance CTP and ATP (Sigma). The sample was concentrated to 1 mM and exchanged into NMR buffer.

NMR spectroscopy

All NMR experiments were carried out using an 800 MHz Agilent DD2 spectrometer equipped with a cryogenically cooled HCN probe. For the detection of exchangeable protons, the spectra were collected at 5°C in 90% H 2 O/10% D 2 O. For nonexchangeable protons, the spectra were collected at 25°C in 100% D 2 O. 1 H, 1 H-NOESY spectra using Agilent's BioPack rna_11noesy 1–1 echo NOESY pulse sequence ( 18 ) were recorded with 150 ms and 250 ms mixing times to assign exchangeable protons in TCEIII and TCEI_III, respectively. 1 H, 15 N-HMQC spectra were recorded using Agilent's Biopack rna_gNhmqc pulse sequence at 5°C in 90% H 2 O/10% D 2 O with jump-return water suppression for the assignment of TCEIII 15 N chemical shifts. For the assignment of nonexchangeable protons in TCEIII, 1 H, 1 H-NOESY spectra were recorded using Agilent's tnnoesy pulse sequence with a mixing time of 250 ms and presaturation for water signal suppression. 1 H, 1 H-TOCSY experiments were recorded using Agilent's tntocsy pulse sequence at 25°C in 100% D 2 O with short (50 ms) and long (200 ms) mixing times for the assignment of TCEIII sugar pucker conformations. 1 H, 13 C-HSQC spectra were recorded for aromatic and ribose carbon chemical shift assignments in TCEIII using Agilent's gChsqc pulse sequence ( 19 ) in sensitivity enhanced mode ( 20 ) for water suppression. Non-exchangeable proton assignments were made from 3D 1 H, 1 H, 13 C-edited NOESY spectra obtained in the both the aromatic and 13 C regions of the spectrum using the Agilent gnoesyChsqc pulse sequence ( 21 ), with WET water suppression ( 22 , 23 ).

Imino residual dipolar couplings (RDCs) for isolated TCEIII and TCEI_III RNA were measured by recording 1 H, 15 N-ARTSY experiments ( 24 ) at 5°C in isotropic media and in the presence of 13 mg/ml (TCEIII) and 18 mg/mL (TCEI_III) Pf1 filamentous phage. For TCEIII, 1 H, 13 C-ARTSY experiments ( 24 ) were recorded in isotropic media and in 13 mg/ml Pf1 filamentous phage at 25°C in 100% D 2 O to measure aromatic and ribose RDCs. The errors in the RDC experiments were calculated based on the peak intensities of the isotropic and attenuated spectra compared to the spectral noise. The data were processed using NMRPipe ( 25 ) and analyzed using NMRDraw ( 25 ) and NMRView ( 26 ).

Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)

SAXS data were collected at the Advanced Light Source SIBYLS beamline 12.3.1 at the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory ( 27 , 28 ) using the high-throughput batch mode. All experiments were measured in SAXS buffer (10 mM sodium phosphate, pH 6.9) and were performed at 10°C. The SAXS data were collected at three different concentrations: 1.5, 3 and 5 mg/ml ( Supplementary Figure S1 ). The sample-to-detector distance was set to 2 m resulting in the scattering vectors (q) detection range of 0.01 to 0.4 Å −1 . The buffer-subtracted frames were analyzed and processed using Scatter ( 29 ). The radius of gyration ( R g ) for each sample was determined by the Guinier approximation with the limits of q · R g < 1.3. The scattering profiles from the three different concentrations were scaled and merged. The pairwise distribution function ( P ( r )) was calculated using Scatter ( 29 ), and the DATGNOM software ( 30 ) was run automatically on the data that were plotted on the P ( r ) plot. The DATGNOM output file was autoloaded into the DAMMIN/F tab in Scatter ( 29 ). DAMMIF ( 31 ) was used to generate 20 independent ab initio dummy models. All models showed excellent agreement with the experimental data with normalized spatial discrepancy (NSD) <1 ( Supplementary Table S1 ). SUPCOMB in the ATSAS software ( 32 ) was used to superimpose the lowest energy calculated structure of TCEI_III with the SAXS ab initio calculated model.

Structure calculations of TCEIII and TCEI_III RNAs

NOE distances were calculated from 2D 1 H, 1 H-NOESY spectra in D 2 O and H 2 O as well as the 3D 13 C-HSQC-NOESY experiment. The volumes in the 1 H, 1 H-NOESY and the 3D 13 C-HSQC-NOESY experiments in D 2 O were normalized based on the average volume of the intranucleotide H5-H6 peaks with r = 2.4 Å distance. NOEs with volumes greater than the average H5-H6 volume were categorized as very strong (1.8–2.9 Å), and NOEs were categorized as strong (1.8–3.5 Å) if the volume was similar to that of the volumes of H5-H6. The medium (2.0–4.8 Å) and weak (2.2–6.5 Å) NOEs were classified accordingly.

For TCEIII stem loop RNA, a total of 200 structures were calculated using a simulated annealing protocol in XPLOR-NIH ( 33 ). The calculations included 337 NOE distances, 46 hydrogen bond constraints, 40 1 H– 13 C and 8 1 H– 15 N residual dipolar couplings, 140 backbone dihedral angles and sugar puckers, and 20 planarity constraints. The 10 lowest energy structures with no NOE violations >0.5 Å, no dihedral angle violations >5°, and no RDC violations >3 Hz were selected to represent the final structural ensemble.

For TCEI_III RNA structure calculations, we generated 2000 initial models using the Mc-Sym RNA modeling pipeline ( 34 ). A reference model of the TCEI stem was created using a predicted model from RNAComposer ( 35 ) with the base pairing of the TCEI stem confirmed by the presence or absence of imino resonances in the 1 H, 1 H-NOESY spectrum. The lowest energy structure previously calculated for the TCEIII stem loop (described above) was also used as a reference model. The initial Mc-Sym models were filtered to 500 models based on agreement to the experimental 1 H- 15 N RDC values for TCEI_III using PALES software ( 36 ). The 500 models were filtered to 10 models based on the likeness to the SAXS experimental data for TCEI_III using the calcSAXS command from XPLOR-NIH ( 33 ). The best model was used for further refinement using a low temperature simulated annealing protocol to determine the high-resolution structure of TCEI_III RNA (XPLOR-NIH) ( 33 ). Due to the evidence of stable base pairing, we included hydrogen bond, planarity and C3′ endo dihedral angle constraints for A5 to U12, G15 to A17, U45 to U51, A62 to A68, A74 to C76, A83–90, G42, C43, G70 and C71 to help maintain geometry during structure calculations. The structure calculations included a total of 13 1 H– 15 N RDCs, 73 dihedral angles, 175 NOEs, and 102 hydrogen bonds. The SAXS data included a total of 50 points with q values between 0.03 and 0.3. We also included P–P distances for the single-stranded nucleotides (as previously described in ( 14 )) to maintain pseudo-A-form geometry. A total of 100 structures were calculated, and 10 structures with no RDC violations >1.7 Hz and SAXS value of 0.49 ± 0.11 were used as the final ensemble with an overall RMSD of 2.9 Å over all heavy atoms.

Protein expression, purification, and site-specific labeling

Glo qRRM1,2 proteins were expressed and purified as described previously ( 9 ). For site-specific labeling of Glo qRRM1,2, two cysteine residues were introduced (T93C in qRRM1 and T189C in qRRM2) by site-directed mutagenesis. The wild-type, T93C, and T189C Glo qRRM1,2 proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli strain BL21-CodonPlus (DE3)-RIL (Agilent) and induced overnight at 20°C with 0.5 mM IPTG. The histidine-tagged (His 6 -tagged) proteins were purified using a 5-mL HisTrap HP column (Cytiva). The proteins were dialyzed in 1× thrombin cleavage buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM CaCl 2 and 1 mM DTT) overnight at 4°C. The His 6 -tag was cleaved with thrombin (Millipore) at room temperature for 2 h. The reaction was stopped with the addition of 1 mM PMSF, and the proteins were purified using a 5-ml HisTrap HP column (Cytiva) to remove the cleaved tag. The proteins were concentrated with a 10 kDa MWCO Millipore concentrator and further purified using a HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 75 pg column equilibrated with storage buffer (20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10% v/v glycerol). After purification, the T93C and T189C proteins were labeled with MTSL ((1-oxyl-2,2,5-tetramethyl-delta3-pyrroline-3-methyl) methanethiosulfonate)) (Toronto Research Chemicals) using the following protocol ( 37 ). Following the overnight labeling reaction, the proteins were exchanged into MTSL NMR buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate pH 6.8, 50 mM NaCl) with 10 kDa MWCO Millipore concentrators to remove excess MTSL from the sample. The proteins were concentrated to 0.3 mM for PRE experiments. The efficiency of the MTSL labeling reaction was examined by LC–MS on a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) interfaced with an M-Class nanoAcquity UPLC system (Waters), and the percentage of spin-labeled protein was estimated based on the ratio of spin-labeled intact protein (mass gain of 185 Da) compared to intact protein without MTSL treatment.

Paramagnetic relaxation enhancement (PRE) experiments

Samples for the PRE NMR experiments were prepared by mixing a 1:1 molar ratio of 15 N-labeled (GTP and UTP) TCEI_III RNA and MTSL-labeled (T93C or T189C) Glo qRRM1,2 in NMR MTSL buffer (same as above). NMR spectra were acquired at 5°C on an 800 MHz Agilent DD2 spectrometer equipped with a cryogenically cooled HCN probe. Two 1 H, 15 N-HMQC spectra were acquired in the oxidized and reduced state. The 1 H, 15 N-HMQC experiments were acquired using Agilent's rna_gNhmqc pulse sequence centered on the 15 N-imino region at 155 ppm, with 1 H and 15 N sweep widths of 22 ppm (92 ms acquisition time) and 25 ppm (31.6 ms acquisition time), respectively. Jump and return water suppression with a maximum excitation at ∼12.8 ppm was used to suppress the water signal. The reduced state was generated by the addition of sodium ascorbate in 10 molar excess. The sample was incubated for 3 h at room temperature before the reduced spectra were collected. The broadening effects, due to the paramagnetic label, were determined for TCEI_III RNA nucleotides by evaluating the intensity ratios of the oxidized (paramagnetic) and the reduced (diamagnetic) spectra ( I para / I dia ). Error bars ( E r ) were calculated from root mean squared noise (N) in a region of spectra with no peaks according to: E r = ( I para / I dia )*(( N para / I para ) 2 +( N dia / I dia ) 2 ) 1/2 . Nucleotides of TCEI_III RNA that were determined to be closest to the paramagnetic label had a calculated I para / I dia < 0.6.

Generation of gfp-glo transgenes and transgenic lines

GFP-Glo proteins were expressed transgenically. Point mutations were introduced into the glo sequences of a previously described gfp-glo transgene ( 9 ) by PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis. The gfp-glo transgene is based on a genomic glo rescue fragment and includes 5′ regulatory sequences for glo expression. All transgenes were inserted into the attP40 landing site by the phiC31 integrase method.

Analysis of viability

Virgin females heterozygous for a deficiency that removes glo ( Df(3R)Exel7310/TM6B, Tb ; Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center #7965) were mated to males with a null glo mutation ( FRT82B glo 162x /TM6B, Tb, Sb ) ( 4 ) carrying a single copy of a wild-type or mutant gfp-glo transgene. Complete rescue is expected to result in 33% of F 1 progeny with the gfp-glo/+; FRT82B glo 162x /Df(3R)Exel7310 genotype. To calculate percent viability, the experimentally determined frequency was divided by the expected frequency. n ≥ 100 F 1 progeny for each transgene.

Immunoblotting

For analysis of GFP-Glo levels during oogenesis, ovaries were dissected from well-fed females in PBS and frozen in liquid nitrogen. For analysis of Nos levels during late-stages of oogenesis, ovaries enriched for late-stage oocytes were generated as described previously ( 5 ), dissected in PBS, and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Because glo mutant animals expressing GFP-Glo RWKY , GFP-Glo RWHY and GFP-Glo KYHY do not survive to adulthood, ovaries were obtained by generating germline clones using the dominant female sterile method ( 38 ). Ovary extract preparation and immunoblotting was performed as described in ( 3 ), using nitrocellulose membrane. The following primary antibodies were used: 1:1000 rabbit anti-Nos (gift of A. Nakamura); 1:1000 anti-Glo (5B7; ( 4 )); 1:10 000 rabbit anti-kinesin heavy chain (Cytoskeleton). Proteins were visualized by chemiluminescence (Roche).

RNA co-immunoprecipitation

Ovaries enriched for late-stage oocytes were generated as described previously ( 5 ) and dissected in PBS. Approximately 50 ovaries per sample were homogenized on ice in an equal volume of RNase-Free IP buffer [10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, 0.1 mM PMSF, 1× complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche), 300 U RNasin (Promega)]. The volume was increased by addition of 200 μl IP buffer and the lysate was cleared by centrifugation at 13 000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The pellet was rehomogenized in 50 μl of IP buffer, then 200 μl IP buffer was added and the sample was centrifuged at 13 000 rpm for 3 min at 4°C. The supernatents were pooled and 25 μl of the cleared lysate was saved for total RNA extraction (Input), 25 μl was saved for protein analysis, and the remainder was incubated with GFP_TRAP®_M beads (ChromoTek) for 2 h at 4°C. Beads were washed 3 × 10 min in 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA. An aliquot of beads was removed and boiled for 10 min in SDS-PAGE sample buffer for immunoblot analysis. The remainder were treated with RQ1 RNase-free DNase (Promega) for 15 min at 37°C. RNA was isolated with TRIzol (Invitrogen), precipitated with isopropanol, and resuspended with DEPC-treated distilled H 2 O. Half of the RNA was reverse transcribed using Superscript IV (Invitrogen) and primers for either nos (5′-ATAGGATCCGAAAGTGTTCCTTGCTA -3′) or tub (5′-CATAATTATTGGATATTGTAATGA-3′) and the other half was treated similarly, except that Superscript IV was omitted (–RT). PCR was performed with nos (5′-GCGATCAAGGCGGAATCG-3′ and 5′-ATAGGATCCGAAAGTGTTCCTTGCTA-3′) or tub (5′-CCAAGCGGCACATTCAATTCGTC G-3′ and 5′-CATAATTATTGGATATTGTAATGA-3′) primers.

RESULTS

NMR solution structure of TCEIII reveals a central U–U base pair that maintains a continuous A-form RNA stem and a large flexible loop

As a first step toward determining a structure of the nos TCEI_III RNA that contains the elements recognized by Glo, we used NMR to determine a high-resolution solution structure of TCE stem loop III (TCEIII, Figure 1 ). A computationally predicted secondary structure of the full TCE suggested that TCEIII forms a discontinuous 10-bp helix with a central U–U bulge, capped by an 8-nt loop (Figure 1A ). Since many structure prediction methods consider only canonical Watson-Crick base pairs, it is important to confirm base pairing through structural experiments, especially to include non-canonical base pairs. We confirmed base pairing within the TCEIII RNA structure, comparing it to predicted pairing, by analyzing a 2D 1 H, 1 H-NOESY spectrum collected from a sample in H 2 O at 5°C ( Supplementary Figure S2 ). Observable imino resonances in a 2D 1 H, 1 H-NOESY correspond to stable hydrogen bonding in base pairs. In contrast, imino protons of unpaired bases are readily exchanged with the solvent protons, and no imino resonance is observed. Thus, the absence of an imino resonance corresponding to a particular uracil or guanine suggests that base is not involved in stable hydrogen bonding.

For the TCEIII stem loop RNA, we identified 11 observable imino resonances ( Supplementary Figure S2 ), nine of which were in the normal chemical shift range for Watson-Crick base pairs (11.5–13.5 ppm for G and 13–15 ppm for U). Because the addition of Mg 2+ often stabilizes the formation of base pairs in RNA ( 39 ), we tested various concentrations of Mg 2+ and did not observe any differences in the NMR spectra. We assigned the nine imino resonances to guanines and uracils in the predicted stem (G42, G44, U45, U47, U49, U51, U63, U65 and G70, Figure 1B ), based on the observable sequential imino-imino crosspeaks in the 2D 1 H, 1 H-NOESY. We also observed imino crosspeaks from U45 and U47 that were outside the normal range for Watson-Crick base pairs (10.15 and 10.43 ppm), suggesting the formation of a non-canonical base pair ( Supplementary Figure S2 ). We assigned these two imino resonances to U46 and U67. Although the predicted secondary structure of the TCEIII RNA placed an unpaired U–U bulge in the middle of the helix due to inability to predict a non-canonical base pair, the resonances correspond to a stable U46–U67 base pair. Sequential imino-imino crosspeaks were not observed for U49, U51 and U63 due to spectral overlap. However, we were able to easily make the assignments for these nucleotides by utilizing the strong correlation between the uracil imino proton and the adenine H2 proton in an A–U base pair ( Supplementary Figure S2 ). We observed strong crosspeaks from U49, U51 and U63 to A64, A62 and A50, respectively, confirming U49–A64, U51–A62 and U63–A50 base pairing. No imino resonance was observed for U61 indicating that, contrary to the prediction, it does not form a stable Watson–Crick base pair with A52 (Figure 1 ), and no hydrogen bond constraints were used in subsequent structure calculations. As expected for loop nucleotides, no imino resonances were observed for uracil and guanine nucleotides between A53 to A60 (U56 and G57). Together the 2D 1 H, 1 H-NOESY spectrum is consistent with a TCEIII structure with a 10-bp helix including a central U-U base pair and a large 10-nt loop (Figure 1B ).

Next, we aimed to determine if the TCEIII RNA structure resembles typical or atypical A-form geometry by estimating the sugar pucker conformations. We determined the sugar pucker conformations within the TCEIII RNA by analyzing a 2D 1 H, 1 H-TOCSY experiment at 25°C with a short (50 ms) and a long mixing time (200 ms). A short mixing time produces strong H1′–H2′ crosspeaks corresponding to the short-range couplings ( J H1′-H2′ ). A longer mixing time is used to enhance long-range couplings from H1′ to the rest of the sugar ring (e.g. H1′–H3′). Two sugar pucker conformations are commonly found in nucleic acids: C2′ endo, typical for B-form DNA and unstructured RNA, and C3′ endo, typical for A-form duplex RNA. The C2′-endo conformation has a J H1′-H2′ coupling of ∼7.9 Hz ( 40 ), which gives rise to strong H1′-H2′ crosspeaks in the TOCSY spectrum. In contrast, no crosspeaks will be observed for nucleotides displaying C3′-endo sugar conformations, because of the weaker J H1′-H2′ coupling of ∼1 Hz ( 40 ). For the TCEIII stem loop RNA, we observed strong H1′-H2′ crosspeaks in the 2D 1 H, 1 H-TOCSY for loop nucleotides A53 through A60 (Figure 1B ), which is consistent with C2′-endo sugar conformations ( Supplementary Figure S3 ). We observed no strong H1′-H2′ crosspeaks for stem nucleotides G42 through U51, A62 through C69 and C71 (Figure 1B ), consistent with the C3′-endo sugar conformations for A-form RNA geometry. Nucleotides A52, U61 and G70 presented with very weak H1′-H2′ crosspeaks, typically observed for intermediate sugar pucker conformations. Therefore, we used no sugar pucker conformational constraints for nucleotides A52, U61 and G70 during structure calculations.

For structural calculations of the TCEIII stem loop RNA, we used a total of 571 structural constraints ( Supplementary Table S2 ) to calculate 200 structures. The ensemble of the 10 lowest energy structures had no NOE violations (>0.5 Å), no RDC violations (>3 Hz), and no dihedral angle violations (>3°) (Figure 2 ). In this ensemble, the helical region of TCEIII was well-defined with an RMSD of 0.20 Å over all 637 heavy atoms of nucleotides G42 to G51 and A62 to C71. However, the overall RMSD of the TCEIII RNA structural ensemble was higher (0.34 Å over all 479 heavy atoms), due to conformational flexibility outside of the helix region.

Figure 2.

The base pairing and sugar pucker information described above indicated that TCEIII contains a large flexible loop. We therefore analyzed 1 H and 13 C NMR chemical shifts and resonance intensities from the 2D 1 H– 13 C-HSQC spectra to identify the flexible regions within the TCEIII structure. We calculated normalized 1 H– 13 C resonance intensities for each nucleotide in TCEIII, dividing by the highest intensity peak for U56 (Figure 3A ). The protons of nucleotide bases in flexible regions should display intense 1 H– 13 C resonances in a 2D 1 H, 13 C-HSQC spectrum ( 41 , 42 ), and nucleotides A55–A58 in the loop region show considerably higher normalized intensity resonances. We also evaluated flexibility within TCEIII by comparing the 13 C chemical shift values of TCEIII bases to typical chemical shifts of free nucleotide monophosphates (NMPs) ( 41 , 43 ). Flexible regions within a structure should mimic the chemical shifts of NMPs with only minor differences, whereas nucleotides in structured regions should display larger differences. We subtracted the 13 C chemical shifts of NMPs from the 13 C chemical shifts of nucleotides in TCEIII to identify flexible regions of TCEIII (Figure 3B ). Loop nucleotides A55–A59 displayed the smallest chemical shift differences versus NMPs, consistent with our analysis of the relative chemical shift intensities (Figure 3A ) and the structural ensemble (Figure 2 ).

Our structure of TCEIII reveals unique structural features that could be important for its function in vivo . Nucleotides U47 to A52 and U61 to A66 have been shown, in vivo and in vitro , to be important for Glo binding (Figure 2 ) ( 4 , 5 , 9 ). Our data show that TCEIII is structured throughout this helical region, except for nucleotides A52 and U61, which showed no NOE evidence of Watson-Crick base pairing. U61 is in an area of the TCEIII structure that appears more flexible, and in the structural ensemble the U61 base alternates between conformations, including fully unpaired, alternate hydrogen bonding with A52, and even Watson–Crick pairing with A52. This is due to the lack of specific restraints on U61. However, further structural information is needed to determine atomic-level detail of TCEIII interaction with Glo.

Structure of the nos TCEI_III RNA indicates two Glo qRRM domains are needed to recognize both G-tract and UA-rich stem elements

In order to use the divide-and-conquer method to determine a high-resolution structure of the TCEI_III RNA, we first validated that the structure of the isolated TCEIII stem-loop is unaltered when in the context of TCEI_III and that the structure of TCEI_III is unaltered when in the context of the full TCE. We compared the chemical shifts of the imino resonances of the isolated TCEIII to the chemical shifts of imino resonances of TCEIII in TCEI_III in 2D 1 H, 1 H-NOESY spectra. The known chemical shifts from the isolated TCEIII structure allowed unambiguous assignments of all nucleotides from TCEIII in the 2D 1 H, 1 H-NOESY spectrum of TCEI_III ( Supplementary Figure S4 ). The mean chemical shift difference between the isolated TCEIII and TCEIII in TCEI_III was <0.087 ppm, providing strong evidence of structural similarity. We also confirmed, through equivalent NMR experiments with TCEI_III and the full TCE, that TCEI_III maintains the same structure when isolated or incorporated into the full TCE RNA ( Supplementary Figure S5 ). The average chemical shift difference of the nucleotides is 0.025 ppm, providing strong evidence that the absence of stem-loop II does not alter the TCEI_III structure compared to its structure when incorporated into the full TCE RNA.

As we did for TCEIII, we confirmed base pairing in stem I of the TCEI_III structure, compared to predicted pairing, by analyzing the 2D 1 H, 1 H-NOESY spectrum. Assignment of imino resonances in the normal range for Watson-Crick base pairs confirmed most predicted pairs (Figure 1 , Supplementary Figure S6 ). Assignment of imino resonances based on visible cross peaks slightly outside of the typical range for G and U imino resonances in Watson–Crick base pairs (11.51 ppm) confirmed the U6-G89 wobble base pair. However, no visible imino resonances were observed for G1, G3, U14, G77, U79 or U91 suggesting these nucleotides do not form stable base pairs. Therefore, the predicted U14–G77 wobble and A4–U91 Watson-Crick base pairs were not included in the structure calculations.

We also measured imino 1 H– 15 N residual dipolar couplings (RDCs) to determine the relative orientations of stem I and stem loop III. From a 1 H, 15 N-ARTSY imino experiment, we extracted 13 unambiguous 1 H– 15 N RDCs for TCEI_III ( Supplementary Figure S6 ). RDCs for nucleotides U49, U51 and U63 were not extracted due to spectral overlap, and the 1 H– 15 N resonance for nucleotide U74 was too weak to be visible in the aligned spectrum. Also, due to the lack of visible sequential imino-imino crosspeaks, the NOE crosspeaks for G10 and G15 are ambiguous, therefore, no RDCs or other restraints were included for these nucleotides. We also omitted nucleotides with the largest chemical shift differences between the isolated TCEIII stem loop and the TCEIII stem loop when it is incorporated in TCEI_III: G42 (0.26 ppm) and G70 (0.12 ppm). These larger differences likely reflect the change in chemical environment for nucleotides like G42 and G70 that are located near the junction in TCEI_III, where TCEI meets TCEIII.

We complemented our NMR experiments with small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) to analyze the global structure of TCEI_III. The Kratky plot for TCEI_III displayed a bell-shaped curve with a slight shoulder peak (Figure 4A ). This indicates a well-folded molecule that is slightly extended rather than a globular, compact structure ( 44 ). The pairwise distribution ( P ( r )) function of TCEI_III, which defines the distance between all electron pairs within the molecule, exhibited multiple peaks, ended with a long tail at larger r values, and yielded a maximum diameter ( D max ) of 98 Å (Figure 4B ). This type of curve also suggests an elongated or irregular shaped biomolecule. Finally, we calculated an ab initio SAXS model using DAMMIF ( 31 ), which revealed an extended bent conformation (Figure 4C ).

For calculations of the high-resolution TCEI_III structure, we generated initial models incorporating NMR-confirmed structures of TCEI and TCEIII and filtered for agreement with RDCs and SAXS data for TCEI_III RNA. We then used a simulated annealing protocol in XPLOR-NIH ( 33 ), which included the 1 H– 15 N imino RDCs, the SAXS data of TCEI_III, and 350 additional structural constraints ( Supplementary Table S2 ). We calculated 100 structures and selected the 10 lowest energy structures with no NOE violations (>0.6 Å), no RDC violations (>1.7 Hz) and no dihedral angle violations (>5°) for a structural ensemble of TCEI_III (Figure 4C ). The ensemble of TCEI_III has an RMSD of 2.9 Å over all heavy atoms and an RMSD of 2.0 Å over the heavy atoms in the A-form RNA helices. The high degree of flexibility within loop, bulge, and terminal regions of the structure (nucleotides G1 to A4, C13 to U14, A41, A52 to U61, A72 to U73, G77 to A82 and U91) contribute to a higher overall RMSD for TCEI_III.

To summarize the accumulated data for the high resolution TCEI_III structure: (i) the structure of TCEIII changes only subtly in the context of TCEI_III. Besides suggesting structural conservation of TCEIII in TCEI_III, the absence of large changes suggests there are few interactions between TCEI and TCEIII. (ii) The NOEs identified in NMR data for the G-tract allowed us to establish basepairing in TCEI, which is close that predicted in Figure 1 . Note that this is less detailed information than for TCEIII, but sufficient for our conclusions about the global architecture of TCEI_III. (iii) The combination of long range restraints (SAXS and RDC) were used to select a model from a large pool for low temperature refinement against all the available data. The generated ensemble agrees well with the ab initio SAXS prediction, which shows an elongated structure with a distinct bend (Figure 4C , D).

We previously showed that to control Nos protein expression Glo requires both of its modes of RNA recognition: G-tract (TCEI) and UA-rich stem (TCEIII) binding ( 9 ). In our NMR and SAXS structures of TCEI_III, the distance between the G-tract in TCEI and the UA-rich stem in TCEIII is approximately 65 Å (Figure 4D ). Although each qRRM domain of Glo possesses residues critical for binding to the G-tract in TCEI and the UA-rich stem in TCEIII (qRRM1: R52, W58; qRRM2:K149, Y155; qRRM3: H484, Y490), the average distance between these residues in one qRRM domain is approximately 25 Å. Therefore, one qRRM domain of Glo cannot bind to both TCEI and TCEIII simultaneously. Instead, a minimum of two qRRM domains of Glo appear to be required to bind the TCE for nos regulation. These structural results are consistent with in vitro RNA-binding assays demonstrating that the individual Glo qRRMs do not bind stably to TCE RNA, but a fragment with Glo qRRMs 1 and 2 binds to the TCE ( 9 ).

Two Glo qRRMs are necessary and sufficient for TCE binding, nos repression, and viability

To test the prediction that two qRRM domains are required for Glo function in vivo , we assayed the effect of point mutations in Glo that alter both G-tract binding and UA-rich stem-binding residues simultaneously ( 9 ). We previously identified alanine substitutions that compromise G-tract binding affinity of Glo qRRM1,2 but have little effect on TCEIII binding (W58 in RRM1 and Y155 in RRM2; Y490 is the equivalent residue in qRRM3). These residues are in loops that are critical for hnRNP F qRRM binding to G-tract RNAs ( 45 ). Moreover, alanine substitutions of R52 and K149 weaken TCEIII binding affinity of Glo qRRM1,2 with little effect on G-tract binding (H484 is the equivalent residue in qRRM3). These residues are in the RNP2 motif of the qRRMs, but are basic rather than the characteristic hydrophobic residues that stack with RNA bases in typical RRMs ( 46 , 47 ). Here we combined these mutations in individual Glo qRRMs to disable both RNA recognition modes in specific domains. Wild-type GFP-Glo or GFP-Glo proteins with mutations in individual qRRMs (qRRM1: R52A,W58A; qRRM2: K149A,Y155A; qRRM3: H484A,Y490A) or in pairwise combinations of qRRMs (qRRM1 + 2: RWKY; qRRM1 + 3: RWHY; qRRM2 + 3: KYHY) were expressed in glo mutant flies (Figure 5A ) and stability of the mutant proteins was confirmed by immunoblotting ( Supplementary Figure S7 ). GFP-Glo mutants with any two qRRMs intact retained the ability to interact with nos mRNA in vivo , as determined by a coimmunoprecipitation assay (Figure 5B , Supplementary Figure S7B ). By contrast, nos was not detected in immunoprecipitates of GFP-Glo mutants with only a single intact qRRM (Figure 5B , Supplementary Figure S7B ).

Figure 5.

Consistent with these results, Nos protein levels were unaffected in oocytes expressing GFP-Glo mutants with any two intact qRRMs but were dramatically increased when GFP-Glo had only a single intact qRRM (Figure 5C ). Thus, at least two qRRMs are required for TCE recognition and translational repression of nos in vivo . Furthermore, the three qRRMs appear to function redundantly to regulate nos , since any two qRRMs are capable of regulating nos translation in vivo .

In addition to regulation of nos during oogenesis, Glo is required for viability to adulthood. Although the targets of Glo in this role are not known, we previously found that the Glo G-tract and UA-rich stem binding modes are both essential, suggesting that Glo may recognize other RNAs through a similar bipartite binding strategy ( 9 ). To determine whether two qRRMs are also required in this context, we tested the ability of the mutant GFP-Glo proteins to rescue the development of glo mutant animals to adulthood. GFP-Glo mutants with any two qRRMs intact largely supported development, with ≥75% survival, whereas mutants with only one intact qRRM were completely unable to. Thus, similarly to the regulation of nos , multiple qRRMs are required for Glo to regulate targets required for viability.

Multifunctional Glo qRRM domains are interchangeable for binding the nos TCE G-tract and UA-rich stem elements

Since each of the Glo qRRMs has the ability to bind to either the G-tract or UA-rich stem, we envisioned several possible models for interaction between two Glo qRRMs and the TCEI_III RNA. We illustrate this for the two domain Glo qRRM1,2 protein (Figure 6A ): (i) the N-terminal qRRM binds to the G-tract and the C-terminal qRRM binds to the UA-rich stem, (ii) the C-terminal qRRM binds to the G-tract and the N-terminal qRRM binds to the UA-rich stem or (iii) the directionality of the tandem qRRMs is irrelevant and interaction is a mixture of models 1 and 2. To distinguish between these models, we used PRE experiments to map where on the TCEI_III RNA the individual Glo qRRM domains interact.

Figure 6.

To perform the PRE experiments, we used a construct of Glo that contains the qRRM1 and qRRM2 domains in tandem (Glo qRRM1,2) (Figure 6B ). The Glo qRRM1,2 protein is well behaved in solution, contains the minimum two qRRM domains for TCE interaction, and was used previously to characterize RNA interaction modes ( 9 ). We introduced single cysteines at equivalent sites in qRRM1 (T93C) and qRRM2 (T189C) for modification with the nitroxide spin label, MTSL. Residues T93 and T189 were selected for labeling, as these residues are solvent accessible and central to residues critical for binding to the G-tract and UA-rich regions (Figure 6C ). We incubated Glo qRRM1,2 T93C-MTSL or T189C-MTSL with isotopically-labeled TCEI_III and collected 2D 1 H, 15 N-HMQC spectra of the complexes in the paramagnetic and diamagnetic states (Figure 6D ). We quantified the PRE by calculating the intensity ratios of the paramagnetic (para) spectrum versus the diamagnetic (dia) spectrum ( I para / I dia ). Values that approach 0 indicate very high proximity of the nucleotide to the paramagnetic tag, whereas values approaching 1 indicate very low proximity ( 48 , 49 ). Nucleotides with substantial attenuation of signal were mapped onto the TCEI_III solution structure (Figure 6E – G ).

Regardless of whether MTSL was located on Glo qRRM1 or qRRM2, we observed similar patterns of attenuated resonances for TCEI_III nucleotides (Figure 6G ), which is consistent with the equilibrium in model 3. For model 1 or model 2, we expected distinctly different patterns of attenuation for labels on qRRM1 versus qRRM2, since T93C-MTSL would be near different nucleotides than T189C-MTSL. Clusters of nucleotides around the G-tract binding site displayed strong decreases in PRE intensity ratio (PRE value < 0.6) for both T93C-MTSL and T189C-MTSL (nucleotides U6, G84, G86 and G89). U12, G87 and G88 nucleotides showed moderate attenuation (PRE value 0.6–0.8) for one of the qRRMs and strong attenuation for the other. The attenuation of these signals suggests that either of the two Glo qRRM domains can associate with the G-tract nucleotides in TCEI_III. In addition, we observed that the signals for nucleotides G16, G44, and G70 near the UA-rich stem in TCEIII were strongly attenuated for both T93C-MTSL and T189C-MTSL. We also noted moderate attenuation in signal for other nucleotides at or near the UA-rich stem: U46, U49/U51/U63 and U67 (note that assignment of U49, U51 and U63 imino resonances is ambiguous due to overlap). The similarity in pattern suggests that either qRRM1 or qRRM2 can come into close association with the UA-rich nucleotides. The lower binding affinity of Glo for the UA-rich stem ( K d = 27 μM) versus the G-tract ( K d = 0.24 μM) ( 9 ) is consistent with the generally more moderate attenuation of signal for the UA-rich stem.

DISCUSSION

Combinations of RNA recognition modules generate the diverse specificities of RNA-binding proteins needed to control gene expression. RRM or KH domains often occur in tandem and together generate distinct RNA recognition specificity to select target mRNAs. Although we have much information about the specificities of RNA-binding domains, including high-throughput screening of sequence specificity, we have relatively little information on how multiple RNA-binding modules bring their individual specificities to RNA target selection. Here we demonstrate that at least two of the three multifunctional Glo qRRMs are required to recognize the nos TCE RNA, one for the TCEIII UA-rich stem and one for the TCEI G-tract (Figure 6H ). Therefore, the efficiency of having two RNA-binding modes in one qRRM is not utilized: one domain does not simultaneously recognize both RNA features. We also find that the three qRRMs are interchangeable: any two of the three Glo qRRMs are sufficient in vivo to repress nos translation and maintain viability. Although qRRMs 1 and 2 are closely spaced with 8 residues between the C-terminal α helix of qRRM1 and the N-terminal β strand of qRRM2, this length linker appears sufficient to bridge the distance between G-tract and UA-rich elements. Alternatively, the C-terminal α helix of qRRM1 could unfold upon RNA binding, as seen for the RRM of Nop15 when it binds to pre-rRNA ( 50 , 51 ). Finally, it does not matter which RNA feature is recognized by the two qRRMs. This striking redundancy of the Glo qRRMs seems excessive, as we might expect some specialization to have evolved.

We suggest several evolutionary benefits from having three redundant multifunctional qRRMs in a single protein. (i) Functional redundancy protects crucial gene regulation. Repression of nos translation during oogenesis is essential to ensure correct anterior patterning in the embryo, yet mutation of one qRRM avoids a biological disruption as nos expression is normal in embryos. Similarly, although we do not know the target RNA(s), Glo with two intact qRRMs supports viability to adulthood. (ii) Redundancy gives Glo the potential to recognize a variety of target RNAs with different combinations of G-tract and UA-rich features. In addition, the G-tracts may be either single-stranded or base-paired. We previously demonstrated that Glo qRRMs bind to short single-stranded G-tracts ( 9 ), and here we observed visible imino proton resonances for the G-tract sequence (G87, G88, and G89) in the TCEI_III RNA (Figure 6D and Supplementary Figure S6 ), indicating that the G-tract remains base paired in the presence of Glo. Therefore, Glo qRRMs appear to differ from hnRNP F qRRMs that disrupt RNA structures when binding to G-tracts ( 45 ). Although the in vivo and in vitro evidence indicates the importance of base pairing for TCE activity ( 4 , 9 , 52 ), it will require additional structural studies to determine how Glo qRRMs specifically recognize duplex RNAs. (iii) Functionally redundant qRRMs with different spacing between the qRRMs adds additional opportunities for distinct RNA recognition. The linker between Glo qRRMs 1 and 2 is short (∼8 aa) whereas qRRMs 2 and 3 are separated by a long linker (∼240 aa). Although the distance between qRRMs does not affect nos regulation as any two qRRMs are sufficient, the spacing may alter specificity for other RNAs. For example, qRRMs 1 and 2 would require RNAs with G-tract and/or UA-rich motifs near each other whereas the long linker between qRRMs 2 and 3 should make that two-domain combination less restrictive in arrangement of G-tract and/or UA-rich motifs in target RNAs. To appreciate the range of specificity and roles of its multifunctional qRRMs, additional RNAs that are controlled by Glo will need to be identified.

Adding to its multifunctionality, Glo also binds to other proteins ( 53 ). Glo was recently shown to interact with Drosophila Fragile X mental retardation protein (dFMRP) to stall ribosomes and decrease the translation elongation rate on nos mRNA. Although we have not yet identified differences in RNA recognition, Glo qRRMs do have different protein binding specificity. Glo interacts with dFMRP via qRRM2, but qRRM1 and qRRM3 do not directly interact with dFMRP ( 53 ). It is tempting to speculate that interaction between qRRM2 and dFMRP alters RNA recognition, and that other protein interactions could modify RNA recognition by the other qRRMs to generate specialization.
