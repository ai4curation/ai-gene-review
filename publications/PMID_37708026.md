The naked mole rat (NMR) is the longest-lived rodent, resistant to multiple
age-related diseases including neurodegeneration. However, the mechanisms
underlying the NMR's resistance to neurodegenerative diseases remain elusive.
Here, we isolated oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) from NMRs and compared
their transcriptome with that of other mammals. Extracellular matrix (ECM) genes
best distinguish OPCs of long- and short-lived species. Notably, expression
levels of CD44, an ECM-binding protein that has been suggested to contribute to
NMR longevity by mediating the effect of hyaluronan (HA), are not only high in
OPCs of long-lived species but also positively correlate with longevity in
multiple cell types/tissues. We found that CD44 localizes to the endoplasmic
reticulum (ER) and enhances basal ATF6 activity. CD44 modifies proteome and
membrane properties of the ER and enhances ER stress resistance in a manner
dependent on unfolded protein response regulators without the requirement of HA.
HA-independent role of CD44 in proteostasis regulation may contribute to
mammalian longevity.

INTRODUCTION

The naked mole rat (NMR) (Heterocephalus glaber) is resistant to aging to such an extent that an increase in mortality risk cannot be detected under captivity.1 Aging is negligible in this species from other points of view as well, as they rarely develop cancers and do not show signs of metabolic and reproductive decline.2,3 These age-related changes are evident in humans, although humans, similar to NMRs, have a much longer lifespan than predicted by body mass. The NMR thus seems to develop anti-aging mechanisms even more efficiently than humans, at least in some aspects. While it is still largely unknown how NMRs resist aging, the signaling mediated by hyaluronan (HA) and its receptor CD44 is emerging as an important regulator of NMR longevity. CD44 is a plasma membrane protein with no catalytic activity that transduces intracellular signals through interaction with other receptors or the assembly of an intracellular signaling complex.4,5 HA, on the other hand, is an extracellular linear polysaccharide, whose functions depend on its polymer length6 due, for example, to its multivalent interaction with the receptors. We have previously shown that NMR produces HA of higher molecular weight than that of other species and that its interaction with CD44 protects NMR cells from cytotoxic stress.7

Cross-species transcriptomic analysis is an unbiased and effective approach to uncover genetic programs defining species maximum lifespan.8–10 Transcriptomic analyses have been conducted on many NMR tissues, such as liver, kidney, and brain.11,12 These three tissues, especially brain, show fewer age-related changes in NMRs than in other mammals.12 However, NMR brain does not contain protective high-molecular-weight HA13 and the mechanism of its delayed aging remains elusive. While this makes the transcriptome of NMR brain of particular interest, comprehensive cross-species transcriptomic comparison of NMR brain has not yet been carried out. Among different types of brain cells, oligodendrocytes and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) are worthy of attention since it has been reported that transcriptomic differences between human and other shorter-lived primates are greater in these cells than in neurons.14

OPCs are the most proliferative cells in the adult brain, constituting 2%–3% and 8%–9% of the total cell population in white and gray matter in the adult rat brain, respectively.15 The ratio of OPCs is even higher in immature animals.16 OPCs distribute throughout the brain and give rise to oligodendrocytes for myelin turnover17 and regenerative remyelination.18 OPCs also modulate neuronal activity19 and immune responses.20 Aging negatively affects proliferation, differentiation, and remyelination capacity of OPCs, which renders demyelinated axons more susceptible to degeneration21–25 and thereby increases vulnerability to chronic demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis. During aging, OPCs acquire hallmarks of cellular aging, such as accumulation of DNA damage and expression of genes associated with cellular senescence,23 and their ability to differentiate into mature oligodendrocytes becomes severely impaired.26

Here, we first isolated OPCs from NMRs and compared their transcriptome with that of other mammals in order to gain insights into how NMRs resist aging. Through comparative transcriptomics of short- and long-lived mammals, we aimed to identify novel longevity mechanisms, especially those that may apply to humans. We found that CD44 levels were high in NMR and human OPCs and low in mouse and rat OPCs. The correlation between maximum lifespan and CD44 levels was also observed in liver, skin, and fibroblasts. Using NMR and mouse OPCs and human fibroblasts, we showed that CD44 enhanced basal ATF6 activity and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress resistance without the requirement of HA. Our results suggest that CD44 may contribute to mammalian longevity via non-canonical activation of ATF6.

RESULTSCross-species transcriptomic comparisons of NMR OPCsWe first attempted to isolate OPCs from newborn NMR brains by magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) using anti-A2B5 antibody,27–29 which recognizes an epitope of ganglioside molecule expressed on glial progenitor cells. Approximately 13% of dissociated brain cells were A2B5 positive and 4.3 × 105 A2B5+ cells could be isolated from one brain sample, which is comparable with the number of A2B5+ cells that can be isolated from newborn mouse brain.30 A2B5+ cells collected from newborn NMR brain start growing as adherent monolayers in liquid culture after around 5 days post isolation (Figure S1A). After 2 weeks of cell culture in serum-free OPC-A medium containing PDGF-AA and FGF-basic, these cells showed a morphology similar to that of mouse OPCs (Figure 1A). Note that, although A2B5-based separation of OPCs has been reported to result in contamination of small numbers of microglia and fibroblasts,31 they do not proliferate in OPC-A medium. Most of these expanded NMR cells retained A2B5 expression (Figure S1B) and the majority of them expressed oligodendrocyte lineage markers Olig2 and Sox10 (Figures S1C and S1D). Unlike these cells, NMR skin fibroblasts (NSFs) did not express A2B5, Olig2, or Sox10. Importantly, RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis showed that NMR brain-derived A2B5+ cells expanded in OPC-A medium expressed OPC marker genes (Pdgfra, Cspg4, Nkx2–2) at much higher levels than in the whole brain. On the other hand, marker genes of oligodendrocytes (Mag, Mbp, Mobp), astrocytes (Gfap, S100b, Slc1a2), microglia (Aif1, Cx3cr1, P2ry12), and neurons (Eno2, Nrgn, Rbfox3/NeuN) were expressed at much lower levels in cultured A2B5+ cells compared with the whole brain (Figure 1B). Taken together, these results indicate that NMR brain-derived A2B5+ cells expanded in OPC-A medium were predominantly OPCs. RNA-seq analysis also showed that myelination-related genes were overrepresented in the genes that were upregulated (fold change [FC] > 2-fold) by differentiation induction in NMR OPCs (Figure S1E). Upregulation of myelin-associated genes Mag, Mbp, Plp1, and Gpr17 in differentiation-induced NMR OPCs was confirmed by real-time qPCR (Figure S1F). Cell cycle genes, on the other hand, were suppressed by induction of differentiation (Figure S1E).In order to get a comprehensive picture of species differences in OPCs, we next compared our NMR OPC RNA-seq data with those of human, mouse, and rat OPCs.32–34 RNA-seq data of NMR, human, rat, and mouse brains35–37 were also included in the analysis (Table S1). As a result, we found that the transcriptome of OPCs showed much greater species differences than that of brains (Figure 1C), suggesting a relatively high contribution of OPCs in shaping species differences in the CNS. While mouse and rat OPCs cluster closely together in a multidimensional scaling (MDS) plot, NMR and human OPCs showed clear separation from the others (Figure 1C). To investigate the gene expression signature that is associated with longevity, we first characterized the genes that are expressed at higher or lower levels in NMR OPCs than either in human, mouse, and rat OPCs (FC >3-fold and adjusted p value <0.05 by one-by-one comparison). Ribosomal genes were expressed at much lower levels in NMR OPCs (Figures 1D and S2A), suggesting that ribosome production and maintenance, which are the major energy consuming processes in the cells, are suppressed in NMR OPCs. Genes encoding OXPHOS and other mitochondrial proteins were also expressed at lower levels in NMR OPCs (Figures 1D and S2A), possibly reflecting their low energy requirement. Consistently, endogenous levels of reactive oxygen species, which are mainly generated as by-products of OXPHOS, were lower in NMR OPCs than in mouse OPCs (Figure S2B). Genes highly expressed in NMR OPCs than in OPCs of other species were dominated by genes related to ECM organization (Figures 1D and S2A). In order to identify the common features of long-lived species’ OPCs, we next investigated the genes that are expressed at higher or lower levels both in NMR and human OPCs than either in mouse and rat OPCs (FC >3-fold and adjusted p value <0.05 by one-by-one comparison) (Figure 1D). We identified high expression of ECM organization-related genes as the sole feature of the genes that are expressed at higher levels both in NMR and human OPCs. Re-analysis of public RNA-seq data14 also indicated that oligodendrocytes and OPCs (OLIG2+ cells) express higher levels of ECM-related genes in humans than in those of shorter-lived primates. Such difference was not observed in NeuN+ neurons (Figures 1E and S2C).CD44 expression correlates with longevity in mammalsAmong the 13 ECM organization-related genes that were expressed at higher levels in NMR and human OPCs, Capn12, Cd44, and Ltbp1 were even more highly expressed in NMRs than in humans (Figures 2A and S2A). Although CD44 can mediate the cytoprotective effect of NMR high-molecular-weight HA,7 the polymer length of HA is relatively short (mostly smaller than 400 kDa)13 in the brain to the extent that it is unlikely that HA-CD44 signals exhibit such cytoprotective effect. If high expression of CD44 contributes to longevity regardless of the presence of extremely high-molecular-weight HA, the correlation between longevity and CD44 levels might be observed in a broader range of species and cell types. In support of this h pothesis, RNA-seq data of cultured fibroblasts of 10 mammalian species38 showed a positive correlation between their maximum lifespans39 and CD44 expressions (Figure 2B). In addition, RNA-seq data of the liver of 27 mammalian species40 indicated a positive correlation between maximum lifespans and CD44 expressions (Figure 2C) regardless of the fact that HA levels are low in this tissue.41 We further tested the relationships between maximum lifespans and CD44 expressions in these datasets using phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS) analysis, which takes into account the lack of independence among species arising from common phylogenetic history. We performed PGLS analyses on 100 phylogenetic trees downloaded from vertlife.org that were generated using randomized parameters in order to take phylogenetic uncertainty into account. In cultured fibroblasts, all PGLS tests showed significant (p < 0.0064) positive relationships between maximum lifespan and CD44 expression levels. In the liver, all PGLS tests showed positive relationships between maximum lifespan and CD44 expression levels and p values were less than 0.05 in 85 out of 100 tests. Species investigated here are phylogenetically diverse and are commonly used for comparative studies of mammals, as can be seen by the fact that all the 10 species used for the analysis of fibroblasts were also used for analysis of the liver. In addition to these data of phylogenetically diverse mammals, RNA-seq data of the skin of 20 Rodentia and two Eulipotyphla,8 which are phylogenetically closely related, also showed a positive correlation between maximum lifespans and CD44 expressions, indicating that high expression of CD44 is not an order-dependent trait irrelevant to maximum lifespan (Figure 2D). These findings suggest that the high expression of CD44 is a common feature of long-lived mammals. High expression of CD44 was confirmed at protein levels in expanded and freshly isolated NMR OPCs (Figures 3A and S2D).To investigate the potential pro-longevity function of CD44, we designed two small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) against NMR CD44. We transfected NMR OPCs with siLuciferase (siLuc) or either one of the two CD44 siRNAs (Figure 3B). Two days later, we supplemented the culture media with a physiological concentration (20 μg/mL)41 of 150–400 kDa HA or with vehicle and then collected the cells for RNA-seq on the following day. As a result, 529 and 426 genes were up- and downregulated, respectively, by both of the CD44 siRNAs (adjusted p value <0.05). On the other hand, no genes were significantly affected by the addition of HA (Figure 3C; Table S2). Interestingly, CD44 knockdown suppressed the genes that were expressed at higher levels in NMR OPCs than in OPCs of all other species (Wilcoxon test, p value = 6.8 × 10−5). CD44 knockdown also suppressed the genes that were expressed at higher levels in both NMR and human OPCs than in mouse and rat OPCs (Wilcoxon test, p value = 3.5 × 10−5) (Figure 3D). These data indicate that CD44 plays a part in shaping OPC gene expression signature that is associated with longer lifespan in this cell type. To gain further insights into the role of CD44 in the transcriptional regulation of NMR OPCs, we next analyzed the enrichment of KEGG pathways, Reactome pathways, and GO biological processes in the CD44-dependent genes. In the genes that were downregulated by CD44 knockdown, most of the overrepresented terms were associated with the ER, unfolded protein response (UPR)/ER stress, and the UPR regulator ATF6 (Figure 3E). Genes that were upregulated by CD44 knockdown did not show any functional enrichment. Transcription factor target enrichment analyses using TRRUST42 and ARCHS443 identified ATF6 as the only transcription factor whose target genes were commonly predicted to be overrepresented in the genes downregulated by CD44 knockdown (Figure 3F).CD44 enhances basal ATF6 activity and ER stress resistanceConsidering that CD44 expression correlates with longevity, our observation that CD44 predominantly promotes ATF6 target gene expressions (Figures 3E–3G) may suggest the contribution of CD44 to ER stress resistance. Indeed, NMR OPCs showed higher resistance against an ER stress inducer, tunicamycin, compared with mouse OPCs in a manner dependent on CD44. CD44 knockdown reduced cell survival against tunicamycin treatment also in mouse OPCs but to a lesser extent than in NMR OPCs, probably due to the low basal expression of CD44 (Figures 3H and 3I). In addition, overexpression of mouse or NMR CD44 enhanced tunicamycin resistance in mouse OPCs (Figures 3J and 3K). Overexpression of another type I transmembrane glycoprotein, LDLR, did not show such effect.To test the relevance of our findings in human cells, we stably knocked down CD44 in IMR90 normal human fibroblasts (Figure 4A), a cell type in which CD44 expression shows a positive correlation with species’ maximum lifespan. IMR90 cells were transduced with retrovirus encoding telomerase (hTert) before establishing CD44 knockdown cell lines in order to avoid replicative senescence. We generated IMR90-hTert cell lines stably expressing luciferase small hairpin RNA (shRNA) (shLuc IMR90-hTert cells) or either of two CD44 shRNAs targeting different sequences of CD44 (shCD44–1 and shCD44–2 IMR90-hTert cells). Similar to NMR OPCs, CD44 knockdown sensitized IMR90-hTert cells to tunicamycin treatment (Figure 4B). In U2OS human osteosarcoma cells, CRISPR-mediated knockout of CD44 enhanced sensitivity to tunicamycin, as well as to another ER stress-inducer thapsigargin, but not to oxidative stress-inducer tBHP (tert-butyl hydroperoxidase) (Figures S3A and S3B). We decided to focus our study on IMR90 cells for the further investigation not only because more resources are available for this cell line7 but also because our ultimate goal is to apply our findings to human health care. Another important merit of using IMR90 cells is that these cells express only the standard form of CD447 and thus the results would not be complicated by the expression patterns of CD44 variants.In order to characterize the effects of CD44 knockdown on the transcriptome of IMR90-hTert cells, we performed RNA-seq on shLuc, shCD44–1, and shCD44–2 IMR90-hTert cells. Strikingly, functional enrichment analysis of genes consistently downregulated by two CD44 shRNAs in IMR90-hTert cells (adjusted p value <0.05) showed results similar to those of CD44 knockdown NMR OPCs (Figures 3E and 4C). That is, downregulation of ER- and ATF6-related genes was the primary feature of CD44 knockdown cells in both NMR OPCs and IMR90-hTert cells. Real-time qPCR of representative UPR-responsive transcripts (DDIT3, DNAJB9, HSP90B1, HSPA5, HYOU1, PDIA4, PPP1R15A, and XBP1) confirmed that only HSP90B1 and HSPA5, which are both targets of ATF6,44 were consistently downregulated by CD44 knockdown (Figure 4D). ATF6 overexpression in CD44 knockdown IMR90-hTert cells rescued the expression of HSP90B1 and HSPA5 as well as cellular resistance to tunicamycin (Figures S3C and S3D). Note that NMR OPCs also exhibited CD44-dependent expression of these two genes (Figure 3G). At the protein level, CD44 knockdown reduced HSPA5 protein levels by 53% in IMR90-hTert cells (Figures 4E and 4F). Protein levels of HSPA5 were also higher in NMR than in mouse in freshly isolated OPCs (Figure S2D).Modest overexpression of CD44 in IMR90 cells7 upregulated putative target genes of ATF643,45 (genes upregulated by ATF6 activating molecule AA14746 and genes co-expressed with ATF6, p values of Wilcoxon test were 1.2 × 10−4 and 1.2 × 10−13, respectively), but not those of the other two major UPR-induced transcription factors, XBP1 and ATF4 (Figure 4G). In addition, we observed significant overlap between putative target genes of ATF6 and the genes that are co-expressed with CD4447 in various types of cells (Figure 4H), suggesting that CD44 contributes to basal expression of ATF6 target genes in a broad range of cells. Multiple transcriptomic datasets (Figures 3E, 3F, 4C, 4G, and 4H), together with qPCR and western blot data (Figures 4D–4F), indicate that CD44 promotes ATF6 target gene expressions.Since HSPA5 is known as the most upstream regulator of UPR signaling pathways,48–50 we tested whether it mediates the effect of CD44 on cellular resistance to tunicamycin. We found that inhibition of HSPA5 did not affect the difference in tunicamycin resistance between control and CD44 knockdown IMR90-hTert cells (Figure 4I), suggesting that CD44 knockdown enhances tunicamycin susceptibility independent of its effect on HSPA5. Next, in order to test whether CD44 affects translocation of ATF6 to the Golgi where ATF6 gets cleaved by S1P/S2P and release active fragment,51 we transfected control and CD44 knockdown IMR90-hTert cells with a vector encoding S1P sitemutated GFP-ATF652 and stained them with antibodies against GFP and organelle marker proteins. As expected, most of GFP-ATF6 co-localized with ER marker HSP47 and a very small portion of GFP-ATF6 co-localized with 58K Golgi protein in unstressed IMR90-hTert cells. CD44 knockdown reduced the portion of GFP-ATF6 co-localizing with 58K Golgi protein by 21% (Figures S4A and S4B), which is both qualitatively and quantitatively consistent with the reduction of ATF6 target genes observed in CD44 knockdown IMR90-hTert cells (Figure S4C).CD44 knockdown did not seem to affect tunicamycin-induced activation of the upstream regulators of the three major branches of UPR pathways, namely IRE1-XBP1, PERK-ATF4, and S1P-ATF6 (Figures S4D and S4E). Responses of their downstream target genes to tunicamycin-induced ER stress were also largely unchanged by CD44 knockdown (Figure S4E). Taken together, CD44 promotes basal ATF6 activity but does not affect UPR gene expressions once exposed to strong ER stress.CD44 enhances ER stress resistance without the need of HAThe siRNAs against IRE1, PERK, and ATF6 and the inhibitors of IRE1, PERK, and S1P all diminished the differences in tunicamycin resistance between control and CD44 knockdown IMR90-hTert cells (Figures 5A–5D). These results, together with the fact that ATF6 overexpression rescued cellular resistance to tunicamycin in CD44 knockdown IMR90-hTert cells (Figure S3D), indicate that the effect of CD44 on ER stress resistance is dependent on UPR master regulators. Previous study showed that low-molecular-weight HA enhances cellular resistance to ER stress through CD44 and its downstream signal transducer Erk and p38.53 In our experimental setting, however, Erk and p38 inhibitors that have been shown to abrogate the effect of low-molecular-weight HA,53 did not diminish the difference in tunicamycin resistance between control and CD44 knockdown IMR90-hTert cells (Figure 5D). In addition, regardless of the polymer length, the presence of HA did not enhance tunicamycin resistance in control and CD44 knockdown IMR90-hTert cells (Figure S5A). Moreover, neither Streptomyces hyaluronidase (HAase) nor a well-validated neutralizing antibody of CD44 (clone KM114)54 affected tunicamycin resistance of IMR90-hTert cells (Figure S5B).It should be noted, however, that knockdown efficiencies of shCD44–1 and shCD44–2 did not correlate with loss of tunicamycin resistance in IMR90-hTert cells, which could be due to the increased XBP1s levels in cells expressing shCD44–2 (Figure 4D). Thus, in order to further confirm our findings, we designed two human CD44 siRNAs whose sequences are different from those of CD44 shRNAs that were used to stably knockdown CD44 in IMR90-hTert cells. Acute knockdown of CD44 using these siRNAs resulted in the reduction of ATF6 target gene expressions and cellular resistance to tunicamycin in IMR90 cells (Figures S5C–S5F). The effects of these CD44 siRNAs on tunicamycin resistance were abrogated by the inhibition of IRE1, PERK, and S1P, but were not affected by supplementation of HA (Figures S5E and S5F). Enhancement of ER stress resistance by CD44 overexpression in mouse OPCs was also blocked by inhibition of UPR regulators but not by HA supplementation (Figures S5G–S5I). Taken together, our data indicate that CD44 enhances ER stress resistance in a manner dependent on ER-resident UPR regulators without the requirement of HA.CD44 localizes to and modulates the ERWe previously identified CD44 binding protein candidates in IMR90 cells by cross-linked immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry.7 In the current study, we defined high-confidence CD44 binding proteins as the proteins detected by mass spectrometry using anti-CD44 antibody with at least 10-fold greater abundance than in control experiment using normal immunoglobulin (Ig)G. This resulted in the list of 41 CD44 binding proteins (Table S3). ER proteins accounted for 16 out of 41 CD44 binding proteins (Figure 6A). Association between CD44 and ER proteins were confirmed by immunoprecipitation-western blot (Figure 6B). While IRE1, PERK, and ATF6 were not in the list, protein-protein interaction analysis using the STRING database55 indicated that three out of eight ATF6 binding proteins, namely, CALR, HSPA5, and HSP90B1, were included in the CD44 binding proteins (Figure 6A).To our surprise, confocal imaging of IMR90 cells co-stained with antibodies against CD44 and organelle markers showed clear and strong co-localization of CD44 with ER markers ERp57, HSP47, and RPN2, but not with mitochondrial marker mtTFA (Figures 6C and 6D). Co-localization was observed regardless of the fixation method (PFA and MeOH). CD44 knockdown reduced the intracellular immunofluorescence signals of CD44, indicating that the signals were not due to the unspecific binding of the antibody (Figures S6A and S6B). We also confirmed that the correlation between immunofluorescence signals of CD44 and ER marker was not due to unspecific binding of the secondary antibodies or fluorescent bleed-through (Figure S6C). The results of CD44 immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry and CD44 immunofluorescence staining collectively indicate that CD44 localizes to the ER and interacts with ER proteins including those associated with the ATF6 pathway in IMR90 cells. NMR OPCs and U2OS cells also exhibited ER localization of CD44 (Figures S6D and S6E).In order to examine how the ER itself is impacted by CD44, we performed proteomic mass spectrometry of the ER isolated from control and CD44 knockdown IMR90-hTert cells after 24 h of tunicamycin treatment (Figure 7A). Expression profiles of ER and ER-associated proteins showed clear separation between control and CD44 knockdown IMR90-hTert cells (Figure 7A; Table S4). Ribosomal proteins (RPL5 and RPL37A) and proteins involved in translocation of nascent polypeptides (SEC61B and SEC62), were in the top 15 proteins that were decreased in CD44 knockdown cells (Figures 7A and 7B). Protein synthesis rate, however, seems irrelevant to CD44-dependent tunicamycin resistance, because low concentrations of protein synthesis inhibitor, puromycin, did not affect tunicamycin resistance in control and CD44 knockdown IMR90 cells (Figures S7A and S7B). The top 15 proteins that were increased in CD44 knockdown cells included collagens (COL1A2, COL6A1, and COL6A3) (Figures 7A and 7B). The effect of CD44 knockdown on COL6A3 was not dependent on ATF6, indicating that it is not a downstream consequence of reduced ATF6 activity (Figure S7C).In addition to its effects on the ER proteome, we found that CD44 knockdown enhanced detergent solubility of ER transmembrane proteins IRE1, PERK, and ATF6 in IMR90-hTert cells. DISCUSSIONIn this study, we isolated and characterized NMR OPCs and found that differences between long- and short-lived mammals were much larger in OPCs than in the whole brain. The only common feature we detected in long-lived mammals’ OPCs was the high expression of ECM-related genes. It is important to note, however, that these differences in OPC transcriptome may be associated with species’ traits irrelevant to maximum lifespan. It is also possible that transcriptomes of OPCs are affected by subtle differences in culture conditions or age at which the cells were harvested. On the other hand, correlation between maximum lifespan and CD44 levels was observed not only in OPCs but also in liver, skin, and fibroblasts. CD44 was partially responsible for shaping transcriptional signature common to long-lived species in OPCs. Moreover, CD44 promoted ATF6 target gene expressions and enhanced ER stress resistance in both NMR and human cells. CD44 localized to the ER and associated with ER proteins including those related to the ATF6 pathway. CD44 substantially modified ER proteome, changed the properties of the ER membrane, promoted ER-to-Golgi transport of ATF6, and conferred ER stress resistance in a manner dependent on IRE1, PERK, and ATF6.Our results suggest that CD44 may regulate longevity not only by mediating HA signals at the plasma membrane7,53 but also by enhancing basal ATF6 activity and ER stress resistance through its function in the ER. UPR plays a crucial role in lifespan regulation in yeast, worm, and fly,53,58–61 and pharmacological activation of ATF6 has recently been reported to protect various tissues from ischemia-reperfusion injury in the mouse.62 In yeast, it has been reported that the enhancement of basal UPR activity extends lifespan.63 The importance of basal UPR activity in maintaining normal physiology has also been well established in mammals.64,65 In addition, experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis and age-dependent myelin disorder can be protected by moderate activation of UPR,66 suggesting that CD44-dependent enhancement of ATF6 activity and ER stress resistance contribute to healthy aging.Limitations of the studyAlthough we showed that CD44 correlated with longevity and enhanced basal ATF6 activity and ER stress resistance, their underlying mechanism is far from completely understood. However, while we showed that CD44 altered detergent solubility of ER membrane proteins, changes induced by CD44 in ER membrane lipid composition were not investigated in the current study. Mechanisms by which CD44 affects membrane properties of the ER also remain unknown. In addition, in the current study, causative relationships were tested by cell culture experiments, and we were only able to test correlative relationships in in vivo samples. Therefore, the physiological relevance of our findings remains speculative and warrants further investigation.

METHOD DETAILSIsolation and culture of mouse and NMR OPCsOPC isolation was performed according to previous protocols27–30 with minor modifications. First, a litter of NMR or C57BL/6J mouse pups (postnatal day 1–10) were euthanized on ice, washed by tap water, and decapitated. Heads were further washed once in 10% bleach, 70% ethanol, and then three times in PBS. Brains were collected from the heads in a clean bench, minced with scissors, and then dissociated with Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotech; cat. 130–092-628) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 400 mg of minced brain was suspended in 1,950 μL of enzyme mix 1 (1,900 μL of buffer X supplemented with 50 μL of enzyme P) and gently mixed by rotation for 15 min at 37°C. After adding 30 μL of enzyme mix 2 (enzyme A 10 μL + buffer Y 20 μL), samples were gently mixed by inverting the tubes. Samples were then triturated with fire-polished Pasteur pipette. Pipettes with three different pore sizes were prepared. Samples were first triturated 10 times using the widest pipette, and then 10 times each using the remaining two pipettes in decreasing diameter. Triturated sample were incubated again at 37°C for 15 min, diluted with 5 volumes of HBSS (with Ca2+/Mg2+), and filtered through 100 μM cell strainer. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation (300 × g, 10 min), washed once with HBSS (with Ca2+/Mg2+), and resuspended in HBSS (Ca2+/Mg2+ free) (70 μL per 1 × 107 cells). Cells were kept on ice for 5 min, mixed with 20 μL anti-A2B5 microbeads (Miltenyi Biotech; cat. 130–093-388), and incubated again on ice for 15 min. Cells were then washed twice with 1 mL MACS buffer (DPBS supplemented with 1 g/L glucose, 0.5% BSA, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 5 μg/mL insulin) and resuspended in 500 μL MACS buffer. MACS LS column (Miltenyi Biotech, cat. 130–042-401) was set on a MACS magnetic stand and equilibrated with 3 mL MACS buffer. Cells in MACS buffer were transferred to the column and the column was washed 4 times with 3 mL MACS buffer while on the magnetic stand. METHOD DETAILS

Isolation and culture of mouse and NMR OPCsOPC isolation was performed according to previous protocols27–30 with minor modifications. First, a litter of NMR or C57BL/6J mouse pups (postnatal day 1–10) were euthanized on ice, washed by tap water, and decapitated. Heads were further washed once in 10% bleach, 70% ethanol, and then three times in PBS. Brains were collected from the heads in a clean bench, minced with scissors, and then dissociated with Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotech; cat. 130–092-628) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 400 mg of minced brain was suspended in 1,950 μL of enzyme mix 1 (1,900 μL of buffer X supplemented with 50 μL of enzyme P) and gently mixed by rotation for 15 min at 37°C. After adding 30 μL of enzyme mix 2 (enzyme A 10 μL + buffer Y 20 μL), samples were gently mixed by inverting the tubes. Samples were then triturated with fire-polished Pasteur pipette. Pipettes with three different pore sizes were prepared. Samples were first triturated 10 times using the widest pipette, and then 10 times each using the remaining two pipettes in decreasing diameter. Triturated sample were incubated again at 37°C for 15 min, diluted with 5 volumes of HBSS (with Ca2+/Mg2+), and filtered through 100 μM cell strainer. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation (300 × g, 10 min), washed once with HBSS (with Ca2+/Mg2+), and resuspended in HBSS (Ca2+/Mg2+ free) (70 μL per 1 × 107 cells). Cells were kept on ice for 5 min, mixed with 20 μL anti-A2B5 microbeads (Miltenyi Biotech; cat. 130–093-388), and incubated again on ice for 15 min. Cells were then washed twice with 1 mL MACS buffer (DPBS supplemented with 1 g/L glucose, 0.5% BSA, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 5 μg/mL insulin) and resuspended in 500 μL MACS buffer. MACS LS column (Miltenyi Biotech, cat. 130–042-401) was set on a MACS magnetic stand and equilibrated with 3 mL MACS buffer. Cells in MACS buffer were transferred to the column and the column was washed 4 times with 3 mL MACS buffer while on the magnetic stand. After washing, the column was removed from the magnet, and the A2B5+ cells were eluted with 2 mL pre-warmed OPC-A buffer and plated on 6 cm dish.

Isolation of the endoplasmic reticulum and mass spectrometryIMR90-hTert cells were treated with 0.5 μg/mL tunicamycin for 24 h and then detached from the dishes using Accutase for endoplasmic reticulum isolation. Cells were collected in 5% FBS/PBS, pelleted by centrifugation (300 × g, 5 min), and resuspended in 2 mL of ice-cold MTE buffer (270 mM D-mannitol, 10 mM Tris-base, 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai Tesque, cat. 04080–11). Following procedures were performed at 4°C. Cells were homogenized using a Teflon pestle by 60 strokes at 4,000 rpm and the samples were centrifuged at 1,400 × g for 10 min. Supernatants were transferred to fresh tubes and centrifuged again at 15,000 × g for 10 min. Supernatants were loaded onto a three-layered sucrose gradient (2 mL of 2 M sucrose buffer +3 mL of 1.5 M sucrose buffer +3 mL of 1.3 M sucrose buffer; each containing 10 mM Tris-base and 0.1 mM EDTA with pH adjusted to 7.6) and the endoplasmic reticulum was banded by centrifugation at 160,000 × g for 60 min. Endoplasmic reticulum layer was collected, transferred to fresh tubes, and the samples were centrifuged again at 160,000 × g for 1 h. After discarding the supernatant, the pellets were air dried and lysed in 2 × Laemmli buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. Endoplasmic reticulum lysates were then run on PAGE and the proteins were digested and recovered using In-Gel Tryptic Digestion Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. 89871) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The recovered digests were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid and separated using Nano-LC-Ultra 2D-plus equipped with cHiPLC Nanoflex (Eksigent) in trap-and-elute mode, with trap column (200 μm × 0.5 μm ChromXP C18-CL 3 μm 120 Å) and analytical column (75 μm × 15 cm ChromXP C18-CL 3 μm 120 Å). Separation was carried out using a binary gradient in which 0.1% formic acid/water and 0.1% formic acid/acetonitrile were used as solvent A and B, respectively. The gradient program was as follows; 2 to 33.2% B for 125 min, 33.2 to 98% B in 2 min, 98% B for 5 min, 98 to 2% B in 0.1 min, and 2% B for 17.9 min. The flow rate was 300 nL/min. The analytical column temperature was set to 40°C. The eluates were infused on-line to TripleTOF 5600+ System with NanoSpray III source and heated interface (SCIEX) and ionized in an electrospray ionization-positive mode. Data acquisition was carried out with an information-dependent acquisition method. The acquired datasets were analyzed using ProteinPilot software version 5.0.1 (SCIEX) for peptide and protein identification. The quality of the database search was confirmed by the false discovery rate analysis in which the reversed amino acid sequences were used as decoy. Relative abundances of the identified proteins were estimated by Progenesis QI for Proteomics software version 4.2, employing Hi-N (3) method (Nonlinear Dynamics). All raw data files with wiff format were imported to generate aggregate and the results of ProteinPilot identification with confidence at least 95% were imported for peptide assignment.
