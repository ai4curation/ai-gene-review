Alanyl-tRNA Synthetase 1 (AARS1) is a bifunctional enzyme with emerging roles in
lactate sensing and protein lactylation (Kla), both driving tumor progression.
However, its clinical significance across diverse cancer types remains largely
unexplored. Leveraging comprehensive bioinformatics analysis across multiple
public repositories, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Genotype-Tissue
Expression (GTEx), Tumor IMmune Estimation Resource (TIMER), The University of
Alabama at Birmingham CANcer data analysis Portal (UALCAN), and Human Protein
Atlas (HPA), we systematically evaluated AARS1 expression and its associations
with TP53 mutations, DNA methylation, prognostic outcomes, and genetic
alterations, as well as its correlation with the immune microenvironment.
Functional enrichment analyses were further conducted to elucidate its molecular
mechanisms. Moreover, immunohistochemistry (IHC) in renal and lung cancers,
complemented by in vitro functional assays in renal cancer cells, validated its
oncogenic role. Our results demonstrate that AARS1 is significantly upregulated
in most malignancies and correlates with poor survival rates. Notably, AARS1
exhibited high diagnostic efficacy (AUC > 0.85) and was inversely associated
with antitumor immune infiltration. Functional downregulation of AARS1
significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of
these cells, whereas overexpression of the enzymatically inactive mutant failed
to rescue these inhibitory effects, further confirming that its pro-tumorigenic
role is dependent on its lactyltransferase activity. This inaugural pan-cancer
study highlights AARS1 as a robust diagnostic and prognostic biomarker,
immunological indicator, and promising therapeutic target across multiple
cancers.

Introduction

Malignant tumors are the second leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for approximately 10 million deaths in 2022, and this number is projected to rise to 35 million by 2050 [1, 2]. Recent evidence indicates that tumor initiation, progression, recurrence, and metastasis are closely influenced by the tumor microenvironment (TME) [3, 4]. Lactylation, a lactate-driven post-translational modification, reshapes tumor cell metabolism by modulating the functions of histone and non-histone proteins [5]. This modification enables tumor cells to adapt to hypoxic and nutrient-deprived conditions, sustaining the activation of the glycolytic pathway [6]. Increasing studies have demonstrated that lactylation directly regulates gene transcription, including oncogene expression and epigenetic modifications, thereby promoting tumor initiation, proliferation, and metastasis [7–9]. Consequently, identifying key regulators of lactylation and elucidating their roles in various cancers is of great importance.

AARS1 is a key member of the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (aaRS) family that catalyzes the attachment of alanine to its cognate tRNA. Dysregulation of AARS1 has been implicated in the development of multiple cancers [10, 11]. Recent studies have identified AARS1 as a novel lactate sensor capable of binding lactate and generating a lactyl-AMP intermediate, which transfers lactate to the lysine residues of target proteins, thereby directly catalyzing lysine lactylation [12]. Mechanistically, AARS1-mediated lactylation of p53 suppresses its liquid–liquid phase separation, DNA binding, and transcriptional activity, resulting in impaired tumor-suppressive functions and enhanced tumor progression. In addition, AARS1 promotes gastric cancer cell proliferation by activating the YAP–TEAD complex through lactylation and establishing a positive feedback loop with the Hippo pathway [13]. Furthermore, AARS1-dependent lactylation of YTHDC1 alters downstream gene expression and reduces the sensitivity of bladder cancer cells to enfortumab vedotin treatment [14]. As a newly identified lactyltransferase, the role of AARS1 in the initiation and progression of various cancers remains largely unknown.

Given the lack of comprehensive pan-cancer studies, we systematically studied AARS1 by analyzing multiple datasets, including TCGA, GTEx project, UALCAN, cBioPortal, and HPA. This underscores the potential significance of AARS1 in cancer research and therapy. We further validated its role in tumorigenesis through IHC analysis of patient tissue samples and functional assays in cancer cells. Overall, our findings indicate that AARS1 can serve as a marker associated with immune cell infiltration and as an indicator of poor prognosis, making it a promising therapeutic target in various cancers.

Gene expression analysisAARS1 expression levels were analyzed using multiple online databases. IHC images of AARS1 in tumor and normal tissues were obtained from the HPA database (http://www.proteinatlas.org/). Prognostic analysis of AARS1 in pan-cancerClinical data obtained from the TCGA and GTEx databases were used to investigate the correlation between the expression levels of AARS1 and the prognosis of various tumors. Prognostic expression pattern analysis was conducted using the Xiantao Academic online platform (https://www.xiantaozi.com/) and the Kaplan–Meier Plotter database (http://kmplot.com/). We compared of overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), and progression-free interval (PFI) between cohorts with high and low AARS1 expression levels across a spectrum of 32 different cancer types. The patient cohort was stratified into two groups based on a 50% expression cut-off, and disparities in survival outcomes were evaluated using the log-rank test. In addition, using the Xiantao Academic Online website (https://www.xiantao.love/), we performed operating characteristic (ROC) curves analysis using the R language.

Correlation between AARS1 expression and TP53 mutation across cancersTP53 is a critical tumor suppressor that preserves genomic stability via multiple pathways [15]. In human cancers, TP53 frequently loses its function due to mutations or deletions, resulting in a markedly increased risk of tumorigenesis [16]. Recent studies have shown that AARS1 functions as a novel lactyltransferase that promotes p53 lactylation. To assess the clinical relevance of this potential interaction, we analyzed AARS1 expression in tumors stratified by TP53 mutation status. Using the UALCAN database, we found that AARS1 expression was significantly higher in BLCA, BRCA, COAD, LUAD, LUSC, HNSC, LIHC, ESCA, pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), and READ with TP53 mutations than in non-mutant groups (Fig. 2D). This observation may reflect the broader context of genomic instability or selective pressures commonly associated with TP53-mutant tumors. Notably, this finding is consistent with previous reports suggesting a possible functional interaction between AARS1 and p53. Furthermore, analysis of the TP53/Rb signaling pathway revealed that AARS1 expression was significantly higher in tumors with pathway alterations, including breast, renal clear cell, uterine corpus endometrial, lung, PAAD, and head and neck cancers (Fig. 2E). These results suggest that AARS1 upregulation may be part of a broader program associated with cell cycle checkpoint dysregulation, rather than a direct consequence of TP53 mutation alone.

AARS1 promotes the growth of ccRCC cells and tumorigenesis in vitroTo further validate the role of AARS1 in tumors, we analyzed AARS1 expression in tumor tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues from LUAD and KIRC patients using immunohistochemical staining and found that it was markedly overexpressed in the tumor tissues of both LUAD and KIRC (Fig. 12A, B). Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) shows strong characteristics of the Warburg effect [17, 18]. Next, we assessed AARS1 expression in human renal tubular epithelial cells (HK-2) and multiple renal cancer cell lines using western blotting. AARS1 was upregulated in several ccRCC cell lines compared to HK-2, with the highest expression observed in OS-RC-2 and 786-O cells (Fig. 12C). We then ectopically overexpressed AARS1 in OS-RC-2 and 786-O cells and knocked down its expression using siRNA. qRT-PCR (Fig. 12D, E) and western blot analyses (Fig. 12F) confirmed the efficient modulation of AARS1 expression. Using lactylation antibody detection, we found that AARS1 knockdown significantly reduced global lactylation and p53 lactylation levels, whereas AARS1 overexpression significantly increased global lactylation and p53 lactylation levels (Fig. 12G, H). Functional assays were performed to assess the effects of AARS1 expressing on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. CCK-8 assays showed that AARS1 overexpression significantly promoted cell proliferation, whereas knockdown markedly inhibited it (Fig. 12I). EdU assays further demonstrated that AARS1 overexpression increased the proportion of proliferating cells, whereas knockdown suppressed proliferation (Fig. 12J). Transwell assays indicated that AARS1 overexpression enhanced migration and invasion of OS-RC-2 and 786-O cells (Fig. 12K, L). Consistently, wound healing assays showed that AARS1 overexpression accelerated wound closure compared to control cells (Fig. 12M, N). Importantly, we constructed an AARS1 mutant (AARS1-5A) that was deficient in lactyltransferase activity, in which all the amino acids that bind lactate in AARS1 were mutated to alanine [12]. Notably, the results of the functional rescue experiments showed that in AARS1-knockdown 786-O and ACHN cells, re-expression of wild-type AARS1 could significantly restore the reduced cell proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities. However, overexpression of the lactyltransferase activity-deficient AARS1-5A mutant completely failed to rescue these phenotypic defects caused by AARS1 knockdown. This result strongly confirms that the pro-tumorigenic effect of AARS1 in clear cell renal cell carcinoma strictly depends on its lactyltransferase activity (Fig. 12O-R). DiscussionThis study provides the first systematic evidence of the clinical significance of AARS1 across a broad spectrum of malignancies. By leveraging multi-omic datasets, we have delineated a comprehensive landscape of AARS1, encompassing its prevalent overexpression, its interplay with TP53 mutations and DNA methylation, and its association with adverse prognostic outcomes, TMB, and oncogenic pathway activation. In ccRCC, AARS1 knockdown significantly impaired cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. These findings preliminarily validate that the oncogenic potential of AARS1 is intrinsically linked to its newly identified lactyltransferase activity, offering a novel perspective on metabolic-epigenetic regulation in cancer.AARS1, a member of the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (AARS) family, is a canonical enzyme that catalyzes the ligation of alanine to its corresponding tRNA, playing a fundamental role in protein translation [19, 20]. Lactylation, a recently identified post-translational modification, functions as an intracellular mechanism for lactate sensing and transfer [21, 22]. Intriguingly, recent studies have identified AARS1 as a pan-lactyltransferase, capable of sensing lactate levels and mediating the transfer of lactate moieties to target proteins [11, 23]. While previous research has implicated AARS1 in tumor proliferation and progression through specific pathways, its systematic role as a newly recognized lactyltransferase across the pan-cancer landscape remains largely unexplored [24, 25]. In this study, we present the first comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of AARS1, systematically evaluating its genomic landscape, expression profiles, prognostic implications, and immunological associations. Our findings provide novel insights into the multifaceted roles of AARS1 in cancer biology and its potential as a metabolic-epigenetic therapeutic target.Our results demonstrate that AARS1 is significantly overexpressed in 11 cancer types, consistent with data from the GTEx and TCGA databases. Notably, AARS1 expression in KIRC exhibited discrepancies between the TCGA and UALCAN databases. This divergence may reflect a cellular adaptation to metabolic stress, where cancer cells maintaining high levels of AARS1 protein to support the translational demands of rapid proliferation or to modulate specific signaling pathways. In the context of KIRC, lactyltransferase activity, rather than steady-state mRNA levels, may serve as the primary functional output. Multiple predicted ubiquitination sites on the AARS1 protein, as shown in Fig. 10E, suggest that its protein stability may be tightly regulated by the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway. In KIRC, abnormalities in the tumor microenvironment may lead to increased deubiquitinase activity, thereby reducing ubiquitination-mediated degradation of AARS1 and enabling its protein to accumulate, even when its transcription level is low. This post-translational stabilization may also be one of the key mechanisms explaining the protein-transcript dissociation in KIRC. Furthermore, analysis of OS, DFS, and ROC curves highlights the prognostic significance of AARS1. While high AARS1 expression correlates with poor prognosis in some cancers, such as ACC, it appears to exert a protective effect in others, including LGG. These findings suggest that the clinical utility of AARS1 as a diagnostic biomarker may be optimized by prioritizing cancers with exceptionally high AUC values, such as HNSC, COAD, and ESCC, rather than adopting a universal pan-cancer approach.Our study revealed a significant upregulation of AARS1 in TP53-mutant tumors, a noteworthy correlation that warrants further investigation. Given that existing literature suggests AARS1 can catalyze the lactylation of p53, this finding provides compelling evidence for a potential metabolic-epigenetic cross-talk between AARS1 and p53 during tumorigenesis. However, our current data do not definitively establish whether this co-occurrence stems from a direct upstream–downstream regulatory axis or a synchronized response to broader oncogenic stressors, such as genomic instability or metabolic reprogramming. Furthermore, elevated AARS1 levels and its associated lactyltransferase activity may indirectly facilitate TP53 inactivation by modulating auxiliary signaling pathways, collectively fueling tumor progression. Consequently, deciphering the precise mechanistic link between AARS1 and the TP53 signaling cascade remains a pivotal objective for future research.Beyond genetic alterations, DNA methylation serves as a critical mechanism for transcriptional control [26]. We observed that AARS1 methylation frequency was significantly diminished in various malignant tissues compared to normal controls, while the somatic mutation rate of AARS1 remained relatively low. This suggests that the overexpression of AARS1 is primarily driven by epigenetic remodeling (hypomethylation) rather than genomic mutations. Consistent with this, reduced DNA methylation levels of AARS1 were closely correlated with a dismal prognosis. Our findings indicate that promoter hypomethylation facilitates AARS1 upregulation, thereby driving malignant phenotypes. Thus, reversing AARS1 epigenetic activation or pharmacologically inhibiting its enzymatic activity may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for suppressing tumor progression.Our study indicates that AARS1 profoundly modulates immune cell functionality and infiltration patterns across the pan-cancer landscape. A series of correlation analyses confirmed that AARS1 expression is inversely associated with immune cell infiltration and the expression of CD8⁺ T cell markers. Previous reports have highlighted that high AARS1 expression in HNSC correlate with diminished CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and upregulation of immunosuppressive molecules, such as Siglec-15 and ITPRIPL1, suggesting a role in tumor immune evasion [24, 27]. Consistently, our pan-cancer analysis demonstrates that elevated AARS1 expression is negatively correlated with CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, implying suppression of cytotoxic immune responses. However, the immunological impact of AARS1 may not solely depend on its absolute expression levels, but rather on its functional enzymatic state. In LUAD, highly expressed AARS1 may actively suppress anti-tumor immunity by lactylating specific substrates. Conversely, in other malignancies, even basal AARS1 expression may drive immunosuppression once its activity is triggered by microenvironmental stimuli, such as elevated lactate levels. Furthermore, AARS1 may also directly modify key immune checkpoints, such as PD-L1, through its lactyltransferase activity, thereby affecting their protein expression or stability. This offers a new molecular mechanism for understanding how tumor metabolic reprogramming shapes the immunosuppressive microenvironment.Our analysis revealed a positive correlation between AARS1 expression and exhausted T cell (Exhausted_ImmucellAI) and cytotoxic T cell (Cytotoxic_ImmucellAI) signatures. This suggests that AARS1, by catalyzing the lactylation of key signaling proteins within the TME, may not only impair innate immunity but also directly or indirectly facilitate T cell exhaustion, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive milieu. It should be noted that this finding is currently based only on bioinformatic inference at the transcriptome level and cannot yet be regarded as direct evidence of T cell functional exhaustion. Future research should use techniques such as flow cytometry or single-cell sequencing to directly examine whether CD8+ T cells in tumors with high AARS1 expression co-express multiple immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3, to confirm their true state of exhaustion. Furthermore, the association between AARS1 and CD8+ T cell infiltration exhibits significant heterogeneity across molecular subtypes. For instance, in BRCA-Basal and BRCA-Her2, AARS1 expression is positively correlated with CD8+ T cell counts, whereas this correlation remains negligible in BRCA-LumB. These findings underscore that the immunomodulatory role of AARS1 is highly tumor-type and subtype-specific, potentially reflecting the diverse metabolic and immune landscapes across cancers. Collectively, these insights position AARS1 as a promising biomarker for predicting prognostic outcomes and therapeutic responses to immunotherapy.Most studies on AARS1 to date have focused on neurological diseases and tumors. Studies have shown that mutations in AARS1 impair its aminoacylation activity in Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease, potentially triggering inflammatory responses and mitochondrial dysfunction [28]. Additionally, AARS1 variants can cause early infantile-onset disorders, often accompanied by epileptic encephalopathy and defects in myelination [29]. However, recent studies show that AARS1 mediates lysine-312 lactylation of the histone methyltransferase complex subunit ASH2L, promoting the malignant progression of hepatocellular carcinoma [30]. And inhibition of AARS1-mediated BLM lactylation by irinotecan alleviates chemoresistance to anthracyclines and affects homologous recombination repair [31]. Collectively, these studies underscore the critical role of AARS1 in regulating protein function and cellular metabolism. Given that genetic variations in AARS1 itself are rare, therapies targeting its overexpression or abnormal activity, such as developing small-molecule inhibitors that specifically block its lactylation catalytic pocket, may represent a broad-spectrum and mutation-resistant strategy. Notably, specific AARS1 inhibitors have been identified, L-alanine, which shares structural similarity with lactate, can competitively block lactate binding to AARS1 and inhibit tumor growth in animal models [12], offering a promising therapeutic strategy for targeting lactylation modifications.KEGG and GO analyses of AARS1 revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation, the TCA cycle, and mitochondrial gene expression, highlighting the critical role of AARS1’s lactate transferase activity in regulating cellular metabolism. Notably, the association of AARS1 with mitochondrial function and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress suggests that its oncogenic effects may be mediated through inter-organelle communication. AARS1 likely modifies mitochondrial or ER-resident proteins via lactylation, thereby optimizing energy metabolism and proteostasis. This metabolic adaptation enables tumor cells to thrive under stress and provides a novel mechanistic explanation for its role in facilitating immune evasion. As a key node in metabolite-translation coupling, AARS1 may sense elevated lactate levels resulting from the Warburg effect and mitochondrial respiration, and through its lactyltransferase activity, convert metabolic signals into post-translational modifications of key target proteins, thus driving tumor progression. Additionally, phosphorylation site prediction analyses indicate that AARS1 possesses numerous phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and acetylation sites. However, the functional roles of specific sites in post-translational modifications and disease processes remain unclear. Previous studies have also reported that methyltransferase-like protein 21C (METTL21C) catalyzes methylation at Lys-943 (AARS1-K943me), modulating protein synthesis in muscle tissues [32]. These findings suggest that AARS1, as a lactate transferase, may regulate cellular lactylation through post-translational modifications, but the precise mechanisms warrant further investigation.Immunohistochemical analysis of tumor and adjacent tissues from LUAD and KIRC patients confirmed that AARS1 is overexpressed in both cancer types (Fig. 12A). KIRC exhibits strong metabolic reprogramming, leading to enhanced glycolysis and increased lactate production, thereby providing abundant substrate for lactylation [33, 34]. Therefore, identifying key molecules involved in lactylation in renal cancer is critical for improving clinical outcomes. Functional assays in ccRCC cells further demonstrated that AARS1 knockdown markedly inhibited cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas AARS1 overexpression produced the opposite effects. These findings provide important insights into the role of AARS1 in metabolic reprogramming and lactylation-mediated tumor progression in ccRCC. Although AARS1 has limited diagnostic and universal prognostic value in KIRC, our preliminary analyses and experiments have validated its clear oncogenic role. This indicates that the carcinogenic mechanism of AARS1 may be independent of changes in its expression levels and relies more on the regulation of its substrates through lactylation modification activity. Therefore, KIRC is an ideal model for studying its lactyltransferase activity rather than the mechanism of carcinogenesis due to increased expression.As a newly discovered lactyltransferase, AARS1 may achieve substrate selectivity through multiple mechanisms. Structurally, AARS1 possesses an evolutionarily conserved amino acid-binding domain capable of recognizing specific amino acids [35]. Recent studies have shown that AARS1 undergoes nuclear translocation upon lactate stimulation, enabling it to access specific substrate proteins within different cellular compartments [13]. In addition, the non-catalytic regions of AARS1 may directly interact with specific motifs on target proteins. Under catalytic action, AARS1 transfers the lactyl group from L-lactate to lysine residues in an ATP-dependent manner. This CoA-independent mechanism may impose constraints on the local microenvironment of target lysines, thereby promoting substrate selectivity [36]. Future research, by identifying the interactome of AARS1, will further elucidate the molecular basis of its substrate specificity.Admittedly, this study is subject to several limitations. Although the conclusions have been validated across multiple datasets, further in vivo or in vitro experiments using human and animal samples are still needed for confirmation. Second, the reported high diagnostic efficacy of AARS1 (AUC > 0.85) was primarily derived from retrospective internal validation within public bioinformatics repositories. Therefore, its actual clinical utility and predictive performance must be rigorously evaluated in independent, prospective clinical cohorts to ensure its reliability as a diagnostic biomarker.In conclusion, this study provides a systematic and integrative characterization of AARS1 expression landscapes, prognostic significance, and biological functionalities through a multi-dimensional bioinformatics lens. our findings highlight the correlation between AARS1 expression, clinical prognosis, and immune infiltration, establishing a compelling theoretical framework for its role as a novel diagnostic, prognostic, and immunological biomarker. Moreover, through functional validation of its lactyltransferase activity in renal cancer cells, this study establishes a robust foundation for future precision oncology strategies targeting the AARS1-mediated metabolic-epigenetic axis.

This post-translational stabilization may also be one of the key mechanisms explaining the protein-transcript dissociation in KIRC. Furthermore, analysis of OS, DFS, and ROC curves highlights the prognostic significance of AARS1. While high AARS1 expression correlates with poor prognosis in some cancers, such as ACC, it appears to exert a protective effect in others, including LGG. These findings suggest that the clinical utility of AARS1 as a diagnostic biomarker may be optimized by prioritizing cancers with exceptionally high AUC values, such as HNSC, COAD, and ESCC, rather than adopting a universal pan-cancer approach.

Our study revealed a significant upregulation of AARS1 in TP53-mutant tumors, a noteworthy correlation that warrants further investigation. Given that existing literature suggests AARS1 can catalyze the lactylation of p53, this finding provides compelling evidence for a potential metabolic-epigenetic cross-talk between AARS1 and p53 during tumorigenesis. However, our current data do not definitively establish whether this co-occurrence stems from a direct upstream–downstream regulatory axis or a synchronized response to broader oncogenic stressors, such as genomic instability or metabolic reprogramming. Furthermore, elevated AARS1 levels and its associated lactyltransferase activity may indirectly facilitate TP53 inactivation by modulating auxiliary signaling pathways, collectively fueling tumor progression. Consequently, deciphering the precise mechanistic link between AARS1 and the TP53 signaling cascade remains a pivotal objective for future research.

Beyond genetic alterations, DNA methylation serves as a critical mechanism for transcriptional control [26]. We observed that AARS1 methylation frequency was significantly diminished in various malignant tissues compared to normal controls, while the somatic mutation rate of AARS1 remained relatively low. This suggests that the overexpression of AARS1 is primarily driven by epigenetic remodeling (hypomethylation) rather than genomic mutations. Consistent with this, reduced DNA methylation levels of AARS1 were closely correlated with a dismal prognosis. Our findings indicate that promoter hypomethylation facilitates AARS1 upregulation, thereby driving malignant phenotypes. Thus, reversing AARS1 epigenetic activation or pharmacologically inhibiting its enzymatic activity may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for suppressing tumor progression.

Our study indicates that AARS1 profoundly modulates immune cell functionality and infiltration patterns across the pan-cancer landscape. A series of correlation analyses confirmed that AARS1 expression is inversely associated with immune cell infiltration and the expression of CD8⁺ T cell markers. Previous reports have highlighted that high AARS1 expression in HNSC correlate with diminished CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and upregulation of immunosuppressive molecules, such as Siglec-15 and ITPRIPL1, suggesting a role in tumor immune evasion [24, 27]. Consistently, our pan-cancer analysis demonstrates that elevated AARS1 expression is negatively correlated with CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, implying suppression of cytotoxic immune responses. However, the immunological impact of AARS1 may not solely depend on its absolute expression levels, but rather on its functional enzymatic state. In LUAD, highly expressed AARS1 may actively suppress anti-tumor immunity by lactylating specific substrates. Conversely, in other malignancies, even basal AARS1 expression may drive immunosuppression once its activity is triggered by microenvironmental stimuli, such as elevated lactate levels. Furthermore, AARS1 may also directly modify key immune checkpoints, such as PD-L1, through its lactyltransferase activity, thereby affecting their protein expression or stability. This offers a new molecular mechanism for understanding how tumor metabolic reprogramming shapes the immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Our analysis revealed a positive correlation between AARS1 expression and exhausted T cell (Exhausted_ImmucellAI) and cytotoxic T cell (Cytotoxic_ImmucellAI) signatures. This suggests that AARS1, by catalyzing the lactylation of key signaling proteins within the TME, may not only impair innate immunity but also directly or indirectly facilitate T cell exhaustion, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive milieu. It should be noted that this finding is currently based only on bioinformatic inference at the transcriptome level and cannot yet be regarded as direct evidence of T cell functional exhaustion. Future research should use techniques such as flow cytometry or single-cell sequencing to directly examine whether CD8+ T cells in tumors with high AARS1 expression co-express multiple immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3, to confirm their true state of exhaustion. Furthermore, the association between AARS1 and CD8+ T cell infiltration exhibits significant heterogeneity across molecular subtypes. For instance, in BRCA-Basal and BRCA-Her2, AARS1 expression is positively correlated with CD8+ T cell counts, whereas this correlation remains negligible in BRCA-LumB. These findings underscore that the immunomodulatory role of AARS1 is highly tumor-type and subtype-specific, potentially reflecting the diverse metabolic and immune landscapes across cancers. Collectively, these insights position AARS1 as a promising biomarker for predicting prognostic outcomes and therapeutic responses to immunotherapy.

Most studies on AARS1 to date have focused on neurological diseases and tumors. Studies have shown that mutations in AARS1 impair its aminoacylation activity in Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease, potentially triggering inflammatory responses and mitochondrial dysfunction [28]. Additionally, AARS1 variants can cause early infantile-onset disorders, often accompanied by epileptic encephalopathy and defects in myelination [29]. However, recent studies show that AARS1 mediates lysine-312 lactylation of the histone methyltransferase complex subunit ASH2L, promoting the malignant progression of hepatocellular carcinoma [30]. And inhibition of AARS1-mediated BLM lactylation by irinotecan alleviates chemoresistance to anthracyclines and affects homologous recombination repair [31]. Collectively, these studies underscore the critical role of AARS1 in regulating protein function and cellular metabolism. Given that genetic variations in AARS1 itself are rare, therapies targeting its overexpression or abnormal activity, such as developing small-molecule inhibitors that specifically block its lactylation catalytic pocket, may represent a broad-spectrum and mutation-resistant strategy. Notably, specific AARS1 inhibitors have been identified, L-alanine, which shares structural similarity with lactate, can competitively block lactate binding to AARS1 and inhibit tumor growth in animal models [12], offering a promising therapeutic strategy for targeting lactylation modifications.

KEGG and GO analyses of AARS1 revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation, the TCA cycle, and mitochondrial gene expression, highlighting the critical role of AARS1’s lactate transferase activity in regulating cellular metabolism. Notably, the association of AARS1 with mitochondrial function and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress suggests that its oncogenic effects may be mediated through inter-organelle communication. AARS1 likely modifies mitochondrial or ER-resident proteins via lactylation, thereby optimizing energy metabolism and proteostasis. This metabolic adaptation enables tumor cells to thrive under stress and provides a novel mechanistic explanation for its role in facilitating immune evasion. As a key node in metabolite-translation coupling, AARS1 may sense elevated lactate levels resulting from the Warburg effect and mitochondrial respiration, and through its lactyltransferase activity, convert metabolic signals into post-translational modifications of key target proteins, thus driving tumor progression. Additionally, phosphorylation site prediction analyses indicate that AARS1 possesses numerous phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and acetylation sites. However, the functional roles of specific sites in post-translational modifications and disease processes remain unclear. Previous studies have also reported that methyltransferase-like protein 21C (METTL21C) catalyzes methylation at Lys-943 (AARS1-K943me), modulating protein synthesis in muscle tissues [32]. These findings suggest that AARS1, as a lactate transferase, may regulate cellular lactylation through post-translational modifications, but the precise mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Immunohistochemical analysis of tumor and adjacent tissues from LUAD and KIRC patients confirmed that AARS1 is overexpressed in both cancer types (Fig. 12A). KIRC exhibits strong metabolic reprogramming, leading to enhanced glycolysis and increased lactate production, thereby providing abundant substrate for lactylation [33, 34]. Therefore, identifying key molecules involved in lactylation in renal cancer is critical for improving clinical outcomes. Functional assays in ccRCC cells further demonstrated that AARS1 knockdown markedly inhibited cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas AARS1 overexpression produced the opposite effects. These findings provide important insights into the role of AARS1 in metabolic reprogramming and lactylation-mediated tumor progression in ccRCC. Although AARS1 has limited diagnostic and universal prognostic value in KIRC, our preliminary analyses and experiments have validated its clear oncogenic role. This indicates that the carcinogenic mechanism of AARS1 may be independent of changes in its expression levels and relies more on the regulation of its substrates through lactylation modification activity. Therefore, KIRC is an ideal model for studying its lactyltransferase activity rather than the mechanism of carcinogenesis due to increased expression.

As a newly discovered lactyltransferase, AARS1 may achieve substrate selectivity through multiple mechanisms. Structurally, AARS1 possesses an evolutionarily conserved amino acid-binding domain capable of recognizing specific amino acids [35]. Recent studies have shown that AARS1 undergoes nuclear translocation upon lactate stimulation, enabling it to access specific substrate proteins within different cellular compartments [13]. In addition, the non-catalytic regions of AARS1 may directly interact with specific motifs on target proteins. Under catalytic action, AARS1 transfers the lactyl group from L-lactate to lysine residues in an ATP-dependent manner. This CoA-independent mechanism may impose constraints on the local microenvironment of target lysines, thereby promoting substrate selectivity [36]. Future research, by identifying the interactome of AARS1, will further elucidate the molecular basis of its substrate specificity.

Admittedly, this study is subject to several limitations. Although the conclusions have been validated across multiple datasets, further in vivo or in vitro experiments using human and animal samples are still needed for confirmation. Second, the reported high diagnostic efficacy of AARS1 (AUC > 0.85) was primarily derived from retrospective internal validation within public bioinformatics repositories. Therefore, its actual clinical utility and predictive performance must be rigorously evaluated in independent, prospective clinical cohorts to ensure its reliability as a diagnostic biomarker.

In conclusion, this study provides a systematic and integrative characterization of AARS1 expression landscapes, prognostic significance, and biological functionalities through a multi-dimensional bioinformatics lens. our findings highlight the correlation between AARS1 expression, clinical prognosis, and immune infiltration, establishing a compelling theoretical framework for its role as a novel diagnostic, prognostic, and immunological biomarker. Moreover, through functional validation of its lactyltransferase activity in renal cancer cells, this study establishes a robust foundation for future precision oncology strategies targeting the AARS1-mediated metabolic-epigenetic axis.
