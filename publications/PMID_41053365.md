Acetyltransferases play a crucial role in biological processes by modifying a
variety of substrates. However, their roles in the virulence of Mycobacterium
tuberculosis (M. tb) are poorly understood. To systematically investigate the
roles of acetyltransferases in M. tb, we constructed an acetyltransferase mutant
library using CRISPR-assisted genome editing and screened for genes that are
essential for mouse infection. Seven acetyltransferases were identified as
essential for lung infection of M. tb. cysE, encoding a serine
acetyltransferase, was confirmed to be required for virulence of M. tb in mice
and its replication in macrophages. Further experiments revealed that mutation
of cysE or inhibition of CysE by small molecular chemical increased sensitivity
to clofazimine treatment. Finally, we demonstrated that cysE is involved in
mitigating oxidative stress, which modulates the virulence and drug resistance
of M. tb. Our study suggests that targeting cysE offers potential for the
development of anti-tuberculosis drugs, particularly for enhancing treatment
regimens for drug-resistant tuberculosis through the synergistic effect with
clofazimine.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tb), resulted in an estimated 10.6 million active cases and 1.3 million deaths in 20221. Although substantial progress has been achieved in elucidating the biological basis of M. tb pathogenicity, the specific contributions of M. tb physiology and metabolism to persistence, pathogenesis, and drug resistance remain poorly understood.

Acetylation is a widespread modification used to regulate various cellular pathways, including gene expression and protein synthesis2. Acetyltransferase enzymes are present in all domains of life and typically transfer the acetyl group (acetyl coenzyme A) to the target substrate in order to regulate its activity or stability3. Acetyltransferase enzymes have been demonstrated to modify various small-molecule substrates, such as tRNAs, polyamines, cell wall components, antibiotics, and toxins. They are also involved in detoxification, synthesis, and transcription processes4. There are 47 acetyltransferase-encoding genes in the genome of M. tb. Some of these enzymes have been implicated in M. tb virulence. For instance, Rv0859 can modulate the immune response to suppress the host’s pro-inflammatory reaction5. Rv2170 encodes an aminoglycoside acetyltransferase that acetylates and alters various antibiotics, leading to drug resistance6. Rv3423.1 functions as an epigenetic regulator that manipulates anti-inflammatory genes to promote bacterial survival7. eis upregulates IL-10 through histone H3 acetylation, which activates the Akt/mTOR/p70S6K pathway and hampers host innate immune defenses by modulating autophagy, inflammation, and cell death in a redox-dependent manner8–11. Although some M. tb acetyltransferases have been implicated in host-pathogen interactions, the functions of the majority of these enzymes in M. tb are still unclear.

To systematically study essential genes in vivo and in vitro, various molecular genetic tools have been developed in M. tb12–14. Transposon insertion sequencing has been widely used to screen the essential genes of M. tb in vivo and in vitro15,16. However, this method is limited to whole-genome screening and cannot be used to investigate genes associated with specific cellular pathways. Recently, CRISPR interference and CRISPR knockout methods have been developed in M. tb and used for genome-wide screening to investigate genetic and chemical-genetic interactions in M. tb in vitro17–21. However, a CRISPR screen for investigation of the genes essential for M. tb virulence in vivo has not been conducted.

The role of acetyltransferases in M. tb virulence was investigated by knocking out acetyltransferase-encoding genes in M. tb using the CRISPR-NHEJ method. The genes essential for survival in vivo were identified through aerosol infection. cysE, which encodes a serine acetyltransferase, was found to be crucial for M. tb survival in macrophages and animals as well as resistance to clofazimine (CFZ). Mutation of cysE altered global gene expression, reducing the pathogen’s ability to counter host-induced redox stress, thereby impacting its survival and drug tolerance. Overall, our study suggests that CysE could serve as a potential drug target.

Discussion and conclusion

Genome-wide CRISPR-based screening technology is a powerful functional genomics tool for multiple investigations of biological processes in M. tb17–21,45. In this study, we constructed a small library of 37 acetyltransferase mutants in M. tb using the CRISPR-NHEJ method and systematically investigated the roles of acetyltransferases during M. tb infection in C57BL/6N mice. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate M. tb genes associated with a specific pathway through aerosol infection. Our study identified seven acetyltransferase genes, including cysE (Rv2335), mshD (Rv0819), mbtK (Rv1347c), Rv2775, Rv1323, fadA (Rv0859), and Rv0428c, required for survival in the mouse lung (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data 2). Among them, mbtK and fadA were previously reported to be necessary for M. tb survival in mice5,29.

Consistent with previous transposon library screening15,16, cysE is required for survival of M. tb in macrophages and mice (Fig. 2). The acetyltransferase CysE is crucial for synthesis of OAS, the precursor of L-cysteine46. The absence of OAS in the ΔcysE mutant may interfere L-cysteine production, leading to reduced levels of L-cysteine-derived antioxidants like free thiols, thereby increasing vulnerability to oxidative stress and decreasing M. tb survival in vivo. This hypothesis is supported by the following three findings: (i) mutation of cysE decreased the thiol level in M. tb and increased sensitivity of M. tb to oxidative stress (Fig. 3b–d and Fig. 4b); (ii) OAS supplementation restored thiol levels and oxidative stress tolerance in the cysE mutant (Figs. 3e, 4b); and (iii) addition of NAC to macrophages rescued the survival defect of the cysE mutant (Fig. 2d, e).

CFZ, a generator of reactive oxygen intermediates, is believed to eradicate M. tb by elevating ROS levels41. Consistent with the finding that cysE mutation heightens sensitivity of M. tb to oxidative stress, the cysE mutation significantly boosted CFZ-induced killing of M. tb (Fig. 5b). This increased bactericidal activity might be due to the increased ROS level in the cysE mutant upon CFZ treatment (Fig. 5f). Mutation of cysE in M. tb decreased the MIC90 and MIC50 values of BDQ, but did not affect its bactericidal activity (Fig. 5a, b). This disparity may be because the absence of cysE in M. tb decreased ATP levels only in the initial stages of BDQ treatment, not upon prolonged BDQ exposure (Fig. 5g). BDQ and CFZ have shown potential synergy in M. tb elimination40. Importantly, the absence of cysE further intensifies the bactericidal efficacy of BDQ and CFZ in vitro and in macrophages, suggesting that CysE is a potential promising target for developing anti-TB drugs, especially in conjunction with BDQ and CFZ. This hypothesis is further supported by the observation that C1, a small molecular chemical inhibitor of CysE identified through in silico screening, enhances the drug efficacy of BDQ and CFZ (Fig. 7b). However, whether C1 specifically target CysE and exhibits similar function in vivo remains to be investigated.

We constructed a small acetyltransferase mutant library in M. tb using CRISPR-assisted genome editing and screened for acetyltransferase-encoding genes required for survival of M. tb in vivo. CysE was identified as one of most required acetyltransferases for M. tb growth in lungs of mice. In addition, the absence of CysE in M. tb enhances the bactericidal effect of CFZ alone or in conjunction with BDQ. Therefore, our study suggests that the CysE is a potential attractive target for the design of anti-mycobacterial drugs and provides new insights for design of effective combined drug therapy regimes to eliminate TB.
