Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection is the main risk factor for gastric
cancer. The SRY-Box Transcription Factor 9 (SOX9) serves as a marker of stomach
stem cells. We detected strong associations between AURKA and SOX9 expression
levels in gastric cancers. Utilizing in vitro and in vivo mouse models, we
demonstrated that H. pylori infection induced elevated levels of both AURKA and
SOX9 proteins. Notably, the SOX9 protein and transcription activity levels were
dependent on AURKA expression. AURKA knockdown led to a reduction in the number
and size of gastric gland organoids. Conditional knockout of AURKA in mice
resulted in a decrease in SOX9 baseline level in AURKA-knockout gastric glands,
accompanied by diminished SOX9 induction following H. pylori infection. We found
an AURKA-dependent increase in EIF4E and cap-dependent translation with an
AURKA-EIF4E-dependent increase in SOX9 polysomal RNA levels. Immunoprecipitation
assays demonstrated binding of AURKA to EIF4E with a decrease in EIF4E
ubiquitination. Immunohistochemistry analysis on tissue arrays revealed moderate
to strong immunostaining of AURKA and SOX9 with a significant correlation in
gastric cancer tissues. These findings elucidate the mechanistic role of AURKA
in regulating SOX9 levels via cap-dependent translation in response to H. pylori
infection in gastric tumorigenesis.

Introduction

Gastric cancer is a prevalent cancer type, with over one million new cases diagnosed annually, making it the fifth most common cancer and fourth most common cause of cancer-related deaths globally(1). The primary risk factor for the development of gastric cancer is Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection, which has been categorized as a class I carcinogen by the World Health Organization (2). Infection with H. pylori leads to the activation of oncogenes and the inhibition of tumor suppressor genes through genetic and epigenetic mechanisms, ultimately promoting the development and progression of gastric cancer (3–7). H. pylori infection is a common and persistent infection that is often contracted during childhood (5). A number of studies have provided substantial evidence indicating that H. pylori infection plays a significant role in the emergence of gastric tumors (8–12). By sustaining a persistent state of inflammation, H. pylori infection leads to histopathological alterations that progress from chronic gastritis to gastric atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and ultimately, to gastric cancer, known as the Correa cascade (13, 14).

The SRY-Box Transcription Factor 9 (SOX9) is a key transcription factor that plays a vital role in the development of the gut, particularly in the differentiation of paneth cells (15, 16). SOX9 overexpression is also involved in cancer progression and tumorigenesis (17, 18). In esophageal malignancies, elevated levels of nuclear SOX9 are associated with lymph node metastasis and poor clinical outcome (19). Likewise, elevated SOX9 expression has been found not only in gastric tissues that undergo precancerous intestinal metaplasia but also in gastric cancer resections, suggesting a role of SOX9 throughout the transformation of gastric glands (20). A recent study has shown that SOX9 marks gastric stem cells and modulates biased symmetric cell division, which appears to be required for the malignant transformation of gastric stem cells (21).

Uncontrolled proliferation of tumorigenic cells is orchestrated by increased levels of regulatory proteins during the mitosis stage of cell division. Aurora kinase A (AURKA) is a mitotic kinase that promotes the maturation of centrosomes and the assembly of the mitotic spindle under physiological conditions (22). Several studies have shown that AURKA is often amplified and overexpressed in various types of cancer (23–26), suggesting the presence of additional non-mitotic functions that contribute to the development and progression of cancer (22). High levels of AURKA in cancer inhibit the function of several tumor suppressors, including p53 family members, and activate various oncogenic signaling pathways promoting cancer cell survival and drug resistance (27–31).

Aberrant activation of cap-dependent translation is an important feature of cancer cells. Cap-dependent translation is mediated by the eIF4F protein complex which is the key regulator of the mRNA-ribosome recruitment phase of translation initiation. The complex is composed of three subunits: eIF4E, which recognizes the mRNA 5′ cap structure; eIF4A, an RNA-helicase; and eIF4G, a scaffolding protein that binds eIF4E and eIF4A simultaneously. Among these subunits, eIF4E serves as the limiting factor in the assembly of the translation initiation complex eIF4F, making it rate-limiting for translation (32, 33). Indeed, dysregulated activation of eIF4E has been associated with elevated protein levels of several genes that play important roles in carcinogenesis, such as c-MYC, cyclin D1, STEAP1, and VEGF (34–37).

In this study we report a mechanistic link between H. pylori infection and SOX9 levels in gastric tumorigenesis. Our findings uncover a novel mechanism by which induction of AURKA by H. pylori induces SOX9 via EIF4E-mediated cap-dependent translation. These findings suggest AURKA-EIF4E as a potentially druggable axis in gastric cancer.

3.Results3.1.Association between AURKA and SOX9 in gastric cancer patients with AURKA-dependent SOX9 regulationAURKA is frequently overexpressed in several cancers, including gastric cancer (23, 30, 45). To identify key molecular functions that are associated with AURKA overexpression, we analyzed the TCGA dataset and eight public GEO databases (GSE122401, GSE54129, GSE66229, GSE15459, GSE110875, GSE26899, GSE26901). This analysis indicated that stem cell differentiation and maintenance processes are among the top enriched molecular functions associated with overexpression of AURKA (Figure 1A). SOX9 not only plays a critical role in the development of the gut but also cancer progression and tumorigenesis. Using Pearson’s correlation test, we uncovered a positive correlation between AURKA and SOX9 expression in gastric tumors (Figure 1B). Moreover, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrated an enrichment for SOX9 gene signature in gastric tumor samples with high expression of AURKA, compared to the samples with low AURKA expression (Figure 1C), suggesting a possible involvement of SOX9 in AURKA-associated gastric cancer. To test this hypothesis, we assessed the impact of inhibiting AURKA by two different RNA interference sequences on SOX9 levels. Interestingly, the knockdown of AURKA resulted in a significant decrease in SOX9 protein level in gastric cancer cells AGS and STKM2 (Figure 1D). Conversely, overexpression of AURKA, using an inducible TET-ON inducible system, led to an increase in SOX9 protein levels, after 48 hours of doxycycline treatment. Notably, SOX9 protein levels returned to basal level after doxycycline removal (Supplementary figure S1A). Using the selective AURKA inhibitor, MLN8237, we found that MLN8237 significantly reduced SOX9 protein levels in AGS and STKM2 gastric cancer cells (Figure 1E–F). In line with these findings, immunofluorescence experiments of AURKA and SOX9, showed a significant decrease of SOX9 immunostaining, following AURKA knockdown (p<0.01, Figure 1G–H, Supplementary figure S1B–C). Similarly, CagA overexpression induced a significant increase in cap-dependent translation activity while AURKA knockdown or inhibition (MLN8237) abrogated this increase in cap-dependent translation activity (p<0.01, Figure 4B). To further investigate the role of AURKA in H. pylori-mediated induction of cap-dependent translation, we knocked-down AURKA expression by RNA interference and checked EIF4E levels following H. pylori infection. As expected, H. pylori infection increased not only AURKA and SOX9 protein levels, but also total and phospho-EIF4E (S209) protein, indicative of its activation. Conversely, the AURKA knockdown dramatically decreased the expression of total EIF4E and phospho-EIF4E (S209) along with decreased SOX9 protein levels (Figure 4C, Supplementary figure S5A–B). To confirm that AURKA regulated SOX9 protein levels through EIF4E, we knocked-down EIF4E expression by RNA interference. Indeed, both AURKA and SOX9 protein levels were induced following H. pylori infection, while EIF4E knockdown markedly inhibited the increase in AURKA and SOX9 protein levels in response to H. pylori infection (Figure 4D). These results suggested a regulatory feedback loop between AURKA and EIF4E mediating SOX9 translation, and that both AURKA and EIF4E are required for the regulation of SOX9 protein levels. To further confirm the regulation of SOX9 at the translation level, we performed a polysomal RNA fractionation and assessed the presence of SOX9 within the fractions of highly translated genes. Although SOX9 RNA did not vary in H. pylori-infected AGS cells, compared to non-infected cells, higher levels of SOX9 polysomal RNA were detected in the highly translated fractions following H. pylori infection (p<0.01, Figure 4E). These results indicated that SOX9 was highly translated following H. pylori infection and confirmed the western blot and immunofluorescence results. Interestingly, the knockdown of AURKA significantly decreased the levels of SOX9 polysomal RNA in the highly translated fractions in (p<0.01, Figure 4F).

To further validate the role of AURKA in regulating EIF4E, we performed a co-immunoprecipitation analysis by pulling down the endogenous AURKA in AGS cells, followed by Western blot analysis to detect endogenous EIF4E. The co-immunoprecipitation data showed that EIF4E was successfully pulled down using an anti-AURKA antibody. Interestingly, higher levels of EIF4E were pulled down following H. pylori infection (Figure 4G). Similarly, the co-immunoprecipitation data in Figure 4F showed that AURKA was successfully pulled down using an anti-EIF4E antibody in the same cells, with higher levels of AURKA being pulled down following H. pylori infection (Figure 4H). These results suggest that AURKA and EIF4E co-exist in the same protein complex. Taken together, these results demonstrate an important role of the AURKA-EIF4E axis in the SOX9 translation, governing its protein levels in response to H. pylori infection.

Based on our results, we hypothesized that the observed differences in EIF4E protein levels between AURKA control and knockdown cells are likely due to alterations in EIF4E protein stability. To validate this hypothesis, AGS cells with AURKA knockdown and control cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX) at a concentration of 100 μg/ml for 0, 2, 4, 8, and 16 hours to impede protein synthesis. Western blot analysis was employed to monitor changes in the degradation rate of EIF4E protein. As illustrated in Fig. 4I and J, knockdown of AURKA in AGS cells markedly reduced EIF4E protein stability compared to control cells (EIF4E protein half-life: 14.9 hours in si-control cells vs. 2.6 hours in si-AURKA cells, p < 0.01). These findings suggest a role for AURKA in maintaining the stability of the EIF4E protein. We next aimed to determine the mechanism by which AURKA regulated EIF4E protein levels. Since ubiquitination is a posttranslational modification that targets proteins to the proteasome for degradation, we tested whether AURKA may regulate EIF4E stability through regulation of its ubiquitination. To this end, we knocked-down AURKA by RNA interference and assessed the ubiquitination levels of EIF4E by pulling down EIF4E in cells treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132. Our data showed that EIF4E was efficiently pulled-down using an anti-EIF4E antibody and demonstrated that AURKA knockdown markedly increased the levels of ubiquitinylated EIF4E, as assessed by an anti-Ubiquitin antibody (Figure 4K). This data indicates that AURKA regulates EIF4E stability through inhibition of its ubiquitination and subsequent targeting for degradation.

Despite efforts to eradicate H. pylori, gastric cancer remains a deadly disease, responsible for over one million new cases, with an estimated 769,000 fatalities in 2020 (48). In this study, we investigated the mechanistic link between H. pylori infection, AURKA, and regulation of SOX9 in gastric cancer.

Gastrointestinal malignancies including gastric cancer often exhibit amplification and/or overexpression of AURKA (22, 30). Previous studies, including ours, have highlighted AURKA’s role in activating several key oncogenic signaling pathways such as NF-κB, β-catenin, and STAT3, while suppressing pro-apoptotic functions of p53 and p73 (45, 49–55). A recent study has shown that SOX9 marks gastric stem cells and modulates biased symmetric cell division, which appears to be required for the malignant transformation of gastric stem cells and gastric tumorigenesis (21). Therefore, we aimed to investigate the possible link between AURKA and SOX9.

GSEA analysis of publicly available datasets revealed an enrichment of several enrichment of stem cell-related genes/pathways in patients with high AURKA expression, including a SOX9 transcription factor gene signature in gastric cancer patients. Pearson’s correlation analysis further supported a connection between AURKA and SOX9 expression levels, suggesting a link between AURKA and stemness through SOX9. Consistent with these findings, inhibition of AURKA led to a substantial decrease in the protein levels of SOX9 in gastric cancer cell lines, while AURKA overexpression increased SOX9 protein levels, suggesting a regulatory role for AURKA in modulating the protein level of SOX9.

Several studies have demonstrated that H. pylori infection of gastric antral glands causes chronic inflammation, thereby fostering a tumorigenic environment characterized by an activation of several signaling pathways (9, 10, 56, 57). Our data demonstrates, for the first time, that H. pylori infection markedly augments SOX9 protein level in gastric cancer cell lines and mouse gastric glands. These findings align with previous studies reporting induction of SOX9 by H. pylori infection (58, 59), although the underlying mechanisms were not fully elucidated. In this context, we present evidence showing that H. pylori infection leads to a rapid increase in endogenous AURKA as well as total and phosphorylated EIF4E, coinciding with the induction of cap-dependent translation. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of AURKA decreased cap-dependent translation, EIF4E levels, and subsequently SOX9 protein levels.

Earlier studies have shown that phosphorylation of EIF4E at Ser209 is essential for its activity [58]. Our data suggest that AURKA regulates the total levels of EIF4E, available for phosphorylation at S209 by the PI3K/Akt signaling in response to H. pylori [55]. Of note, suppressing the translation machinery through EIF4E knockdown resulted in a subsequent decrease in SOX9 protein level, suggesting an EIF4E-dependent mechanism. By assessing SOX9 RNA translation by polysome fractionation, we found that AURKA knockdown decreased the levels of SOX9 RNA in the highly translated fractions, indicative of a decreased translation. We also found that AURKA binds to EIF4E enhancing its stability through inhibition of its ubiquitination and subsequent degradation. Together, the results unveil a novel role for AURKA in governing the cap-dependent translation of SOX9 protein via EIF4E, in response to H. pylori infection.

Recent studies have suggested that H. pylori infection increases the expression of SOX9, which, in turn, plays an important role in gastric carcinogenesis(59). Our finding indicate that, the ability of gastric cancer cells to form spheroids was significantly compromised upon AURKA knockdown and subsequent reduction in SOX9, particularly under H. pylori exposure. Consistent with the role of SOX9 in cellular proliferation and expansion, gastric organoids were fewer in number and smaller in size following AURKA silencing. Utilizing our Krt19Cre/AURKAflox/flox knockout mouse model, we confirmed that AURKA silencing attenuates the H. pylori-induced nuclear accumulation of SOX9 in vivo, accompanied by a significant reduction in gastric mucosa thickness. Of note, a recent study has shown that SOX9 marks gastric stem cells and modulates biased symmetric cell division, a process implicated in malignant transformation of gastric stem cells (21). These findings hold significance as anti-cancer therapies encounter challenges due to the presence of cancer cells with stem-like properties, characterized by their ability for self-renewal, differentiation, and tumor regeneration (60, 61). Immunohistochemical analysis of gastric tumors showed increased immunostaining of AURKA and SOX9 with a notable correlation. Collectively, our findings imply a potential attenuation of stem cell-like characteristics associated with SOX9 following AURKA inhibition, which could contribute to the observed therapeutic efficacy of AURKA inhibition in tumor xenograft animal models (22).

4.1.ConclusionsIn summary, this study emphasizes the significant role of AURKA in the context of H. pylori infection and its possible impact on gastric tumorigenesis. AURKA emerges as a crucial signaling mediator connecting H. pylori infection to the translation of SOX9 via cap-dependent mechanism. Targeting AURKA or EIF4E could be a therapeutic strategy to overcome stemness properties in gastric cancer cells and overcome therapeutic resistance.
