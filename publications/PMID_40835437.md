## Full Text

Abstract

The central amygdala (CeA) is an important neuronal hub that integrates external sensory inputs and information about internal states to regulate a range of innate and learned behaviors, including fear learning and memory. Prior studies, leveraging robust fear conditioning assays, have delineated detailed circuit mechanisms underlying the acquisition and recall of fear memories. However, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying these processes in the CeA remain poorly understood. Here, we investigate the role of the clock output molecule mWAKE/ANKFN1 within the CeA of male mice in fear learning and memory. mWAKE is expressed in multiple neuronal subclusters in the lateral CeA. Surprisingly, mWAKE levels do not exhibit rhythmic expression in the CeA. In line with this observation, expression of the core clock genes PER2 and BMAL also does not cycle in the CeA. Consistent with prior studies, loss of mWAKE function increases intrinsic excitability of CeA neurons. Furthermore, conditional knock-out of mWAKE and chemogenetic activation of CeA mWAKE neurons impair fear learning and memory. Finally, we show that mWAKE levels in a subset of CeA neurons are reduced following fear conditioning. These findings suggest a potential molecular mechanism modulating the activity and function of CeA neurons in fear learning and memory.

Keywords: amygdala, central amygdala, fear learning, fear memory, mWAKE

Significance Statement

The central amygdala (CeA) plays a crucial role in the acquisition and recall of fear learning, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying this process remain incompletely understood. This study shows that an evolutionarily conserved molecule (mWAKE) is enriched in a subset of CeA neurons, acts to reduce neuronal excitability, and is required for fear learning and memory. Although mWAKE functions as a clock output molecule in other contexts, its expression, as well as that of core clock genes, does not cycle in the CeA. Instead, mWAKE levels are reduced after fear conditioning. These findings hint at a potential molecular mechanism that may support the generation of fear memory.

Introduction

Conditioned fear learning enables animals to assign negative valence to seemingly harmless stimuli, helping them better anticipate and respond to potential threats ( Dirikx et al., 2004 ; Hyde et al., 2024 ). The CeA plays a critical role in fear processing and fear learning ( Ehrlich et al., 2009 ; Ciocchi et al., 2010 ; Duvarci et al., 2011 ). This brain region can be subdivided into three main subregions: the lateral CeA (CeL), the medial CeA (CeM), and the capsular CeA (CeC). In the classical model, the CeL receives information from sensory inputs and internal states mainly from the lateral amygdala (LA) and the basal amygdala (BA) and transmits this information to the CeM, which functions as an major output center to downstream subcortical areas ( Pitkänen et al., 1995 ; Jolkkonen and Pitkänen, 1998 ; Li and Sheets, 2018 ). There are also direct output signals from the CeL ( Pitkänen et al., 1995 ; Petrovich and Swanson, 1997 ; Jolkkonen and Pitkänen, 1998 ; Li, 2019 ; Penzo and Moscarello, 2023 ).

Multiple subclusters in the CeL, particularly those labeled by protein kinase C-delta (PKC-δ), corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF), and somatostatin (SST), are functionally interconnected ( Yu et al., 2016 ; Wilson et al., 2019 ) and important for the fear conditioning process ( Haubensak et al., 2010 ; Li et al., 2013 ; Sanford et al., 2017 ). Besides the key role of the CeA as an output hub in fear processing and learning, recent work suggests that the CeA can also relay pain-related information from the parabrachial (PB) nucleus to the LA via indirect projections to promote fear learning and memory ( Han et al., 2015 ; Yu et al., 2017 ). Both LA and CeL receive US (unconditioned stimulus) and CS (conditioned stimulus) signals during the fear learning process and exhibit subsequent neuronal plasticity ( Schroeder and Shinnick-Gallagher, 2004 ; Li et al., 2013 ; Li, 2019 ; Abatis et al., 2024 ). Compared with the LA, the molecular mechanisms underlying fear learning and memory in the CeL are less well defined ( Maren, 2001 ; Rodrigues et al., 2004 ).

We previously characterized WAKE/mWAKE (mammalian WAKE) in flies and mice as a conserved clock output molecule that promotes daily rhythms of neuronal excitability and behavior ( Liu et al., 2014 , 2023 , 2024 ; Tabuchi et al., 2018 ; Bell et al., 2021 ). Based on expression analyses in the mouse brain, mWAKE is enriched in multiple structures of the limbic system, including the CeA ( Bell et al., 2021 ). Interestingly, a constitutive loss-of-function mutation in mWake (also known as Ankfn1 / Nmf9 ) has previously been shown to impair fear learning; however, this finding is complicated by the pleiotropic effects of this mutation, including behavioral hyperactivity ( Zhang et al., 2015 ). In this study, we set out to investigate a specific role for mWAKE in the CeA in fear learning and memory and assess whether it contributes to circadian regulation of fear learning and memory.

Here, we characterize the expression and function of mWAKE in the CeA. mWAKE is expressed in multiple clusters of CeA neurons, which project to a wide range of subcortical brain regions. Although mWAKE expression is under circadian control in other brain regions, such as the LA and dorsomedial hypothalamus (DMH), we found that mWAKE does not cycle in the CeA. Moreover, our data suggest that the core clock molecules PER2 and BMAL do not exhibit rhythmic expression in the CeA. Instead, mWAKE levels in a subset of CeA neurons decrease following fear conditioning, suggesting that its expression can be regulated by timing related to fear learning, rather than the circadian clock. Finally, we show that loss of mWAKE increases excitability of CeA neurons and impairs fear learning and recall. Together, these data reveal a molecular pathway that may tune the excitability and function of a neuronal circuit critical for fear learning and memory.

Materials and Methods

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted with oversight by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were maintained with standard housing, environmental enrichment, and ad libitum food and water, under 20–26°C and 30–70% relative humidity conditions. Animals were raised under a 14:10 h light/dark (LD) cycle and then entrained to a 12 h LD cycle in a satellite facility for ≥14 d prior to behavioral tests. Unless otherwise noted, 2–4-month-old male mice were used for all experiments. Only a single surgery was performed for each animal. All the animals used for experiments were backcrossed at least seven times with C57BL/6J mice from the Jackson Laboratory (JAX, 000664). Genotyping was conducted using TaqMan-based rtPCR probes (Transnetyx). The following mouse strains were used: a V5-tagged mWake allele ( mWake V5 ; Bell et al., 2021 ), an mWake allele with a tdTomato-P2A-Cre cassette integrated into exon 5 ( mWake Cre ; Bell et al., 2021 ), an mWake conditional allele ( mWake flox ; Bell et al., 2021 ), and Sst Cre (JAX, 013044).

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were anesthetized and perfused with ice-cold 1× PBS (in mM: 1.1 KH 2 PO 4 , 155.2 NaCl, 3.0 NaH 2 PO 4 , pH 7.4) and 4% PFA in 1× PBS. Brains were dissected and then postfixed in 4% PFA solution overnight. Then, 40–60 mm sections were collected using a vibratome (VT-1200 S, Leica) and then washed twice with 1× PBS with 0.3% Triton X-100 (PBS-T). Floating slices were blocked (5% goat serum, 1% fetal bovine serum in 1× PBS) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies in blocking solution for 24 h at 4°C. The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit anti-PER2 (MilliporeSigma, AB2202, 1:500), rabbit anti-BMAL1 (Novus Biologicals, NB100-2288, 1:500), rabbit anti-V5 (Cell Signaling Technology, 13202, 1:2,000), mouse anti-PKC-δ (BD Biosciences, 610398), or chicken anti-RFP (Rockland, 600-901-379, 1:1,000). After washing four times in PBS-T for 15 min each, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies in blocking solution overnight at 4°C. The following secondary antibodies were used: Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit (Invitrogen, A-11008, 1:1,000), Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-rabbit (Invitrogen, A21244, 1:1,000), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse (Invitrogen, A11001, 1:1,000), or Alexa Fluor 568 goat anti-chicken (Invitrogen, A11041, 1:1,000). Sections were washed four times in PBS-T, one time in PBS, and then mounted with mounting solution (VectorLabs, H-1500-10). For timed immunostaining experiments under 12 h LD conditions, samples were collected within 30 min after light onset (Zeitgeber Time 0/ZT0; ZT0 refers to time at light onset), the middle of light phase (ZT6), dark onset (ZT12), or the middle of dark phase (ZT18). Imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM700 or Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope.

For quantification of PER2, BMAL1, and V5 immunostaining in the CeA, signals were analyzed using Imaris (v9.5.0, Oxford Instruments). A broad ROI of the CeL was drawn manually in Surface mode. After masking the target signals outside the ROI, the size of spots and the filters to detect signals were adjusted in Spot mode, and the number and location of spots were manually varied until all signals in the ROI were detected. Signal intensity in an individual neuron was quantified as the average intensity within a spot.

In situ hybridization

RNAscope in situ hybridization was performed using the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit V2 (Advanced Cell Diagnostics/ACD, 323100). Mice were anesthetized and perfused with ice-cold 1× PBS and 4% PFA in 1× PBS. Brains were dissected, postfixed in 4% PFA solution for 24 h at 4°C, and then dehydrated in sucrose solutions with increasing concentrations (10, 20, and then 30%) at 4°C, until they sank to the bottom of the solution. Brains were fresh frozen in Tissue-Tek Optimum Cutting Temperature (OCT) solution (VWR, 4583) on dry ice and then stored in −80°C. Brain tissue was sliced into 12 mm sections on the microscope slides (Fisher Scientific, 22-037-246) using a cryostat (Leica VT 1200S). Sections were then used for RNAscope in situ hybridization. Target probes include the following: mWake-C1 (1057631-C1), Prkcd-C2 (441791-C2), and Sst-C3 (404631-C3). Opal Dye (AKOYA Biosciences Opal 4-Color Manual IHC Kit, NEL810001KT, 1:2,000) was applied to develop the signal (520, 570, or 690 nm). Slides were incubated with DAPI for 30 s prior to mounting. Imaging was performed on a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope.

Images were analyzed in ImageJ (version 1.54d). The locations of each cell (ROIs) were captured using DAPI signals through the built-in “Watershed” and “Analyze Particles” functions in ImageJ. Manual adjustments for the size of the ROIs and boundary between two attached cells were applied to maximize mRNA signals covered by ROIs. The number of ROIs containing specific mRNA signals were then counted separately.

Projection analysis

Projection analyses were performed based on synaptophysin-fused GFP expression. AAV1-hSyn1(S)-FLEX-tdTomato-T2A-SypEGFP-WPRE (Addgene, 51509) was injected into the CeA of mWake Cre/+ mice. Two weeks later, mice were anesthetized, and brain tissue was removed, fixed, and then sliced into 60 μm sections. Images were taken using a Keyence microscope (BZ-X710) to visualize broad projection sites, and then detailed projection images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope. Brain regions were identified using the Paxinos and Franklin mouse brain atlas ( Paxinos and Franklin, 2012 ). Density of projections in the target brain regions was subjectively categorized into three groups (weak, moderate, or strong), based on intensity and size of GFP signal.

Secondary analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data

The original scRNA-seq dataset was generated by Hochgerner et al. (2023) , which defined 56 GABAergic subclusters in the amygdala. The prefiltered dataset, associated cluster assignment, and metadata were retrieved from https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.20412573 . Data were analyzed in an R environment (version 4.3.0) on Rocky Linux 9.4. Data were log-normalized and corrected for batch effect using a mutual nearest neighbors method via fastMNN ( Zhang et al., 2019 ) in batchelor [version 1.18.1; d = 50, k = 20, BSPARAM=BiocSingular::RandomParam(deferred=TRUE), and other parameters as default]. Two-dimensional embedding was performed on the corrected log-normalized counts using t -distributed stochastic neighbor embedding ( t -SNE) via runTSNE in scater ( McCarthy et al., 2017 ; version 1.28.0; ntop = 500, perplexity = 89, theta = 05, and other parameters as default). Enrichment of mWake / Ankfn1 in the 56 clusters was determined by a Z -test of the mean mWake/Ankfn1 expression. To better represent the spatial expression pattern, the 56 subclusters were consolidated into eight amygdalar subregions based on the spatial alignments results in Hochgerner et al. (2023) .

Electrophysiological recordings

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed essentially as previously described ( Liu et al., 2024 ) with minor modifications. Briefly, mouse brains from mWake Cre/+ or mWake Cre/Cre mice were removed at ZT0 or ZT12 and placed immediately in ice-cold slicing artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF; in mM: 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH 2 PO 4 , 26 NaHCO 3 , 248 sucrose, 10 glucose, 2.5 CaCl 2 , and 2 MgSO 4 ), saturated with 95% O 2 /5% CO 2 for 2 min. The chilled brain was trimmed coronally with a razor blade and placed in a vibratome chamber (Leica VT1200 S). Then, 350 μm sections were transferred to a recovery chamber filled with recording aCSF (in mM: 124 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH 2 PO 4 , 26 NaHCO 3 , 10 glucose, 2.5 CaCl 2 , and 2 MgSO 4 , 290–300 mOsm), followed by incubation at 32°C for 30 min, and then at room temperature for 1 h. In current-clamp experiments, neurons in the CeA were visualized using infrared differential interference contrast (IR-DIC) microscopy. tdTomato + neurons were identified by their native fluorescence and subsequently patched using glass micropipettes (5–8 MΩ) filled with a potassium-based intracellular solution (in mM: 130 K-gluconate, 5 NaCl, 10 Na 2 C 4 H 8 N 3 O 5 P, 1 MgCl 2 , 0.2 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 2 MgATP, 0.5 Na 2 GTP, pH 7.2–7.3 with KOH, osmolarity 303 mOsm) in whole-cell configuration. Electrophysiological recordings were acquired with a MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices), with data sampled at 20 kHz, low-pass filtered at 2 kHz and digitized via a Digidata 1440A system (Molecular Devices). Clampfit software (Axon, pClamp) was employed to analyze spike properties. Instantaneous frequency data were utilized to generate the frequency–current ( f–I ) curve, covering a current injection range of −40 to 150 pA in 10 pA increments, with a duration of 500 ms for each step. The rheobase, defined as the lowest current necessary to evoke the first action potential, was determined. Resting membrane potential (RMP) was recorded at zero current injection ( I = 0).

Western blot analysis

Brains from 2- to 4-month-old male mice were freshly sliced into 250 μm sections (3–4 sections). CeA tissue from a single mouse was then dissected from the thick sections under a light microscope and homogenized in RIPA solution (MilliporeSigma, R0278) containing 1:100 protease inhibitor cocktail (MilliporeSigma, P8340). Samples were collected on ice within 30 min following ZT0, ZT6, ZT12, and ZT18. The homogenate was incubated on ice for 1 h and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Protein amount was quantified using a Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23227). All samples were diluted to the same protein concentration (1 mg/ml) and 10 ml diluted solution was loaded. The protein was loaded and resolved on SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer to a PVDF membrane at 100 V on ice. Membranes were blocked for 1 h with Intercept Blocking Buffer (LI-COR, 927-60001) and then incubated overnight with rabbit V5-Tag (D3H8Q) antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 13202, 1:2,000) in blocking buffer with 0.2% Tween 20 at 4°C. Blots were then incubated with mouse anti-β-actin antibodies (MilliporeSigma, A5441, 1:5,000) in Intercept Blocking Buffer for 1 h at RT. Anti-mouse (LI-COR, IRDye 680RD, 926-68070) and anti-rabbit (LI-COR, IRDye 800CW, 926-32211) fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:10,000) were applied for 1 h at RT. Then, 1× TBST (137 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, 0.1% Tween 20, pH 7.6) was used to wash the membrane after the primary incubation as well as the secondary incubation, and 1× TBS was used to rinse the detergent on the membrane. The membrane was imaged on an Odyssey Fc Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences).

Stereotaxic surgeries

Ten- to fourteen-week-old male mice were deeply anesthetized using a gas isoflurane system (Kent Scientific, VetFlo-1205S). Mice were secured onto a Stoelting stereotaxis frame (51730UD, 51609M), and all coordinates were zeroed when the glass pipette tip was placed on the bregma. Small (∼0.5 mm) craniotomies were performed to allow for virus injection (50–100 nl at ∼50 nl/min). All viruses were obtained from Addgene, and the following viruses were used: AAV1-phSyn1(S)-FLEX-tdTomato-T2A-SypEGFP-WPRE (#51509), AAV8-hSyn-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry (#44361), AAV9-Ef1a-DIO-EYFP (#27056), AAV9-hSyn-EGFP (#50465-AAV9), and AAV2-hSyn-Cre-tdTomato (#107738-AAV2). AAV2-Cre-tdTomato was diluted 1:30 with saline to avoid toxicity due to Cre expression. Following injections, animals were housed in the satellite facility to recover and express viral genes for a minimum of 2 weeks. Locations of viral injections were confirmed by post hoc fluorescence imaging using 40–60 μm coronal brain slices.

Behavioral analyses

Fear conditioning test

Fear conditioning (FC) tests were performed using the Fear Conditioning Chambers (Maze Engineers) and were performed within ZT0–2 or ZT12–14 windows. Two fear conditioning chambers were chained together to receive simultaneous tones and electric footshocks. Animals were handled for 5 d before the test. Animals were habituated to the behavioral room for 1 h before the test and were exposed to the fear conditioning chambers 5 min before the test. The tests were conducted over 2 d. On Day 1, an acrylic square chamber (33 cm × 25 cm × 28 cm; top wall is transparent, and side walls are white) with metal grids (0.2 cm diameter, spaced 0.5 cm apart) was placed in the sound-attenuating box. Each animal was allowed to explore the square chamber for 5 min (baseline), and then five trials were performed. In each trial, a 30 s tone (80 dB, 1 kHz) was played by the speaker within the box, and then a 2 s electric footshock (0.6 mA) was delivered during the last 2 s of the tone. Intertrial intervals (ITIs) were pseudorandom and ranged from 30 to 120 s (Trial 1, 30 s; Trial 2, 60 s; Trial 3, 120 s; Trial 4, 90 s; Trial 5, 120 s). On Day 2, a round chamber was placed in each sound-attenuating box. Sound stimuli were delivered as on Day 1, but no footshocks were administered. Freezing behavior was captured by ANY-maze software (Stoelting), and freezing percentage (% freezing time during 300 s baseline, 28 s of tones on Day 1, or 30 s of tones on Day 2) was used to quantify fear learning performance. The freezing threshold was set as 500 ms.

V5 expression analysis following fear conditioning

The mWake V5/V5 mouse line was used to quantify the change of mWAKE expression under three conditions: no fear conditioning (NFC), fear conditioning (FC), and footshock alone (FS). For the FC group, animals were exposed to the Day 1 fear conditioning training as described above and then killed 4 h after training. For the FS alone group, animals experienced the five footshocks, but no tone-associated training. Animals were then killed 4 h after the footshocks.

Mechanical sensitivity assay (von Frey test)

The von Frey mechanical sensitivity test was performed at ZT0–2 or ZT12–14 to test whether touch and pain sensitivity was altered. Each animal was placed on a small metal mesh table and immediately covered by an inverted 500 ml glass beaker and then by a larger red semitransparent container, enabling the researcher to visualize movement of the mouse, while reducing the researcher's visibility. After a 20–30 min period for habituation, a researcher blinded to the genotype of the animal used von Frey filaments to deliver mechanical stimuli of varying strengths (0.008, 002, 0.04, 0.07, 0.16, 0.4, 0.6, 1.0, 1.4 g) to the hindfoot. The same foot was tested 10× for each filament strength, and the number of withdrawals was recorded. Animals were only used for a single trial.

Open field test

Open field (OF) assays were performed at ZT0–2 or ZT12–14 in a white chamber measuring 40 cm (length) × 40 cm (width) × 30 cm (height). To habituate the animals to the researcher, mice were handled daily by the researcher for 3–5 d prior to testing. On the experimental day, the mouse was gently placed in the center of the chamber and allowed to move freely. Analyses were performed on the first 5 min following the departure of the researcher. ANY-maze video-tracking software was used to record and analyze the video. The center square of the open field comprised 50% of the total area, and the mouse was considered in the center square only when ≥75% of the mouse's entire body was in this area. Total distance traveled was calculated using the ANY-maze software.

Elevated plus maze

The apparatus used for the elevated plus maze (EPM) test consists of a “cross”-shaped structure with two open arms (30 × 5 cm), two closed arms (30 × 5 cm), and a center platform (5 × 5 cm). The closed arms have high (15 cm) walls enclosing the arms, and the entire apparatus is 30 cm above the floor. For the 5 d prior to testing, mice were handled daily by the researcher. EPM assays were performed within ZT0–2 and ZT12–14 windows. On the experimental day, the mouse was placed in the center platform facing the open arms. The animal was allowed to move freely. The analysis used the first 5 min after the researcher left the room. ANY-maze video-tracking software was used to record and analyze the video. The mouse was considered in the open or closed arm only when ≥75% of the mouse's entire body was in the arm.

Chemogenetics

AAV-DIO-hm3D(Gq)-mCherry virus was injected unilaterally into the CeA of 10–14-week-old mWake Cre/+ mice. Animals were allowed to recover for 2–3 weeks after the surgery, and FC, von Frey, EPM, and OF tests were performed at either ZT0–2 or ZT12–14 during the following 2–3 weeks. CNO (1 mg/kg) was administered by intraperitoneal (IP) injection 40–60 min before performing behavioral assays.

Statistical analysis

Animals were randomly divided into different groups for all experiments. All experiments were conducted by an investigator blinded to the genotypes or experimental conditions. All data from experiments were included for quantification, except for images in immunostaining experiments with excessively high background that precluded accurate analysis. Data processing software used includes ImageJ 1.54d ( Schneider et al., 2012 ), Imaris 9.5 (Oxford Instruments), MATLAB R2020b (MathWorks), and ANY-maze 7 (Stoelting). Statistical analyses and graphing were performed using Excel (Microsoft) and Prism 9 (GraphPad Software). Statistical details, including sample sizes, can be found in the figure legends. Data were presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to test whether a dataset was normally distributed. For comparisons of two normally distributed groups, unpaired Welch's t tests were used. For comparisons of more than two normally distributed groups, one-way ANOVA test was used followed with post hoc Tukey's tests. For assessing rhythmicity, JTK_CYCLE tests were performed ( https://nitecap.org/ ; Hughes et al., 2010 ). For multiple comparisons with two factors, two-way ANOVAs were followed by post hoc Sidak tests.

Results

mWAKE labels a subset of neurons in the CeL that projects broadly to subcortical regions

We previously found that mWAKE is expressed in the CeA, a brain region often considered to be a key output nucleus of the amygdala ( Jolkkonen and Pitkänen, 1998 ; Bell et al., 2021 ). To further characterize these CeA mWAKE cells, we used the mWake V5 transgenic line ( Fig. 1 A ; Bell et al., 2021 ). The CeA is composed of GABAergic neurons, glia, and endothelial cells ( Yeh et al., 2024 ). Immunostaining using anti-V5 and anti-NeuN (a widely used neuronal marker) antibodies revealed that mWAKE-V5 signal is enriched in the CeL and that nearly all mWAKE + cells are neurons ( Fig. 1 B , C ). As expected, higher-magnification imaging showed that mWAKE is predominantly located in the cytosol rather than the nucleus ( Fig. 1 D ). Next, we asked what proportion of neurons in the CeA are labeled by mWAKE. Overall, mWAKE was expressed in ∼20% of neurons in the CeL region ( Fig. 1 C ). Because most neurons in the CeA are GABAergic, we expected that mWAKE + neurons would also likely be GABAergic. We performed RNAscope in situ hybridization using probes for mWake , vGAT , and vGLUT1 and found that most, if not all, mWAKE + neurons are GABAergic (97.1 ± 1.2%; Fig. 1 E , F ).

Figure 1.

The CeA is involved in emotional processing, related to both negative and positive valence emotions ( Izadi and Radahmadi, 2022 ; Moscarello and Penzo, 2022 ; Kong et al., 2025 ). To perform these functions, CeA neurons project to a variety of brain regions, particularly in subcortical areas. We next characterized the projection patterns of CeA mWAKE neurons by injecting a Cre-dependent synaptophysin-GFP (Syp-GFP) virus into the CeA of mWake Cre/+ mice and then imaging CeA mWAKE synaptic terminals ( Fig. 2 A , B ). Most projections were directed to subcortical regions ( Fig. 2 C ). Among the densest projection targets were various thalamic nuclei, substantia innominata (SI), lateral habenula, medial amygdala, nucleus accumbens (NAc), parabrachial nucleus (PBN), zona incerta (ZI), the red nucleus, and substantia nigra compacta (SNc; Fig. 2 C–E , Table 1 ). Overall, the CeA mWAKE projection pattern appears more closely aligned with that previously described for CeA PKC-δ neurons (e.g., SI, SNc, red nucleus; Yu et al., 2017 ), but less prominent projections are also seen for defined targets of CeA SST neurons (e.g., vlPAG; Penzo and Moscarello, 2023 ; Wang et al., 2023 ). Some CeA mWAKE targets receive input from both CeA PKC-δ and CeA SST neurons (e.g., PBN, ZI; Yu et al., 2017 ; Singh et al., 2022 ; Wang et al., 2023 ). Overall, these data suggest that mWAKE neurons are enriched in the CeL and likely comprise a heterogeneous population that includes PKC-δ + and SST + neurons.

Absence of cycling of mWAKE and clock gene expression in the CeA

Previous studies in rodents have suggested that fear learning/memory is under circadian control, with enhanced fear learning and recall during the daytime ( Albrecht and Stork, 2017 ). We thus first asked whether we could also observe rhythmicity in fear learning and memory. We performed fear conditioning assays on wild-type ( C57BL6/J ) mice at either ZT0 or ZT12 and tested fear memory the next day at either ZT0 or ZT12, respectively ( Fig. 3 A , B ). Zeitgeber time refers to time after “lights on.” These data reveal no difference in fear learning on Day 1, but a significant improvement in fear recall for animals at ZT0, compared with ZT12, on Day 2 ( Fig. 3 C ).

Figure 3.

Next, we investigated whether mWAKE might be involved in this diurnal difference in fear memory. Our previous work in flies and mice suggests that WAKE/mWAKE acts downstream of the circadian clock to promote rhythmic neuronal excitability and behaviors ( Liu et al., 2014 , 2023 , 2024 ; Tabuchi et al., 2018 ). Because the CeA has been suggested to harbor a local circadian oscillator ( Lamont et al., 2005 ; Harbour et al., 2014 ), we hypothesized that mWAKE would perform a similar function in this brain region. We performed Western blot analysis using CeA tissue from mWake V5/V5 mice at four different time points. In contrast to our previous studies showing cycling of WAKE and mWAKE levels in flies and other brain regions in mice ( Liu et al., 2014 , 2023 , 2024 ), we did not observe evidence of rhythmic mWAKE expression in the CeA ( Fig. 3 D ). To further address this issue, we performed immunostaining for mWAKE-V5 in the CeA of mWake V5/V5 mice at different time points. Consistent with our Western blot analyses, we found no evidence of rhythmic mWAKE-V5 immunostaining in the CeA ( Fig. 3 E , F ). These data contrast with our previous findings in Drosophila clock neurons and mouse DMH and LA and raise the question of whether clock genes cycle in the CeA region.

We next asked whether we could detect cycling of the core clock protein PER2 in the CeA. We performed immunostaining for PER2 and tdTomato (to label mWAKE + cells) in the CeA in mWake Cre/+ mice at four different time points. Interestingly, contrary to previous reports ( Lamont et al., 2005 ; Harbour et al., 2014 ), we did not observe cycling of PER2 expression in either mWAKE + or mWAKE − cells in the CeA ( Fig. 4 A–C ). To ensure that our immunostaining protocol was able to detect varying levels of PER2 protein, we also analyzed anti-PER2 signals in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) and LA from the same mice. As expected, PER2 signals were significantly higher at ZT12, compared with ZT0, in the SCN and significantly higher at ZT0, compared with ZT12, in the LA ( Fig. 4 D , E ). BMAL1 (brain and muscle Arnt-like 1), another core clock protein, also did not exhibit rhythmic expression in the CeA ( Fig. 4 F–H ). Together, these data suggest that mWAKE and also core clock proteins exhibit no or very weak cycling in the CeA.

mWAKE is expressed in multiple subclusters in the CeA

Our data suggest that mWAKE does not regulate rhythmic behaviors in the CeA, in contrast to other brain regions. To gain clues as to the potential function of mWAKE in the CeA, we next asked what subclusters of CeA neurons express mWAKE. The CeA contains a heterogeneous group of neurons, subdivided into different clusters labeled by markers such as PKC-δ, SST, CRF, and tachykinin 2 (Tac2; McCullough et al., 2018 ). To investigate the molecular identity of mWAKE-expressing CeA neurons, we conducted secondary analysis of a published single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) study performed by Hochgerner et al. (2023) . Of note, mWake transcript levels are typically quite low within a given cell ( Zhang et al., 2015 ; Bell et al., 2021 ; Liu et al., 2023 ); thus, some cells expressing mWake may be missed in this analysis. This scRNA-seq dataset included cells from the entire amygdala and defined 56 GABAergic subclusters. Based on the spatial information provided in the original publication, we consolidated these 56 subclusters into eight larger clusters ( Fig. 5 A , Table 1 ). These analyses show that mWake is enriched in the CeL and intercalated cells of the amygdala (ITC; Fig. 5 B ), a small subregion located between the basolateral amygdala (BLA) and CeA ( Stern et al., 2023 ). Prkcd (encoding PKC-δ) is also highly enriched in the CeL and exhibits overlapping expression with mWake ( Fig. 5 C ), while Sst is more widely expressed, including in the CeL ( Fig. 5 D ). In contrast to Prckd and Sst , Foxp2 colocalized with mWake in cells primarily in the ITC ( Fig. 5 E ), while Crf did not demonstrate prominent labeling of either CeL or ITC ( Fig. 5 F ).

Figure 5.

We next asked which of the 56 GABAergic subclusters expresses mWake at higher levels, compared with the other subclusters. The four GABAergic subclusters with the highest levels of mWake expression were subclusters 3, 4, 8, and 9 ( Fig. 5 G ). Subclusters 3 and 4 map to the ITC region and are colabeled with Foxp2 . The two subclusters with the highest mWake expression were subclusters 8 and 9, both of which are marked by Prckd (subclusters 8 and 9 also express Opiod receptor kappa1/Oprk1 and Ezrin/Ezr , respectively; Fig. 5 B , C , G ). Hochgerner et al. (2023) performed their scRNA-seq analyses at different time points before and after fear conditioning ( Hochgerner et al., 2023 ). Intriguingly, the Prkcd + subclusters 8 and 9 were noted to be among several subclusters exhibiting a marked increase in expression of immediate-early genes (IEGs) and other plasticity-related genes several hours after fear conditioning ( Hochgerner et al., 2023 ).

To confirm the colocalization of mWake with Prkcd and Sst , we next performed RNAscope in situ hybridization assays for CeL neurons. These data demonstrated coexpression of mWake with Prckd and Sst in 38.4 ± 7.5% and 16.3 ± 5.6% of mWake + cells in the CeL, respectively ( Fig. 5 H , I ). To further assess coexpression of mWAKE and PKC-δ, we performed immunostaining using anti-V5 and anti-PKC-δ antibodies in the CeL of mWake V5/V5 mice. Similar to our findings using RNAscope in situ hybridization, we found that 42.1 ± 3.3% of mWAKE + neurons were colabeled with PKC-δ, with higher coexpression in the middle region (on the A-P axis) of the CeL ( Fig. 6 A , B ). We next addressed mWAKE and PKC-δ colocalization in the CeL using a different transgenic line. mWake Cre/+ mice express tdTomato under control of the mWake promoter ( Fig. 2 A ), and so we performed immunostaining using anti-RFP and anti-PKC-δ antibodies in this brain region from mWake Cre/+ mice. These data suggested that 47.1 ± 4.0% of mWake + cells also express PKC-δ + ( Fig. 6 C , D ), again with greater coexpression in the middle region of the CeL. Taken together, these findings reveal that mWAKE is expressed in distinct subclusters in the CeA, including PKC-δ-expressing cells.

Loss of mWAKE induces higher intrinsic excitability in the CeA

In both flies and mice, loss of WAKE/mWAKE leads to increased neuronal excitability in a cell-intrinsic manner, specifically at night ( Liu et al., 2014 , 2023 , 2024 ; Tabuchi et al., 2018 ). Thus, we hypothesized that loss of mWAKE in the CeA would result in greater excitability of CeA mWAKE neurons. To test this hypothesis, we performed ex vivo slice patch-clamp recordings of mWAKE + CeA neurons from control mWake Cre/+ versus mutant mWake Cre/Cre mice at ZT0 and ZT12. The insertion of the Cre-P2A-tdTomato sequence in the coding sequence of mWake not only labels mWAKE + cells but also generates a severe hypomorphic or null allele of mWake ( Bell et al., 2021 ). Because spontaneous firing of CeA mWAKE neurons was not observed in slices, we examined intrinsic excitability of these neurons. At both ZT0 and ZT12, loss of mWAKE resulted in greater intrinsic excitability of CeA mWAKE neurons ( Fig. 7 A–F ). Consistent with the lack of cycling of mWAKE expression, there was no difference in intrinsic excitability between ZT0 and ZT12 for either control or mWake mutant conditions ( Fig. 7 G , H ). These data suggest that reducing mWAKE levels in CeA neurons increases their excitability, but not in a time-dependent manner.

Loss of mWAKE in CeA mWAKE neurons or activation of these cells impairs fear learning

To address the functional role of mWAKE in the CeA, we performed tissue-specific knock-out followed by fear conditioning assays. We injected AAV-Cre virus or AAV-GFP virus (as a control) into the bilateral CeA regions of mWake flox/flox mice ( Fig. 8 A , B , J ) and conducted a series of behavioral assays during ZT0–2 ( Fig. 8 C–I ) or ZT12–14 ( Fig. 8 K–Q ). First, to ensure that any phenotypes observed were not secondary to changes in arousal, baseline anxiety, or pain perception (as can be seen in constitutive mWake mutants or manipulations of LA mWAKE neurons or CeA PKC-δ neurons; Zhang et al., 2015 ; Wilson et al., 2019 ; Singh et al., 2022 ; Liu et al., 2024 ), we performed open field (OF) and elevated plus maze (EPM) tests to assess locomotor and anxiety-like behaviors, as well as von Frey assays to examine touch/pain perception ( Chaplan et al., 1994 ). These assays revealed no differences in locomotor activity, anxiety-like behaviors, or touch sensitivity between animals with loss of mWAKE in the CeA versus controls at either ZT0–2 ( Fig. 8 C–E , I ) or ZT12–14 ( Fig. 8 K–M , Q ). Similarly, baseline freezing behavior was similar between these two groups during these two different time windows ( Fig. 8 F , N ). In contrast, loss of mWAKE in the CeA significantly impaired both fear learning and fear recall the next day, compared with controls, during both ZT0–2 ( Fig. 8 G , H ) and ZT12–14 ( Fig. 8 O , P ).

Figure 8.

Knock-out of mWAKE would be predicted to increase the excitability and thus potentially increase the activity of CeA mWAKE neurons. Thus, we next asked whether activation of these cells would result in a similar phenotype. We chemogenetically activated CeA mWAKE cells by injecting AAV-DIO-hM3D(Gq) virus into the CeA of mWake Cre/+ mice (vs AAV-DIO-eYFP as a control) and administering CNO (clozapine N-oxide, 1 mg/kg) 40–60 min prior to fear conditioning on Day 1 ( Fig. 9 A , B , J ), either at ZT0–2 or ZT12–24. Chemogenetic stimulation of these cells did not affect locomotor, anxiety- or touch-related behaviors in the OF, EPM, or von Frey tests during ZT0–2 ( Fig. 9 C–E , I ) or ZT12–14 ( Fig. 9 K–M , Q ). In addition, activation of mWake + cells in the CeL did not alter baseline freezing behavior during ZT0–2 ( Fig. 9 F ) or ZT12–14 ( Fig. 9 N ). This chemogenetic manipulation significantly impaired fear learning at ZT12–14, but not at ZT0–2 ( Fig. 9 G , O ). Of note, similar to the results from conditional knock-out of mWAKE in the CeA, chemogenetic activation of CeA mWAKE neurons led to impaired fear recall (freezing percentage on Day 2), compared with controls, during both ZT0–2 ( Fig. 9 H ) and ZT12–14 ( Fig. 9 P ).

Figure 9.

Because mWAKE is expressed in a subset of SST + neurons in the CeA, we also asked whether loss of mWAKE in SST + neurons would affect fear learning or memory. We crossed mWake flox/flox mice with Sst Cre mice and performed fear conditioning assays in Sst Cre ; mWake flox/flox mice. No differences were observed in fear learning or memory in Sst Cre ; mWake flox/flox mice, compared with their mWake flox/flox sibling controls in the fear conditioning assay ( Fig. 10 A–C ), as well as OF or EPM tests ( Fig. 10 D–F ) at ZT12–14. Because we did not have access to a PKC-δ-Cre mouse, we were unable to selectively knock out mWAKE in PKC-δ neurons. Taken together, these findings suggest that loss of mWAKE in CeA mWAKE neurons or activation of these cells impairs fear learning/memory.

Figure 10.

mWAKE levels decrease in PKC-δ cells following exposure to fear conditioning

Our data suggest that mWAKE levels in the CeA do not vary in a diurnal manner. However, mWAKE is expressed in CeA PKC-δ neurons that exhibit upregulation of IEGs and plasticity-related genes following fear conditioning. Thus, we asked whether mWAKE levels might also change following fear conditioning. Interestingly, in the scRNA-seq dataset from Hochgerner et al. (2023) , mWake transcript levels exhibited a trend toward being decreased 2 h after fear conditioning. To investigate whether mWAKE protein levels are decreased after fear conditioning, we performed anti-V5 and anti-PKC-δ immunostaining in the CeA of mWake V5/V5 mice 4 h after three different treatments: no fear conditioning, fear conditioning, and footshock alone. Interestingly, we found that mWAKE protein levels decreased 4 h after fear conditioning in PKC-δ + , but not PKC-δ − , cells in the CeA. In contrast, mWAKE levels did not change following footshock alone, in either PKC-δ − and PKC-δ + cells ( Fig. 11 A–C ). Taken together, these data suggest that mWAKE levels in CeA PKC-δ vary dynamically following fear conditioning.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the expression of mWAKE in the CeA and found that mWAKE is present in different neuronal subclusters, including those labeled by PKC-δ and SST in the CeA. Since knock-out of mWAKE in CeA SST cells did not appreciably impact fear learning and memory, we hypothesize that mWAKE acts in CeA PKC-δ neurons to regulate this process. However, it should be emphasized that direct evidence supporting this notion is currently lacking. Moreover, many of the neurons exhibiting the highest levels of mWAKE expression do not coexpress PKC-δ; the biological implications of this observation remain unclear. Another limitation of our study is that experiments were only performed in male animals. Given evidence from animal models and humans that females can exhibit distinct fear-related responses ( Gresack et al., 2009 ; Haskell et al., 2010 ; Shansky, 2015 ), future studies should investigate whether mWAKE also regulates fear learning/memory in females.

Unlike in other mWAKE-expressing brain regions (e.g., DMH and LA; Liu et al., 2023 , 2024 ), mWAKE levels do not cycle in the CeA, which is likely due to absent or weak core clock rhythms in the CeA. In contrast to our data, previous studies have reported oscillations of core clock gene expression in the CeA with peak PER2 expression at ∼ZT12 ( Lamont et al., 2005 ; Segall et al., 2009 ). However, other studies in rodents have found either higher PER2 levels at ∼ZT0 ( Pantazopoulos et al., 2011 ) or no PER2 cycling at all ( Schrader et al., 2011 ). How do we reconcile those findings with our data? From the same animals, we were able to observe the expected cycling of PER2 expression in the SCN and LA, reducing the likelihood of technical issues with our immunostaining protocol or reagents. Alternatively, because PER2 rhythms in the CeA are lost following adrenalectomy ( Amir et al., 2004 ), unintentional exposure of animals to stress during animal handling or husbandry (particularly if occurring in a rhythmic manner) might account for these discrepancies ( Pantazopoulos et al., 2011 ). While our data argue against robust rhythms of clock gene expression in the CeA, weak cycling cannot be ruled out. In addition, it remains possible that the CeA indirectly participates in the circadian regulation of fear learning and memory, by acting downstream of the SCN.

If mWAKE does not act as a clock output molecule in the CeA, what might be its function in this brain region? Interestingly, there is precedent for an mWAKE-related molecule in noncircadian regulation of a biological process. The Drosophila homolog of mWAKE is encoded by wake , which is alternatively spliced to generate a neuronal isoform and a non-neuronal isoform encoding Banderuola (BND; Mauri et al., 2014 ). BND was shown to be required for asymmetric cell division and exhibits dynamic changes in its subcellular localization during the cell cycle; BND in turn regulates the localization of Discs-large (DLG), a scaffold protein known to organize signaling complexes to coordinate a variety of developmental or proliferative processes ( Roberts et al., 2012 ; Mauri et al., 2014 ; Ross et al., 2022 ). Taken together with our previous work showing that WAKE/mWAKE in neurons upregulates the expression of ion channels and receptors at the plasma membrane in a circadian-dependent fashion ( Tabuchi et al., 2018 ; Bell et al., 2021 ; Liu et al., 2024 ), these findings suggest that a fundamental function of WAKE molecules may be to organize signaling complexes in a spatiotemporally specific manner, which can be related to the circadian clock but may also be related to timing that depends on other biological processes.

We find that mWAKE protein levels are decreased ∼4 h after fear conditioning, specifically in PKC-δ-expressing cells. These data suggest that mWAKE in the CeA plays a dynamic, noncircadian role in fear learning and memory. What are the functional implications of these changes in mWAKE levels for these behaviors? We previously showed that loss of WAKE or mWAKE function in Drosophila or mouse neurons resulted in an increase in intrinsic neuronal excitability ( Liu et al., 2014 , 2024 ; Tabuchi et al., 2018 ). Similarly, we find that the intrinsic excitability of CeA mWAKE neurons is greater in mWake mutants, suggesting that mWAKE also acts to suppress the excitability of neurons in this brain region. Taken together, these observations suggest a model whereby mWAKE expression in the CeA is downregulated following fear conditioning, enhancing neuronal excitation/activity to promote fear memory formation. However, because knock-out of mWAKE impairs fear learning/memory, we hypothesize that milder increases in CeA mWAKE neuron excitability/activity (predicted to occur with the physiological decreases in mWAKE expression following fear conditioning) may be required to promote fear learning and memory. Interestingly, mWAKE-expressing CeA PKC-δ neurons were among the small set of subclusters exhibiting the strongest increase of IEG transcription after fear conditioning, and such cells have been suggested to serve as “engram” neurons ( Hochgerner et al., 2023 ). Thus, it is tempting to speculate that changes in mWAKE levels after fear conditioning may facilitate the formation of engrams in the CeA, but future work is needed to test this hypothesis.
