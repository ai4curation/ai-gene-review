BACKGROUND & AIMS: Esophageal adenocarcinoma (EA) and its premalignant lesion,
Barrett's esophagus (BE), are characterized by a strong and yet unexplained male
predominance (with a male-to-female ratio in EA incidence of up to 6:1).
Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified more than 20
susceptibility loci for these conditions. However, potential sex differences in
genetic associations with BE/EA remain largely unexplored.
METHODS: Given strong genetic overlap, BE and EA cases were combined into a
single case group for analysis. These were compared with population-based
controls. We performed sex-specific GWAS of BE/EA in 3 separate studies and then
used fixed-effects meta-analysis to provide summary estimates for >9 million
variants for male and female individuals. A series of downstream analyses were
conducted separately in male and female individuals to identify genes associated
with BE/EA and the genetic correlations between BE/EA and other traits.
RESULTS: We included 6758 male BE/EA cases, 7489 male controls, 1670 female
BE/EA cases, and 6174 female controls. After Bonferroni correction, our
meta-analysis of sex-specific GWAS identified 1 variant at chromosome 6q11.1
(rs112894788, KHDRBS2-MTRNR2L9, PBONF = .039) that was statistically
significantly associated with BE/EA risk in male individuals only, and 1 variant
at chromosome 8p23.1 (rs13259457, PRSS55-RP1L1, PBONF = 0.057) associated, at
borderline significance, with BE/EA risk in female individuals only. We also
observed strong genetic correlations of BE/EA with gastroesophageal reflux
disease in male individuals and obesity in female individuals.
CONCLUSIONS: The identified novel sex-specific variants associated with BE/EA
could improve the understanding of the genetic architecture of the disease and
the reasons for the male predominance.

## Full Text

Esophageal adenocarcinoma (EA) is a highly fatal cancer with increasing incidence in many Western populations during the past 4 decades.1,2 EA is characterized by a strong male predominance, with a male-to-female ratio in incidence of up to 6:1 in the United States.3–5 Although much attention has been given to the striking increase in EA incidence among white male individuals (10-fold increase since 1973 in the United States),6,7 EA incidence has also increased among white female individuals such that the sex ratio has remained relatively stable over time.2 Male individuals are also more likely than female individuals to develop Barrett’s esophagus (BE), the premalignant lesion of EA.8

Reasons for the male predominance in BE and EA remain poorly understood, but likely reflect differing prevalence of environmental risk factors, sex hormonal and reproductive factors and differing effects of underlying susceptibility alleles between male and female individuals.9 Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD),10,11 central obesity,12 and tobacco smoking13,14 are established risk factors for BE and EA. These factors, however, may not explain the sex differences in BE and EA incidence, as their prevalence and effect-size of associations with the risk of BE and EA are not dissimilar between sexes.13,15–20 Previous studies have made efforts to evaluate the influence of sex hormonal exposures and reproductive factors on the risk of EA.21–24; however, these studies were limited by small numbers of EA cases in women and inherent limitations of observational studies, and thus results to date have been mixed.3

Findings from large genomic studies suggest that sex-specific genetic architecture could contribute to sex differences in human disease.25,26 Although genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified more than 20 susceptibility loci for BE and/or EA,27–30 little is known about whether germline genetic effects differ between male and female individuals. We therefore conducted a sex-specific genome-wide meta-analysis to identify sex-specific susceptibility loci for BE and EA.

MethodsStudy PopulationWe used data from patients with EA or BE and control participants from studies in Europe, North America, and Australia, the details of which we have described elsewhere.27,31 Namely, genome-wide genotype data were obtained from 3 GWAS: the international Barrett’s and Esophageal Adenocarcinoma Consortium (BEACON; https://esocan.org/beacon), as well as studies from Cambridge (United Kingdom) and Bonn (Germany).27,29,30,32 The BEACON GWAS included 1508 EA cases, 2406 BE cases, and 2177 controls from 15 epidemiologic studies conducted in North America, Western Europe, and Australia.29 To increase the statistical power, an additional 4541 controls of European ancestry from dbGaP were merged with the BEACON data.27 The dbGaP controls were collected from the following studies after obtaining approval from the respective committees: High Density SNP Association Analysis of Melanoma (phs000187.v1.p1), Genome-Wide Association Study of Parkinson Disease (phs000196.v2.p1), and Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC) Study (phs000524.v1.p1). The Cambridge studies included 995 EA cases from the UK Stomach and Oesophageal Cancer Study, 873 BE cases from the UK Barrett’s Esophagus Gene Study, and 3408 controls from the WTCCC2 including the National Blood Service (UKBS) and 1958 birth (58C) studies.27 The Bonn GWAS included 1609 EA cases, 1037 BE cases, and 3537 controls.27,32 All participants were of European ancestry. Patients with BE were identified by histopathological diagnosis of intestinal metaplasia, and patients with EA had a histopathological diagnosis of adenocarcinoma. Each contributing study complied with their institutional review board requirements and all participants gave informed consent.Genotyping and ImputationGenotyping of buffy coat or whole blood DNA from all participants was performed on high-density single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays (Illumina, San Diego, CA), in accordance with standard quality control (QC) procedures for each participating study.27,33 All genotyped samples and variants met the following inclusion criteria: per variant and per sample missingness ≤ 3%; SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 1%; SNPs with P ≥ .0001 in controls and P ≥ 5 × 10−10 in BE and EA cases for Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, no familial relationships, extreme heterozygosity rate, or outliers. Sex was confirmed genetically for all subjects as part of QC. For the imputation of autosomal chromosomes, we used SHAPEIT version 2.1234,35 for phasing of the genotyped SNPs and IMPUTE2 version 2.3.136 for imputation of missing SNPs, using the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 haplotypes (October 2014 release) as a reference panel.27 An additional flag of -chrX was added when running imputation for the X chromosome. Post-imputation QC was conducted in each study by excluding SNPs with imputation quality score <0.4 or minor allele count (computed as 2*MAF*sample size) <20.37 We included in the analysis only those SNPs that passed the post-imputation QC in all 3 studies (ie, BEACON, Cambridge, and Bonn).Genome-Wide Meta-AnalysisGiven the substantial genetic overlap between BE and EA (rg = 1.0),27,38 we performed genetic association tests comparing a combined BE and EA case group (“BE/EA”) with controls to maximize statistical power. Sex-stratified logistic regression analyses were conducted separately in the BEACON, Cambridge, and Bonn datasets using SNPTEST version 2.5.4-beta3,39,40 adjusted for study-specific top 4 principal components for autosomal chromosomes. Variants were defined as allele dosages based on an additive model.27 The X chromosome was analyzed separately using “newml” in SNPTEST.39,40 We did the sex-specific meta-analysis with the fixed-effects inverse variance–weighting approach in METAL version 2011–03-25, and considered a standard genome-wide significance threshold of 5 × 10−8.41 To evaluate the independence of associated SNPs with Pmeta < 5 × 10−8 in sex-specific analysis, we performed a stepwise selection procedure implemented in GCTA-COJO (GCTA version 1.93.0beta).42 COJO enables conditional and joint association analysis using the sex-specific meta-analysis summary statistics. We used the BEACON dataset comprising 10,632 participants as the reference data to estimate linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns. Only SNPs with P < 5 × 10−8 in both the sex-specific meta-analysis and the conditional analysis were deemed statistically significant and reported. To account for multiple testing, we applied Bonferroni correction with PBONF <0.05 considered statistically significant. In addition to the Bonferroni correction, a less conservative adjusted P value, based on false discovery rate (FDR), was calculated for each SNP. The threshold for FDR significance was 0.05.In addition to the analyses stratified by sex, we examined for SNP-by-sex interactions. We first assessed study-specific interactions using SNPTEST version 2.5.4-beta3 with method “newml,” adjusted for study-specific top 4 principal components,39,40 then pooled the study-specific estimates using a joint 2 degree-of-freedom (2df) meta-analysis (JMA) of the main SNP effect and the SNP-by-sex interaction effect.43 The JMA accounts for the covariance between the SNP regression estimate and the SNP-by-sex regression estimate and has greater statistical power than other methods to detect interaction effects.43 This approach can identify SNPs either having a significant main effect or an interaction effect, or only showing significant associations when the interaction effect is considered.Genomic control correction was applied on all meta-analyses to account for population stratification or unaccounted for relatedness. We used quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots and the genomic inflation factor (λ) to detect potential population stratification in each study as well as in the meta-analyses. We used R 3.6.1 to generate Q-Q and Manhattan plots and LocusZoom version 1.4 to generate regional plots.Functional AnnotationTo explore the biological relevance of identified loci, we performed functional annotation using FUMA v1.3.5e.44 All SNPs in LD (at r2 ≥ 0.6) with the significant SNPs, with Pmeta < .05 in the sex-specific meta-analysis, and with MAF > 0.01 were selected for annotation, which were obtained from ANNOVAR categories,45 combined annotation-dependent depletion (CADD) scores46 and RegulomeDB.47 CADD score is the score of deleteriousness of SNP predicted by 63 functional annotations with the score >12.37 as the threshold to be deleterious and the score >20 indicating the variant is ranked in the top 1% of highest scoring variants.46 RegulomeDB score is a categorical score (ranging from 1a to 7) based on expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) and chromatin marks. The lower of the RegulomeDB score, the more likely it is that the SNP has a regulatory function. We also investigated the biological impact of the susceptibility loci using HaploReg v4.148 and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) v849,50 for gene-expression and regulatory data derived from ENCODE, Roadmap projects, and other resources.Gene-based and Pathway-enrichment AnalysesGene-based analysis was conducted in MAGMA (v1.07)51 using the per-SNP summary statistics obtained from the sex-specific meta-analysis. SNPs were assigned to genes based on their position according to the National Center for Biotechnology Information 37.3 build with no additional boundary placed around the genes. LD between SNPs was estimated using reference data from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 European ancestry samples. It resulted in 18,856 protein-coding genes (each containing at least 1 SNP from the meta-analysis) being tested for male individuals, and 18,863 genes for female individuals. We used a Bonferroni corrected P value to account for multiple comparisons (Pgene < 2.65 × 10−6 [0.05/18,863]). The derived gene-based P values were further used for gene-set analysis implemented in MAGMA. Competitive P value for a specific gene set was computed for 12,165 predefined gene sets curated from MsigDB (version 7.0),52,53 considering known biological and metabolic pathways from Gene Ontology (9996 gene sets), BIOCARTA (289 gene sets), KEGG (186 gene sets), PID (196 gene sets), and REACTOME (1499 gene sets). The Bonferroni-corrected significant threshold for gene-set analysis was set to 0.05/12,165 = 4.11 × 10−6.Transcriptome-Wide Association AnalysisWe applied metaXcan54 methods to integrate the summary statistics from the sex-specific meta-analysis with eQTL information to map genes whose predicted expression levels in esophageal tissues were associated with disease risk. SNP weights and their respective covariance for 2 relevant esophageal tissues (esophagus-gastroesophageal junction and esophagus mucosa) were obtained from PredictDB Data Repository (http://predictdb.org/) based on GTEx v8 database. The total number of genes tested for these tissues were 6241 and 8448, respectively (total across all tissues, 14,689). Genes with P < 3.40 × 10−6 (0.05/14,689) were considered to have gene expression profiles statistically significantly associated with BE/EA.Cross-Trait Genetic CorrelationsLD score regression55 was applied to estimate genetic correlations (rg) between BE/EA and a range of diseases, disorders, and human traits (767 total) based on GWAS summary statistics obtained from publicly available databases in LD-Hub (http://ldsc.broadinstitute.org/).56 Calculations were conducted separately for male and female individuals. The Bonferroni corrected significant threshold was P < 3.26 10−5 (0.05/767/2).

We first assessed study-specific interactions using SNPTEST version 2.5.4-beta3 with method “newml,” adjusted for study-specific top 4 principal components,39,40 then pooled the study-specific estimates using a joint 2 degree-of-freedom (2df) meta-analysis (JMA) of the main SNP effect and the SNP-by-sex interaction effect.43 The JMA accounts for the covariance between the SNP regression estimate and the SNP-by-sex regression estimate and has greater statistical power than other methods to detect interaction effects.43 This approach can identify SNPs either having a significant main effect or an interaction effect, or only showing significant associations when the interaction effect is considered.

Genomic control correction was applied on all meta-analyses to account for population stratification or unaccounted for relatedness. We used quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots and the genomic inflation factor (λ) to detect potential population stratification in each study as well as in the meta-analyses. We used R 3.6.1 to generate Q-Q and Manhattan plots and LocusZoom version 1.4 to generate regional plots.

Functional AnnotationTo explore the biological relevance of identified loci, we performed functional annotation using FUMA v1.3.5e.44 All SNPs in LD (at r2 ≥ 0.6) with the significant SNPs, with Pmeta < .05 in the sex-specific meta-analysis, and with MAF > 0.01 were selected for annotation, which were obtained from ANNOVAR categories,45 combined annotation-dependent depletion (CADD) scores46 and RegulomeDB.47 CADD score is the score of deleteriousness of SNP predicted by 63 functional annotations with the score >12.37 as the threshold to be deleterious and the score >20 indicating the variant is ranked in the top 1% of highest scoring variants.46 RegulomeDB score is a categorical score (ranging from 1a to 7) based on expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) and chromatin marks. The lower of the RegulomeDB score, the more likely it is that the SNP has a regulatory function. We also investigated the biological impact of the susceptibility loci using HaploReg v4.148 and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) v849,50 for gene-expression and regulatory data derived from ENCODE, Roadmap projects, and other resources.

DiscussionSex differences in risk and prognosis are common phenomenon in human diseases.26 Studies have investigated genetic determinants of such sex differences and reported several sex-specific genetic associations across various diseases and traits, such as glioma,58 Alzheimer disease,59,60 and longevity.61 This study was the first attempt to scan sex-specific genetic associations with the risk of BE/EA, with the aim of assessing whether sex-specific genetic architecture could contribute to sex differences in BE/EA risk. We identified 2 female-specific independent loci located in PRSS55-RP1L1 (rs13259457 at 8p23.1) and DPYSL2 (rs17321041 at 8p21.2), and 3 male-specific independent loci located in KHDRBS2-MTRNR2L9 (rs112894788 at 6q11.1), MGST1-LMO (rs35827298 at 12p12.3), and CRTC1 (rs2003476 at 19p13.11) associated with risk of BE/EA. Three of these loci (8p23.1, 8p21.2, and 12p12.3) were not identified in previous BE/EA GWASs, which pooled together male and female individuals.27–30SNP rs13259457 lies ∼23kb downstream of PRSS55 (serine protease 55) and ∼28kb downstream of RP1L1 (retinitis pigmentosa-1-like 1). PRSS55, also known as TSP1 (thrombospondin 1), encodes a secreted protein that possesses important biological functions in tumorigenesis and metastasis.62–64 Secreted TSP1 from esophageal cancer cells is mediated by Rab37 to inhibit p-FAK/p-paxillin/p-ERK migration signaling and promotes the neovasculature of tumor microenvironment and tumor progression.65 Studies have shown that TSP1 was upregulated in the metaplasia-dysplasia-EA sequence in 150 micro-dissected esophageal stroma.66 Furthermore, an expression analysis in a cohort of patients with BE showed that increased expression of TSP1 was associated with almost 4-fold higher risk of progression to EA and poorer survival outcomes in patients with EA.66 The associations between RP1L1 and cancer are largely unknown. Limited studies have reported RP1L1 mutations in gastric cancer,67 and linked RP1L1 mutations with dopamine-agonist resistance in prolactinoma.68 In the present study, rs13259457 exhibited strong interactions with sex, with the effects of risk allele G on BE/EA significantly different by sex. The biological mechanism needs further investigation.SNP rs17321041 is an intronic variant of DPYSL2 (dihydropyrimidinase like 2), also known as CRMP2 (collapsin response mediator protein-2), a gene that encodes a multifunctional adaptor protein that promotes microtubule assembly.69
CRMP2 expressed in tumor tissues and its phosphorylation induced by CDK5 has been observed in the nuclei of tumor cells.70
CRMP2 interacts with neurofibromatosis type 1 (Nf1), a tumor suppressor gene that produces neurofibromin functioning as a negative regulator of Ras.71 Expression and phosphorylation of CRMP2 and CRMP2-neurofibromin interaction have been further linked to cancer proliferation and progression, including breast,72 glioma,73 lung,74 and lymphoma.70 In addition, rs17321041 was recently reported to be associated with diastolic blood pressure by GWAS,75 suggesting potential shared genetic predisposition between BE/EA and hypertension as they have common risk factors (ie, obesity). eQTL analysis showed that rs17321041 is cis-eQTL of PNMA2 (paraneoplastic antigen Ma2) in esophageal tissues. The functions of PNMA2 are not clear. Limited evidence has indicated its role in tumorigenesis, and reported PNMA2 expression could be a biomarker for gastrointestinal neuroendocrine tumors.76,77SNP rs35827298 is located ~50 kb downstream from MGST1 (microsomal glutathione S-transferase 1) and 133kb downstream from LMO3 (LIM domain only 3), and acts as a cis-eQTL of MGST1. MGST1 is a member of the superfamily of membrane-associated proteins in eicosanoid and glutathione metabolism (MAPEG). MGST1 encodes a protein localized to the endoplasmic reticulum and outer mitochondrial membrane that is responsible for detoxifying electrophilic xenobiotics and neutralizing oxidative stress.78,79 Studies have shown that MGST1 is upregulated in various cancer types, such as lung, prostate, brain, and colorectal,79 and linked its expression to tumorigenesis and apoptosis.80 Recently, a study of genetic variations in inflammation-related pathways found that MGST1 variants were associated with increased risk of BE and EA in European populations.57 LMO members are important regulators in cell fate determination and differentiation. The oncogenic role of LMO3 was first described in neuroblastoma, where LMO3 deregulated expression was associated with genesis and progression of neuroblastoma.81 Since then, increasing evidence suggests that LMO3 could promote cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis in hepatocellular carcinoma,82 gastric cancer,83 clear cell renal cell carcinoma,84 and lung cancer.85 These oncogenic roles may be due to the interactions between LMO3 and p53,86 HEN2,87 LATS1,82 or microRNAs.85Gene-based and enrichment analysis implicated additional genes associated with BE/EA, including SLC22A3 and ISL1. SLC22A3 (solute carrier family 22 member 3) belongs to the solute carrier superfamily and were recently reported to be candidate genes of BE and EA in a study investigating eQTLs in esophageal tissues.88 In addition, downregulation of SLC22A3 (solute carrier family 22 member 3) could drive early tumor invasion and metastasis in familial esophageal cancer.89
ISL1 (LIM homeobox 1) encodes LIM-homeodomain transcription factor and promotes tumor progression in multiple cancer types, such as gastric and breast cancer.90,91 A recent GWAS of smoking status also implicated ISL1, suggesting ISL1 may be a common susceptibility gene between BE/EA and their risk factor, smoking.92Strengths of the current study include the advantage of large worldwide consortium of BE and EA with high-quality case-control and cohort parent studies, providing us with a rare opportunity to perform the first-ever genome-wide sex-specific analysis for BE and EA. Our comprehensive downstream analysis revealed the shared genetic predisposition between BE/EA and their established risk factors (ie, smoking, obesity). Our evaluation of genetic correlations between BE/EA and other phenotypes highlighted overlap with GERD in male individuals and obesity in female individuals. A recent study that investigated shared genetic components of obesity-related traits and BE/EA also revealed strong genetic correlation for body mass index only in female individuals.93 These findings may provide valuable additional clues regarding male predominance of BE and EA, and provide sex-specific early detection and treatment strategy. Our study has several limitations. First, although we have used data from a consortia of studies of BE and EA and meta-analyzed all available data to maximize statistical power to identify signals, further data from additional patients, particularly female patients, would have led to identification of more risk loci. Furthermore, we did not have an independent replication dataset. To mitigate the concern of false discovery, we used 20 previously recognized susceptibility loci of BE/EA as “positive controls” and observed similar effect estimates as reported previously. In addition, we applied 2 conservative methods, Bonferroni correction and FDR, to account for multiple testing and found that 3 male-specific loci were significant with FDR adjustment, with one of them (rs112894788) remaining statistically significant with Bonferroni correction. Second, consistent with prior GWAS,27,29 we examined associations with a combined outcome of BE and EA. As such, this may have diluted or eliminated a sex-specific effect that only presents in one disease phenotype (BE or EA). However, when we investigated the associations of the 5 identified sex-specific loci separately for BE and EA, we observed similar effect estimates as found for the combined BE/EA outcome. Furthermore, in prior sex-combined GWAS, the risk loci identified for BE were also associated with EA.27 Third, our sample size may not provide sufficient power for genome-wide interaction analysis. Therefore, we applied joint meta-analysis of SNP main and SNP-by-sex interaction. This method could provide optimal statistical power.43,94,95 Fourth, although we conducted extensive functional prediction for the identified variants, some of the explorations such as gene expression analysis using GTEx were not sex-specific. Validation of these biological functions in cell lines and animal models are also lacking. Further sex-specific experimental studies are needed to reveal the underling biological mechanisms. In addition, the power is limited for pathway-based analysis and cross-trait analysis in female individuals due to the relatively small sample size. Therefore, we cannot rule out the associations between BE/EA in female individuals with the pathways and traits that we identified in male individuals. We do not have data of sex hormone exposure. It is possible that genetic variants interacted with sex hormonal factors to confer the risk of BE/EA differently between sex groups. Furthermore, we focused on common genetic variation (MAF >1%) represented on genotyping platforms, which often exhibit moderate genetic effects. Further large-scale studies based on whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing would be required to identify additional sex-specific associations with rare variants.In conclusion, this meta-analysis identified 3 novel loci associated with the risk of BE/EA in a sex-dependent manner. These findings warrant further mechanistic investigations to how these variants influence the sex differences in BE/EA, and their interactions with other factors that differ by sex (eg, sexual hormones and immune responses96). The identified sex-specific genetic determinants of BE/EA may further guide risk prediction specifically targeted for male and female individuals.
