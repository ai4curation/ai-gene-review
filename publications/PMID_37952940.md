## Full Text

Abstract

Information about dynamic head motion is conveyed by a central “striolar” zone of vestibular hair cells and afferent neurons in the inner ear. How vestibular hair cells are tuned to transduce dynamic stimuli at the molecular level is not well understood. Here we take advantage of the differential expression pattern of tmc1 , tmc2a , and tmc2b , which encode channel subunits of the mechanotransduction complex in zebrafish vestibular hair cells. To test the role of various combinations of Tmc subunits in transducing dynamic head movements, we measured reflexive eye movements induced by high-frequency stimuli in single versus double tmc mutants. We found that Tmc2a function correlates with the broadest range of frequency sensitivity, whereas Tmc2b mainly contributes to lower-frequency responses. Tmc1, which is largely excluded from the striolar zone, plays a minor role in sensing lower-frequency stimuli. Our study suggests that the Tmc subunits impart functional differences to the mechanotransduction of dynamic stimuli.

Significance Statement Information about dynamic head movements is transmitted by sensory receptors, known as hair cells, in the labyrinth of the inner ear. The sensitivity of hair cells to fast or slow movements of the head differs according to cell type. Whether the mechanotransduction complex that converts mechanical stimuli into electrical signals in hair cells participates in conveying frequency information is not clear. Here we find that the transmembrane channel-like 1/2 genes, which encode a central component of the complex, are differentially expressed in the utricle and contribute to frequency sensitivity in zebrafish.

Keywords: striola, Tmc1, Tmc2a, Tmc2b, vestibular hair cells, zebrafish

Introduction

Sensory hair cells of the inner ear transmit information about sound and head movements, and in the lateral line system in fish and frogs, they aid in orientation to water currents. Although all hair cells are activated by mechanical deflections, different end organs within the inner ear and lateral line organs have evolved unique features to subserve the senses of balance, hearing, and “distant touch.” Further specialization within each end organ extends this division of labor. For example, the utricle, which senses linear acceleration and gravity, possesses a central “striolar” zone of hair cells and afferent neurons that is more sensitive to transient, high-frequency stimuli than the peripheral zone ( Goldberg, 2000 ; Songer and Eatock, 2013 ; Eatock, 2018 ). A recent in vivo imaging study of the zebrafish utricle demonstrated that the decomposition of sustained versus transient signals is achieved at the hair-cell level ( Tanimoto et al., 2022 ). Static tilt was preferentially transduced by extrastriolar hair cells, whereas striolar hair cells were preferentially excited by multifrequency (2.2–100 Hz) vibrational stimuli. Gathering evidence also suggests that specialization of function in hair cells can occur at the level of the mechanotransduction complex, which participates in the conversion of mechanical energy into electrical signals. For example, TMC1 and TMC2, which are central subunits of the complex, differ in terms of expression and function among hair cell types ( Kawashima et al., 2015 ; Chou et al., 2017 ; Chen et al., 2020 ; Smith et al., 2020 ; Zhu et al., 2021 ; Kindig et al., 2023 ). Whether the mechanotransduction complex confers some level of frequency selectivity in hair cells remains unclear.

We report here that tmc1/2a/2b are differentially expressed in the extrastriolar and striolar regions of the zebrafish utricle, offering a unique opportunity for assessing frequency sensitivity. As the striolar region is predicted to be more sensitive to transient, high-frequency stimuli in many species, we developed a simple, noninvasive method to assess eye movements in zebrafish larvae in response to high frequencies. We based our assay on previous reports demonstrating that bone-conducted vibration (BCV) at 500 Hz selectively activates central zone afferents in the guinea pig utricle ( Curthoys et al., 2006 , 2016 ; Curthoys and Vulovic, 2011 ). We found that vibration-induced ocular responses in zebrafish larvae were sensitive to a similar range of high frequencies at low g forces (0.02 g ) as those reported for responses to BCV in humans and other species ( Curthoys et al., 2017 ). Although routine swimming in zebrafish involves low velocities and acceleration, during fast movements, the frequency of head movements in the yaw plane can be as high as 85 Hz in 4-day-old zebrafish larvae ( McLean and Fetcho, 2009 ). Moreover, fast startle responses can result in peak accelerations of the body up to 280 ms −2 at 7 days post fertilization (dpf) ( Müller and van Leeuwen, 2004 ; Voesenek et al., 2018 ). The high frequencies and g forces tested here result in accelerations that are presumably experienced during the escape from predators and the capture of prey. The use of high-frequency stimuli in our noninvasive behavioral assay enabled us to (1) measure the biophysical limits of the larval utricle, which appears to be on par with the larval saccule, the end organ dedicated to hearing in fish ( Bhandiwad et al., 2013 ), (2) examine frequency sensitivity more broadly in tmc mutant hair cells in vivo, and (3) take advantage of the differential expression patterns of the tmcs to test for differences in frequency sensitivity. We found that vibration-induced eye movements (VibEMs) were absent or dramatically reduced in mutants carrying a null allele of tomt , tmie , or otogelin (the latter leading to the loss of the utricular otolith). We then tested tmc single and double mutants. Our experiments indicate that tmc2a is required for the sensitivity of utricular hair cells to a wide range of frequencies. In contrast, tmc2b and, to a much lesser extent, tmc1 are required for sensitivity to the lower range of frequencies tested here.

Materials and Methods

Zebrafish care and use

Zebrafish were maintained and bred using standard procedures and followed guidelines of Laboratory Animal Care and Use stipulated by Stanford University. The mutant alleles used for this study include tmie ru1000 and tmie t26171 and the tmc1/2a/2b null alleles from a previous study ( Gleason et al., 2009 ; Pacentine and Nicolson, 2019 ; Smith et al., 2020 ) and were maintained in a top long fin background. Homozygous otogelin mutants and wild-type (WT) siblings were provided by the Bagnall Lab at Washington University, St. Louis ( Roberts et al., 2017 ). Fish embryos and larvae were kept in a 28.5°C incubator in an E3 embryo medium. VibEMs of all homozygous mutant and WT larvae were tested at developmental stages before sex determination occurred.

Genotyping

All alleles were identified by using Kompetitive Allele-Specific PCR genotyping (LGC Genomics), which uses endpoint fluorescence detection to discriminate tagged alleles. otogelin mutants were identified by inspecting the otic vesicle morphologically.

FM vital dye injection

Direct microinjection of 3 µM red-shifted N -(3-triethylammoniumpropyl)-4-(6-(4-(diethylamino)phenyl)hexatrienyl)pyridinium dibromide (FM 4-64; Invitrogen) was conducted as described previously ( Smith et al., 2020 ). Briefly, 2–3 nl of 3 µM FM 4-64 in 0.1 M KCl was injected into 6 dpf otic capsules immediately prior to in vivo confocal imaging. Larvae were anesthetized with 0.03% 3-aminobenzoic acid ethyl ester (MESAB, Western Chemical) and embedded in 1.5% low melting point agarose in 1× E3 on a depression slide throughout injection and imaging. Injected FM 4-64 was not flushed prior to imaging to reduce damage to hair cells, and hair cells were exposed to the dye throughout imaging.

Injection needles were pulled from borosilicate glass capillary tubes (Sutter, item #BF150-86-10, OD: 1.5 mm, ID: 0.86 mm, fire-polished, with filament) using a Model P-97 flaming/brown micropipette puller (Sutter Instrument). Needles were beveled using a BV-10 Microelectrode Beveler as previously described ( Smith et al., 2020 ). Working solutions of FM 4-64 were prepared in 0.1 M KCl from a 300 µM stock on the day of use and protected from light. Needles were calibrated using a slide micrometer to ensure delivery of consistent volumes.

RNA-FISH

RNA-FISH assays used the hybridization chain reaction [(HCR) Molecular Instruments] assay to detect tmc1/2a/2b mRNAs in situ in the zebrafish utricle. DNA oligonucleotide probes targeting the tmc genes were designed by Molecular Instruments and used per the manufacturer's instructions. Each probe was assigned to a separate fluorophore channel to enable multiplex detection of tmc1/2a/2b in each sample.

Larvae were raised in 1× E3 with 0.003% phenylthiourea (PTU) starting at 20–24 h post fertilization (hpf) to prevent pigmentation, and E3 + PTU was replaced daily. At 6 dpf, larvae were collected, anesthetized, and fixed overnight in 4% p -formaldehyde (PFA) in 1× PBS at 4°C. After fixation, larvae were rinsed in PBS, dehydrated and permeabilized with 100% methanol, and stored in 100% methanol at −20°C until use.

RNA-FISH followed the official protocol for HCR in whole-mount zebrafish, from the manufacturer. Briefly, larvae were rehydrated from methanol into phosphate-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST) by stepwise, 5-min incubations in PBST with 75, 50, and 25% methanol, followed by five incubations in PBST. Rehydrated larvae were permeabilized with 10 µg/ml proteinase K in PBST at room temperature for 10–15 min. Larvae were then rinsed with PBST, post-fixed in 4% PFA in 1× PBS for 20 min, and rinsed 5× for 5 min in PBST.

Larvae were incubated in a pre-hybridization buffer for 30 min at 37°C prior to the addition of the probes. A pooled solution of the tmc1/2a/2b probes was prepared in a probe hybridization buffer as described in the manufacturer's protocol. Larvae were incubated in a probe hybridization buffer with probes overnight at 37°C. The next day, larvae were washed 4× for 15 min in a probe wash buffer and then 2× for 5 min in 5× saline-sodium citrate with 0.1% Tween 20 (SSCT) at room temperature.

Amplification hairpin oligonucleotides were prepared and applied as described in the manufacturer's protocol. Individual hairpin h1/h2 solutions were denatured at 95°C for 90 s and cooled to room temperature prior to pooling in the amplification buffer. Larvae incubated overnight in the dark at room temperature in the hairpin solution. The hairpin solution was removed by multiple wash steps in 5× SSCT and stored at 4°C in the dark prior to imaging.

Transmission electron microscopy

Larvae anesthetized with 0.03% 3-aminobenzoic acid ethyl ester were prefixed by immersion in 2.0% glutaraldehyde and 1.0% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Specimens were then fixed with 1.0% OsO 4 in H 2 O for 10 min on ice, followed by fixation and contrast with 1.0% uranyl acetate for 1 h on ice, and then dehydrated with several steps in ethanol and embedded in Epon. Ultrathin sections were stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate.

Confocal microscopy and image analysis

All confocal imaging was performed with an LSM 700 laser-scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss). Three-dimensional views of the entire field of individual cells were generated using Imaris software; two-dimensional section views of individual cells were generated using ImageJ software.

Stimulation and recording equipment

A customized device was constructed to measure the eye movements of zebrafish larvae induced by vibration; the main functional domains include a shaker and an imaging video system for recording eye responses. Larval fish (6–7 dpf, unless otherwise noted) were mounted in a fish-shaped chamber with 1.5% low melting agarose in a dorsal-up position parallel to the horizontal plan. After solidification, the agarose around the head was gently removed using a soft fine wire under a stereoscope to free up the eyes, whereas the rest of the body remained immobilized. Approximately 10 μl of E3 media was added to the exposed head area to keep the larvae moist. After mounting, the chamber was put on a 65 mm petri dish that was directly connected to a mini-shaker (Model 4810, Brüel & Kjær). To avoid slipping during the vibration simulation, 3% methylcellulose was applied to the bottom of the chamber for firm adhesion to the petri dish. For recording eye movements, a 10× objective was connected to a camera (Model FL3-U3-13Y3M-C, Point Gray Pictures) that captured 120-frame/second (fps) with 540 × 450 pixel resolution. The camera was suspended above the larva using a three-dimensional (3-D) micro-positioning stage, which allowed for adjustment of the FOV and focus. All trials were illuminated from an oblique angle above the fish using an LED. A laptop computer was used to run a custom-written MATLAB stimulus generation and video recording program (R2020b, MathWorks). The stimulus signal was sent out from a sound card of the laptop and delivered to an audio amplifier (Model STR-DG510, Sony Corporation) to drive the mini-shaker, producing a controlled vertical vibration.

Calibration of vibration stimuli

The particle motion component was calibrated for frequency and amplitude using a fiber laser Doppler vibrometer (LDV) (Model OFV501, Polytec Inc.). The laser beam was focused on the head region of a fish-shaped holder, which was marked by a piece of adhesive reflecting film. Tone bursts of 50–1,800 Hz with a 50 ms ramp time were created using MATLAB 2020b and sampled at 48 kHz. The root mean square or effective voltage output was acquired by the sound card using a customized MATLAB program for each input amplitude. Quantification and data analysis of VibEM

Two protocols, employing either a pulse stimulus or a step stimulus, were used for inducing VibEM. Each assay included three trials with a 20 s break. In the pulse stimulus VibEM, the protocol consisted of 40 pulses with an interval (50–950 ms) corresponding to 1–10 Hz. Each replicated pulse was a 50 ms tonal stimulus at 1 kHz with an intensity of 3.5 g . In the step stimulus VibEM, a 1 s tone burst with a high frequency (50–1,800 Hz) was given 1 second after the onset of recording.

For quantification of eye movements, a region containing a pigment spot on the outer surface of the eye was selected. To improve the accuracy of quantification of eye movement, pigment spots that had a more central position were used for analysis. Centroids were tracked based on an image morphology processing method with our custom scripts. The amplitude of the waves was defined as half of the peak-to-peak value. For step stimuli, the distance of the centroids of pigment cells with respect to pixel was used for quantifying the eye movement (Δ pixel). Thresholds were determined with a four-parameter biexponential fitting curve. In all experiments, frames that contained obvious movement artifacts or eye movements that did not result in a torsional roll were not included in the analysis. For analysis of the tmc mutants, responses that required higher intensities of 3.5 g were considered as no response.

Experimental design and statistical analysis

For all imaging and behavioral experiments, more than six fish of each genotype were used. For each frequency and intensity, individual fish were tested in three separate trials. Where possible, data points for individual fish are shown. Experiments were repeated on different days, and all images are representative of two independent experiments. All experiments were conducted at developmental stages before sex determination occurred.

The unpaired two-tailed t test in GraphPad Prism 9 was employed to assess the statistical significance between the independent data collected from the WT versus mutant larvae. For threshold comparisons among the tmc mutants, a one-way ANOVA in MATLAB was used as appropriate.

Data and code accessibility

All data and code will be shared by the corresponding author upon request.

Results

Zonal differences in FM styryl dye labeling

The utricle in zebrafish ( Fig. 1 A ) becomes functional as early as 3 dpf ( Mo et al., 2010 ; Bianco et al., 2012 ). The ability to swim and maintain upright posture is quickly acquired during this stage of development. Previous studies have shown that the larval neuroepithelium can be subdivided into a lateral striolar zone, which consists of hair cells with mainly taller bundles, and a medial extrastriolar zone of hair zones with shorter bundles ( Hammond and Whitfield, 2006 ; Inoue et al., 2013 ; Liu et al., 2022 ). In transverse cross sections through the center of the utricle, we also observe taller hair bundles at the lateral edge of the larval utricle at the EM level ( Fig. 1 B ). In addition, images of live hair cells expressing β-actin tagged with GFP indicate that striolar hair bundles are generally larger, although some variability in length and width is seen at the anterior end of this zone ( Fig. 1 C ).

Figure 1.

As shown in the recent calcium imaging study by Tanimoto et al. (2022) , different types of vestibular stimuli preferentially activate different groups of hair cells in zebrafish larvae. In addition, zonal differences in FM 1-43 labeling are detectable in the utricle and saccule in both WT and tmc1/2 mutant hair cells ( Kawashima et al., 2011 ; Herget et al., 2013 ; Smith et al., 2020 ). FM vital dyes permeate mechanotransduction channels in hair cells ( Seiler and Nicolson, 1999 ; Gale et al., 2001 ). To assess differences in labeling in the striola and extrastriola, we labeled utricular hair cells by microinjection of FM 4-64 into the inner ears of Tg(myo6b:β-actin-GFP) larvae ( Fig. 1 D ). We observed that lateral and medial striolar hair cells had higher levels of FM 4-64 labeling of the cell body, whereas labeling was faintly detectable in extrastriolar hair cells ( Fig. 1 D , right panels, 1 E ). The highest levels of dye within the somas were within the lateral edge of the striolar zone, consisting of two to three cells deep where the hair bundle polarity reverses ( Hammond and Whitfield, 2006 ; Inoue et al., 2013 ; Liu et al., 2022 ). These results suggest that clear differences in the permeation of FM exist within subsets of utricular hair cells.

Differential expression of tmc1/2a/2b in the zebrafish utricle

Due to the differential labeling with FM vital dye among the two zones, we next sought to determine whether there was a correlation with the expression of the tmc1/2 subunits, which were previously shown to exhibit functional subtype specificity in the zebrafish inner ear ( Smith et al., 2020 ; Zhu et al., 2021 ). Using RNA-FISH, we observed that tmc1 was predominantly expressed in the extrastriolar region ( Fig. 2 A , left upper panel). Transcripts for tmc2a were broadly expressed across the neuroepithelium with higher signals detected in striolar regions ( Fig. 2 A , right upper panel). The tmc2b paralogue was co-expressed by all cells that were tmc1 -positive but also found in a subset of cells within the striolar region ( Fig. 2 A , lower panels). In total, at least four cell types could be detected ( Fig. 2 B ). Occasionally, tmc1/2b labeling was detected near the outer edge of the neuroepithelium. Due to their position within outer proliferation zones, these cells are likely to be immature.

Figure 2.

We then repeated RNA-FISH using a Tg(myo6b:GFP-CAAX) line that enabled visualization of hair bundles ( Fig. 3 ). We observed that higher levels of tmc2a strongly correlated with the larger hair bundle size in the lateral edge and the variable anterior region of the striolar zone ( Fig. 3 A ). In contrast tmc1 , along with tmc2b , was largely expressed in cells possessing smaller hair bundles located in the medial extrastriolar zone ( Fig. 3 B ). The expression of tmc2b was also observed in a subset of hair cells within the striolar region that overlapped with tmc2a expression ( Fig. 3 C ). A correlation with hair bundle size was not apparent with the tmc2 ohnologues; however, tmc1 was restricted to medial hair cells with smaller bundles. These results predict that striolar zone function is likely to depend more heavily on Tmc2a subunits and perhaps even on Tmc2a/2b combinations.

VibEMs in zebrafish larvae

Previous methods to assess vestibular function in zebrafish larvae have been limited to low-frequency stimuli or have used a mixed spectrum of frequencies ( Mo et al., 2010 ; Migault et al., 2018 ; Gao and Nicolson, 2020 ; Tanimoto et al., 2022 ). We previously reported that vestibular-induced eye movements (VIEM) were reduced in tmc1/2b and tmc2a/tmc2b double mutants rotated at a low frequency of 0.25 Hz ( Smith et al., 2020 ). These results suggest that tmc2a or tmc1 alone cannot compensate for the loss of the two other genes. In contrast, tmc1/2a mutants were comparable to WT siblings, suggesting that tmc2b was sufficient to mediate responses at sub-Hz frequencies. Thus, these results suggested a difference in function among the zebrafish tmc1/2 genes in response to low-frequency stimulation.

In light of the zonal differences in the expression of the tmcs and the role of striola in mediating higher-frequency responses, we sought to test a higher range of frequencies in single and double tmc mutants. Therefore, we developed a simple, noninvasive method to assess eye movements in zebrafish larvae in response to a broad range of frequencies at different intensities. We based our assay on previous reports demonstrating that BCV or air-conducted sound at 500 Hz selectively activate central zone afferents, but not peripheral afferents in the guinea pig utricle ( Curthoys et al., 2017 ). We designed a custom device to deliver vibrational stimuli with a large dynamic range of 50–5,000 Hz to trunk-fixed larvae ( Fig. 4 A ). We calibrated the device using a fiber LDV to assess particle acceleration ( g ) at various driver voltages ( Fig. 2 B ). The larvae were mounted in a horizontal position ( Fig. 4 A ) resulting in stimulation along the vertical axis. Focusing on a centroid on a pigment cell on the outer retinal surface, we observed that vertical vibrational stimuli resulted in a torsional roll of the eyes ( Fig. 4 C , D , Extended Data for Fig. 4 , Movie 1 ). Torsional rolls have been shown to occur from a change in the pitch axis of the head ( Bagnall and Schoppik, 2018 ). This type of eye movement is consistent with the motion of the otolith, which is positioned at a slight tilt with respect to the horizontal axis of the fish, along the anteroposterior axis ( Figs. 1 A , B , 4 D ). Displacements of the otolith along this axis are predicted to excite hair cells that are oriented to respond to nose up and down movements ( Fig. 4 E ) ( Hammond and Whitfield, 2006 ; Liu et al., 2022 ; Tanimoto et al., 2022 ). The backward roll of the eye ( Fig. 4 C , D , Extended Data Movie 1 ) is presumably due to selective inputs to the vestibulo-ocular reflex circuit from striolar hair cells sensitive to nose down motions ( Liu et al., 2022 ).

Figure 4.

Movie 1.

We first determined how fast the reflexive eye movements could be tracked using our system. We imaged larvae exposed to a train of pulses at a frequency of 1.67 Hz with a high intensity of 3.5 g . Each pulse was 50 ms long, during which a tonal stimulus of 1 kHz was given. Spectral analysis of eye movements induced by a 1.67 Hz pulse train at 3.5 g ( Fig. 4 F ) indicated that maximum responses occurred at a similar frequency with some minor components at higher harmonic frequencies ( Fig. 4 G ). We found that we could reliably track eye movements with pulse stimuli up to 10 Hz ( Fig. 4 H ). We then tested the ontogeny of this behavioral reflex using 1.67 Hz pulse trains at different stages of development ( Fig. 4 I ). Consistent with our earlier report on VIEM reflexes ( Mo et al., 2010 ), VibEMs were detectable as early as 3 dpf. We observed that the amplitude of eye movements leveled off at 6–7 dpf; thus, we performed our experiments at this stage of development.

To determine whether hair-cell function is required for VibEMs, we tested two mutants that fully disrupt hair-cell mechanotransduction: tomt tk256c and tmie ru1000 . Both genes are required for the transport and/or localization of the Tmc1/2 subunits to the site of mechanotransduction in fish and mice ( Seiler and Nicolson, 1999 ; Cunningham et al., 2017 , 2020 ; Erickson et al., 2017 ; Pacentine and Nicolson, 2019 ). We found that VibEMs were absent in both mutants ( p = 0.0001; n WT = 10; n tomt tk256c = 9; t = 4.851; df = 17, p = 0.0002; n WT = 12; n tmie ru1000 = 9; t = 4.496; df = 19) ( Fig. 4 J ). We also assessed responses in a weaker allele of tmie, tmie t26171 , which is predicted to result in the removal of the C terminus. The C terminus has been implicated in binding to phospholipids ( Cunningham et al., 2020 ) and is required for targeting Tmie to the hair bundles in zebrafish ( Pacentine and Nicolson, 2019 ). In tmie t26171 mutants, the mean amplitude of eye movements was variable but significantly reduced ( p = 0.0023; n WT = 15; n tmie t26171 = 16; t = 3.342; df = 29) ( Fig. 4 J ). This data indicates that the assay can be used to assess weaker effects on hair-cell function.

Both tomt and tmie are broadly expressed in zebrafish hair cells ( Shen et al., 2008 ; Erickson et al., 2017 ), and the above mutations affect mechanotransduction in all hair cell types. We sought to specifically affect the function of the utricle to address its contribution to the VibEM reflex. We took advantage of a mutation in otogelin , otog c.1522+2T>A , which results in the selective absence of an otolith utricle ( Mo et al., 2010 ; Roberts et al., 2017 ). In contrast, the saccular otolith is still present in otog c.1522+2T>A mutants. We observed that VibEMs were severely decreased in the absence of the utricular otolith ( p = 0.001; n WT = 10; n otog c.1522+2T>A = 8; t = 4.037; df = 16) ( Fig. 4 J ). These results indicate that the torsional eye movements in response to vertical vibrational stimuli are mediated via the utricle.

High-frequency sensitivity of the larval utricle

The experiments using the pulse protocol indicated that eye movements were induced by 1 kHz stimuli. To determine the full range of sensitivity, we developed a step stimulus protocol in which the larvae were exposed to a 1 s stimulus of varying intensity at various frequencies. Figure 5 A illustrates the raw trace of an eye movement in a larva stimulated with 1 kHz (Extended Data for Fig. 5 , Extended Data Movie 2 ). The group data of responses in WT larvae to 100–1,800 Hz are shown in Figure 5 B–G . A summary of the thresholds [movements greater than 1 pixel (1.7 µm)] for frequencies ranging from 50 to 5 kHz indicates that WT larvae are most sensitive to vibration at 300 Hz with a threshold at 0.02 g ( Fig. 5 H ). A similar peak of sensitivity to BCV has been reported in mammals as well ( Curthoys et al., 2017 ). Frequencies above 2 kHz required exceptionally high intensities to elicit a response and represent the upper limit of the range of sensitivity. Overall our data indicate that the sensitivity of the utricle is on par with the reported range of frequency sensitivity for the zebrafish saccule ( Bhandiwad et al., 2013 ).

Contribution of tmc1a/2a/2b to sensitivity

To determine the contribution of the Tmc1/2 subunits to high-frequency sensitivity, we assessed VibEMs in response to vibrational stimuli of 300–1,800 Hz at various intensities in the tmc mutants ( Fig. 6 ). In homozygous mutants carrying a frameshift allele of tmc1 , tmc1 Ex3,+1bp , we observed no differences with WT siblings at any of the frequencies or intensities tested [ Fig. 6 A ; 300 Hz F (1, 14) = 0.39, p = 0.5405; 750 Hz F (1, 14) = 0.01, p = 0.9104; 1,500 Hz F (1, 14) = 0.39, p = 0.5412; 1,800 Hz F (1, 14) = 0.05, p = 0.8286; one-way ANOVA]. In contrast, tmc2a Ex4,−23bp mutants showed decreased responses at the higher frequencies of 1,500 and 1,800 Hz [ Fig. 6 B ; 300 Hz F (1, 13) = 0.04, p = 0.8488; 750 Hz F (1, 12) = 3.3, p = 0.0943; 1,500 Hz F (1, 13) = 7.68, p = 0.0159; 1,800 Hz F (1, 13) = 7.43, p = 0.0173; one-way ANOVA]. Discussion

TMC1 and TMC2 are vital to hair-cell function in fish, mice, and humans, and mounting evidence suggests that the TMCs subserve different roles in hair cells of the inner ear. In the zebrafish utricle, we found that higher levels of tmc2a transcripts are expressed in the striolar regions during larval stages. Striolar zones of otolithic end organs have been shown to be more sensitive to higher-frequency dynamic head movements in mammals; however, the sensitivity of the utricle to high frequencies is not limited to mammals. Despite the absence of the calyx bouton and Type I hair cells in fish, which are thought to be specialized for sensing high-frequency stimuli, central zones in fish show differences in hair-cell morphology ( Popper et al., 1993 ; Inoue et al., 2013 ; Boyle et al., 2018 ; Liu et al., 2022 ). In addition, vestibular afferents in fish display differences in the regularity of resting discharge, including irregular discharge rates ( Moeng and Popper, 1984 ; Boyle and Highstein, 1990 ). In several fish species, previous studies have shown that the utricle and afferents can respond to stimuli up to several hundred Hz or even as high as 1 kHz ( Lu et al., 2004 ; Maruska and Mensinger, 2015 ; Rogers and Sisneros, 2020 ). Moreover, recent evidence indicates that striolar hair cells in zebrafish larvae are predominantly innervated by myelinated afferent fibers that contact fast escape circuits, suggesting that larval striolar regions mainly convey transient or phasic information as seen in other vertebrates ( Liu et al., 2022 ). Finally, a recent calcium imaging study demonstrated that striolar hair cells in the zebrafish utricle are preferentially activated by dynamic stimuli ( Tanimoto et al., 2022 ). Our results are consistent with the above studies, extending the range of sensitivity of the larval utricle up to 1,800 Hz, and demonstrate that vibration-induced ocular responses to high-frequency stimuli are a conserved feature among vertebrates.

Analysis of tmc transcripts at larval stages indicates that the expression pattern of the three tmc genes is complex, yet the pattern largely divides the neuroepithelium into the two main regions of striolar versus extrastriolar hair cells. The most extensively expressed gene in the utricle is tmc2a , with higher levels in striolar hair cells that are recognizable based on their lateral position centered on the line of reversal of hair bundle polarity and expression of a known striolar marker ( Hammond and Whitfield, 2006 ; Inoue et al., 2013 ; Shi et al., 2023 ). In contrast, tmc2b transcripts are abundant in the medial portion of the extrastriolar zone and are also present in a subset of hair cells within the striola. Interestingly, tmc1 is expressed solely in medial extrastriolar hair cells that also express tmc2b . Collectively our data indicate that there are at least several subtypes of hair cells within each zone based on tmc expression.

The spatial distribution of the tmc1/2 genes in the zebrafish utricle suggests that a zonal preference for specific combinations of these genes exists in vestibular hair cells. Previous studies support the notion that the Tmcs are the pore-forming subunits of the mechanotransduction complex ( Pan et al., 2018 ; Jia et al., 2020 ; Jeong et al., 2022 ), and as such, they may confer differences in conductance when combined or expressed singly. Recent evidence suggests that this is the case in the lateral line organ of zebrafish ( Kindig et al., 2023 ). Hair cells expressing tmc2a produce larger microphonic signals, suggesting that larvae are more sensitive to water movements in a particular direction. In the inner ear, presumably evolutionary pressures have led to the expression of certain combinations of the Tmc subunits that are more suitable for various functions. With regard to the detection of linear acceleration in zebrafish presented here, our results suggest that Tmc2a channels are preferable for sensing high-frequency dynamic head motions, whereas channels containing Tmc2b, and to a much lesser extent Tmc1, are better suited for detecting lower-frequency stimuli. Our data suggest that having the two combinations of Tmc2a versus Tmc2a/Tmc2b in striolar hair cells enhances the dynamic range of the system.

Like in other species, the fish utricle is a complex system in which the mechanosensitivity of hair cells may be influenced by several factors including differences in coupling to the otolith, the number of mechanotransduction channels, or differential expression of other components of the mechanotransduction complex. Indeed, we note, along with other previous studies, that there is a general difference in hair bundle size in the two zones of the zebrafish larval utricle and medial hair cells appear to be more closely coupled to the otolith. With regard to our stimulus, the anterior striolar region, which shows the most variability in hair bundle size, with a decrease in bundle size toward the medial border, is best oriented in terms of the excitatory axis of hair bundles to sense nose up and down movements. Indeed, vibrational stimulation in the pitch axis has been shown to preferentially excite anterior striolar hair cells ( Tanimoto et al., 2022 ). Our data suggest that the differential pattern of tmc expression within this region leads to differences in frequency sensitivity in response to dynamic movements in the pitch axis. In single tmc mutants, a reduction in responses to frequencies at the higher range was observed in tmc2a mutants. In contrast, no deficiencies were detected in the tmc1 or tmc2b mutants. The absence of tmc1 in the striolar region presumably makes it dispensable, whereas tmc2a may compensate for the loss of tmc2b . In addition, the analysis of the tmc double mutants revealed several interesting findings. In tmc1/2b mutants, where tmc2a is the only functional gene, no deficits were detected. These results suggest that the Tmc2a subunit alone is capable of mediating a response to the entire range of frequencies. In tmc1/2a double mutants where tmc2b is the remaining functional gene, we observed that low-frequency sensitivity was intact, suggesting that tmc2b positive cells are specialized for lower-frequency stimuli. In contrast, the thresholds in tmc1/2a mutant larvae were significantly raised at higher frequencies, indicating that tmc2b is not sufficient for robust responses to the higher-frequency stimuli used here. Finally, in tmc2a/2b mutants, we observed the most severe reduction in VibEMs. Deficits at all frequencies were observed. Nevertheless, some tmc1 -mediated activity did remain at the lowest frequency tested in these mutants, which was in contrast to the triple tmc1/2a/2b mutants that had no detectable responses at any frequency in our assay. This result suggests that extrastriolar hair cells, which express tmc1 , can make a minor contribution to the detection of dynamic head movements at higher g forces.

In summary, our study suggests that there is a division of labor among the three tmc genes in the zebrafish utricle, which is reflected in their differential expression pattern and functional differences among the tmc mutants. Our data also suggests that high-frequency sensitivity of the striola emerged early during evolution and our findings support the notion that mechanotransduction in hair cells requires specialization at the molecular level for optimal detection of stimuli.
