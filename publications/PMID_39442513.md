Spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) is central to adaptive and innate immune signaling.
It features a regulatory region containing tandem SH2 (tSH2) domains separated
by a helical "hinge" segment keeping SYK inactive by associating with the kinase
domain. SYK activation is triggered when the tSH2 domains bind to a
phosphorylated immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM) found on
receptor tails. Past mutational studies have indicated that ITAM binding
disrupts the hinge-kinase interaction, leading to SYK phosphorylation and
activation. However, the mechanism of this process is unclear, as the ITAM
interaction occurs far from the hinge region. We have determined crystal
structures of three phospho-ITAMs in complex with the tSH2 domains, revealing a
highly conserved binding mechanism. These structures, together with mutational
studies and biophysical analyses, reveal that phospho-ITAM binding restricts SH2
domain movement and causes allosteric changes in the hinge region. These changes
are not compatible with the association of the kinase domain, leading to kinase
activation.

Introduction

Spleen Tyrosine Kinase (SYK), a non-receptor tyrosine kinase, plays a crucial role in signal transduction for activated immunoreceptors in both adaptive and innate immune responses.1 Beyond immunity, SYK is involved in bone development2, platelet activation3, cell adhesion4, and microglial responses to amyloid beta.5,6 Given its central signalling roles in immune pathways, tight regulation of SYK kinase activity within cells is essential. Dysregulated SYK has been implicated in various diseases, including autoimmune disorders, haematological malignancies, Alzheimer’s disease and inflammatory conditions.7 Consequently, SYK has become a focal point for the development of therapeutic agents.8,9

A variety of immune cell receptor complexes, including the Fc receptor (FcR), T cell antigen receptor (TCR), B cell antigen receptor (BCR), and the TREM2 receptor complexes, harbour immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) on their C-terminal cytoplasmic tails. Upon receptor activation, Src family kinases, such as LYN, phosphorylate the tyrosines within the ITAM consensus motif Yxx(L/I)x6-8Yxx(L/I). SYK, and the closely related kinase ZAP-70, feature at their N-termini a pair of SH2 domains linked by a short helical “hinge” region. Phosphorylation of the ITAM sequences triggers SYK/ZAP-70 recruitment to receptor complexes, where each SH2 domain can bind a phosphorylated Yxx(L/I) motif. While the binding of a single SH2 domain to an individual phosphorylated Yxx(L/I) motif is of low affinity (micromolar), the binding affinity to an ITAM with a correctly spaced pair of phosphorylated motifs is remarkably high (nanomolar).10 Structures of the tandem array of SH2 (tSH2) domains of SYK and ZAP-70 have been determined with the dual phospho-ITAMs (pITAMs) of the TCR ε-chain (CD3E) and the TCR ζ-chain (CD247), respectively.11,12 The tSH2 domains binds the pITAMs in a head-to-tail fashion, where the C-terminal SH2 domain (SH2-C) binds the N-terminal Yxx(L/I) motif, while the C-terminal Yxx(L/I) motif binds to a cleft formed by both SH2 domains, which are brought together in the complex.

Binding of the SH2 domains of SYK kinase to receptor tail pITAMs triggers the formation of receptor proximal signalling complexes, which can ultimately lead to activation of the PKC, AKT, and ERK signalling pathways, among others. High-resolution structures of the autoinhibited form of full-length SYK and ZAP-70 shows that the C-terminal kinase domain interacts with the N-terminal tSH2 domains13–15 (see Fig. 1A). In particular, the C-terminal lobe of the kinase domain and part of the interdomain between the kinase and tSH2 domains forms contacts with the hinge region. It is thought that binding of the SH2 domains to a pITAM causes structural rearrangements, that result in the release of the tSH2 from the kinase domain. While autoinhibited SYK exhibits low level of activity, pITAM binding is sufficient to at least partially activate the kinase.14,16–18 Deletion or mutation of the interdomain region within SYK or ZAP-70 that contacts the hinge also causes an increase in kinase activity.13,17,18 Thus, pITAM binding increases SYK kinase activity presumably through triggering the release of the inhibitory tSH2 domains from the kinase domain. In addition to pITAM binding, SYK can also be activated through phosphorylation of tyrosines within the interdomain region.17,18 In activated B cells, SYK is principally phosphorylated within the interdomain region by LYN kinase.19 Thus, SYK appears to be activated through a “switch-like” mechanism (see Fig. 1B), where initial recruitment to phosphorylated receptor tails leads to disengagement of kinase domain from the tSH2 domain and partial activation of SYK. Subsequent phosphorylation of tyrosines within the interdomain region and kinase domain that would typically interact with the hinge, either by LYN or another molecule of SYK in trans, prevents the reassociation of the tSH2 domains. Increased SYK activity leads to further phosphorylation of the activation loop tyrosines (Y518/519) by SYK or other Src kinases20, resulting in a fully active kinase.

The recruitment of SYK to pITAM-bearing cytoplasmic receptor tails serves dual purposes. Firstly, by binding to the pITAM, SYK localizes its activity within an active receptor signalling complex. Secondly, this binding activates SYK, likely by causing the dissociation of the inhibitory tSH2 domains. The tSH2 domains of SYK engage with the kinase domain via the helical hinge region, while the pITAM binds to the opposite surface of the tSH2 domains—more than 30 Å away from the kinase-hinge interaction surface. The precise allosteric mechanism by which pITAM binding triggers hinge-kinase dissociation remains poorly understood.

We have obtained four high-resolution crystal structures of the tSH2 domains of SYK, one in an unbound state (apo) and three when bound to the pITAMs of three different receptors: the Fc receptor γ-chain (FCER1G), the TREM2 adaptor protein (TYROBP/DAP12), and the TCR γ-chain (CD3G). These structures, in conjunction with structure-guided mutagenesis, a time-resolved Förster resonance energy transfer (TR-FRET) assay, small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) and flow-induced dispersion analysis (FIDA) data, provide insights into the activation mechanism. Biophysical measurements confirm that pITAM binding does induce a large conformational change in full-length SYK consistent with displacement of the tSH2 domains from the kinase domain. The binding of pITAMs to SYK not only maintains the proximity of the two SH2 domains but also establishes their orientation, facilitated by specific interactions with the inter-Yxx(L/I) sequence that spans both SH2 domains. By locking the SH2 domains in a specific orientation, they impart a specific conformation on the hinge region, which can twist when the SH2 domains rotate. The fixed conformation of the tSH2 domains is not compatible with binding to the kinase domain, which helps clarify how pITAM binding can lead to the separation of the tSH2 and kinase domains, enabling phosphorylation and activation of SYK.

ResultsApo SYK-tSH2 structureWe solved the crystal structure of the tSH2 domains of SYK without a bound pITAM to a resolution of 1.9 Å (Fig. 2A and S2; Table 1). Within the asymmetric unit, all 3 copies of the protein exhibited engaged SH2 domains, where both the N-terminal and C-terminal SH2 domains (SH2-N and SH2-C) are in close contact, similar to what was observed in a previously reported lower resolution structure (3.2 Å) of the isolated tSH2 domains.21 This conformation closely resembles the pITAM-bound structure and significantly differs from the apo ZAP-70 tSH2 structure22, where the two SH2 domains are rotated away from each other (Fig. S1). Notably, when comparing the apo SYK-tSH2 structures reported here with those reported previously, we observe variability in the positioning of the SH2 domains and the hinge region, indicating that the SYK tSH2 domains in isolation exhibit flexibility (see below). Furthermore, a comparison between the isolated SYK-tSH2 structure and the same region of the full-length SYK structure (Fig. 2B) reveals dramatic rotations of the C-terminal SH2 domain (SH2-C) and the hinge region by approximately 145° and 45°, respectively, underscoring the ability of the tSH2 domains to exist in multiple conformational states suggesting a mobile nature.General description of pITAM-bound SYK-tSH2 structuresCo-crystal structures of the SYK-tSH2 domains bound to phospho-peptides containing the ITAM sequences of FCER1G, TYROBP, and CD3G were solved to 2.2, 2.2, and 2.4 Å, respectively (Fig. 2C; Table 1). Each of these structures contained 6 molecules per asymmetric unit, providing 6 distinct “snapshots” of the pITAM-bound configurations. In all the pITAM-bound structures, the N-terminal Yxx(L/I) motif interacts with the SH2-C, while the C-terminal Yxx(L/I) motif binds to the N-terminal SH2 domain (SH2-N), resulting in an average buried surface area of 1132 Å2 across all ITAM-bound structures. This arrangement holds the SH2 domains in a closed position. Similar to other pITAM-tSH2 domain structures, the phospho-tyrosines are inserted into a positively-charged pocket, while the leucine sidechains fit into an adjacent hydrophobic cavity, resembling a “two-pronged plug” inserting into a “two-holed socket”23, a common SH2 domain binding mechanism. Interestingly, the path of the inter-Yxx(L/I) region spanning the SH2 domains was remarkably similar across these structures (Fig. 2D), suggesting conserved interactions between this region and the SYK-tSH2 domains (see below). Notably, the FCER1G pITAM-bound SYK-tSH2 structure exhibited two conformations of the pITAM (denoted FCER1GA and FCER1GB) within the asymmetric unit. In three of the complexes (FCER1GA), the inter-motif path differed compared to the other structures.pITAM binding restricts movement of SH2 domainsWhen comparing the pITAM-bound tSH2 structures and ITAM-free structures (from this study and Hobbs et al., 2021), a remarkable similarity was observed among the pITAM-bound structures (Fig. 3A). The tSH2 domains within the pITAM-bound structures exhibited an average root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 0.49 ± 0.27 Å (mean ± SD) in a pair-wise alignment. Notably, the tSH2 domains of FCER1GB were less similar to those of FCER1GA and other pITAM-bound structures, presumably due to differences in the nature of pITAM binding (see below). Excluding this conformation from the analysis, the similarity amongst the remaining pITAM-bound SYK structures increased, with an average RMSD of 0.36 ± 0.17 Å. In contrast, the apo tSH2 domains varied much more significantly, showing an average RMSD of 0.99 ± 0.36 Å. When examined in isolation, the SH2 domains showed little variablility across all structures (Fig. S3), with average RMSD values of 0.32 ± 0.15 Å for the SH2-N and 0.28 ± 0.13 Å for the SH2-C. Upon closer examination of the superposed structures, it became evident that the relative position of the SH2 domains in the pITAM-bound structures was significantly more constrained compared to the ITAM-free forms (Fig. 3B). A difference distance matrix (DDM) comparison of the structures, which does not depend on an initial structural alignment, showed tight clustering of pITAM-bound structures (except for FCER1GB), indicating close structural similarity, while ITAM-free structures were considerably more variable (Fig. 3C). In summary, the structural data supports a model in which the pITAMs act as clamps, bringing the SH2 domains together and restricting their relative orientations.To investigate whether the restricted movement of SH2 domains upon pITAM binding also occurs in solution, we conducted small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) experiments on both the unbound (apo) and pITAM-bound tSH2 domains of SYK. Our data exhibited high quality, as indicated by the close agreement of the calculated and actual molecular weights, close agreement between statistics calculated in real- and reciprocal-space (Table S1), and the smooth SAXS curve across various scattering angles (Fig. S4). Notably, upon pITAM binding, there was a slight increase in the calculated molecular weight, which aligns with the expecting mass increase upon formation of the pITAM-tSH2 complex (Table S1). Importantly, pITAM binding induced a decrease in the radius of gyration (Rg), indicating a more compact and globular conformation. Consistent with this observation, the Porod exponent, which reflects the shape and compactness of macromolecules, increased upon pITAM addition, approaching a value characteristic of a hard sphere (a Porod exponent of 4). Finally, the Kratky plot of the SAXS data from pITAM-bound samples passed through the Guinier-Kratky point (√3, 1.124,25) and returned to zero (Fig. 3D and S4B), which is the expected behaviour for globular proteins. In contrast, the data for apo tSH2-SYK passed slightly above this point and didn’t quite return to zero, indicating greater flexibility in the absence of a pITAM. This model rests primarily on two key observations: firstly, the pITAM stimulates kinase activity In vitro14,16–18, and secondly, mutations predicted to cause tSH2 domain dissociation result in kinase activation. 13,17,18 However, it’s important to note that there exists limited biophysical evidence supporting this model. To address this, we purified full length SYK kinase in a largely non-phosphorylated state (Fig. S7A). This preparation of SYK exhibited measurable kinase activity, which could be stimulated by the addition of the FCER1G pITAM peptide (Fig. S7B). Utilizing flow-induced dispersion analysis (FIDA), we calculated the hydrodynamic radius (Rh) of SYK in the absence of pITAM addition (34.2 ± 0.5 Å, SD; Fig. 6A), which closely aligns with the predicted Rh calculated from the crystal structure of the autoinhibited form of SYK, where the tSH2 domains interact with the kinase domain (34.4 Å; Fig. 6B). Intriguingly, addition of the pITAMs from TYROBP, CD3G, and FCER1G significantly increased the Rh of the complex (36.5-37.2 Å) beyond what would be expected based on mass alone and minor conformational changes. When we modelled the movement of the kinase and tSH2 domains to reflect this increase in Rh (Fig. 6C and D), we found that a significant movement of the kinase domain away from the tSH2 domains, such that they were no longer in contact, was necessary (27 Å in the example given). Considering that these two globular regions of SYK make up the majority of the protein (85%), the large increase in Rh strongly suggests that the pITAM triggers a complete separation of the tSH2 domains from the kinase domain.pITAM binding induces allosteric movement of the hinge regionOur SAXS and crystallographic data provide evidence that once bound to an pITAM, the SYK-tSH2 complex becomes interlocked, with the individual SH2 domains adopting a specific orientation relative to each other. When we aligned the SH2-N domains of the unbound and pITAM-bound tSH2 structures, we observed that pITAM binding induced a rotation of the SH2-C domain by 10-11° (Fig. 7A). Simultaneously, across all pITAM-bound structures, we noticed a bending of the backbone at the hinge-SH2 domain boundaries (specifically at residues K116 and M166), resulting in a twisting of the hinge region in an axis roughly perpendicular to the axis of rotation of the SH2 domains (Fig. 7B). Interestingly, in the case of FCER1GB, no rotation of the SH2 domains was observed. However, we did observe a straightening of the first helix in the hinge region, resulting in a similar twisting of the hinge region as seen in other pITAM-bound structures (not shown).Model for SYK kinase activationThe hinge region, which exhibits high sequence conservation between SYK and ZAP-70 (Fig. S8A), mediates the interaction between the tSH2 and kinase domains, through multiple conserved interactions both in the N-terminal kinase lobe and the interdomain region 13–15 (illustrated in Fig. S8B). Our data and previous work have shown that pITAM binding to the tSH2 domains breaks the association between the tSH2 and kinase domains, leading to SYK phosphorylation and activation. An intriguing question arises: could pITAM binding potentially disrupt this interaction by inducing an allosteric movement of the hinge region? When we examine the crystal structures of full-length SYK and ZAP-70, we observe a remarkable alignment over both the kinase and hinge region (RMSD=0.635; Fig. S8C). However, the alignment over the entire protein is relatively poor (RMSD=1.609), primarily due to the very different orientations of the individual SH2 domains. While these differing orientations of the SH2 domains may arise from their flexibility and manner of crystallization, the positioning of the hinge region relative to the kinase domain remains well conserved. To investigate whether it was structurally possible for the pITAM-bound tSH2 domains of SYK to form a complex with the interdomain region and kinase domain, we aligned the pITAM-bound structures to the full-length SYK structure specifically over their hinge regions (RMSD=0.716 ± 0.24; mean ± SD). We found that pITAM-bound tSH2 domains in the various complex structures were not compatible with binding to the kinase domain due to significant clashes between the SH2-N and the C-terminal lobe of the kinase domain (Fig. 7C and S8D). Our proposal (shown in Fig. 7D) is that pITAM binding causes the dissociation of the tSH2 hinge region from the kinase domain by both fixing the orientation of the SH2 domains and inducing twisting of the hinge region in a manner that is not favourable for hinge-kinase domain association. Disruption of this interaction would ultimately lead to phosphorylation and activation of SYK.

pITAM binding causes SYK to undergo large conformational changesThe prevailing model suggests that pITAM engagement leads to the dissociation of the inhibitory tSH2 domains from the kinase domain of either SYK or ZAP-70. This model rests primarily on two key observations: firstly, the pITAM stimulates kinase activity In vitro14,16–18, and secondly, mutations predicted to cause tSH2 domain dissociation result in kinase activation. 13,17,18 However, it’s important to note that there exists limited biophysical evidence supporting this model. To address this, we purified full length SYK kinase in a largely non-phosphorylated state (Fig. S7A). This preparation of SYK exhibited measurable kinase activity, which could be stimulated by the addition of the FCER1G pITAM peptide (Fig. S7B). Intriguingly, addition of the pITAMs from TYROBP, CD3G, and FCER1G significantly increased the Rh of the complex (36.5-37.2 Å) beyond what would be expected based on mass alone and minor conformational changes. When we modelled the movement of the kinase and tSH2 domains to reflect this increase in Rh (Fig. 6C and D), we found that a significant movement of the kinase domain away from the tSH2 domains, such that they were no longer in contact, was necessary (27 Å in the example given). Considering that these two globular regions of SYK make up the majority of the protein (85%), the large increase in Rh strongly suggests that the pITAM triggers a complete separation of the tSH2 domains from the kinase domain.

Model for SYK kinase activationThe hinge region, which exhibits high sequence conservation between SYK and ZAP-70 (Fig. S8A), mediates the interaction between the tSH2 and kinase domains, through multiple conserved interactions both in the N-terminal kinase lobe and the interdomain region 13–15 (illustrated in Fig. S8B). Our data and previous work have shown that pITAM binding to the tSH2 domains breaks the association between the tSH2 and kinase domains, leading to SYK phosphorylation and activation. An intriguing question arises: could pITAM binding potentially disrupt this interaction by inducing an allosteric movement of the hinge region? When we examine the crystal structures of full-length SYK and ZAP-70, we observe a remarkable alignment over both the kinase and hinge region (RMSD=0.635; Fig. S8C). However, the alignment over the entire protein is relatively poor (RMSD=1.609), primarily due to the very different orientations of the individual SH2 domains. While these differing orientations of the SH2 domains may arise from their flexibility and manner of crystallization, the positioning of the hinge region relative to the kinase domain remains well conserved. To investigate whether it was structurally possible for the pITAM-bound tSH2 domains of SYK to form a complex with the interdomain region and kinase domain, we aligned the pITAM-bound structures to the full-length SYK structure specifically over their hinge regions (RMSD=0.716 ± 0.24; mean ± SD). We found that pITAM-bound tSH2 domains in the various complex structures were not compatible with binding to the kinase domain due to significant clashes between the SH2-N and the C-terminal lobe of the kinase domain (Fig. 7C and S8D). Our proposal (shown in Fig. 7D) is that pITAM binding causes the dissociation of the tSH2 hinge region from the kinase domain by both fixing the orientation of the SH2 domains and inducing twisting of the hinge region in a manner that is not favourable for hinge-kinase domain association. Disruption of this interaction would ultimately lead to phosphorylation and activation of SYK.

Model for SYK kinase activation

The hinge region, which exhibits high sequence conservation between SYK and ZAP-70 (Fig. S8A), mediates the interaction between the tSH2 and kinase domains, through multiple conserved interactions both in the N-terminal kinase lobe and the interdomain region 13–15 (illustrated in Fig. S8B). Our data and previous work have shown that pITAM binding to the tSH2 domains breaks the association between the tSH2 and kinase domains, leading to SYK phosphorylation and activation. An intriguing question arises: could pITAM binding potentially disrupt this interaction by inducing an allosteric movement of the hinge region? When we examine the crystal structures of full-length SYK and ZAP-70, we observe a remarkable alignment over both the kinase and hinge region (RMSD=0.635; Fig. S8C). SYK primarily participates in BCR, Fc, and integrin receptor signalling within B cells, mast cells, and myeloid cells.28,29 In contrast, ZAP-70 is specifically involved in TCR signalling and is expressed mainly in T cells 30. Despite their distinct roles in different cell types, both kinases share a common mode of activation. Structurally, they both contain a tandem pair of SH2 (tSH2) domains fused to a C-terminal kinase domain. Normally, the kinases are kept inactive due to inhibitory binding between the tSH2 domains and the kinase domain, principally through the hinge region that connects the two SH2 domains. However, upon recognizing phosphorylated ITAM motifs within the cytoplasmic tails of adaptor proteins of activated receptors, it is thought that the tSH2 domains dissociate from the kinase domain, allowing for kinase phosphorylation and activation. Our biophysical measurements confirm that pITAM binding can indeed induce a large conformational change in SYK, which is consistent with the displacement of the tSH2 domains from the kinase domain.

Although pITAM-bound structures of the tSH2 domains of both SYK and ZAP-70 have been reported11,12, it was less than clear how disruption of the hinge-kinase interaction could be triggered by pITAM binding, which occurs on the opposite face of the tSH2 domain. To address this, we obtained 3 additional pITAM-bound structures of the tSH2 domains of SYK. Together with biophysical analysis and activity assays with site-directed mutant forms of SYK-tSH2, the structures reveal how pITAM binding disrupts the interaction between the tSH2 and kinase domains, leading to kinase activation.

Each high-resolution structure of SYK in complex with a pITAM offered 6 distinct views or “snapshots”, providing a valuable opportunity to reveal both conserved interactions and novel or alternative binding modes. In all pITAM-bound structures, the N-terminal phosphotyrosine (pYN) binds within a pocket of the C-terminal SH2 (SH2-C) domain, where the phosphate forms interactions with 3 arginine sidechains in the αA helix, βB strand and BC loop, with the βB arginine (R195) forming a double hydrogen bond. Both the αA and BC loop arginines are crucial for pITAM binding (we were unable to test the βB arginine mutation). This pattern of interaction is commonly seen in phosphotyrosine recognition by SH2 domains and is deemed canonical.31

Unique to both SYK and ZAP-70, the C-terminal phosphotyrosine (pYC) binds within a pocket formed by both the SH2-N and SH2-C domains. As was seen previously in pITAM-bound SYK and ZAP-70 structures11,12, the pYC is coordinated by both the αA and βB arginines within the SH2-N domain of SYK, which are important for pITAM binding. In most SYK pITAM-bound structures, a lysine residue (K232) from the βF loop of the SH2-C also forms a hydrogen bond with the pYC phosphate - an interaction not previously observed in the SYK-pITAM(CD3E) structure11, possibly due to its lower resolution. ZAP-70 has a threonine in the equivalent position, which is not involved in pITAM recognition. Instead, two residues in the αB helix (Y240 and K244) can form hydrogen bonds with the pYC phosphate. 12 Although the residues are not conserved, in both SYK and ZAP-70, the binding of the pITAM can help bring the SH2 domains together through hydrogen bonding by both SH2 domains to the same phospho-tyrosine. The mutation of K232 in SYK only has a modest effect on pITAM binding, suggesting its limited contribution to affinity. An alternative conformation of the FCER1G pITAM was observed, in which the K232 no longer interacts with the pYC. Instead, R45 from the BC loop of the SH2-N domain is positioned to form a hydrogen bond with pYC, an interaction not previously observed. Interestingly, mutation of R45 causes a reduction in pITAM affinity for all pITAMs tested, suggesting that R45 may more universally interact with the pYC and contribute to pITAM binding. In support of this, thermodynamic binding studies involving SYK and the CD3E pITAM suggest two modes of pITAM interaction exist.32 ZAP-70 cannot form this interaction, since it lacks an equivalent arginine in the SH2-N domain.

The pITAM’s path across the surface that span both SH2 domains is strikingly similar, implying a preserved interaction with the sequence between the Yxx(L/I) motifs and SYK. Indeed, all pITAMs we employed in our structural investigations contain an acidic residue two residues before the pYC, forming salt-bridges with either K247 or K232. In a similar manner, ZAP-70 forms a salt-bridge with a glutamate in the pITAM of CD247 at the same position via K244 (corresponding to K247 in SYK). This interaction may not be universal, since not all ITAM sequences contain an acidic residue at this position (e.g., the Ig-α and -β chains). Additional SYK interactions were noted in the middle of the inter-motif region, invariably involving D249. Mutation of either K247 or D249 result in a general decrease in pITAM binding, underscoring the importance of these interactions. In summary, the inter-motif sequence seems to do more than just act as a molecular spacer or “ruler” between Yxx(L/I) motifs27, but actively aids in SYK binding.

Crystal structures of ITAM-free tSH2 domains of SYK (this work and Hobbs et al.21) display a “closed” conformation, where the SH2 domains are engaged. This contrasts with the ITAM-free ZAP-70 structure, which adopts a more “open” state, where the SH2 domains are positioned further apart, rendering them incompatible with pITAM binding. 22 These differences suggest that the tSH2 domains of SYK undergo only minimal changes upon pITAM binding and are already predisposed to an active state in the absence of a pITAM. 21,33 However, our SAXS data provide additional insights, showing that in the absence of a pITAM, the SYK tSH2 domains do exhibit a degree of flexibility. It is only when a pITAM binds that the tSH2 domains adopt a compact, globular form.

Our structures demonstrate that pITAM binding not only limits the movements of the SH2 domains but also crucially determines their relative orientation. A comparison of structures with and without bound pITAM reveals that rotation of the SH2 domains results in a twisting motion of the hinge region. When a pITAM binds, the orientation of the SH2 domains become fixed, facilitated by specific contacts of the inter-motif region that spans the SH2 domains. As the SH2 domains rotate into their fixed positions, torsional stress would cause the hinge to twist, with bending occurring at loop regions bordering the hinge. This process is analogous to the formation of twists or supercoils in a cord when its ends are rotated. Consequently, pITAM binding can induce a conformational change in the hinge region through an allosteric effect. The primary interactions between the kinase and tSH2 domains, in both SYK and ZAP-70, occur through the hinge region, which is highly conserved between these proteins. By restricting SH2 domain movement and inducing twisting of the hinge, pITAM binding generates a conformation of the tSH2 domains that cannot bind to the kinase domain, as structural modelling predicts that serious steric clashes would occur between the SH2-N and the C-terminal lobe of the kinase if hinge binding were to occur. This results in the release of the kinase domain from the tSH2 domains. Testing this model using molecular dynamic simulations could provide further insights into the dynamic behaviour and conformational changes of the SH2 domains and hinge region upon pITAM binding.

In conclusion, our structural studies of pITAM-bound complexes have shed light on the complex mechanism that underlies the activation of SYK kinase. The binding of a pITAM not only restrains SH2 domain movement but also establishes their relative orientation. By provoking a twisting motion of the hinge region, pITAMs prevent the tSH2 domains from associating with the kinase domain, paving the way for the phosphorylation and activation of SYK (Fig. 7D). This mechanism likely extends to ZAP-70 as well, providing valuable mechanistic insights into the regulation of immune signalling. A deeper understanding of SYK kinase’s molecular activation could help lead to the development of novel, precise therapeutic agents aimed at modulating SYK activity in conditions such as autoimmune diseases, blood cancers, and Alzheimer’s disease.

FIDAThe hydrodynamic radii (Rh) of the full-length SYK protein were determined by flow induced dispersion analysis (FIDA) using a Fida 1 instrument (Fida Biosystems), employing light-emitting-diode induced fluorescence detection with an excitation wavelength of 275 nm and emission wavelengths greater than 300 nm. Standard silica capillaries (Fida Biosystems) with a 75 μm inner diameter, 375 μm outer diameter, 100 cm total length, 84 cm length to detection window, were used for all experiments. Prior to use, the capillaries were coated with high-sensitivity (HS) coating reagent (Fida Biosystems). All FIDA experiments were conducted as follows. Firstly, assay buffer (10 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM TCEP) was used for flushing and equilibrating the HS-coated capillary at 3500 mbar for 60 s. The analyte sample (assay buffer or 21 μM pITAM) was then filled into the capillary at 3500 mbar for 40 s, followed by injection of the indicator (3.5 μM SYK protein alone or premixed with 21 μM pITAM) at 50 mbar for 10 s. Lastly, the indicator was mobilized to the fluorescence detector with analyte sample (assay buffer or 21 μM pITAM) at 400 mbar for 360 s. During the final step, the equilibrium dispersion profile (Taylorgram) was recorded. Taylorgrams were analysed using FIDA software (v2.37, Fida Biosystems) to determine the diffusion coefficient and apparent Rh. Since moderate tailing was observed in the Taylorgrams, the fitting was set to minimal (i.e., 50% Taylorgram fraction) for all data points. The capillary, samples, and buffer vials were kept in a temperature-controlled environment at 25°C inside the instrument. The viscosity of the assay buffer at 25°C was measured using a Lovis 2000 ME microviscometer (Anton Paar). Viscosity compensation was included in the data analyses to correct for any changes in viscosity caused by the addition of the pITAM. All samples were analysed in triplicate. The estimated Rh values of SYK based on structures (actual or modelled) were determined using the HullRad web server.47

Kinase assayThe SYK kinase activity was evaluated using a continuous kinase assay with reagents provided by AssayQuant, following the supplied instructions. In summary, this assay is based on the detection of phosphorylation of a PhosphoSens peptide, containing a SOX fluorophore. Upon phosphorylation, a Mg2+ ion coordinates the phosphate and SOX moiety, resulting in increased fluorescence. The assay was conducted in 20 μl volumes within 384-well black non-binding plates. The full-length SYK kinase, at a final concentration of 4 nM, was diluted in an assay buffer containing 54 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 1 mM ATP, 1.2 mM DTT, 0.55 mM EGTA, 0.012% Brij-35, 10 mM MgCl2, 1% glycerol, and 200 μg/ml BSA. Additionally, 10 μM PhosphoSens peptide (AQT0794) was included either in the presence or absence of 10 μM FCER1G pITAM. The fluorescence was monitored every 30 seconds for 1 hour using a PheraStar FX instrument in kinetic mode, employing an appropriate optics module (FI 350 460). The data was represented as a plot of relative fluorescence over time, deducting the fluorescence of control reactions (which lacked kinase) from the fluorescence recorded in the presence of the kinase.
