Nucleotide excision repair (NER) removes bulky adducts from genomic DNA and
prevents the ultraviolet light-sensitivity disease xeroderma pigmentosum, cancer
and premature ageing1. After initial lesion recognition by XPC in global genome
repair or by stalled RNA polymerases in transcription-coupled repair, a lesion
and surrounding DNA duplex are unwound by TFIIH, which includes the ATPases XPB
and XPD, and additional NER factors XPA, XPF, XPG and RPA, to form a DNA bubble2
comprising around 27 nucleotides. The double strand-single strand (ds-ss)
junction-specific endonucleases XPF and XPG cleave DNA on the 5' and 3' sides of
the lesion, respectively. Here we report the functional steps and atomic
structures of the ATPase-driven and lesion-dependent DNA bubble formation and
arrangement of the complete NER factors for dual incision. The unwinding of
nearly 30 base pairs of DNA depends mainly on the double strand DNA translocase
XPB and the duplex dividers XPA and XPF. XPD binds the lesion strand with XPF at
the 5' ds-ss junction. XPF cuts the lesion strand only after XPG binds the 3'
ds-ss junction. The ERCC1 subunit of XPF facilitates DNA strand separation and
recruitment of RPA to the non-lesion strand. These findings provide insights on
the causes of human diseases and potential targets for enhancing
chemotherapeutic efficacy.

IntroductionDNA base-dimers induced by ultraviolet light (UV) or cisplatin, and bulky lesions caused by various aromatic adducts impede replication and transcription 3,4. These lesions need to be removed promptly by NER. Defective NER is manifested as XP, an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by extreme sensitivity to UV light, skin lesions and cancers. Mutations in genes encoding NER factors are also implicated in Cockayne syndrome (CS) and trichothiodystrophy (TTD), which are associated with impaired development, mental retardation and premature death 5,6. After initial lesion recognition, GGR and TCR both depend on XPA, RPA 7 and the seven-subunit core complex of TFIIH (Core7) 8,9 to unwind 22–29 base pairs of DNA and verify the presence of a bulky DNA lesion 10. XPF and XPG then make dual incisions near the borders of the DNA bubble.The core NER process has been dissected genetically and biochemically 11,12. Human GGR has been reconstituted in a cell-free system with purified NER factors 13–15. To date, however, only lesion recognition by XPC and recruitment of Core7 and XPA to the lesion-containing DNA site have been structurally characterized 16,17. After flipping out damaged base(s), XPC binds the non-lesion strand and DNA duplex downstream of the lesion 18. Core7 is recruited by XPC with XPB binding the dsDNA upstream of the lesion 17. Binding of XPA to the Core7, XPC and DNA complex (C7CD) induces extensive conformational changes in Core7 that shifts the XPC-lesion DNA a helical turn away from XPB and bends dsDNA between XPB and XPD in the C7CAD complex (for Core7, XPC, XPA and DNA) 17. In contrast, without XPC and a lesion, XPA binds the ds-ss junction of a Y-shaped DNA between XPB and XPD, which binds the ssDNA 3ʹ to the duplex (Extended Data or ED Fig. 1a-d) 16. Extensive mapping of the protein-interactome among NER factors and AlphaFold predictions of the full NER complex structure 19 have established a framework for understanding NER. The atomic details of XPF, XPG and RPA assembling on a bulky DNA lesion, as well as the dynamic process of DNA bubble formation leading to dual incision, have yet to be determined.By combining mutagenesis with in vitro and in-cell GGR assays and cryoEM analyses, we have determined the pathway and intermediate structures involved in lesion-DNA bubble formation and strand cleavage during NER. The unequal dependence of NER on the XPB and XPD ATPase activity may be rationalized by their different roles of translocating and unwinding ~20 bp DNA (XPB) versus binding ssDNA and detecting bulky lesions (XPD).Waddling mechanism of DNA translocationMany ATPases are known to translocate along DNA duplexes, but their translocation mechanism is not as well understood as that of ssDNA translocases. Among the different NER complexes we visualized, two RecA-like helicase domains (HD1 and HD2) of XPB are most often in the open (nucleotide-free) state, but they are closed in No-G and DuIS complexes and superimposable with the ADP-bound structure in transcription initiation 48. Yet no ADP or ATP was observed in the cryoEM structure of No-G and DuIS. It is possible that the nucleotide dissociated during cryoEM grid preparation. Nevertheless, the conformational changes of XPB and the DNA duplex can be summarized in a two-step model for translocation of 1 bp per ATP hydrolyzed (Video 7) (Fig. 5c-d). Upon ATP binding and closing of the HD1 and HD2 helicase domains, the non-lesion strand is translocated by 1 nt while maintaining base pairing, resulting in tilting of the DNA base pairs. After ATP hydrolysis, release of ADP and Pi drives translocation of the lesion strand by 1 nt, restoring B-form DNA with an overall translocation of 1 bp. In summary, two key phases of each ATPase cycle — ATP binding and release of ADP and Pi— result in two power strokes, with each strand of the DNA duplex being translated by 1 nt during each power stroke, while all base pairs remain intact. Similar dsDNA translocation is also observed in the nucleosome remodeler Swi/Snf ATPases 49. We refer to this DNA duplex translocation as the “waddling mechanism” due to its resemblance to the way penguins walk.

To date, however, only lesion recognition by XPC and recruitment of Core7 and XPA to the lesion-containing DNA site have been structurally characterized 16,17. Binding of XPA to the Core7, XPC and DNA complex (C7CD) induces extensive conformational changes in Core7 that shifts the XPC-lesion DNA a helical turn away from XPB and bends dsDNA between XPB and XPD in the C7CAD complex (for Core7, XPC, XPA and DNA) 17. In contrast, without XPC and a lesion, XPA binds the ds-ss junction of a Y-shaped DNA between XPB and XPD, which binds the ssDNA 3ʹ to the duplex (Extended Data or ED Fig. 1a-d) 16. Extensive mapping of the protein-interactome among NER factors and AlphaFold predictions of the full NER complex structure 19 have established a framework for understanding NER. The atomic details of XPF, XPG and RPA assembling on a bulky DNA lesion, as well as the dynamic process of DNA bubble formation leading to dual incision, have yet to be determined.

By combining mutagenesis with in vitro and in-cell GGR assays and cryoEM analyses, we have determined the pathway and intermediate structures involved in lesion-DNA bubble formation and strand cleavage during NER. We have elucidated the sequential roles of XPF and XPG in DNA strand separation and displacement of XPC and visualized the dynamic movements of two ATPases (XPB and XPD) and two endonucleases (XPF and XPG), which are orchestrated by XPA and ERCC1.

Reconstitution of global genome repairWe successfully reconstituted GGR in vitro using purified NER factors (Fig. 1a-c). A 94 bp DNA containing an internal Cy5, which mimic a bulky adduct was subjected to dual incision and produced lesion-containing oligonucleotides. In this reconstituted GGR reaction, 80% of substrate was converted to product within 60 min, and the main products were 27–28 nt long. When XPF was omitted, XPG cleaved the lesion strand ~5 nt downstream of Cy5, resulting in Cy5 oligonucleotides of ~70 nt (Fig. 1c). When XPG was omitted, however, XPF had no detectible nuclease activity (Fig. 1c). In the presence of a nuclease-inactive XPG, XPF cleaved ~20-nt upstream of Cy5 and produced Cy5 fragments of ~50 nt. XPG cleaves the lesion strand independent of XPF binding and plays an architectural role in activating XPF for dual incision, as reported previously 20,21. These XPF and XPG cleavage sites are consistent with the NER dual incision sites using genuine UV and bulky adduct lesions 22–24. In all cases the lesion is much closer to the 3ʹ than the 5ʹ end of excised DNA (Fig. 1c).

In addition, preceding the ZnF domain, XPA has gained a newly formed strand (aa 88–101), which fits in a groove in OB-C domain of RPA70 in a non-intersecting path to the ssDNA-binding groove, and the XPA-RPA interaction is thus solidified (Fig. 4c, 4e). The ssDNA-binding OB-AB domains and TriCDE have no direct interaction and move independently, and each undergoes slight adjustments in domain-domain and protein-DNA interactions compared to the previously reported crystal structures of RPA 26,38 (ED Fig. 6). The RPA trimer and its position relative to XPA and the ssDNA also deviate significantly from the AlphaFold prediction 19 (ED Fig. 7d-e). The non-lesion strand is kinked by 90° between OB-AB and TriCDE with RPA outside of the DNA bubble. The relative orientation of TriCDE and OB-AB in DuIM differs from the structures of RPA alone in complex with ssDNA by a 150° rotation (Video 5, ED Fig. 7a-c) 36,37. The 15-nt of unpaired non-lesion strand are bound in the ssDNA-binding groove of OB-ABC, but the DNA trajectories in the DuI complexes deviate from crystal structures by 5–7 Å due to the presence of NER factors. In DuIM ERCC1NLD is adjacent to the unpaired non-lesion strand and directly interacts with each long β hairpin loop of the OB-ABC domains (Fig. 4f, ED Fig. 6f).

Accompanying the XPF-ERCC1 movement, the horseshoe-shaped XPF opens by 8–10° at its two ends (helical and nuclease domains) in DuIM (Video 6). At the helical end, HhH domains of XPF and ERCC1 become disordered as they move away from the XPF catalytic center and no longer block its access by DNA substrate (ED Fig. 4j-k). The XPF structures in DuI complexes are like the apo XPF-ERCC1 33. In DuIM the tip of the helical domain of XPF is in contact with the first 4 nt of the unpaired lesion strand, but the 5ʹ ds-ss junction and lesion strand remain 40 Å away from the XPF active site. As the DNA bubble is 18 nt in size, additional unwinding of DNA to reach 25–30 nt and further protein conformational changes are needed for XPF to access the lesion strand. Based on its location in the DuI complexes, XPF cannot access the upstream DNA duplex and likely cleaves the lesion strand in an unpaired form as the DNA footprinting data indicated 43 rather than a duplex as modeled 19. The product size of dual incision is likely determined by the need for the DNA bubble becoming large enough to allow the lesion strand to access XPF.

XPA and ERCC1 organize the dual incisionAssembly of the seventeen-subunit DuI complex and the sequential conformational changes are coordinated by two small protein subunits, XPA and ERCC1. XPA consists of multiple dispersed structural elements, which interact with the DNA ds-ss junction, XPB, XPC, XPD, XPF and RPA (Fig. 4b). ERCC1, a subunit of XPF, links the two unpaired DNA strands and organizes the ssDNA binding by OB domains of RPA. The H244R mutation in XPA, which occurs at the site that anchors XPA to Core7 (p8 and p52) 17, has been shown to cause defects in XPA recruitment and NER 44. The XPA-ERCC1 interface has been shown to be essential for XPF recruitment 30,31,45. To investigate the importance of previously unexplored molecular interactions mediated by XPA and ERCC1, fifteen variants of XPA and ERCC were designed and stably expressed at comparable levels in the corresponding knockout RPE1 cells (Fig. 4, ED Table 2). NER efficiency was assessed using unscheduled DNA synthesis (mainly reflecting GGR) (Fig. 4h-i) and transcription recovery (mainly reflecting TCR) following UV exposure (ED Fig. 8).Each of five ERCC1NLD variants (#1-#5), designed to perturb the individual interface with XPD (#1), OB-ABC of RPA70 (#2–4), and RPA32WH (#5) (Fig. 4f), support NER at near wildtype levels (Fig. 4h, ED Fig. 8a). We hypothesize that after mediating XPF recruitment by XPA27,30,31, ERCC1NLD acts as a spacer to separate the lesion from the non-lesion strand, and its individual interface with OB-ABC, RPA32WH or XPD accounts for only a fraction of this function. Indeed, when the five WT-like mutants are combined, the resulting ERCC1-all5 behaves as ERCC1 null in NER assays (Fig. 4h), supporting the essential role of ERCC1 in the dual incision. In parallel, four XPA variants, which delete the DNA separation pin (replaced by a Gly or three Glu, #2 and #2*, Fig. 4g) or perturb the TriCDE interface (#3, #4 and combined 3+4, Fig. 4e), exhibit clear NER defects (Fig. 4h, ED Fig. 8a). Unlike the deletion mutants (#2 and #2*), amino acid-substitutions in the DNA separation pin (#1) has no deleterious effect (Fig. 4g-h). Likewise, amino acid substitutions of the XPA residues at the XPD interface (#6) do not disrupt NER because the interface is formed by hydrogen bonds between mainchain atoms (Fig. 4a, insert). The WT-like NER activities of XPA mutants #5 and #7 at the RPA32WH and XPD interface (Fig. 4d, 4h), respectively, may be due to the limited and mild Ala substitutions, as extended and more drastic alterations of the same RPA32WH and XPD interface cause NER deficiency 40. Among the NER-deficient XPA mutants, only deletion of the DNA separation pin (mutations #2 and #2*) reduces recruitment of XPA and downstream ERCC1 to sites of local UV damage (Fig. 4i, ED Fig. 8b). The XPA–RPA (TriCDE) interactions (XPA#3 and #4 encompassing aa 91–113 of XPA) appears dispensable for XPF/ERCC1 recruitment (No-G or DuIS), but essential for dual incision.

Waddling mechanism of DNA translocationMany ATPases are known to translocate along DNA duplexes, but their translocation mechanism is not as well understood as that of ssDNA translocases. Among the different NER complexes we visualized, two RecA-like helicase domains (HD1 and HD2) of XPB are most often in the open (nucleotide-free) state, but they are closed in No-G and DuIS complexes and superimposable with the ADP-bound structure in transcription initiation 48. Yet no ADP or ATP was observed in the cryoEM structure of No-G and DuIS. It is possible that the nucleotide dissociated during cryoEM grid preparation. Nevertheless, the conformational changes of XPB and the DNA duplex can be summarized in a two-step model for translocation of 1 bp per ATP hydrolyzed (Video 7) (Fig. 5c-d). Upon ATP binding and closing of the HD1 and HD2 helicase domains, the non-lesion strand is translocated by 1 nt while maintaining base pairing, resulting in tilting of the DNA base pairs. After ATP hydrolysis, release of ADP and Pi drives translocation of the lesion strand by 1 nt, restoring B-form DNA with an overall translocation of 1 bp. In summary, two key phases of each ATPase cycle — ATP binding and release of ADP and Pi— result in two power strokes, with each strand of the DNA duplex being translated by 1 nt during each power stroke, while all base pairs remain intact. Similar dsDNA translocation is also observed in the nucleosome remodeler Swi/Snf ATPases 49. We refer to this DNA duplex translocation as the “waddling mechanism” due to its resemblance to the way penguins walk.

ConclusionThis study, which includes all the protein factors necessary for dual incision, defines the structural basis of nucleotide excision repair (NER). While mutations in the active sites of helicases or nucleases leading to xeroderma pigmentosum are easily understood, many other mutations occur at the protein-protein and protein-DNA interfaces. The dynamic movements revealed in this study shed light on both interfacial mutations and internal residues that are essential for the integrity of the step-by-step NER assemblies. Furthermore, the dramatic and continuous movement of XPD during the NER process addresses the long-standing question of why adjacent mutations or different amino acid substitutions of a given residue in XPD can lead to Xeroderma Pigmentosum, Cockayne syndrome, or trichothiodystrophy 5,6. This variation arises because the environment surrounding an individual mutation changes dynamically, which allows nearby mutations or different substitutions at the same site to affect NER assembly to varying extents. Finally, since NER also removes DNA damage caused by chemical agents and reduces the efficacy of chemotherapies, the detailed dual-incision structures reveal specific target sites for modulating NER activity and enhancing the killing of cancer cells.

Structure determination and refinement

Beam-induced movement in each movie was corrected using Motion Cor2 55 with dose-weighting applied, and the pixels were binned 2X to 0.83 Å for 105K dataset and 1.03 Å for 81K dataset to generate a set of dose-weighted and another of dose-unweighted micrographs. Gctf 56 was used to estimate CTF (contrast transfer function) parameters on dose-unweighted micrographs. For all data, particles were picked using Gautomatch (www2.mrc-lmb.cam.ac.uk/research/locally-developed-software/zhang-software/) with or without template, and extracted from dose-weighted micrographs, separately. To speed up the data processing, all particles were downscaled to a pixel size of 3.32 Å and a box size of 110X110. 2D classification was carried out in RELION (ver. 3.1) a couple of rounds to remove junk particles. Good 2D classes were selected to generate the 3D initial model using RELION (ver. 3.1) 57. Particles belonging to each type of NER complex were selected and re-extracted for auto-refinement. Non-duplicated particles from templated and non-templated picking processes were selected, combined and auto refined. To improve the conformational purity, several rounds of focused 3D classification without alignment within separate local masks over XPD/XPF, XPC/XPB/XPD, XPC or XPB were applied.

For the No-G complex, selected subsets of particles were re-extracted with a pixel size of 1.66 Å and a box size of 220X220, and subjected to CTF refinement, Bayesian polishing and auto-refinement in RELION (ver. 3.1). The final reconstruction of XPC_DNA and Core region in No-G reached 7.7 Å and 4.3 Å, respectively. The composite map of No-G was generated by aligning and merging the focused maps using UCSF Chimera (ver. 1.14) 58 and postprocessed to 4.3 Å resolution. For DuI complexes, selected subset of particles containing DuIS or DuIM were merged separately and subjected to CTF refinement, Bayesian polishing and auto-refinement in RELION (ver. 3.1). The cryoSPARC 3D variability analysis (v4.6) showed continuous heterogeneity of final DuIS and DuIM particles. The reconstructed map of DuIS was of 3.3 Å and DuIM 3.4 Å resolution (ED Fig. 4). After improvement using focused refinement, the final composite map was assembled. DeepEMhancer was used to improve map quality for model building. For C7CAD·ATP and No-F datasets, data processing was performed by the same procedure. The No-F sample is intrinsically unstable, and the final reconstructed map is at 5.7 Å resolution.
