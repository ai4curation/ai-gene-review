At least nine closely related isoforms of adenylyl cyclases (ACs), the enzymes
responsible for the synthesis of cyclic AMP (cAMP) from ATP, have been cloned
and characterized in mammals. Depending on the properties and the relative
levels of the isoforms expressed in a tissue or a cell type at a specific time,
extracellular signals received through the G-protein-coupled receptors can be
differentially integrated. The present review deals with various aspects of such
regulations, emphasizing the role of calcium/calmodulin in activating AC1 and
AC8 in the central nervous system, the potential inhibitory effect of calcium on
AC5 and AC6, and the changes in the expression pattern of the isoforms during
development. A particular emphasis is given to the role of cAMP during drug and
ethanol dependency and to some experimental limitations (pitfalls in the
interpretation of cellular transfection, scarcity of the invalidation models,
existence of complex macromolecular structures, etc).
