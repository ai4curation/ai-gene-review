Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a highly adaptive Gram-negative pathogen that is
commonly managed with fluoroquinolone (FQ) antibiotics. Even without heritable
resistance mutations, FQ-susceptible cells of P. aeruginosa can survive
treatment in a phenomenon termed antibiotic persistence. Current models of
stationary-phase FQ persistence established with other bacterial species posit
that persister survival is dependent on DNA double-stranded break (DSB) repair
via RecA-mediated homologous recombination (HR). In P. aeruginosa, however, the
effects of RecA loss on FQ persistence vary depending on the culture conditions.
In this current work, we find that stationary-phase P. aeruginosa does not
depend on either of its canonical DSB repair pathways-HR or non-homologous end
joining (NHEJ)-for FQ persistence. By creating cross-pathway deletion strains,
we found that RecA and Ku (but not LigD) are conditionally essential, which
suggests that P. aeruginosa can conduct LigD-independent NHEJ. Using a
chromosomally integrated DSB reporter assay, we further show that P. aeruginosa
can repair DSBs using two non-canonical pathways: LigD-independent NHEJ and
microhomology-mediated repair. Together, these findings demonstrate that P.
aeruginosa does not adhere to the conventional requirement for DSB repair after
FQ treatment and can engage in alternative DSB repair pathways than those
previously described.

Introduction

Unlike the other species, P. aeruginosa is not limited to RecA-mediated homologous recombination (HR) and can repair DSBs through non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) [14–16].

Together, these findings provide new perspective on P. aeruginosa’s DSB repair abilities and their infrequent utilization when it comes to surviving FQ treatment.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa repairs DSBs by MHMRAlthough we originally intended to only screen for NHEJ repair, the DSB repair assay also highlighted an interesting group of samples that appeared to undergo MHMR. MHMR is a pathway-agnostic term used to describe microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ)-like DSB repair outcomes—in which microhomologies of a few nucleotides (fewer than 16 nt) on either end of a DSB synapse are used to facilitate repair—without implicating specific enzymes or molecular mechanisms [26, 27]. We observed MHMR in seven of the 147 DSB reporter colonies that exhibited repair (Fig. 3A). We categorized repair as MHMR if there were at least three nucleotides of homologous overlap in the upstream and downstream sequences; the observed microhomologies ranged from three to eight nucleotides (Fig. 4C and D).We sought to confirm that these repair events are not attributable to non-specific I-SceI digestion and direct religation of sticky ends, as was the case for the −20 nt alternative cut site repair outcome. For each MHMR repair outcome, we placed the microhomology sequences and its surrounding 18 bases into a pHERD30T empty vector (e.g. 3 nt microhomology + 9 nt upstream + 9 nt downstream = 21 nt plasmid insert) (Supplementary Fig. S8A and B). Because the microhomology occurs upstream and downstream of the I-SceI cuts, two plasmids were made for each outcome: one “up” and one “down.” We found that I-SceI could not digest any of the constructed plasmids, strongly suggesting that these repair events were microhomology-mediated and not due to non-specific I-SceI digestion and religation (Supplementary Fig. S8C).Consistent with the eukaryotic literature on MMEJ versus NHEJ, we found MHMR to be Ku- and LigD-independent in prokaryotic P. aeruginosa [26] (Fig. 3). Eukaryotic MMEJ is also considered independent of the RecA homolog, Rad51. Although relative transformation efficiencies between ΔrecA and recA-containing strains were comparable, we never observed MHMR in DSB repair assays conducted with PAO1 ΔrecA or PA14 ΔrecA (308 and 366 screened colonies, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. S5, Extended Data Table 1) [26]. Thus, we cannot rule out a role for RecA in Pseudomonal MHMR.

Canonical DSB repair is not essential for stationary-phase LVX persistence

To determine whether stationary-phase cells rely on NHEJ or RecA-mediated HR to repair damaged DNA and survive LVX treatment (Fig. 1A), we created P. aeruginosa PAO1 and PA14 strains that lack the open reading frames of ku, ligD, or recA [8]. Because HR requires a homologous DNA template for repair, we measured chromosomal content of stationary-phase P. aeruginosa PAO1 by PicoGreen DNA staining and found that roughly 30% of cells have more than one chromosomal copy; therefore, many cells have the extra chromosomal content needed to feasibly conduct HR—albeit significantly fewer than stationary-phase E. coli cultures (Fig. 1B). We previously demonstrated that, under our culture conditions, P. aeruginosa exhausts succinate, the sole carbon source in the culture media, and has been in stationary phase for at least 6 h at the time of LVX treatment [8]. Here we found that, consistent with a recent report, single deletion mutants and ΔkuΔligD NHEJ-null mutants survived just as well as their wild-type counterparts after 24 h LVX treatment (Fig. 1C) [9]. Similarly, we demonstrate that stationary-phase P. aeruginosa missing ku, ligD, or both genes in tandem show no change in their survival after UV irradiation compared to the wild-type, suggesting that NHEJ is not critical for surviving either FQ- or UV-induced DNA damage (Supplementary Fig. S1).

LigD is not essential for P. aeruginosa NHEJ

In our efforts to create cross-pathway deletion mutants lacking both RecA and Ku/LigD, we found that we were able to obtain PAO1 and PA14 ΔligDΔrecA mutants, but not ΔkuΔrecA (Supplementary Table S5). When ΔligDΔrecA mutants were treated with LVX, we found only a small decrease in survival for PAO1 and no effect for PA14 (Fig. 1C). The viability of the ΔligDΔrecA double mutant—and our inability to delete ku and recA together—suggested that LigD might not be necessary for P. aeruginosa NHEJ.

To directly interrogate the essentiality of Ku and LigD for DSB repair via NHEJ, we adapted a DSB repair assay that was developed in Mycobacteria [22]. We cloned two non-continuous fragments of the lacZ gene (which is not native to the P. aeruginosa genome) into PAO1 and PA14 wild-type, Δku, and ΔligD strains (Fig. 2A). The gene fragments are separated by inverse I-SceI restriction sites that, when cut, result in non-complementary overhangs that are unable to be directly ligated together. To induce I-SceI cuts in these reporter-bearing strains, we introduced arabinose-inducible I-SceI on an exogenous plasmid (pI-SceI) by conjugation (Fig. 2B). For most reporter cells, the break proves lethal (Fig. 2C). Alternatively, pI-SceI can be introduced by electroporation, but we found that the relative transformation efficiencies are 10- to 100-fold less when using this protocol; hence, the conjugation protocol was preferentially employed for its higher yield of survivors (Supplementary Fig. S5, Extended Data Table 3). For the surviving cells that formed colonies, sequencing either side of the break sites provides evidence for how cells were able to repair the break.

To our surprise, we found that DSB repair of the I-SceI breaks by NHEJ was an uncommon outcome of the reporter assay. Of the 541 surviving colonies that were screened by PCR and gel electrophoresis, 66% still had both I-SceI sites intact (Extended Data Table 1). We took a subset of these colonies (24 samples across each of the six strains) and confirmed that they all had mutations in pI-SceI that would inactivate or decrease expression of the restriction enzyme (Extended Data Table 4; also see Supplementary Discussion). This is similar to the reported fraction of Mycobacteria colonies that inactivated I-SceI in the original DSB assay (75%) [22].

The most common repair outcome (24% of colonies) was a specific loss of 20 nucleotide (nt) around the cut sites (−20 nt/−0 nt); sequence analysis and in vitro digestion revealed that this outcome is consistent with digestion of an upstream, non-canonical I-SceI cut site that complements the downstream cut site; these complementary ends could be directly religated in a Ku- and LigD-independent manner (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S7A–D). This non-specific restriction was not detected in the original assays in M. smegmatis [22]. To see if we could increase detection of DSB repair events, we scrambled the sequence of the alternative I-SceI restriction site (Supplementary Fig. S7E). However, we did not detect more DSB repair events and the survival after transformation with pI-SceI fell ∼10-fold compared to the originals (Supplementary Fig. S7F). This provides additional evidence that repair of I-SceI-mediated DSBs in P. aeruginosa is rare.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa repairs DSBs by MHMR

Although we originally intended to only screen for NHEJ repair, the DSB repair assay also highlighted an interesting group of samples that appeared to undergo MHMR. MHMR is a pathway-agnostic term used to describe microhomology-mediated end joining (MMEJ)-like DSB repair outcomes—in which microhomologies of a few nucleotides (fewer than 16 nt) on either end of a DSB synapse are used to facilitate repair—without implicating specific enzymes or molecular mechanisms [26, 27]. We observed MHMR in seven of the 147 DSB reporter colonies that exhibited repair (Fig. 3A). We categorized repair as MHMR if there were at least three nucleotides of homologous overlap in the upstream and downstream sequences; the observed microhomologies ranged from three to eight nucleotides (Fig. 4C and D).

We sought to confirm that these repair events are not attributable to non-specific I-SceI digestion and direct religation of sticky ends, as was the case for the −20 nt alternative cut site repair outcome. For each MHMR repair outcome, we placed the microhomology sequences and its surrounding 18 bases into a pHERD30T empty vector (e.g. 3 nt microhomology + 9 nt upstream + 9 nt downstream = 21 nt plasmid insert) (Supplementary Fig. S8A and B). Because the microhomology occurs upstream and downstream of the I-SceI cuts, two plasmids were made for each outcome: one “up” and one “down.” We found that I-SceI could not digest any of the constructed plasmids, strongly suggesting that these repair events were microhomology-mediated and not due to non-specific I-SceI digestion and religation (Supplementary Fig. S8C).

Consistent with the eukaryotic literature on MMEJ versus NHEJ, we found MHMR to be Ku- and LigD-independent in prokaryotic P. aeruginosa [26] (Fig. 3). Eukaryotic MMEJ is also considered independent of the RecA homolog, Rad51. Although relative transformation efficiencies between ΔrecA and recA-containing strains were comparable, we never observed MHMR in DSB repair assays conducted with PAO1 ΔrecA or PA14 ΔrecA (308 and 366 screened colonies, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. S5, Extended Data Table 1) [26]. Thus, we cannot rule out a role for RecA in Pseudomonal MHMR.

DiscussionOur data indicate that DSB repair is not a significant contributor to P. aeruginosa FQ persistence in stationary-phase cultures, in contrast to the requirement for recA and SOS response induction for three other model organisms: E. coli, S. enterica, and S. aureus [10, 12, 13]. We do not dismiss the role that DSB repair may play for P. aeruginosa FQ persistence in other model systems and growth conditions [9, 28]. But in our nutrient-limited, starved P. aeruginosa PAO1 and PA14 cultures, FQ persistence was not dependent on HR or NHEJ (Fig. 1C) [8]. Our assays with HR-NHEJ cross-pathway mutants suggest that other DNA repair enzymes may be relevant to repair of DNA following LVX treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4). As such, the development of targeted inhibitors against Ku, LigD, or RecA to augment FQ treatment of clinical infections—where P. aeruginosa exists in an antibiotic-tolerant state—would not be recommended.To better understand the contributors to P. aeruginosa DSB repair, we implemented an I-SceI-based repair assay in wild-type, Δku, and ΔligD strains [22]. Surprisingly, stationary-phase P. aeruginosa cells very infrequently utilized NHEJ to repair DSB (Fig. 3). However, we found evidence that P. aeruginosa is capable of LigD-independent NHEJ, as NHEJ was observed three different times in PA14 ΔligD. This challenges the canonical essentiality of both Ku and LigD for NHEJ and begs the question: which enzyme(s) is compensating to mediate NHEJ in LigD’s absence? We compared the transcriptomes of ΔligDΔrecA and ΔrecA strains to determine if there is compensatory upregulation of any genes in strains lacking HR and the canonical NHEJ ligase LigD. However, there were no differentially upregulated ligases or DNA repair enzymes (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Table S4). The only other annotated ligase or structural homolog to LigD in P. aeruginosa is the essential replicative ligase, LigA [29]. The lack of other potential ligases and evidence of LigA’s role in LigD-independent repair in other bacterial species lead us to hypothesize that LigA is the likely facilitator of NHEJ in P. aeruginosa ΔligD strains [25, 30–32]. Consistent with this, we were unable to knock down ligA in a PAO1 ΔrecAΔligD background after multiple rounds of cloning (Supplementary Table S5).The DSB repair assay also revealed that P. aeruginosa can utilize a non-canonical MHMR pathway mediated by 3–8 nt microhomologies bridging the break site (Fig. 4C and D). It has been suggested that NHEJ begins with stabilization of the break by microhomologous sequences on opposite sides that anneal together; however, MHMR occurred in NHEJ-proficient (wild-type) and -deficient (Δku) strains, alike (Fig. 3A) [27]. A Ku-/LigD-independent MHMR pathway has not been previously reported for P. aeruginosa, except in patient isolates containing a specific group II intron-like reverse transcriptase [17]. None of our PAO1 and PA14 strains express the reverse transcriptase or any homologous sequences. Thus, we believe these reference strains are employing a different Ku-/LigD-independent MHMR mechanism of repair (Supplementary Fig. S8). We also did not detect any MHMR repair events in recA-null strains, suggesting that RecA may be involved in this repair process in P. aeruginosa (Extended Data Table 1) [33]. Since MHMR results in imperfect repair, this pathway could be a potential contributor to mutagenesis and the development of antibiotic resistance [34].The observed MHMR repair outcomes are similar to the outcomes of E. coli alternative end-joining (alt-EJ) repair of non-complementary 3′ overhangs: both repair mechanisms are Ku- and LigD-independent and involve more extensive bidirectional end resection about the DSB site [30]. However, in a similar chromosomal I-SceI DSB repair assay in E. coli, much larger resections of >1000 nt were often observed, and microhomologies were mostly 1–3 nt, compared to 3–8 nt as observed here in P. aeruginosa [30]. Our data indicate that DSB repair is not a significant contributor to P. aeruginosa FQ persistence in stationary-phase cultures, in contrast to the requirement for recA and SOS response induction for three other model organisms: E. coli, S. enterica, and S. aureus [10, 12, 13]. We do not dismiss the role that DSB repair may play for P. aeruginosa FQ persistence in other model systems and growth conditions [9, 28]. But in our nutrient-limited, starved P. aeruginosa PAO1 and PA14 cultures, FQ persistence was not dependent on HR or NHEJ (Fig. 1C) [8]. Our assays with HR-NHEJ cross-pathway mutants suggest that other DNA repair enzymes may be relevant to repair of DNA following LVX treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4). As such, the development of targeted inhibitors against Ku, LigD, or RecA to augment FQ treatment of clinical infections—where P. aeruginosa exists in an antibiotic-tolerant state—would not be recommended.

To better understand the contributors to P. aeruginosa DSB repair, we implemented an I-SceI-based repair assay in wild-type, Δku, and ΔligD strains [22]. Surprisingly, stationary-phase P. aeruginosa cells very infrequently utilized NHEJ to repair DSB (Fig. 3). However, we found evidence that P. aeruginosa is capable of LigD-independent NHEJ, as NHEJ was observed three different times in PA14 ΔligD. This challenges the canonical essentiality of both Ku and LigD for NHEJ and begs the question: which enzyme(s) is compensating to mediate NHEJ in LigD’s absence? We compared the transcriptomes of ΔligDΔrecA and ΔrecA strains to determine if there is compensatory upregulation of any genes in strains lacking HR and the canonical NHEJ ligase LigD. However, there were no differentially upregulated ligases or DNA repair enzymes (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Supplementary Table S4). The only other annotated ligase or structural homolog to LigD in P. aeruginosa is the essential replicative ligase, LigA [29]. The lack of other potential ligases and evidence of LigA’s role in LigD-independent repair in other bacterial species lead us to hypothesize that LigA is the likely facilitator of NHEJ in P. aeruginosa ΔligD strains [25, 30–32]. Consistent with this, we were unable to knock down ligA in a PAO1 ΔrecAΔligD background after multiple rounds of cloning (Supplementary Table S5).

The DSB repair assay also revealed that P. aeruginosa can utilize a non-canonical MHMR pathway mediated by 3–8 nt microhomologies bridging the break site (Fig. 4C and D). It has been suggested that NHEJ begins with stabilization of the break by microhomologous sequences on opposite sides that anneal together; however, MHMR occurred in NHEJ-proficient (wild-type) and -deficient (Δku) strains, alike (Fig. 3A) [27]. A Ku-/LigD-independent MHMR pathway has not been previously reported for P. aeruginosa, except in patient isolates containing a specific group II intron-like reverse transcriptase [17]. None of our PAO1 and PA14 strains express the reverse transcriptase or any homologous sequences. Thus, we believe these reference strains are employing a different Ku-/LigD-independent MHMR mechanism of repair (Supplementary Fig. S8). We also did not detect any MHMR repair events in recA-null strains, suggesting that RecA may be involved in this repair process in P. aeruginosa (Extended Data Table 1) [33]. Since MHMR results in imperfect repair, this pathway could be a potential contributor to mutagenesis and the development of antibiotic resistance [34].

The observed MHMR repair outcomes are similar to the outcomes of E. coli alternative end-joining (alt-EJ) repair of non-complementary 3′ overhangs: both repair mechanisms are Ku- and LigD-independent and involve more extensive bidirectional end resection about the DSB site [30]. However, in a similar chromosomal I-SceI DSB repair assay in E. coli, much larger resections of >1000 nt were often observed, and microhomologies were mostly 1–3 nt, compared to 3–8 nt as observed here in P. aeruginosa [30]. Without a clear definition for alt-EJ, we opted for the more descriptive terminology of MHMR. This distinction will likely clarify with future studies on the conservation of alt-EJ versus MHMR across bacterial species and the identification of specific enzymatic players.

It is curious why the majority of P. aeruginosa cells did not repair DSBs by NHEJ or MHMR when the alternative was death. One possibility is that PAO1 and PA14 NHEJ enzymes are poorly equipped to address the 3′-non-complementary overhangs formed by double I-SceI digestion [15]. The structures of DSB ends have been shown to affect which enzymes mediate repair and the subsequent outcomes [25, 30]. Therefore, while NHEJ was rarely observed in our experiments, it may be more prevalent in the context of other DSBs in P. aeruginosa and will be the subject of future investigations.

Overall, this work highlights the dispensability of canonical DSB repair for stationary-phase P. aeruginosa FQ persistence and uncovers non-canonical means by which P. aeruginosa can repair DSBs. Analysis of DSB repair outcomes revealed that P. aeruginosa’s repertoire of non-canonical repair includes (i) LigD-independent NHEJ and (ii) Ku-/LigD-independent MHMR. As our understanding of P. aeruginosa DSB repair grows, so does our ability to identify discrete, targetable pathways to augment FQ treatment for recalcitrant pseudomonal infections.
