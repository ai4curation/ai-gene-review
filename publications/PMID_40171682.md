Cell surface oligosaccharides and related polymers are commonly decorated with
acyl esters that alter their structural properties and influence their
interactions with other molecules. In many cases, these esters are added to
polymers that are already positioned on the extracytoplasmic side of a membrane,
presenting cells with a chemical challenge because the high-energy acyl donors
used for these modifications are made in the cytoplasm. How activated acyl
groups are passed from the cytoplasm to extra-cytoplasmic polymers has been a
longstanding question. Recent mechanistic work has shown that many bacterial
acyl transfer pathways operate by shuttling acyl groups through two covalent
intermediates to their final destination on an extracellular polymer. Key to
these and other pathways are cross-membrane acyltransferases─enzymes that
catalyze transfer of acyl groups from a donor on one side of the membrane to a
recipient on the other side. Here we review what has been learned recently about
how cross-membrane acyltransferases in polymer acylation pathways function,
highlighting the chemical and biosynthetic logic used by two key protein
families, membrane-bound O-acyltransferases (MBOATs) and acyltransferase-3 (AT3)
proteins. We also point out outstanding questions and avenues for further
exploration.

INTRODUCTION

Cells across the kingdoms of life are coated with glycopolymers that provide mechanical support, protect against pathogens and other stressors, and mediate interactions with nearby cells.1 These glycopolymers include peptidoglycan and lipopolysaccharides in bacteria,2 cellulose and xylans in plants,3 chitin and glucans in fungi,4 and diverse extracellular and protein/lipid/RNA-linked polysaccharides in animals and other organisms.5–10 To diversify and confer new properties to these polymers, organisms often modify the core backbone structures, chiefly through nonoxidative transformations like acylation and glycosylation.11–23 These modifications can be thought of as analogous to some of the tailoring modifications in natural product biosynthesis24 because they are installed on glycopolymer building blocks after these units have been incorporated into a larger product by an enzyme or set of enzymes required for assembly of the polymer backbone.

Acylations and other tailoring modifications require thermodynamically activated donors, which are only made inside the cell where ATP is abundant.24, 25 It would therefore seem most sensible for cell surface glycopolymers to be assembled and modified in the cytosol. This does occur in some cases,26–31 and the major challenge for the cell is getting these modified polymers to the cell surface (Fig. 1A). Nature has provided a variety of transport systems to do this.32–35 However, many polymers are assembled on the extracytoplasmic side of the lipid bilayer or are immediately translocated there while synthesis occurs (Fig. 1B),36–41 which means any tailoring modifications must also be installed there. Additionally, some polymers synthesized in the cytosol are modified only after they have been transported across the membrane (Fig. 1C).42, 43 To carry out acylation of extracytoplasmic polymers using thioester donors generated in the cytoplasm, cells rely on cross-membrane acyltransferases to move acyl groups across the membrane. How these enzymes accomplish this feat been a longstanding question.44–47

This review will focus primarily on two large classes of proteins involved in cross-membrane acylation of bacterial cell surface polymers: membrane-bound O-acyltransferases (MBOATs)48 and acyltransferase-3 (AT3) proteins.49 Bacterial MBOATs and a sizeable fraction of bacterial AT3 proteins collaborate with another class of enzymes—SGNH hydrolase superfamily transferases—to effect polymer acylation across the cytoplasmic membrane. In MBOAT/SGNH systems, the SGNH transferases are typically separate proteins; in AT3/SGNH systems, the two domains are typically fused into one AT3-SGNH fusion protein. Recent work has identified a striking convergence in the chemical mechanisms of MBOAT/SGNH systems and AT3-SGNH fusion proteins,50–52 but outstanding questions remain. Additionally, the mechanisms of bacterial AT3 proteins that appear to lack an SGNH transferase partner are still a mystery. Here, we critically review what is known and what remains to be learned about these various bacterial cell surface polymer acylation pathways, and we examine several related eukaryotic systems as well.

BACTERIAL MEMBRANE-BOUND O-ACYLTRANSFERASE SYSTEMSThe most well-studied class of cross-membrane acyltransferases across organisms is the membrane-bound O-acyltransferase (MBOAT) family. First recognized as a family by Hofmann,53 MBOAT proteins are found in organisms from all domains of life, with ten enzymatically functional MBOATs encoded in the human genome.54 In humans and other eukaryotes, these enzymes transfer fatty acyl chains to either lipid hydroxyl groups or to serine (or N-terminal cysteine, in one case55, 56) residues on small proteins.57, 58 Well-known examples include diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1 (DGAT1), which acylates diacylglycerols to yield triglycerides, molecules that store metabolic energy;59 and ghrelin O-acyltransferase (GOAT), which octanoylates ghrelin, a hormone that regulates appetite.60, 61 Due to their importance in human physiology and the possibility that some are targets for therapeutics, the MBOAT family has been intensively studied, and structures of several different MBOATs have been reported.62–73 The first of these was of a bacterial MBOAT protein called DltB,62 discussed further below.The DltB structure, along with later structures of MBOAT proteins from humans and other animals, revealed a core fold for MBOATs. As put forward by Coupland, Ansell and coworkers,58 this core fold consists of eight transmembrane (TM) helices surrounding a central membrane-spanning tunnel, with helix-containing re-entrant loops between the third and fourth and between the fifth and sixth TM helices (Fig. 2A). At the non-cytosolic end of the sixth TM helix is a highly conserved histidine residue that is critical for acyltransferase activity.60, 62, 68, 73–83 The second re-entrant loop, RL2, contains a conserved Asn/Asp/His residue (usually His in bacterial MBOATs) that is generally thought to play a role in substrate thioester binding but is not strictly required for activity in all cases.62, 63, 68, 77–79 Other notable residues include a Trp/Tyr in RL2 that has been implicated in tunnel gating in some MBOATs,62, 70, 71, 84 and various conserved residues lining the central tunnel of the protein that contact the pantetheine arm of the acyl donor (Fig. 2B & 2C).From these structures, as well as mechanistic work, a consensus has formed for how eukaryotic MBOATs work: an acyl-CoA donor binds to the MBOAT protein on its cytosolic side, with the acylated pantetheine group extending into the protein’s central tunnel. The conserved histidine residue on core TM helix 6 then acts as a catalytic base to activate the nucleophilic hydroxyl group of the lipid or small protein recipient to promote cross-membrane acyl transfer, though roles for the histidine in substrate positioning or covalent catalysis cannot be ruled out.57, 58, 70–72, 85Despite clear similarities in catalytic machinery, the model for how MBOAT proteins function in eukaryotes does not straightforwardly map onto bacterial MBOATs. Bacterial MBOATs are typically involved in the modification of polymers found on the bacterial cell surface53, 83, 86–91 rather than lipids embedded in the membrane or small secretory proteins that can fit into the MBOAT active site. Moreover, bacterial MBOATs come paired with an SGNH transferase encoded alongside the MBOAT in an operon; both proteins are required for acylation to occur (and in some cases, additional proteins are required as well).82, 83, 86–95 Recently, it has become clear that bacterial MBOATs collaborate with SGNH proteins to effect acylation of extracellular polymers in a process involving two covalent intermediates.51, 52 Below we will describe how this general strategy is used and adapted in the context of several important cell envelope polymer acylation pathways.The Dlt pathway for d-alanylation of lipoteichoic acid.Many Gram-positive bacteria, particularly those in the Firmicutes/Bacillota phylum, have long anionic polymers called teichoic acids that play crucial roles in cell growth, division, pathogenesis, and antibiotic resistance.37, 96–98 These polymers come in two main forms: the lipoteichoic acids (LTAs), which are anchored in the cell membrane by a lipid, and the wall teichoic acids, which are covalently attached to the peptidoglycan cell wall. Teichoic acids are made up of repeating units of sugars and/or sugar alcohols connected by phosphodiester bonds, and they are typically modified by cationic d-alanine esters that modulate their overall charge in order to increase cross-polymer interactions and strengthen the cell envelope.99, 100 These esters are critical for the virulence of Staphylococcus aureus,101
Listeria monocytogenes,102 and other pathogens,103 as they shield cells from certain antibiotics,100, 104, 105 cationic antimicrobial peptides,105–108 and other secreted innate immune cell effectors like lysozyme109, 110 and phospholipase.111 They also play a role in anti-phage defense112, 113 and contribute to a beneficial host-microbiome symbiosis.114, 115Given the importance of this modification, the mechanism by which these esters are installed onto teichoic acids (Fig. 3A) has been of longstanding interest in the field, with the first protein in the pathway being identified and characterized in 1960.116 This protein, DltA, uses ATP and free d-alanine to generate the high-energy precursor AMP-d-Ala.117, 118 Both in its function and in sequence/structure, DltA resembles the adenylation domains of non-ribosomal peptide synthetase mega-enzymes.119–122 As such, it is in retrospect unsurprising that DltA was found to modify a small protein that resembles acyl and peptidyl carrier proteins in fatty acid, polyketide, and non-ribosomal peptide biosynthesis.123, 124 This protein, DltC, bears a 4’-phosphopantetheine group attached at a serine residue, and DltA transfers the d-alanyl group from AMP-d-Ala onto the phosphopantetheinyl arm of DltC, resulting in a d-alanyl thioester.125 For a long time, this remained the general state of knowledge about the biochemistry of the Dlt pathway until it was discovered that the genes encoding DltA and DltC were part of an operon that also encoded two other proteins: the MBOAT protein DltB and the SGNH protein DltD.89, 90It was originally proposed that DltB might act either as a transporter of d-alanyl-DltC or as an enzyme to transfer the d-alanyl group from DltC to a lipid carrier which could then serve as the acyl donor to modify LTA.99 However, the paper reporting the structure of DltB also identified a stable interaction between DltB and DltC and disclosed the structure of this heterodimeric complex, in which DltC binds to DltB at its cytosolic surface.62 Although the pantetheine arm was not visible, the position of DltC and the length of a pantetheine group suggested the arm could extend far enough into DltB’s central tunnel to present the d-alanyl group to the catalytic histidine. The authors proposed that the LTA polymer chain reaches down into DltB’s central tunnel to react with the d-alanyl thioester. Although the structure of the DltB:DltC complex was informative, the model overlooked a role for DltD, the SGNH protein found in all dlt operons. Contemporaneous work showed that DltD is required for LTA d-alanylation in S. aureus,82 consistent with other studies implicating SGNH proteins in bacterial MBOAT acylation pathways.SGNH hydrolase and hydrolase-like proteins, which have been the subject of a recent comprehensive review,126 are a large family of enzymes that use a serine protease-like Ser-His-Asp active site to transfer acyl groups to water or to some other recipient via a covalent acyl-serine intermediate. Some SGNH proteins had been shown to react with high energy acetyl donors to form acetyl esters that can then transfer their acetyl groups to oligomeric acceptors that mimic cell surface glycans.92, 127–133 For example, Moynihan and Clarke showed that the peptidoglycan acetyltransferase PatB (discussed further below) can transfer acetyl groups from p-nitrophenyl acetate to chitooligosaccharides.127 Therefore, it was proposed that DltD becomes d-alanylated and then promotes the final step in the Dlt pathway by offloading its d-alanine to lipoteichoic acid.82 While this proposal was reasonable, how the MBOAT protein DltB might facilitate DltD d-alanylation was a mystery. Owing to spatial incompatibility between the large SGNH domain of DltD and the narrow, difficult-to-access active site of DltB, direct DltB-mediated d-alanyl transfer from DltC to DltD did not seem possible. Therefore, there appeared to be a missing intermediate/step in the transfer process.In 2017 a small gene, dltX, encoding a predicted single-pass membrane protein, was implicated in teichoic acid d-alanylation in Bacillus thuringiensis, although no function was identified.134 At the time, dltX was thought to be present in only a subset of dlt operons, suggesting a specialized function in some organisms and not others.82, 135 By re-examining ~700 putatively dltX-less dlt operons by hand, we found that dltX is present in all of them but had most likely not been annotated because its small size (~150 bp) prevented consistent annotation by genome annotation algorithms.51 The presence of dltX in all dlt operons suggested it played an essential role in LTA d-alanylation, and we began to investigate it. We found that DltX binds simultaneously to DltB and DltD through its TM helix, acting as a linchpin to hold the MBOAT and SGNH proteins together in a heterotrimeric complex. We also identified a highly conserved six-amino acid FxYxxF motif at the C-terminus of DltX, with the tyrosine being completely invariant. This motif proved to be the missing piece needed to solve the puzzle of how MBOAT/SGNH systems function in bacteria. Through a combination of genetics in S. aureus, structural modeling, and in vitro biochemistry with Dlt protein orthologs from Streptococcus thermophilus, we found strong evidence for a chemical mechanism in which DltB catalyzes the transfer of d-alanine from DltC’s phosphopantetheinyl arm to the invariant tyrosine hydroxyl group in the C-terminal hexapeptide motif of DltX.51 This motif, which we called the “acyl shuttle” motif, then ferries d-alanine to the active site of DltD for ultimate transfer to the LTA polymer, with DltD using its catalytic triad to accomplish this transfer via a serine-linked covalent intermediate, as is characteristic of this class of enzymes (Fig. 3A & B). We directly observed the d-alanyl ester intermediate on DltD, but the putative tyrosyl ester intermediate was too unstable for analysis. However, substituting the acyl shuttle motif tyrosine with lysine resulted in formation of a stable d-alanyl amide, supporting the proposed mechanism.From an energetic standpoint, the chemical logic of the Dlt pathway was satisfying: first, DltA uses ATP to generate the high-energy AMP-d-Ala species, and then the d-alanyl group moves step by step down an energy gradient, from a thioester on DltC to an aryl ester on the tyrosine of DltX to a serine ester on DltD. We reasoned that if the mechanism we found for the Dlt pathway was general, then other polymer acylation pathways that use MBOAT/SGNH protein pairs should also have an acyl shuttle motif somewhere. Indeed, alignment of over 14,000 distinct bacterial MBOATs revealed a highly conserved six-amino acid acyl shuttle motif (FxYxxF) fused to their C-termini. Accordingly, we proposed that these pathways follow the same general mechanism as the Dlt pathway, transferring a thiol-linked acetyl group to the tyrosine in an acyl shuttle motif and then to the serine hydroxyl of an SGNH protein, which catalyzes the final transfer.51 The MBOATs in these other bacterial acylation pathways are essentially DltB-DltX fusions, albeit with an additional TM helix to meet the topological demands of placing the acyl shuttle motif on the same side of the membrane as the SGNH transferase active site.Peptidoglycan O-acetylation in Gram-negative bacteria.Peptidoglycan is an essential bacterial cell envelope structure located outside the cytoplasmic membrane that is composed of linear GlcNAc-MurNAc strands crosslinked by oligopeptides.136 In Gram-negative bacteria, the MBOAT/SGNH protein pair PatA/PatB is responsible for acetylating peptidoglycan on the C6-OH of the MurNAc sugars.83, 88, 137 O-acetylation at this position is a cell wall modification found in both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, although it is carried out by different classes of cross-membrane acyltransferases in these two groups of bacteria;138 how Gram-positive bacteria perform peptidoglycan acetylation is discussed later in this review. PatA and PatB heavily O-acetylate peptidoglycan in the Gram-negative pathogens Neisseria gonorrhoeae,83, 139, 140
Campylobacter jejuni,88, 141 and Helicobacter pylori.88, 142 This modification regulates peptidoglycan cleavage by endogenous lytic transglycosylases that act through a mechanism requiring a free C6-OH group on MurNAc,143–145 and cleavage by these lytic transglycosylases in turn regulates cell growth and division.146, 147 There is also evidence that MurNAc acetylation can impact Gram-negative bacterial infections—deleting either patA or patB significantly limits the ability of C. jejuni to colonize the gut in an animal model148—and may modulate innate immune responses by affecting release of peptidoglycan fragments.83, 142, 149, 150PatA is an MBOAT while PatB is an SGNH transferase. As mentioned in the previous section of this review, PatB was shown many years ago to transfer acetyl groups to peptidoglycan in vitro in a mechanism that involves a covalent intermediate;127–129 however, it was unclear what PatA’s role in the pathway was. Our discovery that PatA contains a C-terminal hexapeptide that strongly resembles the C-terminal acyl shuttle motif found in DltX strongly suggested that PatA uses that hexapeptide to form a covalent acetyl-tyrosine intermediate, which is then used as a substrate by PatB.51 As part of a collaboration with Clarke and coworkers, who first characterized PatB, we reconstituted the activity of C. jejuni PatA.52 We found that PatA uses the thermodynamically activated species acetyl-CoA to acetylate the tyrosine in its C-terminal motif. Autoacetylation is dependent on the conserved active site histidine of PatA. Notably, we were able to observe the acyl-tyrosine intermediate in PatA directly, which had not been possible for the more labile DltX acyl-tyrosine intermediate.Reconstitution of acetyl group transfer from PatA to PatB was unsuccessful, but Clarke and coworkers demonstrated that PatB can use a synthetic acyl shuttle motif hexapeptide bearing acetyl-tyrosine as a donor for acetylation of a peptidoglycan mimic.52 Furthermore, AlphaFold modeling predicts an interaction between PatA and PatB, with the periplasmic domain of PatB sitting atop PatA in a manner that leaves abundant space for the necessary flip-flop movement of PatA’s C-terminal peptide from its active site to PatB’s active site.51, 52 Overall, the study on the PatA/PatB system is consistent with the model proposed for the Dlt pathway: PatA transfers acetyl groups from acetyl-CoA to PatB via a covalent acetyl-tyrosine found on a flexible C-terminal peptide that can swing up and down between active sites (Fig. 3C–E).Secondary cell wall polysaccharide O-acetylation in Gram-positive sporulating bacteria.In Bacillus anthracis, B. cereus, and related species, the peptidoglycan cell wall houses covalently linked secondary cell wall polysaccharides (SCWPs) that are involved in anchoring the proteinaceous S-layer “exoskeleton” to the cell’s surface.151–156 These polysaccharides consist of repeating branched oligosaccharide units with a unique trisaccharide terminus at the free, nonreducing end. The middle sugar, a GlcNAc, of this terminal trisaccharide is acetylated at the C3-OH position, a modification believed to play a role in proper S-layer protein deposition and, indirectly, in proper cell division.91, 154–157 An MBOAT-SGNH pair—PatA1 and PatB1—is implicated in the installation of this acetyl group (Fig. 4A).91, 92
B. cereus PatB1, the SGNH hydrolase-like protein of the pair, has been biochemically characterized in vitro as an acetyltransferase: using p-nitrophenyl acetate as an acetyl donor, Clarke and coworkers demonstrated acetyl transfer to short GlcNAc-containing oligosaccharides as well as to a chemically synthesized SCWP terminal trisaccharide.92 The specific activity for transfer to the synthesized terminal unit was higher than that for any of the other tested oligosaccharide species, suggesting this is the native substrate of PatB1. Given that PatA1, like PatA in the Gram-negative peptidoglycan O-acetylation system, contains a C-terminal acyl shuttle motif like that in DltX, and that AlphaFold confidently predicts an interaction between PatA1 and PatB1,51 it is likely that this pair operates analogously to the Dlt and PatAB pathway enzymes (Fig. 3E). We note that in many species there is another MBOAT/SGNH pair encoded by genes directly adjacent to the genes encoding PatA1 and PatB1.158 This pair, PatA2 and PatB2, have also been implicated in assembly of the S-layer exoskeleton,91 but their functions are otherwise unknown.92Alginate O-acetylation in Pseudomonas species and other Gram-negative bacteria.The pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as well as related species of Pseudomonas and some species of Azotobacter, produces an exopolysaccharide called alginate159–161 that is an important component of the hard-to-manage biofilms produced in cystic fibrosis-associated lung infections.162–166 Alginate is made up of mannuronate and guluronate substituents, the latter of which are generated by epimerization of mannuronate units post-polymerization.159–161 The mannuronate units are often O-acetylated at the C2-OH or C3-OH positions,167, 168 and this acetylation results in more robust biofilm formation as well as resistance to clearing by immune cells.169–172 As in the previously discussed pathways, alginate O-acetylation is mediated by an MBOAT/SGNH pair, here known as AlgI and AlgJ.86 However, two other proteins, AlgX and AlgF, are also required for acetylation.93, 94 Both AlgX and AlgF are soluble periplasmic proteins.173, 174 AlgX is an SGNH protein that binds to the outer membrane lipoprotein AlgK, which along with the membrane porin AlgE has been implicated in alginate export.175–178 AlgF appears to have a non-enzymatic function.179The current model for alginate acetylation is that the MBOAT protein AlgI is acetylated on its C-terminal acyl shuttle motif, which offloads the acetyl group onto the SGNH protein AlgJ.51 AlgJ somehow transfers its acetyl group to the active site of AlgX in a process that involves AlgF.131, 179 AlgX then completes the transfer of the acetyl group to the alginate polymer as it is being fed through the outer membrane for secretion (Fig. 4B).94, 131, 175 This full model has yet to be demonstrated experimentally, but the AlgI:AlgJ complex is confidently predicted by AlphaFold51, 179 and supported by experiments, including co-immunoprecipitation studies.179, 180 Furthermore, AlgF seems to bind both AlgX and AlgJ based on in vitro protein-protein binding studies and co-immunoprecipitation, though a confident structural model was predicted by AlphaFold only for AlgF:AlgX but not AlgF:AlgJ.179, 180 Finally, AlgX can transfer acetyl groups to alginate in vitro while AlgJ cannot transfer acetyl to nor bind to alginate in similar experiments.131 Overall, the models for how the initial and final acetyl transfer steps work are plausible, while the major remaining question is how the putative AlgJ to AlgX handoff occurs.Cellulose O-acetylation across diverse bacterial species.Many bacteria produce cellulose, a linear glucose polymer, and some like Pseudomonas fluorescens and related species, as well as various clostridial species, acetylate this cellulose.181–183 In P. fluorescens and other Gram-negative bacteria, the system for cellulose acetylation appears similar to the AlgIJFX alginate acetylation system in that there is an MBOAT/SGNH pair (WssH/WssI), a second SGNH protein (WssF), and a likely adaptor protein (WssG).182, 184 WssHI likely works like AlgIJ,51 and AlphaFold modeling185, 186 is supportive of the two proteins interacting in a manner similar to AlgIJ, PatAB, etc. However, there is evidence that WssI, unlike AlgJ, can transfer acetyl groups directly to cellulose in vitro (Fig. 4C).184 It is possible that in vivo this does not occur and that WssF, like AlgX, acetylates the polymer directly,187 but experimental evidence regarding how this system functions remains limited.In acetylated cellulose-producing clostridial species like Clostridioides difficile and botulinum, genetic analysis suggests that just two genes—encoding the MBOAT/SGNH pair CcsH/CcsI—are required for cellulose acetylation; there are no genes encoding direct orthologs of AlgF or AlgX.183, 188 AlphaFold predicts that CcsH and CcsI interact (see Supporting Information), and given the presence of an acyl shuttle motif on CcsH, it is likely the mechanism for CcsHI function is similar to that used by Dlt and PatAB systems. We suggest that Gram-positive bacteria like these Clostridia do not need proteins like AlgF and AlgX or WssF and WssG to modify exported polymers because they do not have an outer membrane: in Gram-negative organisms, polymer modification and export must be tightly coordinated to maintain outer membrane integrity.189–191Concluding thoughts on the bacterial MBOAT systems.The overwhelming majority of bacterial MBOATs are found in operons with SGNH proteins and either contain an acyl shuttle motif at their C-terminus or cluster with a small protein that contains an acyl shuttle motif.48, 51, 192 These observations suggest that all these MBOATs catalyze transfer of an acyl group to the invariant tyrosine in the acyl shuttle motif, which delivers the acyl group to the SGNH transferase, which catalyzes a final transfer step (Fig. 3E). If so, all these systems likely acylate cell surface polymers that either are comprised of sugars (e.g., peptidoglycan, alginate, cellulose) or are made of subunits derived from carbohydrates (e.g., Type I lipoteichoic acids, which are assembled using the headgroups of phosphatidylglycerol37, 96). For many of these systems, however, the polymer recipients are unknown. For example, some Gram-negative bacteria193–195 like Bordetella pertussis and some Actinobacteria51 like Eggerthella lenta contain dlt-like operons, but these organisms do not make teichoic acids; furthermore, some archaea even encode MBOAT/SGNH pairs.51 These pathways are presumed to modify polymers whose identities are unknown but could be determined by constructing deletion strains and comparing cell envelope fractions or purified components using analytical techniques like NMR.21, 196 Also unknown—even for cases where the recipients have been identified—is how the polymers are recognized and held so that acylation takes place. While in many systems the SGNH protein appears to be sufficient to bind to the polymer substrate to the degree required for acyl transfer in vitro, it is often unclear how polymer binding occurs—though we note that for AlgX, a charged groove extending from the active site to a C-terminal carbohydrate-binding module has been implicated in alginate binding,94, 131 and the PatAB complex has been suggested to bind to peptidoglycan oligomers at the interface between the two proteins.52 Finally, how the more extensive acylation pathways that modify exported polymers in Gram-negative bacteria function remains an open question.179, 184We note in closing that some MBOAT/SGNH systems are proposed targets for therapeutic agents.92, 127, 197 The high conservation of the acyl shuttle motif across organisms and across systems that acylate very different recipients suggests the possibility of making inhibitor scaffolds that can serve as general starting points for optimization. So far, there has been little work on inhibitor discovery for MBOAT/SGNH systems, although we note that we have discovered several biologically active DltB inhibitors in a phenotypic screen,104, 198 including one that was used in a recent cryo-electron microscopy structure of a DltB:inhibitor co-complex.199 Resistance mutations in DltB have confirmed on-target activity of this inhibitor in cells. Additionally, Clarke and coworkers identified an inhibitor of Neisseria gonorrhoeae’s PatB in an in vitro screen and showed that this inhibitor reduces growth of the organism,200 and Weadge and coworkers demonstrated that a repurposed tyrosine kinase inhibitor, Tyrphostin AG 112, inhibits acetylesterase activity of WssI from Achromobacter insuavis in vitro.184 While more work should be done to study the effects of MBOAT/SGNH pathways on bacterial pathogenesis/virulence, we anticipate that recent progress in understanding these systems’ mechanisms will spur further efforts to develop inhibitors. Such inhibitors are likely to be (or can be designed/engineered to be201, 202) selective for bacterial MBOAT and SGNH proteins over their human counterparts due to low sequence similarity and distinct substrate specificity.

BACTERIAL MEMBRANE-BOUND O-ACYLTRANSFERASE SYSTEMS

The most well-studied class of cross-membrane acyltransferases across organisms is the membrane-bound O-acyltransferase (MBOAT) family. First recognized as a family by Hofmann,53 MBOAT proteins are found in organisms from all domains of life, with ten enzymatically functional MBOATs encoded in the human genome.54 In humans and other eukaryotes, these enzymes transfer fatty acyl chains to either lipid hydroxyl groups or to serine (or N-terminal cysteine, in one case55, 56) residues on small proteins.57, 58 Well-known examples include diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1 (DGAT1), which acylates diacylglycerols to yield triglycerides, molecules that store metabolic energy;59 and ghrelin O-acyltransferase (GOAT), which octanoylates ghrelin, a hormone that regulates appetite.60, 61 Due to their importance in human physiology and the possibility that some are targets for therapeutics, the MBOAT family has been intensively studied, and structures of several different MBOATs have been reported.62–73 The first of these was of a bacterial MBOAT protein called DltB,62 discussed further below.

The DltB structure, along with later structures of MBOAT proteins from humans and other animals, revealed a core fold for MBOATs. As put forward by Coupland, Ansell and coworkers,58 this core fold consists of eight transmembrane (TM) helices surrounding a central membrane-spanning tunnel, with helix-containing re-entrant loops between the third and fourth and between the fifth and sixth TM helices (Fig. 2A). At the non-cytosolic end of the sixth TM helix is a highly conserved histidine residue that is critical for acyltransferase activity.60, 62, 68, 73–83 The second re-entrant loop, RL2, contains a conserved Asn/Asp/His residue (usually His in bacterial MBOATs) that is generally thought to play a role in substrate thioester binding but is not strictly required for activity in all cases.62, 63, 68, 77–79 Other notable residues include a Trp/Tyr in RL2 that has been implicated in tunnel gating in some MBOATs,62, 70, 71, 84 and various conserved residues lining the central tunnel of the protein that contact the pantetheine arm of the acyl donor (Fig. 2B & 2C).

From these structures, as well as mechanistic work, a consensus has formed for how eukaryotic MBOATs work: an acyl-CoA donor binds to the MBOAT protein on its cytosolic side, with the acylated pantetheine group extending into the protein’s central tunnel. The conserved histidine residue on core TM helix 6 then acts as a catalytic base to activate the nucleophilic hydroxyl group of the lipid or small protein recipient to promote cross-membrane acyl transfer, though roles for the histidine in substrate positioning or covalent catalysis cannot be ruled out.57, 58, 70–72, 85

Despite clear similarities in catalytic machinery, the model for how MBOAT proteins function in eukaryotes does not straightforwardly map onto bacterial MBOATs. Bacterial MBOATs are typically involved in the modification of polymers found on the bacterial cell surface53, 83, 86–91 rather than lipids embedded in the membrane or small secretory proteins that can fit into the MBOAT active site. Moreover, bacterial MBOATs come paired with an SGNH transferase encoded alongside the MBOAT in an operon; both proteins are required for acylation to occur (and in some cases, additional proteins are required as well).82, 83, 86–95 Recently, it has become clear that bacterial MBOATs collaborate with SGNH proteins to effect acylation of extracellular polymers in a process involving two covalent intermediates.51, 52 Below we will describe how this general strategy is used and adapted in the context of several important cell envelope polymer acylation pathways.

We reasoned that if the mechanism we found for the Dlt pathway was general, then other polymer acylation pathways that use MBOAT/SGNH protein pairs should also have an acyl shuttle motif somewhere. Indeed, alignment of over 14,000 distinct bacterial MBOATs revealed a highly conserved six-amino acid acyl shuttle motif (FxYxxF) fused to their C-termini. Accordingly, we proposed that these pathways follow the same general mechanism as the Dlt pathway, transferring a thiol-linked acetyl group to the tyrosine in an acyl shuttle motif and then to the serine hydroxyl of an SGNH protein, which catalyzes the final transfer.51 The MBOATs in these other bacterial acylation pathways are essentially DltB-DltX fusions, albeit with an additional TM helix to meet the topological demands of placing the acyl shuttle motif on the same side of the membrane as the SGNH transferase active site.

Peptidoglycan O-acetylation in Gram-negative bacteria.Peptidoglycan is an essential bacterial cell envelope structure located outside the cytoplasmic membrane that is composed of linear GlcNAc-MurNAc strands crosslinked by oligopeptides.136 In Gram-negative bacteria, the MBOAT/SGNH protein pair PatA/PatB is responsible for acetylating peptidoglycan on the C6-OH of the MurNAc sugars.83, 88, 137 O-acetylation at this position is a cell wall modification found in both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, although it is carried out by different classes of cross-membrane acyltransferases in these two groups of bacteria;138 how Gram-positive bacteria perform peptidoglycan acetylation is discussed later in this review. PatA and PatB heavily O-acetylate peptidoglycan in the Gram-negative pathogens Neisseria gonorrhoeae,83, 139, 140
Campylobacter jejuni,88, 141 and Helicobacter pylori.88, 142 This modification regulates peptidoglycan cleavage by endogenous lytic transglycosylases that act through a mechanism requiring a free C6-OH group on MurNAc,143–145 and cleavage by these lytic transglycosylases in turn regulates cell growth and division.146, 147 There is also evidence that MurNAc acetylation can impact Gram-negative bacterial infections—deleting either patA or patB significantly limits the ability of C. jejuni to colonize the gut in an animal model148—and may modulate innate immune responses by affecting release of peptidoglycan fragments.83, 142, 149, 150PatA is an MBOAT while PatB is an SGNH transferase. As mentioned in the previous section of this review, PatB was shown many years ago to transfer acetyl groups to peptidoglycan in vitro in a mechanism that involves a covalent intermediate;127–129 however, it was unclear what PatA’s role in the pathway was. Our discovery that PatA contains a C-terminal hexapeptide that strongly resembles the C-terminal acyl shuttle motif found in DltX strongly suggested that PatA uses that hexapeptide to form a covalent acetyl-tyrosine intermediate, which is then used as a substrate by PatB.51 As part of a collaboration with Clarke and coworkers, who first characterized PatB, we reconstituted the activity of C. jejuni PatA.52 We found that PatA uses the thermodynamically activated species acetyl-CoA to acetylate the tyrosine in its C-terminal motif. Autoacetylation is dependent on the conserved active site histidine of PatA. Notably, we were able to observe the acyl-tyrosine intermediate in PatA directly, which had not been possible for the more labile DltX acyl-tyrosine intermediate.Reconstitution of acetyl group transfer from PatA to PatB was unsuccessful, but Clarke and coworkers demonstrated that PatB can use a synthetic acyl shuttle motif hexapeptide bearing acetyl-tyrosine as a donor for acetylation of a peptidoglycan mimic.52 Furthermore, AlphaFold modeling predicts an interaction between PatA and PatB, with the periplasmic domain of PatB sitting atop PatA in a manner that leaves abundant space for the necessary flip-flop movement of PatA’s C-terminal peptide from its active site to PatB’s active site.51, 52 Overall, the study on the PatA/PatB system is consistent with the model proposed for the Dlt pathway: PatA transfers acetyl groups from acetyl-CoA to PatB via a covalent acetyl-tyrosine found on a flexible C-terminal peptide that can swing up and down between active sites (Fig. 3C–E).

The middle sugar, a GlcNAc, of this terminal trisaccharide is acetylated at the C3-OH position, a modification believed to play a role in proper S-layer protein deposition and, indirectly, in proper cell division.91, 154–157 An MBOAT-SGNH pair—PatA1 and PatB1—is implicated in the installation of this acetyl group (Fig. 4A).91, 92
B. cereus PatB1, the SGNH hydrolase-like protein of the pair, has been biochemically characterized in vitro as an acetyltransferase: using p-nitrophenyl acetate as an acetyl donor, Clarke and coworkers demonstrated acetyl transfer to short GlcNAc-containing oligosaccharides as well as to a chemically synthesized SCWP terminal trisaccharide.92 The specific activity for transfer to the synthesized terminal unit was higher than that for any of the other tested oligosaccharide species, suggesting this is the native substrate of PatB1. Given that PatA1, like PatA in the Gram-negative peptidoglycan O-acetylation system, contains a C-terminal acyl shuttle motif like that in DltX, and that AlphaFold confidently predicts an interaction between PatA1 and PatB1,51 it is likely that this pair operates analogously to the Dlt and PatAB pathway enzymes (Fig. 3E). We note that in many species there is another MBOAT/SGNH pair encoded by genes directly adjacent to the genes encoding PatA1 and PatB1.158 This pair, PatA2 and PatB2, have also been implicated in assembly of the S-layer exoskeleton,91 but their functions are otherwise unknown.92

Alginate O-acetylation in Pseudomonas species and other Gram-negative bacteria.The pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as well as related species of Pseudomonas and some species of Azotobacter, produces an exopolysaccharide called alginate159–161 that is an important component of the hard-to-manage biofilms produced in cystic fibrosis-associated lung infections.162–166 Alginate is made up of mannuronate and guluronate substituents, the latter of which are generated by epimerization of mannuronate units post-polymerization.159–161 The mannuronate units are often O-acetylated at the C2-OH or C3-OH positions,167, 168 and this acetylation results in more robust biofilm formation as well as resistance to clearing by immune cells.169–172 As in the previously discussed pathways, alginate O-acetylation is mediated by an MBOAT/SGNH pair, here known as AlgI and AlgJ.86 However, two other proteins, AlgX and AlgF, are also required for acetylation.93, 94 Both AlgX and AlgF are soluble periplasmic proteins.173, 174 AlgX is an SGNH protein that binds to the outer membrane lipoprotein AlgK, which along with the membrane porin AlgE has been implicated in alginate export.175–178 AlgF appears to have a non-enzymatic function.179The current model for alginate acetylation is that the MBOAT protein AlgI is acetylated on its C-terminal acyl shuttle motif, which offloads the acetyl group onto the SGNH protein AlgJ.51 AlgJ somehow transfers its acetyl group to the active site of AlgX in a process that involves AlgF.131, 179 AlgX then completes the transfer of the acetyl group to the alginate polymer as it is being fed through the outer membrane for secretion (Fig. 4B).94, 131, 175 This full model has yet to be demonstrated experimentally, but the AlgI:AlgJ complex is confidently predicted by AlphaFold51, 179 and supported by experiments, including co-immunoprecipitation studies.179, 180 Furthermore, AlgF seems to bind both AlgX and AlgJ based on in vitro protein-protein binding studies and co-immunoprecipitation, though a confident structural model was predicted by AlphaFold only for AlgF:AlgX but not AlgF:AlgJ.179, 180 Finally, AlgX can transfer acetyl groups to alginate in vitro while AlgJ cannot transfer acetyl to nor bind to alginate in similar experiments.131 Overall, the models for how the initial and final acetyl transfer steps work are The pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as well as related species of Pseudomonas and some species of Azotobacter, produces an exopolysaccharide called alginate159–161 that is an important component of the hard-to-manage biofilms produced in cystic fibrosis-associated lung infections.162–166 Alginate is made up of mannuronate and guluronate substituents, the latter of which are generated by epimerization of mannuronate units post-polymerization.159–161 The mannuronate units are often O-acetylated at the C2-OH or C3-OH positions,167, 168 and this acetylation results in more robust biofilm formation as well as resistance to clearing by immune cells.169–172 As in the previously discussed pathways, alginate O-acetylation is mediated by an MBOAT/SGNH pair, here known as AlgI and AlgJ.86 However, two other proteins, AlgX and AlgF, are also required for acetylation.93, 94 Both AlgX and AlgF are soluble periplasmic proteins.173, 174 AlgX is an SGNH protein that binds to the outer membrane lipoprotein AlgK, which along with the membrane porin AlgE has been implicated in alginate export.175–178 AlgF appears to have a non-enzymatic function.179

The current model for alginate acetylation is that the MBOAT protein AlgI is acetylated on its C-terminal acyl shuttle motif, which offloads the acetyl group onto the SGNH protein AlgJ.51 AlgJ somehow transfers its acetyl group to the active site of AlgX in a process that involves AlgF.131, 179 AlgX then completes the transfer of the acetyl group to the alginate polymer as it is being fed through the outer membrane for secretion (Fig. 4B).94, 131, 175 This full model has yet to be demonstrated experimentally, but the AlgI:AlgJ complex is confidently predicted by AlphaFold51, 179 and supported by experiments, including co-immunoprecipitation studies.179, 180 Furthermore, AlgF seems to bind both AlgX and AlgJ based on in vitro protein-protein binding studies and co-immunoprecipitation, though a confident structural model was predicted by AlphaFold only for AlgF:AlgX but not AlgF:AlgJ.179, 180 Finally, AlgX can transfer acetyl groups to alginate in vitro while AlgJ cannot transfer acetyl to nor bind to alginate in similar experiments.131 Overall, the models for how the initial and final acetyl transfer steps work are plausible, while the major remaining question is how the putative AlgJ to AlgX handoff occurs.

Concluding thoughts on the bacterial MBOAT systems.The overwhelming majority of bacterial MBOATs are found in operons with SGNH proteins and either contain an acyl shuttle motif at their C-terminus or cluster with a small protein that contains an acyl shuttle motif.48, 51, 192 These observations suggest that all these MBOATs catalyze transfer of an acyl group to the invariant tyrosine in the acyl shuttle motif, which delivers the acyl group to the SGNH transferase, which catalyzes a final transfer step (Fig. 3E). If so, all these systems likely acylate cell surface polymers that either are comprised of sugars (e.g., peptidoglycan, alginate, cellulose) or are made of subunits derived from carbohydrates (e.g., Type I lipoteichoic acids, which are assembled using the headgroups of phosphatidylglycerol37, 96). For many of these systems, however, the polymer recipients are unknown. For example, some Gram-negative bacteria193–195 like Bordetella pertussis and some Actinobacteria51 like Eggerthella lenta contain dlt-like operons, but these organisms do not make teichoic acids; furthermore, some archaea even encode MBOAT/SGNH pairs.51 These pathways are presumed to modify polymers whose identities are unknown but could be determined by constructing deletion strains and comparing cell envelope fractions or purified components using analytical techniques like NMR.21, 196 Also unknown—even for cases where the recipients have been identified—is how the polymers are recognized and held so that acylation takes place. While in many systems the SGNH protein appears to be sufficient to bind to the polymer substrate to the degree required for acyl transfer in vitro, it is often unclear how polymer binding occurs—though we note that for AlgX, a charged groove extending from the active site to a C-terminal carbohydrate-binding module has been implicated in alginate binding,94, 131 and the PatAB complex has been suggested to bind to peptidoglycan oligomers at the interface between the two proteins.52 Finally, how the more extensive acylation pathways that modify exported polymers in Gram-negative bacteria function remains an open question.179, 184We note in closing that some MBOAT/SGNH systems are proposed targets for therapeutic agents.92, 127, 197 The high conservation of the acyl shuttle motif across organisms and across systems that acylate very different recipients suggests the possibility of making inhibitor scaffolds that can serve as general starting points for optimization. So far, there has been little work on inhibitor discovery for MBOAT/SGNH systems, although we note that we have discovered several biologically active DltB inhibitors in a phenotypic screen,104, 198 including one that was used in a recent cryo-electron microscopy structure of a DltB:inhibitor co-complex.199 Resistance mutations in DltB have confirmed on-target activity of this inhibitor in cells. Additionally, Clarke and coworkers identified an inhibitor of Neisseria gonorrhoeae’s PatB in an in vitro screen and showed that this inhibitor reduces growth of the organism,200 and Weadge and coworkers demonstrated that a repurposed tyrosine kinase inhibitor, Tyrphostin AG 112, inhibits acetylesterase activity of WssI from Achromobacter insuavis in vitro.184 While more work should be done to study the effects of MBOAT/SGNH pathways on bacterial pathogenesis/virulence, we anticipate that recent progress in understanding these systems’ mechanisms will spur further efforts to develop inhibitors. Such inhibitors are likely to be (or can be designed/engineered to be201, 202) selective for bacterial MBOAT and SGNH proteins over their human counterparts due to low sequence similarity and distinct substrate specificity.

The first TM helix of the AT3 houses a highly conserved R/K-x10-H motif, with the Arg/Lys residue near the cytosolic end of the helix and the His residue near the extracytoplasmic end (Fig. 5C).42, 50, 215 In all AT3 proteins where its importance has been examined, this histidine was required for activity;42, 50, 215, 216 like the highly conserved histidine found in the active site of MBOAT proteins, this histidine is proposed to act as a catalytic base.50 The Arg/Lys residue at the cytosolic end of the helix is proposed to play a role in binding to the phosphates of acyl-CoA donor molecules,42, 50, 214 with the Arg/Lys–to–His distance being approximately the length of a phosphopantetheine arm, as noted by others.42, 50 There is also an RxxR motif on the cytosolic end of the third TM helix,204 and it is thought that one or both of these residues also helps anchor the acyl-CoA phosphates.50, 214 Supporting this proposed role, substitution of the conserved basic residues on TM helices 1 and 3 with alanine resulted in loss of activity in several AT3 proteins.42, 50, 204, 215, 217, 218 Other conserved residues line the predicted central tunnel of the AT3 domain, and some of these may also be involved in substrate binding.42, 50, 204, 215, 219 Among these other residues are a well-conserved histidine in a YxxH motif located on TM helix 9. However, there are still some questions about the mechanisms of AT3-SGNH proteins. Unlike in MBOAT/SGNH systems where the acyl shuttle motif is easily recognized either on the MBOAT itself or on a separate small protein, there is no conserved acyl shuttle motif in AT3-SGNH proteins. In vivo mutagenesis studies on the AT3 standalone lipopolysaccharide O-acetyltransferases OacA and OacB from Shigella flexneri have shown that arginine residues in the R/K-x10-H and RxxR motifs are required for activity,215, 217 as in AT3-SGNH fusions. A more rigorous bioinformatic analysis will be required to assess this so as to guide future mechanistic biochemistry studies.

Bacterial AT3-SGNH fusion proteins.

AT3-SGNH fusion proteins consist of an N-terminal AT3 domain followed by an additional TM helix and then a C-terminal extracytoplasmic SGNH hydrolase-like domain that is required for activity.42, 50, 218, 220 Examples include OatA (Fig. 5), which O-acetylates the C6-OH group of MurNAc sugars in the peptidoglycan cell wall of Gram-positive bacteria to provide lysozyme resistance;221 various Oaf proteins, which O-acetylate sugars in the lipopolysaccharide/lipooligosaccharide layer of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria to protect against phage;204, 218 and SucT, which O-succinylates arabinose sugars in mycobacterial cell envelope arabinogalactan and lipoarabinomannan polymers to regulate other modifications and boost antibiotic resistance.222–224 Of these, only OatA from S. aureus has been biochemically characterized in its full-length form. That work, by Clarke and coworkers, serves as the paradigm for our mechanistic understanding of AT3-SGNH fusions.50

Peptidoglycan acetylation by OatA.Clarke and coworkers reported the in vitro reconstitution of OatA. They showed that OatA uses acetyl-CoA to acetylate linear peptidoglycan fragments and peptidoglycan mimics, with acetylation dependent on the conserved histidine and arginine residues in TM helix 1 and on the second Arg in the RxxR motif at the base of TM helix 3. These studies supported the importance of these residues in substrate binding and turnover. The active site residues of the SGNH domain were also required for acetyl transfer. In the most important breakthrough for understanding how these AT3-SGNH fusion proteins work, Clarke and coworkers showed that when the active site serine in the SGNH domain was substituted with an alanine, a covalent acetyl-protein intermediate accumulated in the reaction; they identified the site of acetyl modification as Tyr206 in the AT3 domain. Based on this, the authors proposed a model involving acetyl transfer from acetyl-CoA to Tyr206 and then to the SGNH domain active site serine before final transfer to peptidoglycan (Fig. 6A). This chemical mechanism involving an acyl-tyrosine intermediate is conceptually similar to the mechanism described for bacterial MBOATs in which a tyrosine-containing acyl shuttle motif forms a covalent intermediate to ferry the acyl group from a thioester donor to the active site of an SGNH protein.

Bacterial standalone AT3 proteins.Despite being far more common,49 little is known about standalone AT3 proteins compared to their AT3-SGNH fusion counterparts other than that they too carry out polysaccharide acylation, with an important example being IcaC-mediated succinylation of poly-N-acetyl-glucosamine (PNAG) in S. aureus (Fig. 7).229
In vivo mutagenesis studies on the AT3 standalone lipopolysaccharide O-acetyltransferases OacA and OacB from Shigella flexneri have shown that arginine residues in the R/K-x10-H and RxxR motifs are required for activity,215, 217 as in AT3-SGNH fusions. Moreover, the presumed catalytic histidine on TM helix 1 was also identified as necessary for the function of S. flexneri OacB215 and the Streptococcus pneumoniae capsular polysaccharide O-acetyltransferase WcjE216 (this residue was not examined in studies on S. flexneri OacA). Substrate feeding studies using isolated membranes or cell-free extracts,230–232 combined with the conservation and functional importance of residues lining the predicted donor binding tunnel,215, 219, 233 support the idea that the standalone AT3s, like AT3-SGNH fusions, use acyl-CoA molecules as donors for their chemistry. Beyond these findings, how standalone AT3s work is unknown.Distinct from AT3-SGNH acyltransferases, no partner transferases have so far been identified for any standalone AT3 proteins. AT3 standalones remain functional when heterologously expressed211, 231, 234, 235 or when transferred laterally between organisms by phage,208, 236, 237 suggesting that they do not require other proteins to effect acyl transfer. Although some AT3 standalone proteins act on glycosylated small molecules or lipids,203 others transfer acyl groups to sugars that are part of large glycans like LPS or poly-N-acetyl-glucosamine. It is unclear how these small integral membrane proteins can selectively bind such large recipients to catalyze specific acyl transfer reactions. Also unclear is how these standalone AT3 proteins operate from a chemical perspective. The tyrosines identified as important for acyl transfer in OatA are not conserved in standalone AT3 proteins. It is possible that these standalone proteins use a different chemical mechanism from AT3-SGNH fusions despite sharing the same AT3 domain fold and the use of acyl-CoA as a donor substrate. As already mentioned, sequence alignments for standalone AT3 proteins suggest that among the aligned sequences are a substantial fraction of proteins that lack residues proposed to be important for catalysis. A more rigorous bioinformatic analysis will be required to assess this so as to guide future mechanistic biochemistry studies.

EUKARYOTIC CROSS-MEMBRANE ACYLTRANSFERASESIn eukaryotes, MBOAT proteins are widely distributed but are relegated primarily to modifications of lipids and proteins, as described in a previous section of this review. AT3 domain-containing proteins are found in fungi as well as metazoans but have only been studied in Caenorhabditis elegans and Drosophila melanogaster; their roles in these model organisms are not yet well-defined.238–241 Humans do not have a canonical AT3 domain-containing protein, although other mammals do. It is thought that the AT3-encoding gene found in most mammals degenerated to a pseudogene in humans at some point after the ancestors of gorillas and humans diverged evolutionarily.242 Humans do have an enzyme with an AT3-like domain: the sialic acid 9-O-acetyltransferase CASD1, a member of the Cas1p-type 10-TM acyltransferase family that is found across animals, plants, and fungi.132, 243, 244 In 2010, Anantharaman and Aravind proposed that the Cas1p 10-TM domain should be united with the canonical AT3 family based on sequence conservation analysis.245 Among the residues shared by the Cas1p-10-TM acyltransferases and AT3 families are the Arg/Lys and His residues found near the N- and C-termini of the first core fold TM helix, respectively, and an Arg near the cytosolic end of core TM helix 3 (Fig. 8A). Below, we briefly discuss the state of knowledge on CASD1, the “RWA” plant proteins of the same family that are involved in cell wall glycopolymer acetylation, and a non-AT3 class of TM acyltransferases with one well-characterized member found in humans.Capsule structure 1 domain proteins.In 2008, Janbon and coworkers determined the Cryptococcus neoformans Cas1p protein to be an enzyme that O-acetylates the fungal capsular polysaccharide glucuronoxylomannan.243 They also identified a homolog (CASD1) encoded in the human genome, which would later be found to be responsible for 9-O-acetylation of sialic acids132, 244 (Fig. 8B)—important “capping” residues in the cell surface glycans of vertebrates and other animals that regulate cell-cell interactions.246 The acetyl modification is required for cell survival and drug resistance in acute lymphoblastic leukemia cells,247, 248 and CASD1 has been proposed as an anti-cancer therapeutic target. Proteins like Cas1p and CASD1 have a single N-terminal TM helix followed by a large extracytoplasmic SGNH hydrolase-like domain and then a C-terminal AT3-like domain embedded in some additional TM helices (Fig. 8C). These proteins are thought to fulfill their roles in glycan acetylation prior to the glycans’ arrival at the cell surface,38, 47 with CASD1’s likely localization being in the Golgi membrane.132Unlike bacteria, which typically have no membrane-enclosed organelles, acetyl-CoA is found extracytoplasmically in many eukaryotes. In animals, this includes in the Golgi lumen thanks to the action of the SLC33A1 transporter,249, 250 close homologs of which are found in fungi but notably not plants.251 Given this, it is not too surprising that in vitro reconstitution of the CASD1 SGNH hydrolase-like domain revealed that this domain can use acetyl-CoA as the donor for acetylation of CMP-N-acetylneuraminic acid (CMP-sialate; Fig. 8B),132 the sugar donor for sialyltransferases.252 But if the protein’s key activity does not require the use of acetyl-CoA directly from the cytosol, what role does the AT3-like domain play? As the authors of the CASD1 study put it, “the situation may be more complex in vivo.” The effects of altering conserved AT3-like domain residues such as the histidine in TM helix 1 should be investigated. If CASD1 can use acetyl-CoA directly from the cytosol, as originally proposed by Varki and coworkers long before the gene encoding this protein had been identified,47 it is not clear from AlphaFold modeling how the acetyl group can reach the SGNH domain. If the AT3-like domain does not collaborate with the SGNH domain to effect acyl transfer to a single recipient, it may acylate another recipient (Fig. 8D).The reduced wall acetylation (RWA) protein family in plants.The same study that reported Cas1p to be a glycopolymer acetyltransferase also identified four Cas1p homologs in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana.243 Mutant plants with a quadruple deletion of the genes encoding all four Cas1p homologs,253, 254 or even a single deletion of one of the genes—RWA2,255 displayed significantly reduced O-acetylation of a number of plant cell wall glycopolymers, including hemicelluloses like xyloglucan and pectic polysaccharides like homogalacturonan (Fig. 8E). Now known as Reduced Wall Acetylation (RWA) proteins, these putative acetyltransferases consist only of an AT3-like polytopic TM domain with no fused SGNH domain (Fig. 8F). However, these proteins are thought to act alongside another family of proteins, the trichome birefringence-like (TBL) family, members of which typically have a single-pass TM helix followed by a long, likely unstructured linker and then an SGNH domain that is predicted to be in the Golgi lumen where most plant cell wall polysaccharide biosynthesis occurs.39, 256–258 While the evidence (comprehensively reviewed elsewhere259, 260) implicating RWA and TBL proteins in plant cell wall glycopolymer O-acetylation is strong and includes several examples of in vitro reconstitution of TBL protein glycopolymer acetyltransferase activity,130, 261 a complete model for how these two families work together to carry out O-acetylation has yet to emerge.Currently, two competing theories for how these proteins work can be envisioned (Fig. 8G). In one, RWA proteins transfer acetyl groups from cytosolic acetyl-CoA to some acceptor on the lumenal side of the Golgi membrane for use by TBL proteins, perhaps in concert with other partner proteins as well.259, 262 In the other, RWA proteins transport acetyl-CoA molecules into the Golgi lumen for use in direct glycopolymer acetylation by TBL proteins.263 Given the homology to canonical AT3 family proteins, the former theory is attractive; it is conceivable that a tyrosine acts as an acetyl intermediate in the pathway. However, much work remains to be done in this system to clarify the mechanism, including experimental interrogation of conserved residues such as the putative catalytic histidine. Additionally, efforts should be made to determine whether RWA and TBL proteins interact with each other (or any other proteins), as this will greatly inform the overall model.Heparan-α-glucosaminide N-acetyltransferase (HGSNAT)-like family proteins.Another class of cross-membrane acyltransferases involved in glycopolymer modification is the HGSNAT-like family of proteins. The majority of these proteins are found in bacteria—including the model organism Bacillus subtilis and human pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii.264 It is to date unknown what bacterial HGSNAT-like proteins might do, but the prototypical example of the overall family is human HGSNAT itself, which is responsible for N-acetylation of heparan sulfate glucosamine residues in the lysosomal lumen.44–46, 265 This acetylation allows the polymer—normally found at the cell surface—to be degraded and thus recycled, and dysfunctional variants of this enzyme are responsible for mucopolysaccharidosis IIIC/Sanfilippo syndrome type C.266, 267Recently, three papers reported cryo-electron microscopy structures of HGSNAT dimers in various substrate-bound states, revealing a model for how acetylation occurs (Fig. 9A).268–270 In this model, cytosolic acetyl-CoA binds in the central cavity of the polytopic TM portion of the protein, with the substrate’s phosphates making contacts with conserved basic residues. Then, a conserved histidine catalyzes acetyl group transfer to glucosamine by acting as a catalytic base to deprotonate the sugar’s free amine, allowing it to attack the acetyl-CoA thioester (Fig. 9B). A conserved asparagine assists in stabilizing the thioester oxygen, reminiscent of the proposed role for the conserved Asn/His/Asp of MBOATs. One of the three groups who obtained a HGSNAT structure also implicated a conserved aspartic acid residue in lowering the pKa of the catalytic histidine for more effective deprotonation of the glucosamine.268 These structures, combined with in vitro reconstitution experiments performed using purified HGSNAT, provided strong evidence for direct acyl group transfer to the polymer, differentiating this system from bacterial AT3-SGNH fusions and MBOAT-SGNH pairs while providing a template for thinking about possible direct transfer by AT3 standalone proteins. However, it is important to note that unlike typical AT3 standalones, HGSNAT has an additional non-enzymatic extracytoplasmic domain at its N-terminus with a β-sandwich fold that is predicted to bind to the heparan sulfate chain.268Recently, it was proposed that HGSNAT and AT3 proteins be grouped together as part of one superfamily—the “Transmembrane Acyl Transferase” (TmAT) superfamily.271 After the initial submission of this Review, the merit of this proposed grouping was reinforced by a rigorous sequence- and structure-based computational analysis of HGSNAT-like proteins, AT3 proteins, and several other related families.272 This analysis, conducted by Thomas and coworkers, established a conserved TmAT protein fold with a shared acyl-CoA binding site. Notably, however, the locations of proposed catalytic histidine residues do not appear to be shared between HGSNAT and AT3 proteins. Additionally, there is at least one subgroup of TmAT proteins that is not thought to rely on a histidine at all for catalysis of acyl transfer—the PIGW/GWT1 family. These proteins, which have recently been structurally characterized for the first time, act as glycophosphatidylinositol (GPI) acyltransferases and may use an aspartate as a catalytic base.273 Appreciation for the apparent diversity in catalytic residue location and identity amongst proteins of the TmAT superfamily will be important for future mechanistic interrogation.

Capsule structure 1 domain proteins.

In 2008, Janbon and coworkers determined the Cryptococcus neoformans Cas1p protein to be an enzyme that O-acetylates the fungal capsular polysaccharide glucuronoxylomannan.243 They also identified a homolog (CASD1) encoded in the human genome, which would later be found to be responsible for 9-O-acetylation of sialic acids132, 244 (Fig. 8B)—important “capping” residues in the cell surface glycans of vertebrates and other animals that regulate cell-cell interactions.246 The acetyl modification is required for cell survival and drug resistance in acute lymphoblastic leukemia cells,247, 248 and CASD1 has been proposed as an anti-cancer therapeutic target. Proteins like Cas1p and CASD1 have a single N-terminal TM helix followed by a large extracytoplasmic SGNH hydrolase-like domain and then a C-terminal AT3-like domain embedded in some additional TM helices (Fig. 8C). These proteins are thought to fulfill their roles in glycan acetylation prior to the glycans’ arrival at the cell surface,38, 47 with CASD1’s likely localization being in the Golgi membrane.132

Unlike bacteria, which typically have no membrane-enclosed organelles, acetyl-CoA is found extracytoplasmically in many eukaryotes. In animals, this includes in the Golgi lumen thanks to the action of the SLC33A1 transporter,249, 250 close homologs of which are found in fungi but notably not plants.251 Given this, it is not too surprising that in vitro reconstitution of the CASD1 SGNH hydrolase-like domain revealed that this domain can use acetyl-CoA as the donor for acetylation of CMP-N-acetylneuraminic acid (CMP-sialate; Fig. 8B),132 the sugar donor for sialyltransferases.252 But if the protein’s key activity does not require the use of acetyl-CoA directly from the cytosol, what role does the AT3-like domain play? As the authors of the CASD1 study put it, “the situation may be more complex in vivo.” The effects of altering conserved AT3-like domain residues such as the histidine in TM helix 1 should be investigated. If CASD1 can use acetyl-CoA directly from the cytosol, as originally proposed by Varki and coworkers long before the gene encoding this protein had been identified,47 it is not clear from AlphaFold modeling how the acetyl group can reach the SGNH domain. If the AT3-like domain does not collaborate with the SGNH domain to effect acyl transfer to a single recipient, it may acylate another recipient (Fig. 8D).

The reduced wall acetylation (RWA) protein family in plants.The same study that reported Cas1p to be a glycopolymer acetyltransferase also identified four Cas1p homologs in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana.243 Mutant plants with a quadruple deletion of the genes encoding all four Cas1p homologs,253, 254 or even a single deletion of one of the genes—RWA2,255 displayed significantly reduced O-acetylation of a number of plant cell wall glycopolymers, including hemicelluloses like xyloglucan and pectic polysaccharides like homogalacturonan (Fig. 8E). Now known as Reduced Wall Acetylation (RWA) proteins, these putative acetyltransferases consist only of an AT3-like polytopic TM domain with no fused SGNH domain (Fig. 8F). However, these proteins are thought to act alongside another family of proteins, the trichome birefringence-like (TBL) family, members of which typically have a single-pass TM helix followed by a long, likely unstructured linker and then an SGNH domain that is predicted to be in the Golgi lumen where most plant cell wall polysaccharide biosynthesis occurs.39, 256–258 While the evidence (comprehensively reviewed elsewhere259, 260) implicating RWA and TBL proteins in plant cell wall glycopolymer O-acetylation is strong and includes several examples of in vitro reconstitution of TBL protein glycopolymer acetyltransferase activity,130, 261 a complete model for how these two families work together to carry out O-acetylation has yet to emerge.Currently, two competing theories for how these proteins work can be envisioned (Fig. 8G). In one, RWA proteins transfer acetyl groups from cytosolic acetyl-CoA to some acceptor on the lumenal side of the Golgi membrane for use by TBL proteins, perhaps in concert with other partner proteins as well.259, 262 In the other, RWA proteins transport acetyl-CoA molecules into the Golgi lumen for use in direct glycopolymer acetylation by TBL proteins.263 Given the homology to canonical AT3 family proteins, the former theory is attractive; it is conceivable that a tyrosine acts as an acetyl intermediate in the pathway. However, much work remains to be done in this system to clarify the mechanism, including experimental interrogation of conserved residues such as the putative catalytic histidine. Additionally, efforts should be made to determine whether RWA and TBL proteins interact with each other (or any other proteins), as this will greatly inform the overall model.

Heparan-α-glucosaminide N-acetyltransferase (HGSNAT)-like family proteins.Another class of cross-membrane acyltransferases involved in glycopolymer modification is the HGSNAT-like family of proteins. The majority of these proteins are found in bacteria—including the model organism Bacillus subtilis and human pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii.264 It is to date unknown what bacterial HGSNAT-like proteins might do, but the prototypical example of the overall family is human HGSNAT itself, which is responsible for N-acetylation of heparan sulfate glucosamine residues in the lysosomal lumen.44–46, 265 This acetylation allows the polymer—normally found at the cell surface—to be degraded and thus recycled, and dysfunctional variants of this enzyme are responsible for mucopolysaccharidosis IIIC/Sanfilippo syndrome type C.266, 267Recently, three papers reported cryo-electron microscopy structures of HGSNAT dimers in various substrate-bound states, revealing a model for how acetylation occurs (Fig. 9A).268–270 In this model, cytosolic acetyl-CoA binds in the central cavity of the polytopic TM portion of the protein, with the substrate’s phosphates making contacts with conserved basic residues. Then, a conserved histidine catalyzes acetyl group transfer to glucosamine by acting as a catalytic base to deprotonate the sugar’s free amine, allowing it to attack the acetyl-CoA thioester (Fig. 9B). A conserved asparagine assists in stabilizing the thioester oxygen, reminiscent of the proposed role for the conserved Asn/His/Asp of MBOATs. One of the three groups who obtained a HGSNAT structure also implicated a conserved aspartic acid residue in lowering the pKa of the catalytic histidine for more effective deprotonation of the glucosamine.268 These structures, combined with in vitro reconstitution experiments performed using purified HGSNAT, provided strong evidence for direct acyl group transfer to the polymer, differentiating this system from bacterial AT3-SGNH fusions and MBOAT-SGNH pairs while providing a template for thinking about possible direct transfer by AT3 standalone proteins. However, it is important to note that unlike typical AT3 standalones, HGSNAT has an additional non-enzymatic extracytoplasmic domain at its N-terminus with a β-sandwich fold that is predicted to bind to the heparan sulfate chain.268Recently, it was proposed that HGSNAT and AT3 proteins be grouped together as part of one superfamily—the “Transmembrane Acyl Transferase” (TmAT) superfamily.271 After the initial submission of this Review, the merit of this proposed grouping was reinforced by a rigorous sequence- and structure-based computational analysis of HGSNAT-like proteins, AT3 proteins, and several other related families.272 This analysis, conducted by Thomas and coworkers, established a conserved TmAT protein fold with a shared acyl-CoA binding site. Notably, however, the locations of proposed catalytic histidine residues do not appear to be shared between HGSNAT and AT3 proteins. Additionally, there is at least one subgroup of TmAT proteins that is not thought to rely on a histidine at all for catalysis of acyl transfer—the PIGW/GWT1 family. These proteins, which have recently been structurally characterized for the first time, act as glycophosphatidylinositol (GPI) acyltransferases and may use an aspartate as a catalytic base.273 Appreciation for the apparent diversity in catalytic residue location and identity amongst proteins of the TmAT superfamily will be important for future mechanistic interrogation.

CONCLUSIONS AND OUTLOOK.Tremendous progress has been made recently in our understanding of cross-membrane acyltransferases involved in the modification of cell surface polymers. Key work on AT3-SGNH fusion proteins and MBOAT/SGNH pathways have highlighted the convergent evolution of tyrosine acylation as an intermediate step in acetyl transfer across membranes.50–52 At the same time, structural characterization of human HGSNAT has revealed that such an intermediate step is not strictly necessary to achieve cross-membrane acylation.268–270 Both multi-step and direct transfer strategies begin with a cytosolic, thermodynamically activated acyl thioester at the end of a phosphopantetheinyl arm. The arm is linked either to a nucleotide, as in acyl-CoA donors, or to a carrier protein. The acyl donors dock to binding sites at the cytosolic side of cross-membrane acyl transferases. The length of a pantetheine arm (~18 Å), a moiety whose origins are likely prebiotic,274, 275 allows it to reach more than halfway through the central tunnel in cross-membrane acyltransferases where it is transferred to a recipient nucleophile that is activated by a catalytic base (usually histidine) near the extracytoplasmic end of this tunnel. In the case of direct transfer systems, the recipient nucleophile is the final destination in the pathway. In the case of the multi-step transfer systems that are common in bacterial pathways that modify extracellular polymers, the first recipient is a tyrosine in the cross-membrane acyltransferase itself or in an associated protein. This tyrosine shuttles the acyl group to an SGNH protein that performs the final transfer step to the polymeric recipient.The side chain hydroxyl of tyrosine has a pKa (~10–10.5) that sits between that of a phosphopantetheine thiol (~9.6–9.8) and a serine hydroxyl (~13–14),276, 277 so the use of tyrosine as the intermediate acyl carrier ensures that each transfer step is energetically favorable. Tyrosines are also used for covalent catalysis by other enzymes, with known examples being in DNA topoisomerases/recombinases,278–281 sialidases,282–284 and a newly explored cyclic glucan synthase.285 Interestingly, there is at least one example from natural product biosynthesis where a tyrosine is proposed to form a covalent intermediate in an acyl transfer pathway.286–290 In this case, a tyrosine in the acyltransferase LnmK is acylated by methylmalonyl CoA; the acyl group is then transferred to an acyl carrier protein for use in the pathway that makes leinamycin.The current pace of new discoveries regarding what cross-membrane glycopolymer acyltransferases do and how they do it is exciting. Recent mechanistic studies have opened up the field, providing paradigms and contrasting examples for how Nature moves acyl groups from the cytosol to extracytoplasmic polymers that play important roles in biology. There are many questions still to be answered, some of which we have drawn attention to here.
