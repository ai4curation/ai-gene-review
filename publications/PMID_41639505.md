Tumorigenesis and progression are driven by dysregulated genes and signaling
pathways, with cellular metabolic reprogramming being a hallmark that supports
neoplastic growth. Alanyl-tRNA Synthetase 1 (AARS1), a key enzyme with dual
roles in metabolism and gene expression regulation, has emerged as a critical
focus in cancer and disease research. As a member of the aminoacyl-tRNA
synthetase (AARS) family, AARS1 canonically catalyzes the attachment of alanine
to its cognate transfer RNA (tRNA) to ensure fidelity in protein synthesis.
Emerging evidence reveals non-canonical roles of AARS1 in tumor biology,
including regulation of metabolic reprogramming, cell proliferation, apoptosis,
and intracellular signal sensing-all of which directly impact tumor growth and
patient prognosis. Beyond cancer, dysregulated AARS1 (via abnormal expression or
pathogenic mutations) is linked to a spectrum of non-malignant disorders,
including Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) disease (a hereditary peripheral
neuropathy), adult-onset leukoencephalopathy, recurrent acute liver failure, and
sulfide dysplasia. These associations arise from disrupted cellular homeostasis
and impaired physiological functions caused by AARS1-mediated pathway
dysregulation. Notably, AARS1's ability to sense lactate and catalyze lysine
lactylation-a newly identified post-translational modification (PTM)-represents
a novel mechanism linking metabolic dysregulation to disease pathogenesis.
Dysregulated AARS1 contributes to tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and
treatment resistance, while its mutations drive the onset of several
neurodegenerative and metabolic disorders. Unraveling the molecular mechanisms
of AARS1, particularly its lactylation-related functions, will deepen our
understanding of cellular metabolism in disease and identify novel therapeutic
targets for precise diagnosis and treatment of tumors and other disorders.
Furthermore, AARS1 holds significant promise as a diagnostic biomarker and
therapeutic target, offering new avenues for precision medicine in both oncology
and non-malignant conditions.

Introduction

Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (AARSs) are a family of evolutionarily conserved enzymes that serve as the “molecular link” between genetic code and protein synthesis. By catalyzing the covalent attachment of specific amino acids to their corresponding transfer RNAs (tRNAs), AARSs ensure the accurate translation of mRNA into functional proteins—an essential process for maintaining cellular homeostasis across all living organisms [1]. Over the past three decades, research has expanded beyond the canonical translational roles of AARSs, uncovering their non-canonical functions in cell signaling, metabolism, and stress response—many of which are dysregulated in disease [2, 3].

Alanyl-tRNA Synthetase 1 (AARS1), a member of the AARS family, has gained increasing attention due to its unique dual roles in protein synthesis and disease pathogenesis. In tumor cells, AARS1 exhibits two pivotal non-canonical functions: acting as an intracellular lactate sensor and catalyzing lysine lactylation—a Post-Translational Modification (PTM) first identified in 2019 that regulates metabolism, gene expression, and cell fate in cancer [4, 5]. This lactylation-mediated mechanism provides a direct link between the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis in tumors) and malignant progression, offering a new lens to understand metabolic reprogramming in cancer [5, 6].

Beyond oncology, AARS1 is implicated in multiple non-malignant disorders. Pathogenic mutations in the AARS1 gene are a well-established cause of Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 2 N (CMT2N), an inherited peripheral neuropathy characterized by progressive motor and sensory dysfunction [7–9]. Additionally, AARS1 mutations have been linked to adult-onset leukoencephalopathy with axonal spheroids and pigmented glia (ALSP), recurrent acute liver failure, and sulfide dysplasia—highlighting its pleiotropic roles in maintaining nervous system and metabolic homeostasis [10–13].

Despite these advances, critical gaps remain in our understanding of AARS1, particularly concerning the precise atomic details of its lactate-sensing ability, the complete repertoire of its lactylation substrates beyond well-studied transcription factors, and the nuanced tissue-specific mechanisms by which AARS1 mutations drive diverse non-cancer diseases. This review comprehensively summarizes the molecular biology of AARS1, its lactylation-mediated functions in tumors, and its role in non-malignant disorders. By integrating recent structural, biochemical, and clinical evidence, we aim to highlight AARS1 as a potential therapeutic target and diagnostic biomarker, while critically assessing current knowledge, identifying existing limitations, and outlining future directions to address these unresolved questions in the field.

This suggests that AARS1 nuclear translocation is a late event in malignant progression.AARS1-mediated lysine lactylation: a link between metabolism and oncogenesisThe Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis) in tumors leads to excessive lactate accumulation (10–20 mM in the tumor microenvironment, vs. 1–2 mM in normal tissues) [4, 5]. AARS1 senses this lactate and catalyzes lysine lactylation—a PTM that regulates protein function by altering charge, structure, or protein-protein interactions [4–6]. Beyond its prominent role in cancer, lysine lactylation has also been observed in non-malignant contexts, including histone modifications mediated by HDAC1, underscoring its widespread biological relevance [20].Mechanism of AARS1-mediated lactylationAARS1’s ability to switch between canonical aminoacylation and non-canonical lactylation is a remarkable example of metabolic plasticity. This shift is mechanistically linked to its aminoacylation domain, which possesses a binding site capable of accommodating both alanine and lactate. The reaction proceeds via two ATP-dependent steps, analogous to its canonical function (Fig. 3):
Lactate Activation and Competitive Binding: In the presence of ATP, AARS1 can bind either alanine or lactate. When lactate levels are low, AARS1 primarily binds alanine, forming alanyl-AMP, which is then transferred to tRNA^Ala for protein synthesis. However, under conditions of high lactate concentration, such as those found in the tumor microenvironment, lactate competes effectively with alanine for binding within the aminoacylation domain [5, 6]. This competitive binding event is crucial: high lactate levels shift the equilibrium towards the formation of a lactate-AMP intermediate. Recent studies have elucidated that high lactate concentrations can induce conformational changes in AARS1, favoring the activation of lactate over alanine, thereby reprogramming AARS1’s enzymatic preference from aminoacylation to lactylation (Li et al., 2024). This metabolic sensing and functional switch is a pivotal mechanism by which AARS1 bridges altered tumor metabolism with downstream signaling.Lactate Transfer: The lactyl moiety from lactate-AMP is covalently attached to the ε-amino group of lysine residues in substrate proteins, generating lactylated proteins and releasing AMP [5, 19]. The precise concentration thresholds and kinetic parameters governing the switch between aminoacylation and lactylation are key areas of ongoing research, but the competition for binding within the aminoacylation domain, influenced by local lactate levels, is understood to be the primary driver of this metabolic plasticity.

Under conditions of high lactate concentration, characteristic of the tumor microenvironment, lactate competes with alanine for binding within the aminoacylation domain of AARS1. This activation step involves the transfer of the adenylate moiety from ATP to lactate, with the concomitant release of pyrophosphate (PPi). The activated lactyl group from the lactate-AMP intermediate is then covalently transferred to the ε-amino group of specific lysine residues on substrate proteins, resulting in the formation of lactylated proteins and the release of AMP.Proteomic studies have identified > 500 lactylation substrates of AARS1 in tumor cells, including transcription factors (p53, YAP), metabolic enzymes (PKM2, LDHA), and DNA repair proteins (MRE11) [5, 6, 18, 19]. These substrates are involved in cell cycle regulation, metabolism, and stress response—key pathways for tumor survival. It is important to acknowledge that proteomic studies, while powerful for initial identification, can sometimes yield false positives. Therefore, rigorous validation of high-confidence lactylation targets using orthogonal assays, such as in vitro lactylation assays with purified recombinant AARS1 and specific peptides or proteins, is crucial. Recent studies have employed such validation strategies for key substrates like lactylations [21], providing stronger evidence for AARS1’s role in modifying these proteins. Lactylation of p53: a canonical oncogenic mechanismThe tumor suppressor p53 is a major AARS1 lactylation substrate. In normal cells, p53 is activated by DNA damage, inducing expression of downstream target genes (e.g., p21, Bax) that trigger cell cycle arrest or apoptosis [5, 6]. In tumors, AARS1 lactylates p53 at two key residues in its DNA-binding domain: K120 and K139 (Fig. 4): (1) Disrupted DNA Binding: Lactylation of K120 (a residue critical for p53-DNA interaction) reduces p53’s affinity for target gene promoters by 4–5-fold [5] (2). Impaired Liquid-Liquid Phase Separation (LLPS): p53 forms nuclear condensates via LLPS to enhance its transcriptional activity. Lactylation of K139 disrupts these condensates, further inhibiting p53 function [5]. In patients with wild-type p53 (≈ 50% of human cancers), AARS1 expression and p53 lactylation levels are inversely correlated with overall survival. For example, in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients with wild-type p53, high AARS1/p53 lactylation is associated with a 2.8-fold higher risk of death compared to low levels [5]. ConclusionAlanyl-tRNA Synthetase 1 (AARS1) has emerged as a multifunctional protein with critical roles in protein synthesis, metabolic sensing, and disease pathogenesis. Its ability to sense lactate and catalyze lysine lactylation links the Warburg effect to tumor progression—offering a novel mechanism for targeting cancer metabolism. In non-malignant disorders, AARS1 mutations drive neuropathy (CMT2N), neurodegeneration (ALSP), and liver failure via distinct mechanisms (e.g., NRP1 dysregulation, ER stress).While significant progress has been made, key challenges remain: resolving the structural basis of AARS1’s lactyltransferase activity, identifying tissue-specific lactylation substrates, and developing selective therapeutics that avoid disrupting canonical protein synthesis. Addressing these gaps will not only deepen our understanding of AARS1’s biology but also unlock its potential as a therapeutic target and biomarker for a spectrum of diseases—from cancer to rare neurological disorders.Future research should integrate structural biology (e.g., cryo-EM of AARS1-lactate complexes), single-cell omics (to map AARS1’s cell-type-specific functions), and clinical trials (to test AARS1 inhibitors in cancer and CMT2N). By bridging basic and translational research, AARS1 could become a cornerstone of precision medicine for diverse disorders.
