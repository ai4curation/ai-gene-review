BACKGROUND: The senses of touch and proprioception evoke a range of perceptions
and rely on the ability to detect and transduce mechanical force. The molecular
and neural mechanisms underlying these sensory functions remain poorly defined.
The stretch-gated ion channel PIEZO2 has been shown to be essential for aspects
of mechanosensation in model organisms.
METHODS: We performed whole-exome sequencing analysis in two patients who had
unique neuromuscular and skeletal symptoms, including progressive scoliosis,
that did not conform to standard diagnostic classification. In vitro and
messenger RNA assays, functional brain imaging, and psychophysical and kinematic
tests were used to establish the effect of the genetic variants on protein
function and somatosensation.
RESULTS: Each patient carried compound-inactivating variants in PIEZO2, and each
had a selective loss of discriminative touch perception but nevertheless
responded to specific types of gentle mechanical stimulation on hairy skin. The
patients had profoundly decreased proprioception leading to ataxia and dysmetria
that were markedly worse in the absence of visual cues. However, they had the
ability to perform a range of tasks, such as walking, talking, and writing, that
are considered to rely heavily on proprioception.
CONCLUSIONS: Our results show that PIEZO2 is a determinant of mechanosensation
in humans. (Funded by the National Institutes of Health Intramural Research
Program.).

## Full Text

The ability to sense force, which is known as mechanosensation, provides humans and other animals with important information about the environment; it is crucial for social interactions, such as comforting or caressing, and is required for motor coordination. A number of anatomical classes of somatosensory neurons with distinct selectivity for mechanical stimuli have been identified, but the way in which these inputs combine to provide the richness of the human sense of touch remains unclear.1,2 Similarly, proprioception is considered to be essential for posture and controlled movement, but little is known about the underlying mechanisms and the precise role of this sense.3

At a molecular level, the identification of PIEZO proteins as mechanosensory ion channels4 has advanced our understanding of touch detection.5,6 PIEZO2 is expressed in subsets of somatosensory neurons4,7 and Merkel cells.8,9 In mice, PIEZO2 has been shown to be essential for a variety of mechanosensory responses, including aspects of light touch7–10 and proprioception.11 Almost nothing is known about the role of PIEZO2 in humans, but it has been established that PIEZO2 is critically important for mechanotransduction in vitro,12 and genetic variants in PIEZO2, which are presumed to have gain-of-function effects on the protein, have been linked to arthrogryposis in patients.13 We describe two unrelated patients who carry inactivating variants in PIEZO2 and have prominent mechanosensory deficiencies.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Using the Shapiro-Wilk test, we determined that the electrophysiologic data obtained from transfected human embryonic kidney cells were non-normally distributed, a finding consistent with the work of others.27 We therefore analyzed these data using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney test.

We compared the sensory testing results for the two patients with the results for five control participants (the father of Patient 2 and four healthy adults age- and sex-matched to Patient 1) using the Crawford–Howell t-test.28 This modified t-test is designed to compare data from one or a few patients with data from a control group regardless of the size of the control group. Because the sensory thresholds of persons in the control group matched those previously reported by others, we assumed that these data sets had normalized distributions. We did not include age- and sex-matched controls for Patient 2 because this patient’s phenotype closely matched that of Patient 1.

We compared the kinematic reaching task results for the two patients with the results for three control participants (age- and sex-matched to Patient 1) using the Mann–Whitney test, because individual participants were tested across several weeks and thus data were not perfectly matched (as supported by the Shapiro–Wilks test of normality). We did not include age- and sex-matched controls for Patient 2 because this patient’s phenotype closely matched that of Patient 1.

We compared brain activation data (obtained through functional MRI) from Patient 1 with the data from six control participants (healthy adults age- and sex-matched to Patient 1) using a standard statistical package (FSL software). For whole-brain analysis, we identified the voxels that showed the greatest signal change from rest in response to brushing (top 1%) and that met a P value of less than 0.01, corresponding to a z score of 2.3. These voxels were considered to make up clusters of statistically significant activation only if they formed a contiguous space (clustered together). We considered clusters to be activated if they differed between rest and the application of stimulus at a P value of less than 0.05. For directed searches, peak brain activation values were extracted from each region of interest for each participant. Regions of interest were defined on the basis of previous research.14–16 These regions were considered to be activated if they contained voxels within the top 1% of signal change and met a P value of less than 0.01, corresponding to a z score of 2.3.

We used the same set of control participants for both the sensory testing and the functional MRI and a different set for the kinematic reaching task. The small sample sizes should be taken into consideration when interpreting the statistical inference procedures for the sensory testing, functional MRI, and kinematic reaching task.

RESULTSCLINICAL AND GENETIC CHARACTERISTICS OF THE PATIENTSPatient 1 presented at 18 years of age and Patient 2 at 8 years of age with similar skeletal deformations, including congenital hip dysplasia, finger contractures, foot deformities, and early severe progressive scoliosis (Fig. 1A and Table 1). Both patients had a history of hypotonia, with delayed head control and shallow breathing during infancy, delayed walking (neither patient achieved independent ambulation until between 6 and 7 years of age), and early impairments of fine motor skills. The parents reported that the patients also had difficulty with self-feeding and dressing. The family histories were unremarkable, and known medical, metabolic, and genetic causes were ruled out.On examination, the patients were noted to have unsteady gaits (in Patient 2, walking was possible only with support). They each had increased stride-to-stride variability in force and step length, an inability to walk with eyes closed (see Video 1, available at NEJM.org), and the presence of Romberg’s sign; these findings are all consistent with a sensory ataxia. In addition, they had difficulties performing reaching tasks, which were exacerbated when eyes were closed, and pseudoathetosis (spontaneous movements of outstretched arms; see Video 2) (Table 1, and Table S1 in the Supplementary Appendix). The clinical sensory examinations revealed profoundly reduced proprioception and vibratory sense. The patients were unable to detect a Rydel–Seiffer tuning fork that was vibrating at the standard frequency of 128 Hz and at the maximum amplitude of 0/8. The Babinski sign was absent, as were tendon reflexes, except at the brachioradialis in Patient 2. There was no obvious weakness; however, strength on testing was variable and very dependent on the patients’ concentration and direct visual control of the task.In both patients, there was no evidence, other than the progression of scoliosis, that the neurologic deficits were progressive. There was no evidence of impaired cognition. MRI of the head and spine was otherwise unremarkable, specifically without any abnormalities of the cerebellum or the dorsal columns. Electrodiagnostic testing revealed reduced amplitudes of sensorynerve action potentials (SNAPs) and borderline, low-to-normal sensory-nerve conduction velocities, findings consistent with mild sensory axonal neuropathies (Table S1 in the Supplementary Appendix). Motor-nerve conduction studies showed normal motor amplitudes and distal latencies, and there was no evidence of denervation on electromyography.We concluded that both patients had a unique phenotype, and we therefore performed whole-exome sequencing.17 Our analysis revealed that both patients carried at least one nonsense variant within the coding region of the same gene, PIEZO2. Further analysis revealed that Patient 1 was compound heterozygous for two alleles with premature stop codons (c.4723C→T, p.R1575* and c.5053C→T, p.R1685*) (Fig. 1B). Despite the fact that Patient 2 had a different ancestral origin from Patient 1 and had a different surrounding haplotype, Patient 2 also carried the nonsense variant c.5053C→T, p.R1685*. On the other allele, Patient 2 carried a missense variant affecting the same codon (c.5054G→C, p.R1685P) (Fig. 1B, and Table S2 in the Supplementary Appendix). Sanger sequencing of PIEZO2 in the two patients and in unaffected family members confirmed the presence of these variants and indicated recessive inheritance (Fig. 1B, and Fig. S1 in the Supplementary Appendix).FUNCTIONAL CHARACTERIZATION OF THE PIEZO2 VARIANTSEach of the premature stop codons in PIEZO2 occurred proximal to the region encoding the presumptive pore domain18,19 (Fig. S2A in the Supplementary Appendix) and proximal to the deletions created in Piezo2 in conditional knockout mice.7,8 To test the hypothesis that each of these variants inactivate the channel, we transfected a line of human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293) with plasmids encoding mouse Piezo2, in which each of the variants had been introduced into the homologous region of the gene. HEK293 cells were chosen because this cell line does not endogenously express PIEZO2 and has only minimal mechanically evoked currents (Fig. 1C). We considered this to be a reasonable test because human PIEZO2 and mouse PIEZO2 are highly homologous (91% identical and 95% similar), and the sequence flanking the putatively pathogenic variants is well-conserved (Fig. S3 in the Supplementary Appendix). We observed that the truncated mouse PIEZO2 proteins were translated but nonfunctional; heterologous expression of Piezo2 constructs in HEK293 cells yielded measurements of mechanically gated inward currents above baseline noise only in cells expressing the wild-type Piezo2 construct (P = 0.03 at indentations ≥5 µm, by the nonparametric Mann–Whitney test) (Fig. 1C; and Figs. S2, S3, and S4 in the Supplementary Appendix).To test for the presence of alternatively spliced functional messenger RNA (mRNA) that lacks the variants, we performed reverse-transcriptase–polymerase-chain-reaction (RT-PCR) amplification of RNA purified from skin-biopsy samples, as PIEZO2 is normally expressed in Merkel cells. PIEZO2 transcripts were barely detectable in the sample from Patient 1, implying nonsense-mediated decay20 of PIEZO2 mRNA (Fig. 1D). We obtained a PCR product from Patient 2 that was derived from normally spliced PIEZO2 mRNA; sequencing analysis revealed that this product was derived predominantly from expression of the allele containing the missense variant (R1685P). Moreover, the transcripts amplified from complementary DNA from the parents of Patient 1 and from the mother of Patient 2 were highly enriched for the normal PIEZO2 allele, confirming the instability of PIEZO2 mRNA containing the premature stop codons p.R1575* and p.R1685*. (For further information, see Fig. S5 in the Supplementary Appendix.)VIBRATION SENSING AND TOUCH DISCRIMINATIONWe evaluated the patients’ performance on several sensory tests. We began by assessing mechanosensory responses on glabrous skin (e.g., skin on the palm and fingertips), which is highly sensitive to touch.21 Both patients had significantly diminished sensitivity to light punctate touch (geometric mean [±SD] threshold, 4.2±0.7 g in the two patients vs. 2.9±0.2 g in the control group; P<0.001 for Patient 1 and P = 0.007 for Patient 2) (Fig. 2A). Also, both patients were unable to correctly detect a probe that was vibrating at a frequency and amplitude tuned to activate pacinian corpuscles and calibrated so that the control participants could invariably sense it (mean percentage of vibrations correctly detected at 150 Hz, 40±14 in the two patients vs. 98±0.5 in the control group; P<0.001 for both patients) (Fig. 2A).To better understand the functional consequences of PIEZO2-dependent touch deficits, we performed a standard two-point touch discrimination test on glabrous skin. The control participants distinguished between a one-point stimulus and a two-point stimulus with 100% accuracy when the two points were separated by 10 to 13 mm, whereas the two patients had only 40% accuracy on this test, a rate no better than chance (P<0.001 for both patients) (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the thresholds of mechanical (pinprick and pressure) pain in Patient 1 were similar to those in the control group (mean thresholds, 5.5±0.5 g in Patient 1 and 5.0±0.5 g in the control group for pinprick pain and 4.0±1.4 kg per square centimeter and 3.7±1.4 kg per square centimeter, respectively, for pressure pain) (Fig. 2C). Thresholds for thermal detection and pain in both patients were also similar to those in the control group (mean thresholds, 33.5±0.04°C in the two patients and 34.4±2.1°C in the control group for detection of warmth, 29.8±1.4°C and 30.1±1.2°C, respectively, for detection of coolness, and 47.8±2.3°C and 44.3±4.7°C, respectively, for heat pain) (Fig. 2D).We next assessed mechanosensory responses on hairy skin. FUNCTIONAL CHARACTERIZATION OF THE PIEZO2 VARIANTSEach of the premature stop codons in PIEZO2 occurred proximal to the region encoding the presumptive pore domain18,19 (Fig. S2A in the Supplementary Appendix) and proximal to the deletions created in Piezo2 in conditional knockout mice.7,8 To test the hypothesis that each of these variants inactivate the channel, we transfected a line of human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293) with plasmids encoding mouse Piezo2, in which each of the variants had been introduced into the homologous region of the gene. HEK293 cells were chosen because this cell line does not endogenously express PIEZO2 and has only minimal mechanically evoked currents (Fig. 1C). S2, S3, and S4 in the Supplementary Appendix).To test for the presence of alternatively spliced functional messenger RNA (mRNA) that lacks the variants, we performed reverse-transcriptase–polymerase-chain-reaction (RT-PCR) amplification of RNA purified from skin-biopsy samples, as PIEZO2 is normally expressed in Merkel cells. We obtained a PCR product from Patient 2 that was derived from normally spliced PIEZO2 mRNA; sequencing analysis revealed that this product was derived predominantly from expression of the allele containing the missense variant (R1685P). (For further information, see Fig. S5 in the Supplementary Appendix.)

DISCUSSIONWe describe two patients with autosomal recessively inherited loss-of-function variants in PIEZO2, which encodes a stretch-gated ion channel. Clinically, we observed general losses in vibration detection, touch discrimination (specifically on glabrous skin), and joint proprioception. We conclude that PIEZO2 is required to transduce the mechanical forces at the sensory afferent terminals in these various contexts in humans. In mice, PIEZO2 is found in sensory ganglia, the specialized sensory endings in skin, and in muscle spindles but not in the dorsal columns. Mice lacking PIEZO2 expression in sensory neurons show no evidence of sensory neuron loss, and the morphologic features of their afferent sensory endings remain intact. These findings in mice, taken together with the fact that other senses (pressure, pain, itch, and temperature) were unaffected in the patients, make a ganglionopathy or a dysfunction of the posterior columns unlikely, and thus we conclude that these patients have a specific deficit in mechanotransduction.Whereas in mice, homozygous inactivation of Piezo2 is associated with perinatal death,7 humans can survive without this channel. The difference in outcome is due presumably to differences between mice and humans and remains to be fully explained. However, the fact that both patients were born with hip dysplasia suggests a function for PIEZO2 in utero in humans, as well. Some aspects of the joint and skeletal phenotypes in these patients resemble those described in persons carrying dominantly acting PIEZO2 alleles that affect channel dynamics and have been linked to distal arthrogryposis and Gordon’s syndrome; these conditions are otherwise associated with features that are different from those reported here.13,23 PIEZO2 may have a direct role in skeletal development. However, an equally plausible explanation is that loss of PIEZO2 function in Patient 1 and Patient 2 resulted in prenatal impairment of proprioception, which thus caused abnormal joint positioning and may be the primary cause of arthrogryposis, hip dysplasia, and progressive scoliosis.Descriptions of persons with altered ability to sense pain have highlighted the importance of nociceptive input to health and survival.24,25 This study of PIEZO2-deficient patients offers similar insights into touch and proprioception. Patient 1 and Patient 2 have major deficits in discriminative touch perception and loss of proprioception that noticeably affect movement control and posture, but they remarkably are still able to perform complex movements by relying on compensatory inputs, such as vision. In addition, we found that both patients’ perception of other forms of somatosensation, including pain from high-threshold mechanical stimuli and the touch responses evoked by gentle stroking on hairy skin, remains intact. The findings of this study and those of others2 show the way in which distinct and independent peripheral lines of input1,26 combine to provide the rich sensory experience of human touch and the perception of our bodies in space.
