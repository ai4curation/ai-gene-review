During spermiogenesis, nuclear remodeling occurs where histones are sequentially
replaced by transition proteins (TNPs) and protamines, a process essential for
sperm maturation. Although the degradation of histones and TNPs is thought to be
essential for sperm nuclear remodeling, the underlying mechanisms, particularly
those governing TNP degradation, remain poorly understood. In this study, we
investigated the role of the ankyrin repeat-containing SOCS box protein 9 (ASB9)
during spermiogenesis and found that its deficiency causes TNP2 retention,
leading to a failure of the histone-to-protamine transition in both humans and
mice. This disruption consequently causes male infertility, characterized by
sperm head malformation and impairments in fertilization and early embryonic
development. Mechanistically, we found that ASB9 assembles a testis-specific
Cullin-RING ligase (CRL) complex-TNP2-ASB9-ELOB/C-CUL5-RBX1-that mediates the
ubiquitin-dependent degradation of TNP2 to facilitate the histone-to-protamine
transition during spermiogenesis. Collectively, our study uncovers the mechanism
underlying TNP2 degradation and highlights the critical role of ASB9 in male
fertility through the CRL complex-mediated ubiquitination pathway, thereby
expanding the fundamental understanding of nuclear remodeling during
spermiogenesis.

## Full Text

Spermiogenesis is a precisely controlled developmental process that involves several key events, including elongation and condensation of nuclei, formation of acrosome and flagellum, and elimination of excess cytoplasm, all regulated by tightly coordinated molecular and biochemical mechanisms. A critical aspect of this process is chromatin remodeling, commonly known as the histone-to-protamine transition, which is required for nuclear elongation and condensation (1). During this transition, histones initially incorporate specific histone variants, such as H2A.L.2 and TH2B, which facilitate the attachment of transition proteins (TNPs) TNP1 and TNP2 to the chromatin (2, 3). The two TNPs then assist recruitment and processing protamines (PRMs) PRM1 and PRM2, ultimately replacing histones and TNPs so that protamines become the primary packaging protein in the condensed sperm genome (4). This change dramatically reduces the nuclear volume, yielding a compact and streamlined nucleus that is a necessary condition for male fecundity (3). If any disruptions occur during this transition, it can lead to sperm with improperly condensed chromatin, often accompanied by aberrant morphology and impaired motility, ultimately compromising fertilization and early embryonic development (5, 6).

The removal of histones and TNPs represents a pivotal step in chromatin remodeling. Emerging research has shown that retention of histones or TNPs contributes to sperm-morphology–related male infertility, leading to fertilization failure and embryonic developmental arrest in mice. Such phenotypes have been observed in models with deficiency of DOT1L, TSSK6, SIRT1, or CHD5 (5, 7–9). While the ubiquitin–proteasome system is the primary machinery responsible for protein degradation, the molecular mechanism governing proteasomal degradation of histones and TNPs remains poorly understood. RNF8 stands as the only well-characterized RING-domain ubiquitin E3 ligase that catalyzes ubiquitination of histones during murine spermiogenesis (6). PHF7 was later identified as a novel E3 ligase that specifically ubiquitylates and degrades H2A, and its deletion leads to mouse male infertility due to abnormal histone retention in elongated spermatids (10). To date, no studies have investigated the degradation mechanisms of the TNPs TNP1 or TNP2. Furthermore, the degradation of histones and TNPs in humans has not been explored.

The Cullin (CUL)-RING ligase (CRL) complex, composed of multiple subunits, represents the most extensive category of E3 ubiquitin ligases, accounting for approximately 20% of proteasomal degradation (11). The CRL complex has a conserved structural framework consisting of a substrate receptor, a CUL protein, a RING-box (RBX) protein, and an adapter protein such as SKP1 or elongin B/C complex (12). The ankyrin repeat-containing SOCS box proteins (ASBs) comprise the largest family of substrate receptors for the CRL complex, and consist of 18 members (ASB1-18) (13). Each ASB contains two conserved domains: a C-terminal SOCS box and N-terminal ankyrin repeats (14). Noticeably, multiple ASB members (ASB1, 3, 4, 8, 9, 12, 15, 17) show abundant expression in murine testes (15). Among them, ASB1, expressed mainly in steps 10 to 15 spermatids, is required for nuclear shaping via SQOR ubiquitination, while ASB17, localized around spermatid heads, maintains ectoplasmic specialization junctions by promoting ubiquitin-mediated ESPN degradation (15, 16). Interestingly, ASB9 expression is low in pachytene spermatocytes but significantly increases in condensing spermatids, suggesting a role in sperm reshaping (17). ASB9 has been found to bind Creatine Kinase brain-type (CKB) and promote its degradation in HEK293T cells (18). However, whether it plays a role in ubiquitin-mediated degradation during spermiogenesis remains unclear.

In this study, we identified a loss-of-function mutation in ASB9 in an infertile family with two affected men. The proband exhibited abnormal sperm morphology, predominantly characterized by head malformations. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further revealed loose chromatin in the sperm heads. Asb9 knock in (KI) and knockout (KO) mouse models were established, displaying sperm head morphology abnormalities and impaired nuclear remodeling. These defects were attributed to a failed histone-to-protamine transition, driven by abnormal retention of TNP2 and a subsequent reduction of PRMs, particularly PRM2, in mature sperm from the ASB9-deficient patient and mice. Mechanistically, we found that ASB9 forms a testis-specific TNP2–ASB9–ELOB/C–CUL5–RBX1 CRL complex, which mediates the degradation of TNP2 via the ubiquitination pathway, thereby enabling a successful histone-to-protamine transition. Therefore, our study identified an ASB9-mediated E3 ubiquitin ligase complex that promotes sperm head chromatin remodeling by degrading TNP2 during the histone-to-protamine transition in humans and mice.

Computer-assisted sperm analysis revealed that both KO and KI mice showed reduced sperm motility and concentration (Movies S1–S3 and Dataset S3). As expected, the spermatid head deformities disrupted normal acrosome morphology during late spermiogenesis in both KO and KI mice, resulting in disorganized acrosomal architecture (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). The acrosome–acroplaxome–manchette cytoskeletal system is a coordinated and interconnected structure that plays a critical role in sperm morphogenesis (19). We thus speculated that the abnormal acrosomal structure might lead to morphological defects in the acroplaxome and manchette. As expected, immunofluorescence staining revealed a loosened and disorganized acroplaxome structure in KO and KI mice, whereas in WT mice, the acroplaxome was well organized within the subacrosomal space, anchoring the acrosome to the nuclear membrane (20) (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). In addition, in both mice, steps 13 to 14 elongating spermatids displayed markedly abnormal manchette organization, including hyperelongated and discontinuous structures, in contrast to the typical perinuclear cup-shaped configuration in WT mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). The manchette plays an essential role in regulating the assembly of flagellar accessory structures through its storage and transport functions (21, 22). Therefore, the flagellar defects observed in ASB9-deficient sperm may result from abnormal manchette organization. Considering that the acrosome–acroplaxome–manchette system also plays a crucial role in shaping the spermatid head (19), the disruption of this system further exacerbates the head deformities. Collectively, ASB9 deficiency impairs spermiogenesis, primarily due to abnormal sperm head development.

DiscussionRecent studies show that ASBs play crucial roles in mice, with ASB1 loss causing male infertility and ASB2 deficiency resulting in embryonic lethality and developmental defects (15, 16, 28). However, no ASBs have been functionally linked to any human disease. Here, we demonstrated the causal relationship between ASB9 nonfunction and male infertility in humans and mice. Thus, our findings established a genotype–phenotype correlation for ASBs in human disease, while highlighting the critical role of ASB9 in reproduction. Certainly, identifying pathogenic mutations in ASB9 in unrelated infertile patients in the future would offer compelling evidence to validate the causal link between ASB9 deficiency and impaired human spermiogenesis.During spermiogenesis, a large proportion of histones are progressively removed from spermatid chromatin and sequentially replaced by TNPs and PRMs, ensuring proper chromatin remodeling and sperm nuclear condensation (29). The initial step in this histone-to-protamine exchange is histone ubiquitination, which destabilizes nucleosome structure by loosening chromatin compaction (2, 30). The subsequent degradation of TNPs is also crucial, as it determines the successful incorporation of PRMs (4). However, research on the protein degradation mechanisms underlying the histone-to-protamine exchange remains largely unknown. Currently, RNF8 and PHF7 are the only known E3 ubiquitin ligases that directly mediate histone degradation, whereas no E3 ubiquitin ligase has yet been identified for the degradation of TNPs (6, 10). In this study, we revealed ASB9 as a molecule regulating the histone-to-protamine transition in humans and mice. The proband harboring a loss-of-function mutation in ASB9 exhibited sperm head malformations accompanied by chromatin decondensation, leading to fertilization failure and embryonic developmental arrest, phenotypes that were recapitulated in ASB9-deficient mice. Immunofluorescence staining showed that KO mice exhibited abnormal retention of TNP2 in steps 15 to 16 spermatids, accompanied by reduced PRMs, particularly PRM2. IP-MS and Co-IP assays identified TNP2 as an ASB9-interacting protein, suggesting that ASB9 may regulate TNP2 expression through their interaction to facilitate chromatin remodeling. Co-IP assay further demonstrated that ASB9 interacts with the P1 motif of TNP2 through its fourth ankyrin repeat domain. In addition, we observed significantly reduced TNP2 K48-linked ubiquitination in KO testes compared to WT testes, a phenomenon recapitulated in cultured cells overexpressing ASB9 and TNP2. Interestingly, it has been reported that PRM2 overexpression in HEK293T cells induces chromatin condensation (31). Accordingly, we cotransfected HEK293T cells with ASB9, TNP2, and PRM2, or with TNP2 and PRM2 in the absence of ASB9. Immunofluorescence revealed that in the presence of ASB9, PRM2 signals were strongly localized in the nuclei, whereas TNP2 staining appeared weak, and a subset of nuclei displayed pronounced chromatin condensation. In contrast, without ASB9, PRM2 signals were markedly reduced, TNP2 staining was relatively enhanced, and chromatin condensation was slightly diminished compared with that observed in ASB9-expressing cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S20). Therefore, ASB9 facilitates chromatin condensation by promoting TNP2 degradation during spermiogenesis. Compared with TNP2 retention, TNP deficiency results in a much milder phenotype. Tnp1 KO mice exhibit subfertility, characterized by mild abnormalities in sperm morphology and reduced motility (32). Notably, elevated levels of TNP2 and PRM2 precursors were detected in Tnp1 KO spermatids, suggesting that TNP2 and immature PRM2 partially compensate for TNP1 deficiency (32). Consequently, chromatin in Tnp1 KO spermatids displays large rod-like structures instead of the fine chromatin fibrils seen in WT spermatids, but without apparent chromatin decondensation. Similarly, Tnp2 KO males are also subfertile, showing increased sperm morphological defects and decreased motility (33, 34). Compensatory increases in TNP1 and in precursor and partially processed PRM2 were observed in Tnp2 KO spermatids, allowing chromatin condensation to proceed largely to completion (33, 34). Therefore, these findings indicated that TNP retention is more detrimental than TNP deficiency, as the latter can be partially compensated by other proteins (such as the reciprocal TNP1 or TNP2, and PRM2 precursors), whereas TNP retention directly interferes with PRM2 processing and effectively blocks chromatin condensation. In conclusion, ASB9 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase–associated protein that targets TNP2 for ubiquitin-mediated degradation, thereby facilitating the histone-to-protamine transition during spermiogenesis.As a modular E3 ligase, the CRL complex centers on a CUL scaffold that anchors RBXs at the C-terminus and connects to a broad range of substrate receptors via adapter proteins at the N-terminus, allowing for dynamic and specific ubiquitination of target proteins (11). The ASB protein family, the largest class of SOCS-box–containing E3 ligase substrate receptors, features ankyrin repeat domains for substrate recognition and SOCS-box domains that mediate specific interaction with CUL5 via the ELOB/C adapter complex (11). Several ASBs have defined substrates, and CKB has been established as a target of ASB9-mediated ubiquitination degradation in HEK293T cells (11, 18). Cryo-EM enabled construction of the full CKB–ASB9–ELOB/C–CUL5–RBX2 complex, showing that CKB binds to the N-terminal ankyrin repeat domain of ASB9, the BC box in the SOCS domain of ASB9 recruits ELOB/C and CUL5, and the C-terminal Winged-Helix B domain of CUL5 binds RBX2 (11). Interestingly, our IP-MS of mouse testicular lysates identified CUL5 and the ELOB/C complex as interactors of ASB9. Although RBX2 was not detected, RBX1 was identified, indicating that a substrate-bound ASB9–ELOB/C–CUL5–RBX1 complex exists in the testes. Since the direct interactions among ASB9, ELOB/C, and CUL5 have already been established (11), we further demonstrated through protein purification and domain-specific Co-IP assays that RBX1 interacts with the CUL1 domain of CUL5 via its modified RING finger domain. Moreover, TNP2 was identified as an interactor of ASB9 and showed increased expression in Asb9 KO mice. These findings suggested that TNP2 may serve as a substrate of the ASB9–ELOB/C–CUL5–RBX1 complex during spermiogenesis. Knockdown of ELOB, ELOC, CUL5, or RBX1 impaired the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of TNP2 in cultured cells, confirming that the ASB9–ELOB/C–CUL5–RBX1 complex is essential for TNP2 degradation. In addition, proteomic analysis and Western blotting revealed reduced levels of ELOB, ELOC, CUL5, and RBX1 in KO testes and in ASB9-depleted HeLa cells. Moreover, individual knockdown of ELOB/C, CUL5, or RBX1 similarly reduced the expression of the remaining components. These results suggested that disruption of any single component may destabilize the entire complex. Taken together, these findings demonstrated that ASB9 promotes TNP2 degradation in the testes by assembling itself together with ELOB/C, CUL5, and RBX1 into an E3 ligase complex through direct interactions, highlighting the essential role of the TNP2–ASB9–ELOB/C–CUL5–RBX1 CRL complex in ensuring proper histone-to-protamine transition during spermiogenesis.In addition to head malformations, ASB9-deficient sperm also exhibited structural abnormalities in the acrosome and flagellum, as well as head–tail junction defects, in both humans and mice. In KO/KI mice, although acrosome formation in round spermatids appeared unaffected, acrosomal morphology became abnormal during spermatid elongation. Because the acrosome is a specialized membranous organelle at the anterior of the sperm nucleus, spermatid head malformation inevitably leads to acrosomal defects. The acrosome–acroplaxome–manchette cytoskeletal system constitutes an integrated and highly coordinated apparatus that is essential for sperm morphogenesis (19). Accordingly, in addition to acrosomal defects, we also observed abnormalities in the acroplaxome and manchette in both KO and KI mice. Proper sperm tail formation depends on efficient protein transport during spermatid elongation, and intramanchette transport has been shown to play a crucial role in delivering structural flagellar proteins to the basal body at the base of the developing tail (21, 22). Moreover, the manchette serves as a platform for the storage and delivery of head–tail coupling apparatus (HTCA)–associated proteins (21, 22). Therefore, disruption of proteins that regulate manchette structure and function often results in abnormal flagellar formation and defects in head–tail junction assembly (21, 22). Therefore, the defects in flagellum and head–tail connection observed in ASB9-deficient sperm are likely caused by the aberrant manchette. Collectively, these findings suggested that ASB9 deficiency leads to spermatid head malformation, which subsequently disrupts the acrosome–acroplaxome–manchette complex, resulting in acrosomal and flagellar structural abnormalities as well as HTCA defects. The disruption of this complex, in turn, further exacerbates the severity of spermatid/sperm head malformations. Recent studies show that ASBs play crucial roles in mice, with ASB1 loss causing male infertility and ASB2 deficiency resulting in embryonic lethality and developmental defects (15, 16, 28). However, no ASBs have been functionally linked to any human disease. Here, we demonstrated the causal relationship between ASB9 nonfunction and male infertility in humans and mice. Thus, our findings established a genotype–phenotype correlation for ASBs in human disease, while highlighting the critical role of ASB9 in reproduction. Certainly, identifying pathogenic mutations in ASB9 in unrelated infertile patients in the future would offer compelling evidence to validate the causal link between ASB9 deficiency and impaired human spermiogenesis.

During spermiogenesis, a large proportion of histones are progressively removed from spermatid chromatin and sequentially replaced by TNPs and PRMs, ensuring proper chromatin remodeling and sperm nuclear condensation (29). The initial step in this histone-to-protamine exchange is histone ubiquitination, which destabilizes nucleosome structure by loosening chromatin compaction (2, 30). The subsequent degradation of TNPs is also crucial, as it determines the successful incorporation of PRMs (4). However, research on the protein degradation mechanisms underlying the histone-to-protamine exchange remains largely unknown. Currently, RNF8 and PHF7 are the only known E3 ubiquitin ligases that directly mediate histone degradation, whereas no E3 ubiquitin ligase has yet been identified for the degradation of TNPs (6, 10). In this study, we revealed ASB9 as a molecule regulating the histone-to-protamine transition in humans and mice. The proband harboring a loss-of-function mutation in ASB9 exhibited sperm head malformations accompanied by chromatin decondensation, leading to fertilization failure and embryonic developmental arrest, phenotypes that were recapitulated in ASB9-deficient mice. Immunofluorescence staining showed that KO mice exhibited abnormal retention of TNP2 in steps 15 to 16 spermatids, accompanied by reduced PRMs, particularly PRM2. IP-MS and Co-IP assays identified TNP2 as an ASB9-interacting protein, suggesting that ASB9 may regulate TNP2 expression through their interaction to facilitate chromatin remodeling. Co-IP assay further demonstrated that ASB9 interacts with the P1 motif of TNP2 through its fourth ankyrin repeat domain. In addition, we observed significantly reduced TNP2 K48-linked ubiquitination in KO testes compared to WT testes, a phenomenon recapitulated in cultured cells overexpressing ASB9 and TNP2. Interestingly, it has been reported that PRM2 overexpression in HEK293T cells induces chromatin condensation (31). Accordingly, we cotransfected HEK293T cells with ASB9, TNP2, and PRM2, or with TNP2 and PRM2 in the absence of ASB9. Immunofluorescence revealed that in the presence of ASB9, PRM2 signals were strongly localized in the nuclei, whereas TNP2 staining appeared weak, and a subset of nuclei displayed pronounced chromatin condensation. In contrast, without ASB9, PRM2 signals were markedly reduced, TNP2 staining was relatively enhanced, and chromatin condensation was slightly diminished compared with that observed in ASB9-expressing cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S20). Therefore, ASB9 facilitates chromatin condensation by promoting TNP2 degradation during spermiogenesis. Compared with TNP2 retention, TNP deficiency results in a much milder phenotype. Tnp1 KO mice exhibit subfertility, characterized by mild abnormalities in sperm morphology and reduced motility (32). Notably, elevated levels of TNP2 and PRM2 precursors were detected in Tnp1 KO spermatids, suggesting that TNP2 and immature PRM2 partially compensate for TNP1 deficiency (32). Consequently, chromatin in Tnp1 KO spermatids displays large rod-like structures instead of the fine chromatin fibrils seen in WT spermatids, but without apparent chromatin decondensation. Similarly, Tnp2 KO males are also subfertile, showing increased sperm morphological defects and decreased motility (33, 34). Compensatory increases in TNP1 and in precursor and partially processed PRM2 were observed in Tnp2 KO spermatids, allowing chromatin condensation to proceed largely to completion (33, 34). Therefore, these findings indicated that TNP retention is more detrimental than TNP deficiency, as the latter can be partially compensated by other proteins (such as the reciprocal TNP1 or TNP2, and PRM2 precursors), whereas TNP retention directly interferes with PRM2 processing and effectively blocks chromatin condensation. In conclusion, ASB9 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase–associated protein that targets TNP2 for ubiquitin-mediated degradation, thereby facilitating the histone-to-protamine transition during spermiogenesis.

As a modular E3 ligase, the CRL complex centers on a CUL scaffold that anchors RBXs at the C-terminus and connects to a broad range of substrate receptors via adapter proteins at the N-terminus, allowing for dynamic and specific ubiquitination of target proteins (11). The ASB protein family, the largest class of SOCS-box–containing E3 ligase substrate receptors, features ankyrin repeat domains for substrate recognition and SOCS-box domains that mediate specific interaction with CUL5 via the ELOB/C adapter complex (11). Several ASBs have defined substrates, and CKB has been established as a target of ASB9-mediated ubiquitination degradation in HEK293T cells (11, 18). Cryo-EM enabled construction of the full CKB–ASB9–ELOB/C–CUL5–RBX2 complex, showing that CKB binds to the N-terminal ankyrin repeat domain of ASB9, the BC box in the SOCS domain of ASB9 recruits ELOB/C and CUL5, and the C-terminal Winged-Helix B domain of CUL5 binds RBX2 (11). Interestingly, our IP-MS of mouse testicular lysates identified CUL5 and the ELOB/C complex as interactors of ASB9. Although RBX2 was not detected, RBX1 was identified, indicating that a substrate-bound ASB9–ELOB/C–CUL5–RBX1 complex exists in the testes. Since the direct interactions among ASB9, ELOB/C, and CUL5 have already been established (11), we further demonstrated through protein purification and domain-specific Co-IP assays that RBX1 interacts with the CUL1 domain of CUL5 via its modified RING finger domain. Moreover, TNP2 was identified as an interactor of ASB9 and showed increased expression in Asb9 KO mice. These findings suggested that TNP2 may serve as a substrate of the ASB9–ELOB/C–CUL5–RBX1 complex during spermiogenesis. Knockdown of ELOB, ELOC, CUL5, or RBX1 impaired the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of TNP2 in cultured cells, confirming that the ASB9–ELOB/C–CUL5–RBX1 complex is essential for TNP2 degradation. In addition, proteomic analysis and Western blotting revealed reduced levels of ELOB, ELOC, CUL5, and RBX1 in KO testes and in ASB9-depleted HeLa cells. Moreover, individual knockdown of ELOB/C, CUL5, or RBX1 similarly reduced the expression of the remaining components. These results suggested that disruption of any single component may destabilize the entire complex. Taken together, these findings demonstrated that ASB9 promotes TNP2 degradation in the testes by assembling itself together with ELOB/C, CUL5, and RBX1 into an E3 ligase complex through direct interactions, highlighting the essential role of the TNP2–ASB9–ELOB/C–CUL5–RBX1 CRL complex in ensuring proper histone-to-protamine transition during spermiogenesis.

In addition to head malformations, ASB9-deficient sperm also exhibited structural abnormalities in the acrosome and flagellum, as well as head–tail junction defects, in both humans and mice. In KO/KI mice, although acrosome formation in round spermatids appeared unaffected, acrosomal morphology became abnormal during spermatid elongation. Because the acrosome is a specialized membranous organelle at the anterior of the sperm nucleus, spermatid head malformation inevitably leads to acrosomal defects. The acrosome–acroplaxome–manchette cytoskeletal system constitutes an integrated and highly coordinated apparatus that is essential for sperm morphogenesis (19). Accordingly, in addition to acrosomal defects, we also observed abnormalities in the acroplaxome and manchette in both KO and KI mice. Proper sperm tail formation depends on efficient protein transport during spermatid elongation, and intramanchette transport has been shown to play a crucial role in delivering structural flagellar proteins to the basal body at the base of the developing tail (21, 22). Moreover, the manchette serves as a platform for the storage and delivery of head–tail coupling apparatus (HTCA)–associated proteins (21, 22). Therefore, disruption of proteins that regulate manchette structure and function often results in abnormal flagellar formation and defects in head–tail junction assembly (21, 22). Therefore, the defects in flagellum and head–tail connection observed in ASB9-deficient sperm are likely caused by the aberrant manchette. Collectively, these findings suggested that ASB9 deficiency leads to spermatid head malformation, which subsequently disrupts the acrosome–acroplaxome–manchette complex, resulting in acrosomal and flagellar structural abnormalities as well as HTCA defects. The disruption of this complex, in turn, further exacerbates the severity of spermatid/sperm head malformations. In addition, proteomic detected decreased WT1 in KO testes, a protein expressed in Sertoli cells. It has been reported that interactions exist between germ cells and Sertoli cells, and that abnormal spermatogenesis can lead to the downregulation of gene transcription in Sertoli cells, including Wt1 (35, 36). We further found that the reduced WT1 in KO testes resulted from transcriptional downregulation. Therefore, we speculated that the loss of ASB9 causes defective spermiogenesis, which in turn may indirectly affect Sertoli cell function.

In conclusion, we identified ASB9 as a previously unrecognized regulator of the histone-to-protamine transition during spermiogenesis, whose deficiency leads to defective sperm nuclear condensation in both humans and mice. Furthermore, we uncovered a testis-specific CRL complex—TNP2–ASB9–ELOB/C–CUL5–RBX1—that is essential for removing TNP2 from spermatid DNA during chromatin remodeling, thereby revealing the molecular mechanism governing TNP2 degradation. This study also provided insights into the role of ASB9 in human fertility, opening avenues for exploring the relationship between ASBs and human disease. Collectively, our findings deepened the understanding of protein posttranslational regulation underlying the histone-to-protamine exchange during spermiogenesis and offer important clues for the etiology and clinical management of male infertility.
