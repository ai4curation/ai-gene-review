The noncanonical inflammasome, mediated by murine caspase-11 and its human
orthologs caspase-4 and caspase-5, detects intracellular lipopolysaccharide
(LPS) and triggers pyroptotic cell death. Upon LPS binding through their
amino-terminal caspase activation and recruitment domains (CARDs), these
inflammatory caspases oligomerize and activate. However, how LPS binding drives
caspase-4/11 activation remains unclear. Here, we show that caspase-4/11 CARDs
are intrinsically unstructured in their resting state and adopt an α-helical
conformation upon LPS engagement. This structural rearrangement promotes CARD
oligomerization, with electron-capture charge reduction-coupled native mass
spectrometry and other techniques illustrating that hexa-acylated LPS induces
the formation of large oligomers composed of eight or more protomers, whereas
underacylated lipids trigger smaller assemblies. Using hydrogen-deuterium
exchange mass spectrometry and cross-linking mass spectrometry, we identified a
hydrophobic cleft critical for LPS binding and potential intersubunit interfaces
that facilitate oligomerization. These findings uncover key structural features
underlying LPS recognition and activation of the noncanonical inflammasome,
providing mechanistic insight into an essential arm of the innate immune
response.

INTRODUCTION

The innate immune system serves as the first line of defense against microbial invasion by initiating rapid inflammatory responses that restrict pathogen dissemination, promote tissue repair, and recruit effector immune cells. Central to these responses are pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) (1, 2), which detect conserved microbial components known as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and endogenous damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released from damaged or dying cells (3, 4). Among these molecular patterns, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which is a key structural component of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, is one of the most potent and well-characterized PAMPs.

Extracellular LPS is recognized by Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), in cooperation with accessory proteins MD-2 and CD14, to activate pro-inflammatory signaling cascades (5–7). In parallel, LPS is also sensed by transient receptor potential (TRP) channels on nociceptive neurons, leading to neurogenic inflammation and pain (8–10). However, in addition to these extracellular sensors, a distinct and critical pathway is activated by the detection of intracellular LPS: the noncanonical inflammasome (11–14).

The noncanonical inflammasome is mediated by murine caspase-11 and its human orthologs, caspase-4 and caspase-5 (11). These inflammatory caspases function as cytosolic PRRs that directly bind LPS through their N-terminal caspase activation and recruitment domains (CARDs) (12). Upon LPS binding, caspase-4/11 undergo oligomerization and activation, resulting in the proteolytic cleavage of downstream targets, including the pore-forming protein gasdermin D (GSDMD) and the precursor cytokine pro–interleukin-18 (pro–IL-18) (12, 15–20). Cleaved GSDMD forms membrane-inserted pores that drive pyroptotic cell death and promote the release of mature cytokines and other intracellular inflammatory mediators (17, 21–26). Although this pathway plays a critical role in antimicrobial host defense, its dysregulation has also been implicated in the pathogenesis of various autoinflammatory and infectious diseases (27).

Despite the physiological significance of the noncanonical inflammasome, the molecular mechanisms by which LPS binding structurally drives caspase-4/11 oligomerization and activation remain poorly understood. Here, we demonstrate that the CARDs of caspase-4/11 adopt a largely unstructured conformation in their resting state and transition into an α-helical conformation upon LPS binding. This conformational shift is accompanied by the oligomerization of the CARDs. Hexa-acylated LPS promotes the assembly of large CARD oligomers containing eight or more protomers, whereas underacylated lipids induce the formation of smaller assemblies. Using hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS) and cross-linking mass spectrometry (XL-MS), we identified a hydrophobic pocket critical for LPS binding as well as potential interfacial contacts that mediate CARD oligomerization. Together, these findings advance our mechanistic insight into LPS sensing and LPS-induced noncanonical inflammasome activation.

In the absence of a ligand, the CD spectrum of caspase-4 CARD did not show distinct peaks above 210 nm, indicative of a largely unstructured conformation (Fig. 1F, black). Higher-order assembly of CARD is necessary for activation

To precisely investigate the oligomerization state of caspase-4/11 upon LPS binding, we used native mass spectrometry (nMS) to monitor the assembly of the caspase-11 CARD in the absence and presence of the LPS-Ra or lipid IVa. Consistent with our SEC data, MBP–caspase-11CARDInsect appeared predominantly as well-resolved monomers with a deconvolved mass of 54.6 kDa in the absence of LPS (fig. S3A). A minor population of species with varying MWs was also observed, likely arising from truncated proteoforms or low-level contaminants. No evidence of oligomerization was detected in the absence of LPS (fig. S3A).

Upon incubation with LPS-Ra, the MBP–caspase-11CARDInsect/LPS-Ra complex was quite apparent in the higher mass range. This complex exhibited broad, overlapping peaks spanning several thousand mass/charge ratio (m/z) units, indicative of an exceptionally heterogeneous population (Fig. 2A). Because of this extensive peak overlap, individual charge states of the MBP–caspase-11CARDInsect/LPS-Ra oligomers cannot be sufficiently resolved and standard deconvolution could not be performed. To overcome this limitation and characterize the oligomeric state despite the heterogeneity, we isolated narrow fractions (15 m/z units wide) from these broad distributions using a quadrupole mass filter and subjected each fraction to electron-capture charge reduction (ECCR) (38). Lower MW species in the 500- to 510-kDa range (e.g., 503.6 and 513.9 kDa) may reflect LPS-deficient or heterogeneously decorated complexes. The MW differences between adjacent oligomeric species were ~9 kDa, closely corresponding to the mass of two LPS-Ra molecules (~4.3 kDa each), further suggesting that the observed heterogeneity of the complex likely arises from variation in the number of bound LPS-Ra molecules. Collectively, these data suggest that the MBP–caspase-11CARDInsect/LPS-Ra complex predominantly forms octamers, with an average stoichiometry of approximately two to three LPS-Ra molecules per CARD monomer (Fig. 2B). Consistent with this, native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) analysis showed that LPS-Ra induced a complete transition of MBP–caspase-11CARDInsect to the oligomeric state at lipid-to-protein molar ratios above 2.5:1 (fig. Our recent studies revealed that caspase-11 CARD interacts with multiple lipid species bearing a conserved pattern that contains at least 14-carbon acyl chains (28). This observation led us to hypothesize the existence of a specific structural motif within the CARD responsible for recognizing the LPS acyl chains. Notably, we observed rapid deuterium uptake in residues 25 to 37 and significantly elevated uptake in residues 4 to 22 in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 3A), suggesting that these regions are highly dynamic and solvent exposed. HDX-MS analysis using PGPC (1-palmitoyl-2-glutaryl phosphatidylcholine), a recently identified endogenous oxidized phospholipid inhibitor of caspase-11, provided additional mechanistic insight. Similar to LPS-Ra, PGPC binding reduced deuterium uptake at both regions of 4 to 22 and 25 to 37, indicating that PGPC likely engages the same hydrophobic pocket within the CARD (Fig. 3, C, E, and G). This shared binding site may explain PGPC’s ability to antagonize LPS-induced oligomerization and activation of caspase-11. However, the extent of protection by PGPC was less pronounced and more transient than that observed with LPS-Ra, consistent with the weaker binding affinity of PGPC (12, 28). Residues 78 to 91, the region not significantly protected by LPS-Ra, exhibited increased protection in the presence of PGPC (Fig. 3C), suggesting that PGPC may extend its interaction to this region, consistent with a previous study (28).

Amino acid sequence analysis of residues 4 to 37 revealed multiple hydrophobic residues that are highly conserved among caspase-4, caspase-5, and caspase-11 across different species (fig. S4D). Structural modeling using AlphaFold (39) predicted that residues 4 to 37 form helices α1 and α2 in caspase-11 CARD (Fig. 3H). Hydrophobic residues from these two helices form a gigantic hydrophobic pocket (Fig. 3I). The pocket also involves the helix α5, formed by residues 66 to 78, where we observed a modest reduction in deuterium uptake upon LPS and PGPC binding (Fig. 3, A to G). The resulting hydrophobic pocket is ~20 Å long, 14 Å wide, and 8 Å deep, sufficiently large to accommodate up to four acyl chains of LPS (Fig. 3, H and I). Although some of these hydrophobic residues are also conserved in the caspase-1 CARD, they do not form a similar pocket in that context (fig. To further assess the functional relevance of this pocket in LPS recognition, we introduced hydrophilic substitutions at key hydrophobic residues and analyzed the behavior of the resulting mutants expressed in E. coli. Together, we conclude that the hydrophobic pocket formed by residues 4 to 37 and residues from helix α5 is a critical structural feature mediating the interaction of caspase-4/11 with LPS and other lipid ligands.

After this filtration, we identified a unique cross-linked peptide pair involving lysines K9 and K62, which span a Cα-Cα distance of 27.4 Å, falling well within the commonly accepted 26- to 30-Å range for intersubunit DSS cross-links (40) (Fig. 5C). Moreover, K9 and K62 are separated by helices α2 and α5 within the CARD structure (Fig. 5C), further supporting that the K9-K62 linkage represents an intersubunit cross-link. This suggests that K9, K62, and/or their surrounding regions constitute part of the interface mediating caspase-11 CARD oligomerization.

Residues K53/R54/W55 and K62/K63/K64, previously reported to be critical for noncanonical caspase-11 inflammasome activation (12), are located outside the defined hydrophobic LPS-binding pocket but in close proximity to this XL-MS–defined oligomerization interface (Fig. 5C), suggesting that these residues may contribute to CARD oligomerization rather than directly participating in LPS recognition. To further validate these findings, we introduced mutations targeting these key residues. As expected, triple mutations K53E/R54E/W55A and K62E/K63E/K64E almost completely disrupted caspase-11 CARD oligomerization when expressed in E. coli, and single mutations R54D and K64E also partially impaired the oligomer formation (Fig. 5D).

In addition to K9-K62, two other unique cross-linked peptide pairs—K44-K64 and K38-K67—were identified in caspase-11 CARD oligomers and may also contribute to oligomerization (Fig. 5B). The Cα-Cα distances for K44-K64 and K38-K67 are ~15.0 and 17.8 Å, respectively, placing them below the 24-Å Euclidean distance cutoff. However, the distances between their side-chain amino nitrogen (Nζ) atoms are 16.5 and 22.1 Å, respectively, which exceed the theoretical 11.4 Å maximum for the DSS cross-linker (40) (Fig. 5, E and F). Although one might argue that conformational flexibility could bring Nζ atoms closer due to lysine side-chain dynamics, structural analysis shows that the side chains of K44-K64 and K38-K67 extend in opposite directions (Fig. 5, E and F), limiting the likelihood of such fluctuations bridging the gap. Besides, neither of these cross-links were observed in the monomeric mutant caspase-11 CARD, suggesting that they are intersubunit cross-links. In addition to the K44-K64 and K38-K67 linkage, K19, a previously reported residue critical for noncanonical inflammasome activation (12), was also found to cross-link with K9. The Cα-Cα and Nζ-Nζ distances between K9 and K19 are ~21.3 and 28.7 Å, respectively, approaching the Euclidean cutoff. Structural mapping reveals that K19 is positioned near K44 and outside the LPS-binding pocket (Fig. 5E), suggesting that K19 and its neighboring residues may be involved in oligomerization instead of LPS binding. Together, these data suggest that K19, K44, K64, K38, K67, and the nearby regions may form additional interfaces that mediate CARD-CARD interactions during oligomerization.

We next used a similar approach to investigate oligomerization in the caspase-4 CARD. Caspase-4 CARD monomers, purified from E. coli with detergent and from insect cells, were incubated with and without the presence of LPS-Ra and then subjected to XL-MS analysis. A total of 60 and 21 unique cross-links were identified, respectively (fig. S5A). Notably, cross-links involving K8/9-K53/K64 and K9-K49 and K49-K64—corresponding to the K9-K62 and K44-K64 intersubunit cross-links observed in caspase-11 CARD—were both detected in the caspase-4 CARD/LPS-Ra complex (fig. S5, B to D). The corresponding K38-K67 cross-link was not identified in caspase-4 CARD, likely due to the absence of lysine residue in the analogous region. Together, our XL-MS data confirm the formation of caspase-4/11 CARD oligomers upon LPS binding and identify potential interfaces that mediates the CARD-CARD oligomerization.

The formation of high-MW complexes composed of multiple protein components, which collectively function as supramolecular organizing centers (SMOCs), is a critical step in the activation of many inflammasomes, including ALR and NLR family inflammasomes (41, 42). The caspase-4/11 noncanonical inflammasome appears to use a conceptually similar strategy, despite being composed of a single protein that functions both as the receptor and the effector (43). In this study, we found that the CARDs of caspase-4/11 undergo high-order oligomerization upon binding to hexa-acylated LPS, forming octameric complexes that likely represents the minimal functional unit required for noncanonical inflammasome activation. In contrast, other lipids such as the penta-acylated LPS-Rs and the tetra-acylated lipid IVa induce only lower-order assemblies, likely tetramers, which are insufficient to initiate robust signaling. Moreover, the endogenous antagonist PGPC, which contains only a single acyl chain, binds caspase-11 CARD but maintains it in an inactive monomeric state.

Although our findings support a working model in which LPS acylation state dictates CARD oligomerization and inflammasome activation, we also recognize that certain underacylated LPS variants may activate caspase-11 under specific cellular conditions. For example, penta-acylated lipid A from Francisella novicida mutants has been shown to trigger caspase-11 activation in interferon (IFN)–primed macrophages (13, 44). These in vivo observations suggest that context-dependent factors, such as type I IFN signaling or guanylate-binding proteins (GBPs) (45–49), may lower the threshold for activation, allowing even incomplete CARD oligomers (e.g., tetramers) to initiate a partial response.

Dimerization has long been considered the canonical mechanism for caspase activation, particularly for the catalytic domains (50–53). However, this may not apply to CARD-mediated oligomerization of inflammatory caspases. Inflammasomes such as NAIP-NLRC4, NLRP3, and CARD8 are known to form assemblies containing more than 10 CARDs (54–57). Such high-order assemblies are thought to establish a signaling threshold that filters out biological noise (58). This concept is particularly relevant for the noncanonical inflammasome as the CARD in the noncanonical inflammasome recognizes a range of phosphorylated lipids, including those found in host cell membranes (28). The requirement for lipids with at least six acyl chains to trigger noncanonical inflammasome high-ordered assembly and activation serves as a protective mechanism, ensuring selective activation in response to pathogen-associated lipids while preventing self-activation and autoimmune responses.

How the caspase-4/11 CARDs recognize LPS has remained a long-standing question. In the well-characterized LPS-TLR4-MD2 complex, the acyl chains of LPS insert into a hydrophobic cleft within MD2 (59). We propose that caspase-4/11 CARDs may use a similar mechanism. Using HDX-MS, we identified a hydrophobic pocket formed by helices α1, α2, and α5 in caspase-4/11 CARD that is critical for recognizing the acyl chains of LPS. However, a previously reported crystal structure of an MBP-tagged caspase-11 CARD suggested that this same hydrophobic surface is instead essential for oligomerization rather than LPS binding (60). We noticed that a truncated construct of caspase-11 CARD lacking the 10 flexible N-terminal residues was used in that study. Although this truncation facilitated a rigid fusion of caspase-11 CARD to the MBP tag, thus aiding crystallization, it also abolished the LPS-binding ability of the fusion protein. This is likely due to reduced flexibility between the MBP tag and the CARD, as well as disruption of the hydrophobic LPS-binding pocket caused by the artificial formation of a single extended helix from helices α1 and α2. Moreover, this truncated fusion protein failed to form octamers or higher-ordered oligomers, instead stabilizing as a tetramer even when expressed in E. coli (60). We also used an MBP tag in this study but retained the nontruncated CARD and included a flexible TEV protease cleavage site between the MBP tag and CARD, minimizing the potential for MBP-induced artifacts. This design preserved the structural and functional integrity of the CARD, as evidenced by the fact that our MBP-tagged caspase-4/11 CARDs efficiently bind LPS and form oligomers similar to their His-tagged counterparts.

We do not yet know the precise stoichiometry of the caspase-4/11 CARD/LPS-Ra, in part due to its heterogeneity, which likely reflects variation in the number of bound LPS-Ra molecules. This challenge is further compounded by the intrinsic polydispersity of LPS. Although a previous work has shown that caspase-4 CARD can disaggregate LPS micelles into smaller protein-LPS complexes via direct interactions (37), differential ionization and charge-state heterogeneity of LPS may still affect mass measurements. To mitigate these effects, we used nMS coupled with quadrupole isolation and ECCR, which collapses charge states, improves resolution, and enables quantitation of reproducible mass increments corresponding to LPS-pair additions. Nevertheless, ECCR remains a gas-phase, indirect approach. Future application of complementary methods such as charge-detection MS or surface-induced dissociation could further refine the structural characterization of these heterogeneous protein-lipid complexes.

Despite these limitations, our data support a potential interaction model in which the hydrophobic pocket of a single caspase-4/11 CARD is likely too small to accommodate all the acyl chains of one LPS molecule. It is plausible that LPS binding is coordinated by multiple CARDs, with the acyl chains from a single LPS molecule engaging several CARDs. In this model, LPS acts as a lipid-based scaffold or “molecular glue” that brings multiple CARDs together, promoting oligomerization. This model is supported by our observation that disrupting the LPS-binding capability of caspase-4/11 CARD through mutations in the hydrophobic pocket leads to a transition from oligomer to monomer when expressed in E. coli. Similar interaction models have been described for other innate immune receptors. For instance, TLR1-TLR2 heterodimerization is dependent on the triacylated lipid Pam3CSK4, in which one acyl chain inserts into TLR1 and the other two interact with TLR2 (61). Likewise, in the TLR4-MD2-LPS complex, five of the six acyl chains of LPS are buried within the MD2 pocket, whereas the sixth acyl chain is exposed to engage TLR4 from an adjacent heterodimer. As a result, penta-acylated LPS-Rs and tetra-acylated lipid IVa fail to induce the formation of the TLR4-MD2 tetramer (59). This analogy further supports our model that full engagement of no less than six acyl chains is critical for productive oligomerization in the noncanonical inflammasome.

CARD is a well-characterized protein module that plays a key role in multiple cell death pathways, including pyroptosis, necroptosis, and apoptosis. To date, hundreds of CARDs or CARD-containing proteins have been identified, most of which function as protein-protein interaction modules that mediate signalosome assembly and downstream signal transduction (62). To facilitate the formation of such signalosomes, many CARDs are known to assemble into filamentous structures (57, 63–65). Unexpectedly, despite the high sequence similarity with other CARDs, caspase-4/11 CARDs appear to form a structurally distinct assembly, where no filaments were observed. Using XL-MS, we identified several potential intersubunit cross-links in the caspase-4/11 CARD oligomer. Although we cannot precisely locate the oligomerization interfaces through these cross-linked peptides, these potential contacts, however, are not fully conserved with the well-known type I/II/III interfaces in caspase-1 CARD oligomers (fig. S4D), suggesting that caspase-4/11 CARD may use a unique assembly mechanism.

In summary, our findings reveal a lipid-driven assembly mechanism distinct from canonical filamentous CARD oligomers and provide a framework for understanding lipid-specific activation of the noncanonical inflammasome. However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which caspase-4/11 CARD engages acyl chains and undergoes oligomerization are incompletely addressed. Resolving these questions will require high-resolution structural studies of caspase-4/11 CARD oligomers in complex with various LPS species.
