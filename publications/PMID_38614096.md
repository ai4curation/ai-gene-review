Cytokines regulate immune responses by binding to cell surface receptors,
including the common subunit beta (βc), which mediates signaling for GM-CSF,
IL-3, and IL-5. Despite known roles in inflammation, the structural basis of
IL-5 receptor activation remains unclear. We present the cryo-EM structure of
the human IL-5 ternary receptor complex, revealing architectural principles for
IL-5, GM-CSF, and IL-3. In mammalian cell culture, single-molecule imaging
confirms hexameric IL-5 complex formation on cell surfaces. Engineered chimeric
receptors show that IL-5 signaling, as well as IL-3 and GM-CSF, can occur
through receptor heterodimerization, obviating the need for higher-order
assemblies of βc dimers. These findings provide insights into IL-5 and βc
receptor family signaling mechanisms, aiding in the development of therapies for
diseases involving deranged βc signaling.

Introduction

Cytokines are secreted proteins that mediate cell-cell communication in the immune system1,2. Binding of cytokines to cell surface receptors leads to activation of receptor-associated Janus Kinase (JAK) proteins which phosphorylate each other as well as downstream Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (STAT) proteins, triggering nuclear translocation and regulation of gene expression. Cytokines can be classified by their use of shared receptors which transduce signals for multiple cytokines within a family. Colony stimulating factor 2 receptor subunit beta (CSF2RB), also known as cytokine receptor common subunit beta or common beta (βc), is a shared receptor that mediates signaling of three key cytokines – granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), interleukin-3 (IL-3), and interleukin-5 (IL-5). Upon binding to their specific co-receptors (GMRα, IL-3Rα and IL-5Rα, respectively), these cytokines engage the constitutively dimeric βc to initiate downstream signaling3.

Common beta family cytokines were originally determined to play a key role in the expansion and differentiation of certain blood cells in hematopoiesis. However, these common beta family cytokines contribute more expansively to the regulation of inflammation4. These cytokines not only play a role in physiological inflammation for pathogen clearance, but a significant role in the pathophysiology of inflammatory disease. IL-5 can contribute to allergic and eosinophilic inflammatory disease through the aberrant stimulation and expansion of eosinophils4. In recent years, therapies targeting IL-5 have been approved for use in asthma and obstructive pulmonary disease4, highlighting the important role of this cytokine in human disease.

Despite the importance of IL-5 and its common beta family members in the physiology of inflammation, there remains controversy in the fundamental molecular details through which these cytokines signal5. Various unliganded and binary complex structures provided insight into the molecular mechanisms of this family6–10 and the crystal structures of the ternary GM-CSF11 and IL-312 complexes show higher-order dodecameric assemblies, leading to the proposal that further dimerization of βc dimers can drive signaling across this family. This is contradicted by earlier literature which suggested JAK recruitment to both private and common receptors13 and proposed a heteromeric signaling mechanism for the family, similar to most other JAK/STAT cytokines1,2. Given this contradiction, and the key role this family plays in the physiology of inflammation, elucidation of the specific mechanisms that drive their signaling may guide improved therapeutic development.

As a starting point for delineating the structural principles that drive signaling by the common beta receptor cytokines, we have resolved the structure of the IL-5 receptor ternary signaling complex by cryo-electron microscopy at 3.6 Å resolution. In this structure, the central IL-5 cytokine dimer is engaged on either side of one of its helical bundle regions by both βc and IL-5 cytokine homology regions, with the additional D1 domain of IL-5 receptor capping the top of the cytokine. Given that we observe only a hexameric complex, we imaged both GM-CSF and IL-3 ternary complexes and likewise observe only the corresponding hexameric ternary complexes, and not dodecamers. In addition, we look at ligand dependent IL-5 receptor assembly in live cells with single molecule imaging and further validate our understanding of the signaling competent minimal hexameric stoichiometry. Finally, we employ an ortho-chimeric receptor system which, in conjunction with live-cell JAK binding assays, establishes that βc family signaling is compatible with the canonical heteromeric receptor signaling model. This understanding of IL-5 and βc family signaling and receptor architecture provides important mechanistic clarification for the further development of βc-targeted therapies.

Dual-color SMCT revealed that IL-5Rα is monomeric in the resting state and forms a very low, but statistically significant level of homodimers upon IL-5 stimulation in the absence of βc (Figure 3E). In turn, we confirmed that βc is highly dimerized in the absence of IL-5 (Figure 3F), consistent with its intertwined dimeric structure10, which remains unaltered after the addition of IL-5. Furthermore, the presence of IL-5Rα does not influence the formation of βc dimers (Figure S2B). As expected, addition of IL-5 induces significant heterodimerization of βc and IL-5Rα as well as a modest degree of homodimerization of IL-5Rα in the presence of βc (Figure 3G,H). When comparing IL-5Rα homodimers in the absence and presence of βc, it was prominent that IL-5Rα homodimers in the absence of βc induce FRET, while the major fraction IL-5Rα homodimers in the presence of βc does not induce FRET, which is in accordance with the larger distance of the IL-5Rα subunits in the hexameric complex (Figure S2C). Assembly of a hetero-hexameric complex was furthermore supported by the diffusion properties extracted from single molecule trajectories (Figure S2E). Thus, the relatively high diffusion constant of IL-5Rα in the presence of βc strongly decreased upon stimulation with IL-5, while, under the same conditions, the relatively low diffusion constant of βc dimers hardly changed. For dodecameric complexes, however, heavily reduced mobility of βc would be expected.Given that two-color co-tracking is unable to distinguish between the formation of the lower-order hexameric IL-5 complexes and the proposed higher-order dodecameric complexes, triple-color SMCT was used to track events involving three copies of either βc (Figure 3J) or IL-5Rα (Figure 3L). Importantly, three-color SMCT of βc or IL-5R in the presence of both receptors identified only minor, and only significant for IL-5R, increased levels of higher order complex formation upon IL-5 stimulation. These values are substantially lower than values determined (~14.4 and 10.5%) for a pre-trimerized leptin receptor used as a positive control (Figure 3J,L). While this does not rule out the possibility of higher order assemblies in other cellular compartments, these results, in conjunction with the dual-color smFRET experiments, strongly support the physiological prevalence of IL-5 induced tetramers and hexamers at the plasma membrane, consistent with the stoichiometry seen in our cryoEM samples.Association of JAKs to common and private receptors of the βc familyIn JAK/STAT cytokine signaling, two JAKs are required to be present and within reach of one another to properly trans-activate and initiate signaling. In our proposed ‘canonical’ hexameric signaling mechanism, JAK is required on both the private and common receptor chains that are in close proximity after cytokine binding and complex formation, as reported for other JAK/STAT cytokine receptor dimers. A main tenet of the dodecameric signaling model is that the distance of JAKs in βc dimers is too great to enable activation unless a higher order complex that results in two βc being within close proximity to allow βc-associated JAK trans-activation is formed. This model assumed that βc family private receptors cannot engage JAK molecules, which has been controversial5,11. If this would be the case, βc-βc would seem to be the only possible way to activate JAK signaling by these cytokines. We stress-tested this issue.We sought to test whether JAKs are constitutively associated with IL-5 receptor subunits in a live cell environment. To address this, we adopted the approach of live cell micropatterning, a technique that facilitates robust quantification of interactions by capturing bait proteins in the plasma membrane into defined patterns24. To this end, HeLa cells transiently transfected with the various private (IL-5Rα, IL-3Rα, GM-CSFRα) and common (βc) receptors fused to an extracellular HaloTag-mTagBFP were cultured on HaloTag ligand (HTL)-micropatterned surfaces (Figure 4J, S3). This led to the capture and spatially reorganization of the HaloTag-mTagBFP-receptor within the cell membrane. To evaluate the interactions between JAKs and their respective receptors, we introduced JAK-FS constructs, comprising the FERM/SH2 domain of JAK1, JAK2, or TYK2 fused with mEGFP. The JAK-FS domain selectively interacts with box 1 and box 2 motifs on the receptor, making it an ideal tool to explore JAK-receptor interactions. The relative binding affinity was then estimated from the relative contrast of the JAK-FS micropatterns. Strikingly, we observe that all three private receptors are able to bind JAKs significantly above baseline (Figure 4K). IL-5Rα, IL-3Rα, and GM-CSFRα are all seen to preferentially associate with JAK2, with some promiscuity observed for IL-5Rα (JAK1) and IL-3Rα (TYK2). In contrast, βc exhibited strongest association with JAK1, although substantial binding was also seen with JAK2 and TYK2. We further validated the non-canonical involvement of JAK1 and TYK2 in IL-5 signaling via immunoprecipitation and subsequent Western Blotting (Figure S4). These findings corroborate that the βc private receptors can, indeed, engage JAKs.Heterodimeric receptor complexes drive STAT5 signalingGiven both βc common and private receptors can engage JAKs, signaling via the hexameric complex can be readily envisaged. We therefore explored whether IL-5 signaling could be driven by heteromeric receptor dimerization between βc and its private receptor, as occurs with other JAK/STAT cytokines. To this end, we developed a chimeric receptor platform to test receptor ICD heterodimerization in a controlled manner. Building upon our work on orthologous IL-2 signaling mechanisms25,26, we engineered an ortho-chimeric receptor system to dimerize IL-5 receptor and βc ICDs with 1:1 stoichiometry. We achieve this by fusing the human ortho-IL-2Rβ26 ECD to the transmembrane region (TM) and ICD of βc and the ECD of common gamma (γc) to the TM and ICD of each of the private receptors (IL-5Rα, IL-3Rα, GM-CSFRα) (Figure 4D–F). Since this receptor system is not fully orthogonal on cells which natively express γc, we performed these assays on HEK 293 cells constitutively expressing STAT5b, allowing for fully functional, orthogonal signaling in response to the addition of human ortho-IL-2. In this system, ortho-IL-2 elicits dose-dependent signaling across all three chimeras (Figure 4G–I). In the absence of signaling output from either individual receptor in response to the cytokine, we attribute this signaling to the heterodimerization of βc and private receptor (IL-5Rα, IL-3Rα, GM-CSFRα) ICDs. These results support a model of βc family signaling through heterodimerization of the private receptors with βc, which is achieved in the minimal hexameric signaling complexes resolved by cryoEM.

CryoEM analysis of βc family receptor complexes

Since the discovery of the common beta family cytokines in the 1980s, ternary complex structures for both monomeric cytokines of this family (GM-CSF and IL-3) have been determined11,12. However, the structure of the complex involving the dimeric cytokine, IL-5, has remained recalcitrant to crystallization, with only binary complex components being solved to date14,15. While this complexity and likely flexibility is a major roadblock for structural characterization via X-ray crystallography, cryoEM is an alternative approach to solving the structures of cytokine signaling complexes that had previously eluded structural characterization16–20. Here, we used this technique to characterize the structure of the extracellular domains of two βc and two IL-5Rα bound to two dimers of IL-5 (a 2:2:2 hexamer when defining the dimeric IL-5 as a single entity, per established naming convention) transiently expressed from Expi293 (HEK 293) cells (Figure 1A). We then used reference-free 2D averaging to characterize the architecture of the resultant hexameric assembly (Figure 1B). In addition, focused refinement of the cytokine binding regions at each end of the hexameric complex resolved a 3.6 Å reconstruction of the 1:1:1 βc:IL-5Rα:IL-5 ternary complex (Figure 1C, S1).

At the cytokine binding region of the IL-5 complex, IL-5Rα and βc bind on either side of one helical bundle of IL-5. A second pair of receptors is sterically precluded from binding at the other side of IL-5 by the bound receptors. While this complex has many architectural similarities to that of other cytokine families, indicative of conserved structural themes which occur across cytokine signaling, there are a few features of the IL-5 complex that are distinct to the βc family and IL-5.

First, the domain swapped nature of βc results in the contribution of domains from both monomers of the dimeric βc to binding on each flank (Figure 1C). On each side of the hexamer, the D1 domain of the distal βc monomer reaches across to form the upper portion of the conserved cytokine homology region (CHR), while the lower domain of the CHR is contributed by the proximal βc. This CHR binds the helical bundle of one end of IL-5 at its canonical hinge region, forming interactions largely with helices 2 and 3 of IL-5 (Figure 1D). In this interaction, IL-5 helix 2 largely interacts with the distal βc D1 domain, with charge complementarity provided by IL-5 R87 and βc D104, with the additional support from a few hydrogen bonds at the periphery of the interface (Figure 1D). In turn, the proximal βc D4 domain largely interacts with IL-5 helix 1, with βc tyrosines 365 and 421 packing against E33 of the cytokine.

Second, IL-5Rα binds not only at the hinge of its conserved CHR, formed between D2 and D3, but also with the additional D1 Ig domain which hinges over top of the cytokine to cap the interaction (Figure 1E), similar to IL-13 receptor complexes21. Whereas βc interacts solely with one monomer of the dimeric IL-5, IL-5Rα interacts equally with both monomers. Once again, at the CHR interaction, the receptor is interfacing largely with the helical regions of the cytokine, interacting specifically with helix 3 of the same monomer that βc binds and the domain swapped helix 4 of the other monomer (Figure 1E). The Ig domain interacts largely with the loop regions of the cytokine, binding to the 1–2 loop of the first monomer and the 3–4 loop of the second monomer in a largely charge-mediated manner with IL-5 contributing 4 aspartic acid residues to the interface (Figure 1F).

Previous structural studies of the related βc family members GM-CSF and IL-3 bound to their receptors found that these complexes ‘double’ to form higher order dodecamers in the crystal lattice11,12. However, we did not observe the equivalent dodecamer complex formation by IL-5 when analyzed using cryoEM. To more thoroughly assess this for other βc family members, we asked whether imaging of GM-CSF and IL-3 complexes formed detectable dodecamers in a setting unconstrained by crystallographic contacts. We assembled and analyzed these receptor complexes using cryo-EM (Figure 2A, S1). As with IL-5, under these conditions IL-3 and GMCSF exclusively form hexameric complexes with their receptors, with the distal D1, proximal D4 CHR binding on one face of the cytokine, and the D2/D3 CHR of the private receptor binding at the other face, capped by the D1 Ig domain (Figure 2B, C). This cryoEM data suggests the extracellular domains from members of this family form lower-order hexameric complexes.

Association of JAKs to common and private receptors of the βc familyIn JAK/STAT cytokine signaling, two JAKs are required to be present and within reach of one another to properly trans-activate and initiate signaling. In our proposed ‘canonical’ hexameric signaling mechanism, JAK is required on both the private and common receptor chains that are in close proximity after cytokine binding and complex formation, as reported for other JAK/STAT cytokine receptor dimers. A main tenet of the dodecameric signaling model is that the distance of JAKs in βc dimers is too great to enable activation unless a higher order complex that results in two βc being within close proximity to allow βc-associated JAK trans-activation is formed. This model assumed that βc family private receptors cannot engage JAK molecules, which has been controversial5,11. To address this, we adopted the approach of live cell micropatterning, a technique that facilitates robust quantification of interactions by capturing bait proteins in the plasma membrane into defined patterns24. To this end, HeLa cells transiently transfected with the various private (IL-5Rα, IL-3Rα, GM-CSFRα) and common (βc) receptors fused to an extracellular HaloTag-mTagBFP were cultured on HaloTag ligand (HTL)-micropatterned surfaces (Figure 4J, S3). The use of shared receptor components to mediate diverse signaling outputs is a key principle in cytokine biology. The common beta cytokine family is a paradigm, with the shared use of βc to modulate expansion, activation, and regulation of inflammation across a diverse array of cell types. Here we show that the dimeric cytokine IL-5 recruits βc and IL-5Rα to form a hetero-hexameric signaling complex and that this structural organisation is conserved for the family members IL-3 and GM-CSF. The current model of βc signaling proposes that the minimal hexameric complexes of βc cytokines are not signaling competent, but rather require association into dodecamers to enable JAK activation11,27. This model assumed that βc private receptors did not efficiently engage JAKs. Here we demonstrate that βc private receptors recruit JAKs and that 1:1 heterodimers between βc private receptors and βc in hexameric complexes represent the minimal necessary and sufficient unit of signaling.

While we have provided an array of evidence for the formation of these heteromeric signaling competent complexes with our electron microscopy, chimeric signaling assays, and live cell imaging assays, this is in turn supported by the literature. The formation of these heteromeric, signaling competent forms was largely the prevailingly proposed mechanism for these receptors prior to the initial structural characterization of ternary complexes11. In the case of IL-5 signaling, it has been shown that JAK2 and JAK1 are constitutively associated with IL-5Rα and βc13, which correlates with what we observed in our live-cell micropatterning experiments for these receptors. At the time of these prior studies, the specific nature of JAK binding to cytokine receptors was poorly resolved. Over time, the characteristic box 1/2 regions, which bind JAK FERM and SH2 domains, were structurally resolved28,29 and these regions can now be predicted readily through curation of sequence alignments of the regions in question30. Indeed, Ferrao and Lupardus include all three common beta family private receptors amongst their alignment of canonical box 1/2 regions in cytokine receptors.

While our current analysis does not preclude the possibility of a small population of higher order complexes, we propose that the large excess of hexamer formed at the cell surface is responsible for initiation of JAK/STAT signaling. However, we cannot exclude that weak interactions between hexameric complexes may occur in other cellular compartments, e.g. in early endosomes, which have been proposed to accumulate and cluster cytokine receptor signaling complexes and promote downstream signaling31–33. Further experimentation is required to determine if there is a physiological role for this potential sub-population, perhaps as a regulatory mechanism or in alternate pathways that can be potentiated by the βc family and thus enhance plasticity of βc cytokine signaling as recently proposed for IL-312.

Heteromeric signaling in the common beta family is similar to the mechanism used in the common gamma receptor family. In common gamma signaling, the common gamma receptor (γc) constitutively binds JAK3, while the various private receptors (IL-2Rβ, IL-4R, IL-7R, IL-9R, IL-21R) constitutively bind JAK1 and have STAT binding sites for downstream signaling. This is the inverse system to common beta family signaling, with common gamma family having the STAT binding sites, which drive downstream signaling, on the private receptors. In contrast to common beta signaling, this allows for the use of a diverse array of STAT signaling across the family, and the ability to exert differential effects upon a single cell type. In contrast, with the STAT binding site on the βc receptor in common beta signaling, the diversity of signaling output is limited by differential expression of the private receptors across cell types, allowing for differential regulation of distinct cell subsets, but not on within a singular cell.

As in other types of cytokine signaling, there is context-dependent therapeutic potential in both the inhibition and potentiation of common beta family signaling. Due to the role these cytokines play in the expansion and differentiation of cellular subsets, the use of recombinantly expressed cytokine has been explored for its therapeutic benefit. Given the relatively targeted activity of IL-5 on eosinophils, its use has largely been excluded, while IL-3 and GM-CSF have been explored in the clinic. For example, sargramostim, a recombinant GM-CSF, has been approved for use in cases of myelosuppression4. On the other side of the dichotomy, inhibition of all three family members has been explored in detail, with multiple successes currently in the clinic. In the case of IL-5, there are two blocking antibodies that bind IL-5 to inhibit signaling (mepolizumab and reslizumab) and one blocking antibody that binds IL-5Rα (benralizumab), all three of which are approved for use in eosinophilic asthma. Given the extent of exploration in the therapeutic inhibition of common beta family signaling and the relative lack of potentiation strategies beyond the use of recombinant natural cytokines, there may be unexplored avenues for the potentiation of common beta signaling by modulating receptor binding affinities and dynamics.

Resolution of the IL-5 receptor complex and the heteromeric signaling of the common beta family reveals the molecular basis for how these therapeutically important cytokines signal. The hexameric assemblies of this family form on live cells and signal in the absence of higher order dodecamers. This structural and functional understanding of IL-5 and common beta signaling provides a platform for the further development of therapies that modulate the common beta receptor family, from therapeutics treating myelosuppression to inhibitors of aberrant inflammation.

Limitations of the StudyUsing both CryoEM and single-molecule imaging on live cells, we observed that IL-5 associates with IL-5Rα and βc in almost exclusively hexameric form. It is possible that cryoEM is not conducive to higher order dodecamer complex formation that may otherwise be observed via crystallography. While we show that our single-molecule imaging is very sensitive to tracking oligomer formation, it is possible that there are levels of dodecameric assemblies on the cell surface which are unobserved. Additionally, given our focus on the events occurring at the cellular membrane to initiate signaling, we cannot preclude a potential role for the dodecamer in intracellular mechanisms such as in the early endosome.

frelD=NdNm
Similarly, for homodimerization experiments, the relative dimeric fraction (frelD) can be calculated from the average number of monomeric receptors (Nm) as well as the average number of receptors being part of dimers (Nd):

frelD=NdNd+Nm
A certain fraction of receptors being part of dimers are labelled with the same dye (false-positive monomers), and thus are not directly countable in our assay. We aim to correct Nd for these receptors. Therefore, Nd can be described by the total number of detected dimers nd12* co-localized in channel 1 and 2 plus the total number of undetectable dimers in channel 1 (nd11) and channel 2 (nd22):

Nd=2(nd12*+nd11+nd22)
Here, we have a factor of 2 because we count dimers with nd, but Nd stands for monomers being part of dimers.For statistical correction for those dimers that are labelled with the same color (nd11; nd22), the overall channel distribution of emitters needs to be considered and a labeling ratio needs to be estimated (rl):

rl=nm1*nm1*+nm2*
With nm1*, total number of detected emitters in channel 1, and nm2*, total number of detected emitters in channel 2.Based on labeling probability and combinatorics, one can derive the following equation for the number of emitters being part of dimers without having to determine nd11 and nd22:

Nd=nd12*rl(1−rl)
Following the same principle, the total number of monomers Nm can be described by subtracting the total number of emitters being part of dimers from all detected emitters:

Nm=nm1*+nm2*−nd12**(1+12rl*(1−rl))
For Trimerization experiments, the relative trimeric fraction (frelT) can be calculated from the average number of monomeric receptors (Nm) as well as the average number of receptors being part of trimers (Nt):

frelT=NtNt+Nm
The total number of emitters being part of trimers can be estimated from the number of detected trimers nt123* (labelled with three different dyes) and the number of undetectable trimers (nt) (labeled with at least two of the same dye):

Nt=3(nt123*+nt111+nt222+nt333+nt112+nt122+nt113+nt133+nt223+nt233)
For statistical correction for those trimers that are labelled with the same color two times or three times (nt), one again needs to take the overall channel distribution of emitters into account and estimate a labeling ratio (ri):

ri=nmi*nm1*+nm2*+nm3*
Based on the labeling ratios for all three channels r1, r2 and r3, the total number of trimers can be derived by labeling probabilities and combinatorics, leading to:

Nt=nt123*2r1r2r3
To estimate the total number of monomeric receptors (Nm), the observed data needs to be corrected for false-positive monomers based on falsely labeled dimers and trimers. The total number of dimers needs to be calculated beforehand:

Nd=2(nd11+nd22+nd33+nd12+nd13+nd23) with ndij=ndij*−ntiij−ntijj
Taking the labeling ratios into account, the total number of dimers can thus be described as:

Nd=ndij*ri⋅rj−(ri+rj)2⋅r1⋅r2⋅r3nt123*
With ndij* representing the detected dimers of a random channel pair (e.g. 560 and 640) and ri and rj representing the respective labeling ratios for these channels.For the estimation of the total numbers of monomers, the total number of dimers and trimers needs to be subtracted from all detected receptors:

Nm=nm1*+nm2*+nm3*−12(ndij*ri⋅rj−(ri+rj)2⋅r1⋅r2⋅r3nt123*)−nd12*−nd13*−nd23*−nt123*(2+16r1⋅r2⋅r3−(r1+r2)2r3−(r1+r3)2r2+(r2+r3)2r1)
Analysis of live cell micropatterning experimentsImage analysis and image processing were performed using ImageJ/ Fiji (NIH, Bethesda, MD). Image processing comprised cropping, scaling, rotation as well as adjustment of brightness and contrast levels. The fluorescence contrast of the bait proteins (Cbait) was calculated as:

Cbait=(Ibait,in−Ibait,out)(Ibait,in−Ibg)

where Ibait,in and Ibait,out denote the mean pixel intensities from selected ROIs inside and outside, respectively, of the HTL-functionalized areas and Ibg the background intensity from the glass surface obtained from a ROI outside the region covered by the cells. The contrast of the prey proteins Cprey was accordingly obtained from the background corrected mean pixel intensities from selected ROIs inside and outside HTL-functionalized areas:

Cprey=(Iprey,in−Ibg)(Ipray,out−Ibg)
Since Cprey is dependent on Cbait, Cprey was corrected for variances in Cbait by dividing Cprey by Cbait:

Cprey,corr=CpreyCbait
For statistical analysis, multiple cells were analyzed and the calculated contrast values were visualized in a box plot. Two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov-Tests were performed to calculate statistical significances.

Quantification and Statistical AnalysisQuantification of single molecule tracking experimentsOverall, the value displayed on the y-axis of the single molecule experiments represents the fraction of detected receptors (particles) being part of dimers or higher order complexes.For heterodimerization experiments, the relative dimeric fraction can be calculated from the average number of one of the monomeric receptors (Nm) and the average number of receptors being part of dimers:

frelD=NdNm
Similarly, for homodimerization experiments, the relative dimeric fraction (frelD) can be calculated from the average number of monomeric receptors (Nm) as well as the average number of receptors being part of dimers (Nd):

frelD=NdNd+Nm
A certain fraction of receptors being part of dimers are labelled with the same dye (false-positive monomers), and thus are not directly countable in our assay. We aim to correct Nd for these receptors. Therefore, Nd can be described by the total number of detected dimers nd12* co-localized in channel 1 and 2 plus the total number of undetectable dimers in channel 1 (nd11) and channel 2 (nd22):

Nd=2(nd12*+nd11+nd22)
Here, we have a factor of 2 because we count dimers with nd, but Nd stands for monomers being part of dimers.For statistical correction for those dimers that are labelled with the same color (nd11; nd22), the overall channel distribution of emitters needs to be considered and a labeling ratio needs to be estimated (rl):

rl=nm1*nm1*+nm2*
With nm1*, total number of detected emitters in channel 1, and nm2*, total number of detected emitters in channel 2.Based on labeling probability and combinatorics, one can derive the following equation for the number of emitters being part of dimers without having to determine nd11 and nd22:

Nd=nd12*rl(1−rl)
Following the same principle, the total number of monomers Nm can be described by subtracting the total number of emitters being part of dimers from all detected emitters:

Nm=nm1*+nm2*−nd12**(1+12rl*(1−rl))
For Trimerization experiments, the relative trimeric fraction (frelT) can be calculated from the average number of monomeric receptors (Nm) as well as the average number of receptors being part of trimers (Nt):

frelT=NtNt+Nm
The total number of emitters being part of trimers can be estimated from the number of detected trimers nt123* (labelled with three different dyes) and the number of undetectable trimers (nt) (labeled with at least two of the same dye):

Nt=3(nt123*+nt111+nt222+nt333+nt112+nt122+nt113+nt133+nt223+nt233)
For statistical correction for those trimers that are labelled with the same color two times or three times (nt), one again needs to take the overall channel distribution of emitters into account and estimate a labeling ratio (ri):

ri=nmi*nm1*+nm2*+nm3*
Based on the labeling ratios for all three channels r1, r2 and r3, the total number of trimers can be derived by labeling probabilities and combinatorics, leading to:

Nt=nt123*2r1r2r3
To estimate the total number of monomeric receptors (Nm), the observed data needs to be corrected for false-positive monomers based on falsely labeled dimers and trimers. The total number of dimers needs to be calculated beforehand:

Nd=2(nd11+nd22+nd33+nd12+nd13+nd23) with ndij=ndij*−ntiij−ntijj
Taking the labeling ratios into account, the total number of dimers can thus be described as:

Nd=ndij*ri⋅rj−(ri+rj)2⋅r1⋅r2⋅r3nt123*
With ndij* representing the detected dimers of a random channel pair (e.g. 560 and 640) and ri and rj representing the respective labeling ratios for these channels.For the estimation of the total numbers of monomers, the total number of dimers and trimers needs to be subtracted from all detected receptors:

Nm=nm1*+nm2*+nm3*−12(ndij*ri⋅rj−(ri+rj)2⋅r1⋅r2⋅r3nt123*)−nd12*−nd13*−nd23*−nt123*(2+16r1⋅r2⋅r3−(r1+r2)2r3−(r1+r3)2r2+(r2+r3)2r1)

Analysis of live cell micropatterning experimentsImage analysis and image processing were performed using ImageJ/ Fiji (NIH, Bethesda, MD). Image processing comprised cropping, scaling, rotation as well as adjustment of brightness and contrast levels. The fluorescence contrast of the bait proteins (Cbait) was calculated as:

Cbait=(Ibait,in−Ibait,out)(Ibait,in−Ibg)

where Ibait,in and Ibait,out denote the mean pixel intensities from selected ROIs inside and outside, respectively, of the HTL-functionalized areas and Ibg the background intensity from the glass surface obtained from a ROI outside the region covered by the cells. The contrast of the prey proteins Cprey was accordingly obtained from the background corrected mean pixel intensities from selected ROIs inside and outside HTL-functionalized areas:

Cprey=(Iprey,in−Ibg)(Ipray,out−Ibg)
Since Cprey is dependent on Cbait, Cprey was corrected for variances in Cbait by dividing Cprey by Cbait:

Cprey,corr=CpreyCbait
For statistical analysis, multiple cells were analyzed and the calculated contrast values were visualized in a box plot. Two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov-Tests were performed to calculate statistical significances.
