Abstract

Mutated in colorectal cancer (MCC ) was originally identified as a candidate gene for familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) but further study identified adenomatous polyposis coli ( APC ) as responsible for FAP and the physiologic/pathologic roles of MCC remained poorly understood. Recently, MCC promoter methylation was discovered as a frequent early event in a distinct subset of precursor lesions and colorectal cancer (CRC) associated with the serrated CRC pathway. Here we provide the first evidence of the biological significance of MCC loss in CRC and the molecular pathways involved. We show MCC expression is dramatically decreased in many CRC cell lines and the distinct subset of sporadic CRC characterized by the CpG island methylator phenotype and BRAF V600E mutation due to promoter methylation as reported previously. Importantly, we find MCC interacts with β-catenin and that re-expression of MCC in CRC cells specifically inhibits Wnt signaling, β-catenin/T-cell factor/lymphoid-enhancer factor-dependent transcription and cellular proliferation even in the presence of oncogenic mutant APC. We also show that MCC is localized the nucleus and identify two functional nuclear localization signals. Taken together, MCC is a nuclear, β-catenin-interacting protein that can act as a potential tumor suppressor in the serrated CRC pathway by inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin signal transduction.

Keywords: colorectal cancer, MCC, Wnt, β-catenin, serrated

Introduction

The mutated in colorectal cancer ( MCC ) gene was originally isolated as a candidate tumor suppressor for familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) ( Ashton-Rickardt et al., 1991 ; Kinzler et al., 1991b ). However, further studies revealed that the mutation of the adenomatous polyposis coli ( APC ) gene and not MCC is responsible for FAP and the initiation of the majority of sporadic colorectal cancer (CRC) ( Groden et al., 1991 ; Kinzler et al., 1991a ; Nishisho et al., 1991 ). Subsequently, few studies on the physiologic and/or pathologic roles of MCC were carried out.

MCC codes for a protein of 829 amino acids that is highly conserved across many species but has little homology to other known proteins. The three previous studies hinted that MCC may play an important role in several cellular processes involved in the development of cancer ( Bouwmeester et al., 2004 ; Matsumine et al., 1996 ; Senda et al., 1999 ). MCC was previously found to be localized to the cytoplasm and membrane-cytoskeletal components ( Senda et al., 1999 ). Also both MCC and MCC2 , the only MCC homologous gene isolated, contain a C-terminal PSD-95/Discs-large/ZO-1 (PDZ)-domain-binding ligand sequence ( Ishikawa et al., 2001 ; Nourry et al., 2003 ; Senda et al., 1999 ). MCC was also found to negatively regulate cell cycle in NIH3T3 cells ( Matsumine et al., 1996 ), suggesting a role of MCC in cell growth regulation. Moreover, MCC is highly expressed in murine colonic surface epithelial cells and other types of well-differentiated cells ( Matsumine et al., 1996 ; Senda et al., 1999 ) indicating a potential general physiologic role for MCC in cell differentiation. Most recently, MCC was found to play a possible role in the negative regulation of the nuclear factor kappa B (NFκB) pathway ( Bouwmeester et al., 2004 ) which is known to play important roles in CRC pathology ( Agarwal et al., 2005 ; Fukuyama et al., 2006 ; Greten et al., 2004 ; Luo et al., 2004 ).

Little is known of a specific role of MCC in CRC. Although an presumed inactivating MCC mutation in the mouse alone failed to induce any evident CRC, the homozygous mice displayed a slightly higher proliferation rate of the epithelial crypt cells ( Heyer et al., 1999 ). In earlier studies, loss of heterozygosity (LOH), deletions, and mutations of the MCC gene were found in human CRC ( Ashton-Rickardt et al., 1991 ; Kinzler et al., 1991a ; Kinzler et al., 1991b ; Nishisho et al., 1991 ). Most recently, promoter methylation of MCC was found to be a frequent early event in colorectal cancer. MCC promoter methylation was highly associated with a distinct subset of CRC characterized by the CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP) and BRAF V600E mutation ( Kohonen-Corish et al., 2007 ) which constitute the serrated CRC pathway. Serrated CRC comprises a morphologically and genetically distinct group of CRC and precursor lesions in which CRC development and progression appear driven by an alternative molecular pathway ( Jass et al., 2002 ). Cumulatively, this evidence highly favors a role of MCC in CRC carcinogenesis. However, currently almost nothing is known about the signal transduction pathways that MCC participates in that are biologically significant to MCC loss in CRC. From these previous reports and our observation that many CRC cell lines lack MCC expression, we hypothesized that MCC might possess significant, undiscovered physiological/pathological roles in colorectal epithelial cell biology and CRC tumorigenesis. Here for the first time, we show that MCC is present in the nucleus of cells and interacts with β-catenin. Importantly, we provide the first evidence that MCC can function as a tumor suppressor in CRC by suppressing Wnt signaling, β-catenin/T-cell factor (TCF)/lymphoid-enhancer factor (LEF)-dependent transcription and cellular proliferation even in the presence mutation of the master negative regulator of Wnt signaling in the intestinal epithelium, APC.

Results

MCC is lost by promoter methylation in CIMP+ sporadic CRC tumors and cell lines and is associated with BRAF mutation

As a previous study had found a significant correlation of MCC promoter methylation to CIMP and BRAF V600E mutation ( Kohonen-Corish et al., 2007 ), we decided to analyze MCC promoter methylation quantitatively in a separate independent cohort of 24 CIMP positive (CIMP +) and 24 CIMP negative (CIMP -) colorectal tumors from a consecutive series of colorectal cancer patients of with known CIMP and BRAF/KRAS mutation status. MCC methylation was detected in 28 out of 48 sporadic cancer specimens (58.3%). MCC methylation was detected in all but 2 CIMP + tumors (P < 0.0001), in all 13 tumors with the BRAF V600E mutation (P=.0001), and in all but 1 MSI-H tumors (P=.0005) ( Table 1 ). There was no correlation with the presence of KRAS mutation. Our results are in strong agreement with the previous study and add further support of the strong association of MCC promoter methylation with this distinct type of CRC characterized as CIMP +, MSI-H, with BRAF V600E mutation.

Six of eight human CRC lines tested including RKO, DLD-1, Caco-2, and HT29 ( Fig. 1A ) as well as Ls174T and SW48 (data not shown) had little detectable expression of MCC irrespective to the mutational or LOH status of the APC gene ( Fig. 1A ). Only the HCT116 and SW480 CRC cells express detectable levels of MCC ( Fig. 1A ). As, MCC promoter methylation is responsible for MCC loss in CIMP + and BRAF mutated cases of primary CRC ( Kohonen-Corish et al., 2007 ), we investigated whether MCC promoter methylation was responsible for the loss of MCC expression seen in our panel of CRC cell lines. Indeed, MCC promoter methylation correlated well to loss MCC protein expression in the CRC cell lines consistent with the previous study ( Fig. 1B ). It is noteworthy that MCC expression was lost in both the CRC cell lines, RKO and HT29, which have the BRAF V600E mutation mirroring the association seen in clinical samples.

Figure 1. MCC is found in both the cytoplasm and nucleus but lost in many CRC cell lines by promoter methylation.

Nuclear localization of MCC

Interestingly in cells that express MCC, we detected MCC in both the cytoplasm and nucleus ( Fig. 1A and Supplemental Fig 1 ). The nuclear localization of MCC had not been previously reported. A motif search of MCC revealed intriguing amino acid stretches including several potential nuclear localization signals (NLSs) and nuclear exclusion signals (NESs), as well as, a typical tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2)-binding sequence, a serine-rich domain, and a PDZ domain-binding motif ( Fig. 1D ). First, we determined the domains of MCC responsible for its nuclear localization. Both N -terminally (ΔN279 [not shown], ΔN536) and C -terminally (ΔC11, ΔC67, ΔC266) truncated MCC proteins were able to localize to both the cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions ( Fig. 1C ). However the central domain (279 - 536 a.a.) of MCC containing a putative NES but no putative NLSs, is found exclusively in cytoplasmic fraction ( Fig. 1C ). This data overall indicates that the N- and C-termini of MCC likely includes both NLS and NES sequences while the central 1/3 of MCC has no NLS sequence ( Fig. 1D ). Immunofluorescent staining confirmed the subcellular localization of the various truncated MCC proteins in COS and RKO cells (data not shown).

The potential NLS sequences of MCC include a classical bipartite NLS with an amino acid sequence quite similar to that of p53 and other known nuclear localized proteins ( Fig. 2A ). Moreover, several more NLS-like and possible NES-like sequences are present in MCC ( Fig. 2A ). We tested whether the putative NLSs in MCC are in fact functional. Both the bipartite NLS (766-782) and N -terminal arginine-rich stretch (R-rich NLS, 267-278) of MCC confer strong nuclear localization in the RKO ( Fig. 2B ) and COS7 cells (data not shown) when linked to EGFP. Furthermore, treatment of RKO cells expressing the full length MCC with the active nuclear export inhibitor, leptomycin B (LMB), dramatically increases nuclear localization of MCC ( Fig. 2C ). These studies strongly suggest that besides being a cytoplasmic protein, endogenous MCC also resides in the nucleus of the cell, contains 2 functional NLS sequences, and that MCC is likely shuttled in and out of nucleus by the basic importin-exportin-dependent mechanism.

Figure 2. Functional confirmation of the biNLS and arginine-rich NLS sequences of MCC.

MCC suppresses both oncogenic and Wnt-stimulated β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent transcription in CRC cells

As MCC had previously been described to be a potential inhibitor of the NFκB transcription factor ( Bouwmeester et al., 2004 ), we decided to investigate the potential MCC regulation of several important transcription factors in CRC including of NFκB and β-catenin. Transient MCC re-expression significantly suppressed both the low basal and the high Wnt3a-stimulated β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity in RKO cells in a dose-dependent manner ( Fig. 3A, B ). Importantly, MCC also significantly suppressed even the strong basal β-catenin/TCF/LEF activity in DLD-1 ( Fig. 3A ) and SW480 ( Fig. 3C ) cells caused by oncogenic APC mutations. This suppression of β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity is specific, as surprisingly the high constitutive NFκB-dependent promoter activity was not altered by transient MCC expression in any of the CRC cell lines ( Fig. 3A and data not shown). Additionally, stable expression of MCC significantly reduces the strong basal β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity in the SW480 MCC over-expressing cell lines as well as the Wnt3a-stimulated β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity of the MEF MCC over-expressing cell lines as compared to parental control cells ( Fig. 3C and data not shown). Transient and stable expression of MCC also suppressed the basal and Wnt3a-stimulated β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity of RKO and HT29 CRC ( Fig. 3A, 3B and data not shown). The MCC-mediated suppression of β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity was specifically relieved by MCC siRNA treatment of both parental and MCC over-expressing SW480 cells which exclusively suppressed MCC protein levels and correspondingly increased β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity as well as the β-catenin transcriptional targets, c-myc and cyclin D ( Fig. 3D and data not shown).

Figure 3. MCC specifically suppresses β-catenin/TCF/LEF transcriptional activity.

MCC interacts with β-catenin, blocks β-catenin/TCF/LEF DNA binding, and can affect endogenous β-catenin localization/levels

Stable expression of MCC specifically suppresses β-catenin/TCF/LEF but not SRE and AP-1 DNA-binding activity in CRC cells ( Fig. 4A , left panel). Also, as expected from the unaltered NFκB-dependent promoter activity, the basal and TNFα-induced NFκB-DNA binding activity in MCC over-expressing stable cell clones is not significantly changed (data not shown). Western analysis demonstrated that stable expression of MCC does not significantly reduce nuclear β-catenin levels in SW480 but does increase the cytoplasmic levels of β-catenin ( Fig. 4B ). Additionally confocal analysis of MCC and β-catenin localization in the MCC over-expressing SW480 and RKO cells demonstrated that MCC is localized in both the cytoplasm and nucleus and that β-catenin is mostly excluded from the nucleus in these cells ( Supplemental Figure #1 ). As expected, when MCC mRNA and protein levels are reduced by siRNA, the β-catenin/TCF/LEF but not the AP-1 DNA binding is dramatically increased in both the parental and MCC over-expressing SW480 cells ( Fig. 4A , right panel) as expected from the increase β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity observed with MCC siRNA ( Fig. 3D ). We found that endogenous MCC interacts specifically with endogenous β-catenin with interaction enhanced upon Wnt stimulation in MEF cells ( Fig. 4C ). Finally, transient over-expression of MCC but not p65 NFκB in COS-7 cells decreases total β-catenin by 35-52% depending on MCC expression levels while not altering the level of ERK or β-actin ( Fig. 4D ). These results indicate that MCC interacts with β-catenin and specifically suppresses β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent transcriptional activity downstream to the destruction complex by interfering with the DNA-binding activity of β-catenin/TCF/LEF in the nucleus potentially through re-localization of β-catenin to the cytoplasm and indirectly increasing its degradation.

Figure 4. MCC interacts with β-catenin and inhibits β-catenin/TCF/LEF DNA binding.

MCC domain/s responsible for suppression of β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent transcription

N-terminal deletions of MCC uncovered both a significant (^^^) domain (130-278 aa segment) and a minor (^) domain (279-536 aa segment) necessary for repressing β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent transcription ( Fig. 5A, B ). Suppression of β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity by the removal of the C -terminal segments (ΔC293, ΔC65) is not as extensive as the full length or the ΔN130 MCC constructs ( Fig. 5A, B ). This data indicates that there is a second major cooperating domain (###) at MCC’s C -terminus that is necessary along with the N -terminally located domain (^^^) to suppress β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity. The most significant functional segments of MCC, 130-278 aa (^^^) and 536-829 aa (###), for repression of β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity contain putative NES sequences as well as the arginine-rich NLS-like stretch (striped box) and the biNLS (speckled box) respectively. This data indicates that MCC repression of β-catenin/TCF/LEF transcriptional activity may require MCC functionally capable of proper nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling.

Figure 5. MCC domains necessary for β-catenin/TCF/LEF transcriptional suppression.

MCC inhibits basal and Wnt3a-stimulated CRC cell proliferation

Matsumine et a l. previously demonstrated that transient MCC over-expression suppressed cell growth in NIH3T3 murine fibroblast cells ( Matsumine et al., 1996 ). Stable expression of MCC in MEFs, MBA-MD-231 breast cancer, as well as RKO, SW480, and HT29 CRC cells suppresses basal proliferation ( Fig. 6A, B and data not shown). Stable expression of MCC also suppressed Wnt3a-stimulated proliferation of SW480, MEFs, MBA-MD-231, and RKO cells ( Fig. 6C ). Intriguingly, both the SW480 and HT29 CRC cells have constitutively transcriptionally active β-catenin as a result of oncogenic mutations in APC indicating that MCC can act as a suppressor of cellular proliferation even in the presence of these oncogenic Wnt pathway mutations.

Discussion

In contrast to APC ( Gregorieff & Clevers, 2005 ), the molecular mechanism and signaling pathways of MCC important to colorectal epithelial cell biology and CRC remain largely unknown. Here we provide the first evidence of the molecular signaling pathways that MCC participates in and the biological significant functions of MCC in CRC.

We find that MCC expression is absent or dramatically decreased in many CRC cell lines regardless of their APC mutational status ( Fig. 1A ). The loss of MCC protein correlates to the methylation of the MCC promoter in these CRC cell lines ( Fig. 1B ). This data may indicate a selective advantage of MCC loss independent to APC loss in CRC cells. Recently, MCC promoter methylation has been found as a frequent early event in 45-52% of 187 cases of primary CRC. MCC promoter methylation was highly significantly associated with Cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor type 2A ( CDKN2A ) methylation, CIMP, and BRAF V600E mutation but was not associated with either APC methylation or KRAS mutations ( Kohonen-Corish et al., 2007 ). This group also found that MCC methylation was more common in serrated polyps than adenomas thus associating MCC methylation with a distinct spectrum of precursor lesions from those with APC methylation which give rise to CRC through the alternative serrated CRC neoplasia pathway ( Jass, 2005 ; Kohonen-Corish et al., 2007 ). Our study of MCC promoter methylation in an independent cohort of CRC patients solidly supports the finding of MCC promoter methylation in this distinct subset of sporadic CRC that characterizes the serrated CRC pathway.

Here we present the first evidence of the potential biological significance of the MCC loss seen in this distinct spectrum of CRC that rarely demonstrate APC loss. We demonstrate that analogous to APC, MCC can play an important role as a potential tumor suppressor in CRC by inhibiting both Wnt/β-catenin signal transduction and cellular proliferation (Fig. 3 , 4 , and 5 ). We show MCC not only inhibits murine fibroblast proliferation as had been previously demonstrated ( Matsumine et al., 1996 ), but also inhibits both basal and Wnt3a-stimulated MEF, CRC, and breast cancer cellular proliferation ( Fig. 6 ). Previously MCC was reported as a potential negative regulator of the transcription factor NFκB ( Bouwmeester et al., 2004 ). However, we saw no effect of MCC expression on either the constitutive or TNFα-induced NFκB activity seen in our CRC cell lines ( Fig. 3 and data not shown). Perhaps MCC is unable to inhibit the activation of NFκB in CRC cells, which is constitutively activated by a number of oncogenic mechanisms. Nevertheless, we do demonstrate that expression of MCC in CRC cells dramatically inhibits Wnt signaling and β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity even in the presence of mutated oncogenic APC ( Fig. 3 ). However unlike APC, MCC does not seem to directly facilitate the destabilization of β-catenin but instead interacts with β-catenin and specifically interferes with the binding of the β-catenin/TCF/LEF transcriptional complex to DNA in the nucleus ( Fig. 4 ). β-catenin/TCF/LEF-DNA binding is known to be suppressed by specific phosphorylation of TCF/LEF by Nemo-like kinase (NLK), which is activated by TGF-beta activated kinase 1 (TAK1) ( Ishitani et al., 2003 ; Ishitani et al., 1999 ). Hence, MCC could possibly promote phosphorylation and inactivation of the β-catenin/TCF/LEF transcriptional complex indirectly by modulating the activity of these kinases. Further study will be needed to address the potential regulation of these kinases and the phosphorylation of β-catenin/TCF/LEF transcriptional complex by MCC. Another more plausible hypothesis based on our current data is that MCC can suppress β-catenin/TCF/LEF-DNA binding directly perhaps by interacting with β-catenin and affecting its subcellular localization thus indirectly its degradation. We found that MCC does interact with β-catenin but failed to co-immunoprecipitate with APC, TCF-4, or LEF-1 ( Fig. 4C and data not shown). MCC also causes an increase in the cytoplasmic levels of β-catenin and a decrease in total β-catenin levels ( Fig. 4B and C , Supplemental Figure #1 ). The studies of the direct interactions of MCC with β-catenin and potentially other Wnt pathway members in regulating the subcellular localization, β-catenin stability, and suppression of DNA-binding by the β-catenin/TCF/LEF transcriptional complex are on-going.

Our evidence indicates that nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling of MCC may be important for suppressing β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent transcription. We demonstrate for the first time that MCC is localized, not only to cytoplasmic and membrane fractions as indicated by previous studies ( Senda et al., 1999 ), but also to the nucleus likely through two functional NLSs we identify at the termini of MCC (Fig. 1 and 2 ). Significantly, the biNLS at the C -terminus and the arginine-rich NLS at the N -terminus as well as several potential NES sequences are located in the two most significant segments of MCC necessary to repress β-catenin/TCF/LEF-dependent promoter activity ( Fig. 4 ). The tumor suppressor APC has several functional NES and NLS sequences which have been shown to affect the nuclear-cytoplasm shuttling of ß-catenin ( Henderson, 2000 ; Rosin-Arbesfeld et al., 2000 ; Zhang et al., 2000 ). However additional β-catenin nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling mechanisms are possible as even in APC-mutant CRC cells, β-catenin is efficiently exported from the nucleus ( Eleftheriou et al., 2001 ). Collectively our data that show that MCC interacts with β-catenin and blocks β-catenin/TCF/LEF-DNA binding as well as the potential importance of MCC nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling in suppression of β-catenin-dependent transcription provide evidence of a novel regulatory pathway for β-catenin by MCC. Our future studies will address the potential importance of MCC subcellular localization and nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling in suppressing Wnt signaling.

In summary, we found MCC suppresses cell proliferation and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in CRC cells. Like APC, MCC interacts with β-catenin and inhibits Wnt/β-catenin-dependent transcriptional activity although through a distinct mechanism. Our data combined with the frequent epigenetic loss of MCC seen in a distinct set of CRCs and precursor lesions independent of APC loss ( Kohonen-Corish et al., 2007 ) indicate that MCC could play an significant tumor suppressor role in this distinct subset of CRCs, perhaps equivalent to that of APC in the traditional adenoma-carcinoma CRC sequence, by inhibiting β-catenin/Wnt signaling and restraining colorectal epithelial cell proliferation when it is vitally important early during CRC initiation. MCC inactivation therefore could be an initiating Wnt pathway-activating event in this group of CRC arising through the alternative serrated CRC neoplasia pathway. As our in-vitro data indicate that MCC represses β-catenin signaling in CRC cells and as MCC epigenetic silencing is particularly frequent in sporadic CIMP+, MSI-H CRC tumors, one would predict to observe robust β-catenin-dependent effects in these tumors. Predisposition to tumor budding at the invasive margin of CRC has been strongly linked to upregulation of β-catenin signaling ( Conacci-Sorrell et al., 2003 ; Zlobec et al., 2007 ). However, tumor budding is often low or completely absent in sporadic CIMP+, MSI-H CRC tumors ( Jass, 2007 ; Jass et al., 2003 ; Prall & Ostwald, 2007 ; Zlobec et al., 2007 ), where MCC epigenetic silencing is most often seen. This inconsistency between our in-vitro data and the pathology of this subset of CRC may be explained by the striking peritumoural lymphocytic infiltration (PTL) seen frequently in sporadic CIMP+, MSI-H CRC tumors ( Jass, 2007 ). Specifically an inverse relation is seen between tumor budding and PTL and it has been suggested that the relative lack of tumor budding observed in this CRC subset may well be due to the high PTL frequently seen in CIMP+, MSI-H CRC tumors ( Jass et al., 2003 ; Shinto et al., 2005 ). Findings in DNA mismatch repair-proficient CRC suggest that PTL and their associated tumor infiltrating lymphocytes may destroy budding tumor cells ( Jass, 2007 ; Zlobec et al., 2007 ). Therefore, this could also be true for the lack of tumor budding seen in sporadic CIMP+, MSI-H CRC tumors in which MCC is silenced but further investigation is needed.

Finally, the concurrent MCC silencing and the BRAF V600E mutation seen in MSI-H/CIMP+ sporadic CRC and its precursor lesions does not seem to be a chance association and supports the theory that MCC , in addition to BRAF , may be a key gene in activating molecular pathways related to development of sporadic MSI-H/CIMP+ CRC via the serrated neoplasia pathway. Therefore, further study is needed to determine the role of MCC in normal colorectal epithelial cell biology and how loss of MCC interacts with BRAF V600E in the serrated CRC neoplasia pathway.

Materials and Methods

Construction of plasmids

Plasmid harboring the full length MCC cDNA (pME18S) was obtained from Dr. Kazuo Maruyama (University of Tokyo, Tokyo, Japan). Using this as a template, the full-length and truncated MCC cDNAs were amplified by PCR with appropriate primer sets and subcloned into the pcDNA3/4A vector (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Double stranded oligonucleotides for the bipartite nuclear localization signal (NLS) and the arginine-rich NLS sequences of MCC gene were subcloned upstream to the enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) in the pEGFPN3 vector (Clontech, Mountain View, CA). All plasmids were verified by DNA sequencing.

Establishment of MCC stable transformants

The empty pcDNA3/4A vector or pcDNA3/4A containing full length MCC was transfected into each cell line and stable pools and clones were selected with G418. Multiple clones for each cell line were isolated and the MCC level was verified by Western blotting and reverse transcriptase-PCR (RT-PCR).

Transfection and promoter assays

β-catenin/TCF/LEF- or NFκB-dependent transcriptional activity was determined as described previously ( Agarwal et al., 2005 ). Exponentially growing cells in 24-well plates were transfected with either the β-catenin/TCF/LEF-(pOT) or NFκB-dependent (NFκB) luciferase reporter construct alone or co-transfected with the indicated MCC plasmid with Lipofectamin 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California). Luciferase reporter plasmids for pOT and NFκB were gifts from Dr. Bert Vogelstein (John Hopkins, Baltimore, MD) and Dr. Bryan Williams (Monash Institute of Medical Research, Clayton Victoria, Australia) respectively. The renilla luciferase construct pRL-TK (Promega, Madison, WI) was co-transfected as a normalization control. After an overnight incubation, the cells were stimulated with either control or Wnt3a medium, harvested, and lysed. 20 μl of each lysate was incubated with luciferase substrates for firefly and Renilla and luciferase assays were performed with the dual luciferase reporter assay system (Promega). The β-catenin/TCF/LEF- or NFκB-dependent firefly luciferase activity was normalized with the value of the corresponding renilla-dependent luciferase activity and the ratio (n = 3, mean ± S.D.) was statistically analyzed.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA)

The β-catenin/TCF/LEF and NFκB binding sites from the cyclin D1 and the immunoglobulin gene promoters respectively were synthesized by ITD Technology Inc. (Coralville, IA) and used as probes. Oligonucleotides for SRE and AP-1 were purchased from Santa Cruz. EMSA and the supershift assays were performed on nuclear lysates as previously described ( Sizemore et al., 2002 ).

MCC suppression by siRNA

A Smart Pool Set of siRNAs for MCC from Dharmacon (Chicago, IL) was used to suppress MCC expression. A control siRNA pool was to control for non-specific effects of transfection and siRNA. The MCC or control siRNA pools were transfected with SiLentFect Lipid (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) according to manufacturer’s instructions into cells growing in 24-well plates and harvested after the indicated number of days incubation.

Reverse transcriptase (RT)-PCR

Cells were harvested, lysed and cleared by brief centrifugation as previously described ( Sizemore et al., 2002 ). Total RNA was isolated from 6 or 24-well culture plates using Trizol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Approximately 1 μg of total RNA from each sample was reverse-transcribed as described by the manufacturer, using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit from Bio-Rad (Hercules, CA). Gene sequences available from the National Center for Biotechnology Information GenBank and Unigene databases were selected to design primers. Optimum primer sequences were selected after verification for gene-specific complementation using the National Center for Biotechnology Information Blast program. PCR primers for GAPDH are previously reported ( Fukuyama et al., 2006 ). The PCR primers for MCC is as follows: MCC (GenBank accession number, NM 002387), forward, 5′- TACGAATCCAATGCCACA-3′, reverse, 5′ -AGCTTCATGAGCAGGGCCTT-3′ (248 bp). cDNA concentrations for each sample were normalized by using GAPDH as a control gene.

Immunostaining

Cells were grown on glass coverslips to 50-60% confluency. Prior to staining, cells were washed 2 times with PBS, fixed for 10 min in methanol. Cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 mins. After blocking with 1% BSA in PBS for 60 mins, the samples were stained with the indicated antibody in 1% BSA/PBS for 2h at room temperature. After 3x washing with PBS, the samples were stained with the appropriate FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen Corp. Carlsbad, CA) for 1h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with 4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) contained in mounting solution (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA). Cells were analyzed with a Leica confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Exton, PA).
