# Targeting fungal lipid synthesis for antifungal drug development and potentiation of contemporary antifungals.
**Authors:** Gutierrez-Perez C, Cramer RA
**Journal:** NPJ Antimicrob Resist (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s44259-025-00093-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44259-025-00093-4)

## Content

Two of the three most commonly used classes of antifungal drugs target the fungal membrane through perturbation of sterol biosynthesis or function. In addition to these triazole and polyene antifungals, recent research is identifying new antifungal molecules that perturb lipid biosynthesis and function. Here, we review fungal lipid biosynthesis pathways and their potential as targets for antifungal drug development. An emerging goal is discovering new molecules that potentiate contemporary antifungal drugs in part through perturbation of lipid form and function.

Human fungal infections represent a significant global health burden, affecting millions of individuals annually and posing a challenge to healthcare systems worldwide1–3. The most commonly occurring presentations in humans are fungal mucosal and dermatophytic infections. For example, over 2.1 billion cases of fungal skin infections were estimated to occur in 20164. Treatment for dermatophytic infections includes topical antifungals and oral terbinafine, griseofulvin, itraconazole or fluconazole5. Systemic and invasive fungal infections are less common; however, they are difficult to diagnose and treat. Difficulties in diagnosis and treatment are reflected in the estimated ~1.5 million people per year who succumb to fungal infections, which is about the same as the number of people worldwide that succumb to tuberculosis and three times as many as those that succumb to malaria1,2. With the rise of antifungal drug resistance and the limited arsenal of effective antifungal drugs, there is a pressing need for innovative therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, outside of evolved drug resistance, there are multiple pathogenic fungal species that are intrinsically resistant to most if not all antifungals such as Mucor spp., Lomentospora prolificans, Scedosporium spp., and Fusarium spp. among others6–9. Therefore, developing new antifungals with novel modes of action is an urgent need to combat the increasing incidence of disease caused by human pathogenic fungi.

Currently, the antifungal drug classes used clinically against systemic and invasive fungal disease are triazoles, polyenes, allylamines, echinocandins, and antimetabolites10. Echinocandins target synthesis of cell wall 1,3-β-glucans11, while antimetabolites such as 5-fluorocytosine inhibits DNA and RNA synthesis after being metabolized into 5-fluorouracil12. The remaining three antifungal classes (triazoles, polyenes, allylamines) target synthesis of the integral cell membrane component ergosterol (triazoles, allylamines) or target it directly (polyenes). Ergosterol is a critical membrane component that maintains membrane structure and fluidity similar to cholesterol in human cells13. Antifungals that target ergosterol disrupt membrane structure and fluidity and are widely used in the treatment of human fungal infections. However, though often effective, these drugs are associated with host toxicity and potential drug-drug interactions along with emerging drug resistance that can reduce their efficacy and complicate their use in antifungal therapy.

Fungal cell membranes play a pivotal role in fungal physiology, serving as a dynamic barrier essential for cell integrity, nutrient uptake, and signal transduction14–17. The plasma membrane, serving as the interface between the cell and its environment, consists of a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins which facilitate various cellular processes such as nutrient uptake, signal transduction, and cell-cell communication17–20. As mentioned, unique to fungal membranes is the presence of ergosterol, a sterol analogous to cholesterol in mammalian cells, which modulates membrane fluidity and permeability13. Additionally, specialized lipid domains like membrane rafts play roles in organizing membrane-associated proteins and regulating their activity16. Meanwhile, the fungal cell wall, an external layer surrounding the plasma membrane, provides structural support and protection21,22. Composed primarily of chitin, glucans (including 1,3-beta-D-glucan), and proteins, the cell wall confers rigidity to the cell while allowing flexibility for growth and morphogenesis21,22. The interplay between the plasma membrane and the cell wall is crucial for maintaining cell shape and integrity, as well as mediating interactions with the surrounding environment23,24.

Consequently, disrupting the function and integrity of the fungal cell membrane represents an ideal approach to combat fungal infections. This strategy has proven successful as evidenced by the use of either Amphotericin B or triazoles to treat a majority of patients with systemic and invasive fungal disease25–29. In addition to ergosterol, an opportunity exists to target other membrane components for antifungal therapy development. Additional structural and functional lipids of cell membranes include phospholipids, sphingolipids, and carbohydrate-linked lipids30–32.

This review describes the unique structural and biochemical features of fungal membrane lipid synthesis pathways and their potential as antifungal drug development targets (Fig. 1). Historically, the model organism Saccharomyces cerevisiae has been central in defining fungal lipid synthesis pathways and will act as the backbone for this review with findings in other pathogenic fungal species such as Candida albicans, Cryptococcus neoformans and Aspergillus fumigatus compared and contrasted. Gene nomenclature for each respective species is utilized throughout the review. Candida albicans is a dimorphic fungal pathogen and a common member of the human microbiota that can cause infections, ranging from superficial to life-threatening systemic diseases. Cryptococcus neoformans is a polysaccharide encapsulated yeast that primarily causes life-threatening meningitis and pulmonary infections. Aspergillus fumigatus is a ubiquitous filamentous fungus that is the causative agent of a spectrum of human diseases collectively termed aspergillosis with invasive aspergillosis the most lethal. While acknowledging the conservation of key metabolic pathways across fungal species, the focus on S. cerevisiae provides a well-characterized framework for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying lipid synthesis and its significance in fungal membrane biology. It is important to note however, that there are species and even strain specific differences throughout the fungal kingdom that can be harnessed for more effective antifungal discovery, and discovery in S. cerevisiae may or may not yield applicable results outside of model yeast. The overall aim of this review is to highlight current antifungal compounds and putative druggable targets involved in synthesizing each membrane component to provide valuable insights for designing and optimizing novel antifungal agents that target fungal membranes.

Beyond energy production, acetyl-CoA is a key precursor for lipid biosynthesis, including fatty acids, sterols, and sphingolipids (Fig. 1). Fungi can generate acetyl-CoA through multiple metabolic pathways, allowing them to adapt to diverse host environments and fuel key processes essential for survival and virulence. The primary routes are through pyruvate oxidation as a result of glycolysis, fatty acid catabolism through beta-oxidation, acetate assimilation through acetyl-CoA synthase (ACS), amino acid catabolism, ethanol metabolism and through other TCA cycle intermediates. For the purposes of this review, we have highlighted acetyl-CoA derived from glucose to simplify the incorporation of other glycolysis-derived lipid synthesis precursors glycerol-3-P and inositol 3-P (Fig. 1). However, other acetyl-CoA synthesis pathways described above uniquely contribute to fungal pathogenesis in different species. For example, beta-oxidation of fatty acids is essential for virulence in C. neoformans, but not C. albicans33,34. Acetate assimilation is relevant for full virulence of A. fumigatus and C. glabrata but is not critical for C. neoformans virulence35–37. Rather than acetate assimilation, other pathways such as the conversion of citrate to acetyl-CoA by ATP-citrate lyase, beta-oxidation of fatty acids and the glyoxylate cycle greatly contribute to host survival of C. neoformans38–41. The glyoxylate cycle has been extensively studied in C. albicans, and the key enzyme isocitrate lyase is essential for virulence42. By integrating multiple metabolic pathways to generate acetyl-CoA, fungi ensure a continuous supply of this crucial metabolite for energy production, lipid biosynthesis, and virulence regulation. While not extensively discussed in this review, targeting pathways that contribute to acetyl-CoA synthesis could provide new strategies to disrupt fungal growth and pathogenicity, offering potential avenues for antifungal drug development.

Ergosterol is a bioactive lipid that belongs to the sterol class of molecules, is a 5,7-diene oxysterol, and serves as one of the main components of cell membranes in fungi13. Ergosterol is crucial for maintaining the structural integrity and fluidity of the membrane, regulating membrane permeability, and facilitating various cellular processes, including signal transduction and vesicular trafficking24,43–45. The ergosterol synthesis pathway occurs via a complex pathway involving multiple enzymatic steps, with several key enzymes representing targets for antifungal drug development (Table 1)13. In yeast, synthesis of ergosterol is the result of three different pathways localized in the mitochondria, vacuoles, and the endoplasmic reticulum: the mevalonate pathway, the farnesyl pyrophosphate biosynthetic pathway, and the final ergosterol synthesis pathway46,47. The mevalonate pathway is conserved across eukaryotes and involves acetyl-CoA conversion to mevalonate by the subsequent actions of acetyl-CoA C-acetyltransferase Erg10p, hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase Erg13p, and HMG-CoA reductases Hmg1/2p48. The rate-limiting step in ergosterol synthesis is the conversion from HMG-CoA to mevalonate through Hmg1/2p49. ERG10 and ERG13 are essential genes in S. cerevisiae, suggesting that their products may be viable antifungal drug targets50. Human inhibitors of acetyl-CoA C-acetyltransferase (ACAT, arecoline hydrobromide) and hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase (HMGCS, L-659-69951) exist, but have yet to be tested against fungi and if they inhibit the fungal homologs of ACAT (ERG10p) or HMGCR (ERG13p)52,53. Arecoline hydrobromide has been used as an antiparasitic to treat tapeworm in dogs54. A. fumigatus has two copies of the erg10 gene (erg10A, erg10B), both of which have been suggested to be essential for growth55,56. Acetyl-CoA C-acetyltransferase (encoded by erg10A) has been postulated as an antifungal drug target due to its essentiality in vitro, however, drug development must focus on the structural differences with the human homolog (ACAT) in order to prevent human toxicity56. The erg10B gene was identified to be conditionally essential for growth in A. fumigatus using a conditional gene replacement strategy, however the phenotype is reported to be static as a switch to non-repressive conditions restores growth55. Additional studies in Candida albicans show that a bacterial polyene inhibits Erg10p, resulting in reduced cell viability in vitro57. Furthermore, ERG10 is essential in C. albicans58. To further validate Erg10p as a putative drug target, in vivo pathogenicity studies using the conditional erg10A and erg10B strain (PalcA::erg10A56, PniiA-ERG10B55) in A. fumigatus and a conditional mutant in C. albicans should be conducted.

It is difficult to ascertain whether Erg13p is a promising drug target as there is a lack of studies focused on the ERG13 gene in pathogenic fungi. However, ERG13 is essential for growth in S. cerevisiae and in C. albicans50,58. ERG13 transcript levels are responsive to antifungal treatment across different species, suggesting this gene is relevant to the antifungal response. In C. albicans ERG13 transcript levels are reduced in response to amphotericin B59. In A. fumigatus erg13A and erg13B transcript levels are reduced in response to voriconazole60, and only erg13B transcript levels are increased in response to amphotericin B61. In C. neoformans serotype A, ERG13 transcript levels are increased in response to fluconazole62. Altered transcript levels of this gene in response to antifungals suggest that inhibition of Erg13p could alter susceptibility to antifungals, which could be useful for developing antifungal drugs that potentiate triazole efficacy. Therefore, further study of ERG13 is necessary to determine whether there is a potentiation or antagonism of existing antifungals as a result of Erg13p inhibition.

Targeted inhibition of Hmg1p/2p is the mode of action of the statin class of drugs such as the natural statin lovastatin and the synthetic statin Fluvastatin63,64. Statins inhibit human Hmg1p/2p and are therefore used to lower cholesterol63,65. Against fungi, statins have synergy with triazoles against multiple fungal species in vitro66–68. Targeted inhibition of both Hmg1p and Hmg2p through statins is effective at inhibiting growth against dermatophytes in vitro which suggests statins should be tested for clinical use against superficial dermatophyte infections5. However, targeting Hmg1p/2p can be problematic for invasive disease due to how highly conserved Hmg1p/2p are between fungi and humans69. There is limited clinical evidence of statins being effective against invasive fungal disease in vivo, therefore further animal and subsequent clinical studies are required to determine whether targeted inhibition of Hmg1p/2p provides clinical benefit. Human serum concentrations of statins are estimated to be 50-100-fold lower than the antifungal MIC in vitro for most fungi, which presents a significant challenge to repurposing this drug70–72. Even though there is in vitro evidence supporting synergy between statins and triazoles, point mutations in HMG1 have been shown to induce resistance to triazoles in A. fumigatus73,74. This could be problematic for combination therapy of statins and triazoles against A. fumigatus as this treatment could result in cross-resistance to both antifungals. Altogether, statins could provide a blueprint for developing new fungal specific HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors.

In the second stage of ergosterol synthesis, mevalonate is converted to farnesyl pyrophosphate, which is a precursor to not only ergosterol, but ubiquinone, dolichol, and prenylated proteins75–77. This stage encompasses six different reactions mediated by the genes ERG12, ERG8, ERG19 (MVD1), IDI1, and ERG2046,78–82. There are currently no reported compounds with antifungal properties that target genes involved in the synthesis of farnesyl pyrophosphate from mevalonate. Interestingly, all these genes are essential in S. cerevisiae, which indicates further studies are needed in pathogenic fungi50. In the pathogenic fungi A. fumigatus, C. neoformans and C. albicans there are single gene copies for ERG12, ERG8, ERG19 (MVD1), IDI1, and ERG20 (Table 1). In A. fumigatus erg10, erg12, erg8, and erg20 are essential83. C. albicans genes ERG12, ERG8, MVD1, and ERG20 are essential for growth in vivo and for virulence as shown using strains containing each respective gene under control of an inducible promoter58. ERG8 is of particular interest for developing antifungal therapies, due to its essentiality in A. fumigatus and C. albicans and lack of a human protein homolog, which would allow for fungal specificity (Table 1). Although ERG12, ERG19, IDI1 and ERG20 have human homologs, the fungal and human proteins share less than 50% identity, suggesting there is the potential for developing compounds with fungal specificity (Table 1). Overall, the second stage of the ergosterol synthesis pathway shows potential for novel antifungal development due to the essentiality of multiple genes across pathogenic and non-pathogenic fungi. Further characterization of the genes in this part of the ergosterol synthesis pathway is needed, including in vivo pathogenicity and antifungal drug susceptibility studies using conditional gene expression mutants in pathogenic species.

The last stage of ergosterol synthesis encompasses the steps needed to convert farnesyl pyrophosphate to ergosterol. Thirteen genes make up this part of the pathway: ERG9, ERG1, ERG7, ERG11, ERG24, ERG25, ERG27, ERG29, ERG6, ERG2, ERG3, ERG5, and ERG447. Of these, ERG9, ERG1, ERG7, ERG11, ERG24, ERG25, ERG27, and ERG29 are essential genes in S. cerevisiae50. In A. fumigatus, erg9, erg1, and erg27 have been reported to be essential genes83,84 and loss of both gene copies of erg24, erg25 and erg11 is lethal55,85,86. In C. albicans ERG9, ERG1, ERG7, ERG11, ERG27 are essential for growth in vitro and virulence58,87, while ERG24 is only essential for virulence58. Multiple antifungals exist that target enzymes encoded by these genes and validate their viability as drug targets. Squalenstatins inhibit the squalene synthase Erg9p but are not currently used to treat fungal infections in part due to mammalian toxicity88,89. Allylamines, such as terbinafine, noncompetitively bind Erg1p, which mediates the conversion between squalene and squalene epoxide90. Morpholines such as amorolfine target two proteins in the late ergosterol synthesis pathway, the C-14 sterol reductase Erg24p and the C-8 sterol isomerase Erg2p91. Triazoles target the lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase Erg11p10,92–94. Other antifungal compounds that target Erg11p include VT-1598 and VT-1129 with activity reported against Cryptococcus and Aspergillus species (note: Erg11p in Aspergillus species are named Cyp51A and Cyp51Bp)95,96. Additionally, VT-1598 shows antifungal activity against C. auris97. VT-1129 and VT-1598 have a tetrazole rather than a triazole as the heme iron-binding moiety, which increases specificity to fungal Erg11p98. The fungal specificity is an advance for this drug class as it reduces off-target inhibition of mammalian P450 enzymes.

The genes mediating the last five steps of ergosterol synthesis in S. cerevisiae are not essential (ERG2, ERG3, ERG4, ERG5, ERG6)50. Single gene null mutants of these last five enzymes yield a mixture of ergosterol intermediates which can still be incorporated into the cell membrane. However, the altered membrane composition results in increased sensitivity to a variety of stressors and antifungals99. The fungal proteins Erg6p, Erg2p, Erg5p, and Erg4p lack human homologs, which highlights their attractiveness as antifungal targets (Table 1)65. Furthermore, pathogenic fungi such as A. fumigatus, C. albicans and C. neoformans have a single copies of ERG5, ERG6, and ERG2 (Table 1), although these genes are not essential. A. fumigatus has two copies of erg4, neither of which is essential individually (Table 1)100. Loss of ERG5 shows increased susceptibility to azoles in C. albicans, C. glabrata, C. auris and A. fumigatus45,101. In A. fumigatus, erg6 is essential for viability and downregulation of erg6 results in reduced virulence in vivo102. Interestingly, ERG6 is not essential in C. albicans, S. cerevisiae or C. neoformans, but loss of function of ERG6 results in virulence defects in C. neoformans and C. albicans58,103. Two different groups have reported identification of Erg6p inhibitors active against C. albicans (H55 and NP256), of which H55 has shown efficacy in vivo against azole resistant strains104,105. There could be some concern targeting Erg6p as loss of function ERG6 mutations have been shown to contribute to amphotericin B resistance in C. glabrata and C. neoformans103,106. However, as clinical amphotericin B resistance is rare, there is the implication that mutations in ERG6 come with a considerable fitness cost in either growth or virulence. While a limitation of targeting these proteins is their non-essential nature in vitro, the loss of virulence observed as a result of ERG658,103, ERG2107 and ERG458 loss suggests they could be viable drug targets in the right host setting. Thus, further research is needed to better understand the potential of targeting these enzymes for therapeutic development in the specific host and disease environments in vivo through animal studies in combination with and without contemporary antifungal therapies.

The three pathways described above culminate in the synthesis of ergosterol, which itself is targeted by polyenes such as Amphotericin B and natamycin108,109. The mode of action of polyenes has been a focus of research for years and recent data suggest they directly bind to ergosterol and form a sponge-like aggregate that destabilizes the plasma membrane108. As polyenes target a bioactive molecule rather than an enzyme, acquired resistance to polyenes is rare compared to other antifungals with enzyme targets, but resistance occurs, for example, in Candida auris isolates110,111. While the exact mechanism of Amphotericin B (AmpB) resistance in C. auris is unclear, studies suggest changes in the ergosterol synthesis pathway and subsequent alterations in cell membrane permeability play a role. Transcriptomic analysis of C. auris AmpB-resistant strains show an enrichment of genes involved in lipid and ergosterol biosynthesis, and further testing of these strains confirmed a decrease in cell membrane permeability relative to AmpB susceptible strains112. Furthermore, Muñoz et al. showed that ergosterol synthesis pathway genes (ERG1, ERG2, ERG6 and ERG13) are induced in response to AmpB in C. auris. Dual mutations in ERG3 and ERG11 and single mutations in ERG2, ERG3, ERG5, ERG6 result in AmpB resistance in C. auris in vitro113–115. Acquired resistance to AmpB as a result of mutations in genes encoding enzymes in the ergosterol synthesis pathway are also seen in C. albicans and C. neoformans99,110,116–118. This mechanism of resistance makes sense in the context of AmpB’s mode of action, as altering ergosterol composition or content in the cell membrane could reduce the efficacy of AmpB. Other studies have shown that single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in a gene encoding the DNA-damage response protein Mec3p113, or transcriptional activator FLO8119 contribute to AmpB resistance through unidentified mechanisms. Changes in cell wall composition could also be contributing to AmpB resistance, as Shivarathri et al. observed that AmpB resistant strains were cross-resistant to cell wall disturbing agents and resistant strains showed an increase in the phosphorylation of the cell wall integrity MAP kinase Mkc1p in response to AmpB treatment112. While further study is clearly needed to explain the AmpB resistance observed in C. auris isolates, it seems possible that isolate specific resistance mechanisms are an important consideration, perhaps related to the ecological niche the respective isolate came from.

Despite their outstanding antifungal activity, host toxicity has been an issue with AmpB use due to the non-specific binding of cholesterol120. Liposomal AmpB is the most widely used form due to its reduced toxicity121. Additionally, a promising renal-sparing polyene was recently developed that does not bind human cholesterol122, and there is currently work being done to develop orally bioavailable a AmpB123–125. The polyene natamycin is used to treat fungal keratitis caused by Fusarium and Aspergillus species, but is not used systemically126.

Intriguingly, data suggest targeting ergosterol storage mechanisms might have therapeutic potential. Ergosterol can be stored in the cell in the form of sterol esters (SE). SEs are synthesized by the two acyl-CoA:sterol acyltransferase (ASAT) related enzymes, Are1p and Are2p, and require fatty acids127,128. Neither of these enzymes are essential, but the double null mutant has been reported to be inviable or have a strong phenotypic defect in S. cerevisiae127,128. Moreover, a human acyl-CoA:cholesterol acyltransferase inhibitor, HWY-289, inhibits Are1p and Are2p in C. albicans resulting in growth inhibition129. This data proposes Are1/Are2 inhibitors as a potential novel therapeutic, however in vivo pathogenicity studies are needed to validate whether inhibition of these genes reduces fungal growth or virulence in a mammalian host environment. Taken together, the success of targeting sterol metabolism for antifungal therapy is unquestioned and opportunities remain to discover new chemical matter that inhibits this pathway effectively in vivo. The challenge of human toxicity associated with the inhibition of key steps in sterol metabolism exists, but the potential remains to identify fungal specific compounds with limited host toxicity and potent antifungal activity.

Fatty acids are organic molecules consisting of a hydrocarbon chain with a carboxylic acid group at one end. They are important for various biological processes, including energy storage, membrane structure, and cell signaling. Fatty acids serve as building blocks for complex lipids such as phospholipids and sphingolipids, contributing to the structure and function of cell membranes. Targeting fatty acid biosynthesis, as described below, is a promising approach to identify and develop new antifungal molecules for mono and combination therapies.

Like ergosterol synthesis, fatty acid synthesis starts with acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA is converted to malonyl-CoA through a rate-limiting reaction mediated by the acetyl-CoA carboxylase Acc1p130. Malonyl-CoA is then elongated to palmitic acid (C16:0) by the fatty acid synthetases Fas1p and Fas2p131–133. Palmitic acid can be further elongated by Fas1p/Fas2p into stearic acid (C18:0)131–133. Unsaturated fatty acid synthesis (UFA) occurs when delta(9) fatty acid desaturase Ole1p (SdeAp in Aspergillus species) creates a double bond at the 9th carbon in palmitoyl-CoA, to form oleic acid (C18:1n9)134. This desaturation step is the rate-limiting step in UFA biosynthesis, which is why OLE1 is an essential gene50,134. UFA are essential for cellular survival due to their role in mediating cell membrane fluidity, which is necessary for the cell to appropriately respond to stress and for essential cell membrane transporter function135–138.

FAs can further be elongated by elongases Elo1p, Elo2p, and Elo3p to synthesize medium-chain (MC), long-chain (LC), and very long-chain (VLC) fatty acids139,140. The genes encoding the elongases (ELO1, ELO2, ELO3) are not essential individually, but ∆ELO2∆ELO3 double-mutants are not viable in S. cerevisiae50,140. Pathogenic fungi A. fumigatus and C. neoformans only have one copy of the ELO proteins (Table 2), which may suggest that this single copy is essential in these organisms and could be an attractive drug target. However, neither of these genes in A. fumigatus or C. neoformans have been tested to assess whether they are essential or have a role in fungal growth or virulence in vivo. After synthesis, FAs need to be activated into their fatty acid-CoA form by long chain fatty acyl-CoA synthetases Faa1p and Faa4p to be used in multiple biological processes such as energy production and incorporation into complex lipids (phospholipids, sphingolipids)142. Although neither FAA1 nor FAA4 are essential individually in S. cerevisiae, a double null mutant is not viable143. These genes are not well characterized in C. albicans, C. neoformans or A. fumigatus and it has not been determined whether these genes are essential or play a role in pathogenesis. In the insect pathogenic fungus, Beauveria bassiana, a ∆faa1 strain had decreased virulence in a Galleria mellonella insect model144. Furthermore, a Faa1p inhibitor triacsin C, inhibits growth of Malassezia spp.145. Although further studies of FAA1/FAA4 are necessary in pathogenic fungi, targeted inhibition of fatty acyl-CoA synthetases would theoretically result in FA inactivation, rendering them unusable by the cell. Therefore, a long chain fatty acyl-CoA synthetase inhibitor has significant potential to inhibit fungal growth.

The acetyl-CoA carboxylase, Acc1p, can be inhibited by Firsocostat, and Soraphen A146–148. Firsocostat is effective at inhibiting C. albicans growth in vitro and in vivo and shows synergy with the azoles fluconazole and voriconazole and polyene Amphotericin B146. Soraphen A is effective at inhibiting Aspergillus nidulans growth149. Additionally, there are agricultural antifungals such as aryloxyphenoxypropionates and cyclohexanediones that target Acc1p. The small molecule AR-12 inhibits acetyl-CoA synthetase, which is upstream of Acc1 and is effective at inhibiting growth of Candida spp., Cryptococcus neoformans, Mucor spp., Fusarium spp., Blastomyces spp., Histoplasma spp., and Coccidioides spp. in vitro and is effective in vivo against C. neoformans150,151. Furthermore, AR-12 potentiates fluconazole activity and echinocandins in Candida resistant isolates150.

Fatty acid synthetases (FAS) Fas1p/Fas2p can be inhibited by cerulenin and NPD6433152,153. FAS1 and FAS2 loss of function mutants have been shown to have decreased virulence in Candida and Cryptococcus spp.154,155, supporting targeted inhibition of the FAS enzymes to be a viable antifungal drug strategy. In Aspergillus species, a second set of FAS enzymes are required for the synthesis of secondary metabolites such as the mycotoxin sterigmatocystin156. It is therefore possible that a FAS inhibitor could inhibit mycotoxin and secondary metabolism production in Aspergillus species, which could be useful not just as a human antifungal but also for crop protection.

As mentioned, the rate limiting step in UFA synthesis is mediated by the Δ9 fatty acid desaturase Ole1p. OLE1 in S. cerevisiae and its homologs in pathogenic fungi A. fumigatus (sdeA), C. albicans and C. neoformans are essential for growth154,157–160. There are a multitude of Δ9 fatty acid desaturase (Ole1p) inhibitors available for the human homolog of Ole1p (SCD1), which should be tested against pathogenic fungi161. Stearoyl-CoA desaturase inhibition is a current topic of investigation in fungi, due to the essentiality of the gene and deleterious effects of down-regulating the enzyme. The natural diyne-furan fatty acid EV-086 can inhibit Ole1p in Candida, Aspergillus, and Trichophyton spp.162. Moreover, multiple putative fungal Ole1p inhibitors have been found to be active against C. albicans163,164. Recently, the small molecule MBX-7591, a putative fungal stearoyl-CoA desaturase inhibitor, was identified in a high throughput screen for molecules active against A. fumigatus in the presence of fluconazole160. However, MBX-7591 also has potent antifungal activity against A. fumigatus, C. neoformans and Rhizopus spp. among other species in the absence of triazoles160. Intriguingly, MBX-7591 has synergy with triazoles and can restore azole susceptibility to azole resistant A. fumigatus isolates160. MBX-7591 showed in vivo efficacy at inhibiting growth of A. fumigatus during infection initiation in a mouse model of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA).

Direct inhibition of Ole1p is not the only viable avenue to inhibiting the synthesis of UFA, as there are multiple regulatory steps necessary for Ole1p activation. The main regulators of OLE1 are Mga2p and its paralog Spt23p165,166, which in turn are activated through the E3 ubiquitin ligase Rsp5p167. The RSP5 gene is essential in S. cerevisiae and C. albicans in standard laboratory conditions, but not in C. neoformans159,168. Interestingly, RSP5 is essential for growth in vivo and at high temperature in C. neoformans168, suggesting a specialized and essential role for Rsp5p in the context of infection. Individually neither SPT23 nor MGA2 are essential in S. cerevisiae due to functional redundancy, but loss of both genes is lethal166. In C. albicans, the sole homolog to S. cerevisiae MGA2 and SPT23 is essential159. Based on C. neoformans having only one copy it would be expected that loss of MGA2 is lethal, however, surprisingly, the loss of function mutant is viable in standard laboratory conditions169. Based on the phenotype observed with an RSP5 null mutant, it is possible that MGA2 would be essential for growth in vivo. These results suggest that there might be differences in the activation of UFA synthesis in the context of infection in C. neoformans compared to other yeast such as C. albicans and S. cerevisiae that are relevant specifically to pathogenesis.

A potential drawback to targeting FA synthesis is the potential for fungi to scavenge host FA which could nullify the effect of fungal FA synthesis inhibition. However, the loss of pathogenicity when inhibiting the FAS enzymes in C. neoformans154 and the in vivo efficacy of MBX-7591 in A. fumigatus160 suggest that the potential host contribution of FA is insufficient for phenotypic rescue of FA synthesis inhibition, at least in mouse models of fungal disease. However, host complementation of molecules targeting metabolic pathways in fungi must always be tested and considered.

Overall, FA synthesis inhibition is a validated and viable strategy to inhibit fungal growth because FAs are precursors to multiple integral plasma membrane components. Continuing the work already done in identifying Acc1p, Fas1p/2p and Ole1p inhibitors is necessary to expand the FA synthesis inhibitor repertoire. Furthermore, attempting to identify inhibitors of Rsp5p or Ole1p activators Spt23p and Mga2p could be a viable strategy to combat fungal infections by inhibiting UFA synthesis. Novel strategies in FA synthesis inhibition include developing antifungals that inhibit fatty acyl elongases and fatty acyl-CoA synthetases, as inhibition of either would result in FAs unable to be used in sphingolipid and phospholipid synthesis. It seems that molecules that inhibit FA synthesis are often synergistic with the triazoles, and this presents an attractive combination therapy possibility, especially in light of the growing incidence of triazole drug resistance across many species of human pathogenic fungi.

Phospholipids contain a phosphate group, glycerol, and fatty acid chains. They are important for the structure and function of cell membranes, serving as the primary components of lipid bilayers that separate cellular compartments. There are five major phospholipid classes in fungal cell membranes, phosphatidic acid (PA), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylserine (PS), and phosphatidylinositol (PI)170.

Phosphatidic acid (PA) acts as a precursor to the other phospholipid classes. PA is synthesized through the glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) pathway171. G3P is synthesized from glucose, glycerol, or minorly through the dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) pathway172. PA synthesis through the G3P pathway is mediated by the glycerol-3-P acyltransferases Gpt2p and Sct1p, which add fatty-acyl CoAs to G3P to yield the precursor lyso-PA172–174. Lyso-PA is a bioactive lipid precursor to PA. Lyso-PA undergoes a second acetylation and fatty-acyl CoA addition through 1-acyl glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase Slc1p and Ale1p to synthesize PA172,175,176. PA synthesis through the DHAP pathway is also mediated by Gpt2p and Sct1p, to produce 1-acyl DHAP172. Subsequently, the 1-acyl DHAP reductase Ayr1p converts 1-acyl DHAP to lyso-PA172,177. Sct1p shows a preference for G3P as one of its substrates, and for palmitoyl-CoA for its fatty-acyl substrate178. Recently, Sct1p was shown to compete with Ole1p for the substrate palmitoyl-CoA178. Subsequently over-expression of SCT1 induces a decrease in unsaturated fatty acids in S. cerevisiae178. If Sct1p competes for binding to palmitoyl-CoA with Ole1p in pathogenic fungi, drug development of a compound that functions similar to Sct1 and out-competes Ole1p for binding to its substrate could potentially result in a potent inhibitor of UFA synthesis. As discussed above, inhibition of UFA synthesis inhibits fungal growth and/or viability and potentiates triazole activity.

None of the genes that encode for the enzymes in the PA synthesis pathway are essential in S. cerevisiae, yet double mutants in every step of the pathway are inviable, further highlighting the importance of PA in cell viability. C. neoformans and A. fumigatus have one copy of SCT1/GTP2, suggesting the gene could be essential but this remains to be experimentally tested (Table 3). As an important precursor to phospholipids, inhibition of PA synthesis is an attractive drug target. However, the existence of multiple redundant enzymes at each step of the pathway makes the development of an antifungal more challenging because it would have to effectively inhibit multiple enzymes. The phospholipid PA is in a major branchpoint between the DAG and CDP-DAG synthesis pathways171. CDP-DAG is the precursor for cardiolipins, and the phospholipids PI and PS179. From PS, fungi can further synthesize PE and PC30. The CDP-DAG pathway is the primary route for synthesizing PE and PC in the absence of ethanolamine and choline. DAG synthesized from PA can be used to synthesize PE and PC through the Kennedy pathway but this reaction requires ethanolamine and choline180. Additionally, DAG can be used to store lipids by converting them to triacylglycerol (TAG) through two different pathways mediated by Dag1p and Lro1p181. Loss of lipid storage does not affect cellular viability, as evidenced by the S. cerevisiae quadruple mutant strain (DGA1ΔLRO1Δ ARE1ΔARE2Δ) that does not produce TAG or SE but remains viable127,181. The effect of lipid storage loss in pathogenic fungi has not been extensively studied and therefore it is unclear whether lipid storage mechanisms are potential antifungal drug targets.

The CDP-DAG pathway starts with the conversion of PA to CDP-DAG by CDP-DAG synthase Cds1p182,183. CDS1 is essential for viability in S. pombe, and C. albicans159,184,185. For PI synthesis, CDP-DAG can be converted to PI through the PI synthase Pis1p with the addition of inositol synthesized from glucose-6-phosphate by Ino1p and Inm1p186. PI is an important precursor for GPI anchors in the cell wall and is therefore necessary for cellular viability. As a result, a C. albicans PIS1∆ strain is inviable and PIS1 is essential for cell wall formation187. The genes involved in PI synthesis (PIS1, INO1 and INM1) have not been studied in A. fumigatus nor C. neoformans. The recently developed prodrug fosmanogepix targets GPI anchor synthesis by inhibiting Gwt1p, which mediates the inositol acylation of glucosaminyl phosphatidylinositol (GlcN-PI) resulting in GPI anchor synthesis inhibition188. Inhibition of PI synthesis results in reduced GPI anchor synthesis and, as shown by fosmanogepix, is a viable approach for antifungal therapeutic development. Fosmanogepix is effective in vitro and in vivo against multiple pathogenic fungi such as C. albicans, C. neoformans and A. fumigatus189,190 and is currently in phase III clinical trials for treatment of invasive candidiasis (described in clinical trial NCT05421858).

To synthesize PS, CDP-DAG is converted to PS through the ER-resident phosphatidylserine synthase Cho1p191. In C. albicans, CHO1 is a non-essential gene in vitro, however it is essential for virulence in vivo192,193. In contrast, in C. neoformans, CHO1 is essential for viability194. cho1 has not been characterized in A. fumigatus. Recently, CBR-5884, a small molecule inhibitor of Cho1p was identified and shown to be fungistatic against C. albicans in vitro but has yet to be tested against other pathogenic fungi193. CHO1 lacks a human homolog (Table 1) which adds to its attractiveness as a prospective antifungal drug target.

PS is decarboxylated to PE through Psd1p and Psd2p195–197. In C. albicans the loss of function of both PSD1 and PSD2 results in a strain that is avirulent in mice198. From PE, PC can be synthesized through the subsequent actions of methyltransferases Cho2p and Opi3p199,200. None of the genes involved in synthesizing PS, PE, or PC in the CDP-DAG pathway are essential in S. cerevisiae. This is likely due to PE and PC synthesis also occurring through the Kennedy pathway if ethanolamine and choline are available or from lyso-PE and lyso-PC through Ale1p-mediated acylation201,202. However, PE synthesis through the CDP-DAG pathway is essential for virulence in C. albicans, suggesting the CDP-DAG pathway is the major contributor of PE in the host for C. albicans rather than the Kennedy pathway192,198. A similar hypothesis has been suggested to explain the essentiality of CHO1 and PS synthesis through the CDP-DAG pathway in C. neoformans194. These hypotheses suggest that targeted inhibition of the CDP-DAG pathway is a viable antifungal strategy against specific fungal species. PE has been postulated to modulate fungal virulence in C. albicans, as a ∆CHO2∆OPI3 double null mutant strain (also known as ∆PEM1∆PEM2), which truncates PC synthesis through the CDP-DAG pathway, results in hypervirulence. The authors postulated that increased PE accumulation is responsible for the increased virulence198,203. In C. neoformans, PC synthesis by Opi3p is not essential and loss of function does not alter fungal virulence204. In C. albicans OPI3 is also not an essential gene205. In A. fumigatus, opi3 is not essential in vitro, but a loss of function mutant has a drastic fitness cost that is rescued by the addition of exogenous PC in vitro (unpublished data), supporting that PC can still be synthesized through the Kennedy pathway. Virulence of the ∆opi3 mutant has not been tested in A. fumigatus.

Altogether the CDP-DAG pathway is an important precursor to phospholipids and is therefore an exciting prospect for antifungal drug development. In particular targeting enzymes that synthesize PI (Pis1p), PS (Cho1p) and PE (Psd1p and Psd2p) show promise due to the essential nature of the genes and/or their lack of virulence in vivo. As loss of genes that encode proteins that produce PC through the CDP-DAG pathway (CHO2 and OPI3) are viable and lack virulence defects, these enzymes are not as interesting from a drug development standpoint. Based on the analysis of this pathway, it is reasonable to hypothesize that having un-impaired synthesis of PI, PS and PE through the CDP-DAG pathway is more important for cell survival and virulence than synthesis of the final phospholipid product PC, likely due to its ability to also be synthesized through the Kennedy pathway.

The Kennedy (salvage) pathway relies on the transport of exogenous ethanolamine and choline through the transporter Hnm1p and on DAG synthesis from PA through Mg2 + -dependent phosphatidate (PA) phosphatase Pah1p206. HNM1 has not been extensively characterized in pathogenic fungi. In C. albicans, loss of function of PAH1 results in severe hyphal defects in vitro and reduced virulence in murine models207. In C. neoformans PAH1 appears to be essential, as loss of function mutants were unable to be generated208. A pah1 homolog has not been characterized in A. fumigatus. Altogether Pah1p shows promise as a drug target as its loss in C. albicans reduced virulence and its function in C. neoformans is essential for growth.

Ethanolamine and choline are critical components of the Kennedy pathway and are phosphorylated by the protein kinases Eki1p and Cki1p respectively, and subsequently converted to CDP-ethanolamine and CDP-choline by cytidylyltransferases Ect1p and Pct1p209–213. The final step in PE and PC synthesis through the Kennedy pathway is mediated through the phosphotransferases Ept1p and Cpt1p respectively214,215. As mentioned above, based on the pathway redundancy exhibited by the CDP-DAG and the Kennedy pathway, none of the enzymes in the Kennedy Pathway are essential in S. cerevisiae50. In contrast to S. cerevisiae, C. albicans only uses Ept1p to modulate the last step in PE and PC synthesis198. Inhibiting PE and PC synthesis in C. albicans through the Kennedy pathway by deleting EPT1 results in reduced kidney fungal burden in a systemic candidiasis murine model, while over-expressing EPT1 results in hypervirulence198. These results further highlight that PE content modulates fungal virulence in C. albicans and additional studies are warranted in other human pathogenic fungi.

Overall, phospholipid synthesis is a complicated pathway to target with antifungals because there are multiple redundant enzymes and pathways. The most promising novel antifungal strategies for targeting phospholipid synthesis rely on inhibiting PA synthesis, PE synthesis or PI synthesis based on virulence studies in pathogenic fungi and their important role as precursors to other phospholipids (for PA, PE), and GPI anchors (for PI). The lack of human homologs to PA synthesis genes GPT2, SCT1, SLC1 or AYR1, increases the attractiveness of targeting PA synthesis as an antifungal development strategy due to the decreased likelihood of host toxicity (Table 3). Along these lines, the alkyl-phospholipid analog miltefosine has antifungal properties against yeasts, dimorphic fungi, molds, and dermatophytes216–227 and has synergy with Amphotericin B and voriconazole216,217,223. The mode of action of this drug is not yet elucidated, but there are multiple hypotheses that center on the inhibition of phospholipid synthesis as miltefosine is a phospholipid analog. Further research is needed to develop antifungals around phospholipid biosynthesis pathways.

Sphingolipids are a class of lipids characterized by their sphingoid base backbone and are important for various cellular functions, including cell structure, signaling, and regulation of cell growth and death. Sphingolipids are composed of a sphingoid base that can be dihydrosphingosine (DHS) or phytosphingosine (PHS), attached to fatty acids and a polar head. Multiple compounds with antifungal activity that target sphingolipids have been identified over the years and it remains an attractive antifungal drug target.

Sphingolipid synthesis requires activated palmitoyl-CoA from the FA synthesis pathway and the amino acid L-serine. These two compounds are converted to 3-ketodihydrosphingosine (KDHS) through the serine palmitoyltransferase complex (SPT)228. This is the first-rate limiting step in sphingolipid synthesis and is therefore the target for multiple regulatory pathways such as those mediated by the Orm1 and Orm2 proteins and the TOR and calcium/calcineurin pathways229. SPT is made up of three subunit proteins (Lcb1p, Lcb2p, and Tsc3p)228,230. In S. cerevisiae, Lcb1p and Lcb2p are required for the function of SPT, while Tsc3p is necessary for optimal function of SPT228,230. KDHS gets converted to dihydrosphingosine (DHS) by the 3-ketosphinganine reductase Tsc10p231. DHS gets hydroxylated to phytosphingosine (PHS) through the sphinganine C4-hydroxylase Sur2p232. DHS and PHS are the two sphingoid bases for sphingolipids, and both can be used in ceramide synthesis or act as bioactive signaling lipids after phosphorylation (DHS-1P, PHS-1P, S1P). Of the genes in this early stage of sphingolipid synthesis, integral SPT components (LCB1, LCB2) and TSC10 are essential for viability in S. cerevisiae50.

The antifungal myriocin inhibits Lcb1p and Lcb2p and is effective against Candida, Cryptococcus, and Aspergillus species233,234. However, myriocin exhibits high toxicity in vivo due to high similarity between fungal and mammalian SPT, limiting its use235. Sphingofungins isolated from A. fumigatus inhibit SPT and showed promise for their antifungal properties against Cryptococcus spp. in vitro but have yet to be validated in vivo236,237. TSC3 does not have homologs in C. albicans, C. neoformans or A. fumigatus and is therefore not an exciting strategy for antifungal development (Table 4). SUR2 is not essential for viability because DHS can be used to synthesize ceramides and complex sphingolipids, but the SUR2 loss of function mutant shows severe sphingolipid composition changes in S. cerevisiae232,238 which could result in altering virulence in pathogenic fungi. Targeted inhibition of Sur2p could prove a viable antifungal strategy with one example being the bacterial virulence factor and fungal toxin Syringomycin E. This result suggests inhibition of ceramide synthesis is a viable strategy for antifungal drug development targeting C. neoformans. Phytoceramide/dihydroceramide are α-hydroxylated by Scs7p into α-hydroxyphytoceramideα-hydroxydihydroceramide, which is the precursor to complex sphingolipids243. Complex sphingolipids include inositol-P-ceramide (IPC), mannose-inositol-P-ceramide (MIPC) and mannose-(inositol-P)2-ceramide (M(IP)2C). IPC is synthesized by the phosphoinositol transferase Aur1p with the addition of phosphoinositol (PI) head group244,245. Subsequently, IPC gets mannosylated to MIPC by the MIPC synthase complex (Csg1p, Csg2p and Csh1p) through the addition of GDP mannose246,247. Finally, MIPC has another phosphoinositol head group added through the Inositolphosphotransferase Ipt1p to yield M(IP)2C248. Complex sphingolipids can be recycled through hydrolyzation to yield DHS/PHS ceramides and phosphoinositol groups by lipase Isc1p, and ceramidases Ypc1p and Ydc1p249–251.

Of the genes involved in synthesizing ceramides and complex sphingolipids in S. cerevisiae, only AUR1 is essential for viability and is the target for the antifungals aureobasidin A and australifungin252. Ceramide synthesis (Lag1p, Lac1p) can be inhibited by fumonisin B1 and this inhibition is an effective strategy to inhibit growth of Aspergillus, Candida and Cryptococcus species in vitro253,254. An existing liability of australifungin A and fumonisin B1 as antifungal drugs is the toxicity to mammalian cells255,256. Meanwhile aureobasidin A, a large natural product, is difficult to synthesize in sufficient quantities in vitro. Rustmicin was identified as a potent inhibitor of inositol phosphoceramide synthase and active against C. neoformans, however, low stability and high drug efflux are two limitations of this molecule257. Inhibiting sphingolipid metabolism through loss of function of ISC1 results in decreased virulence in C. neoformans, suggesting its potential as a drug target258.

S. cerevisiae does not produce glycosylceramides, but some pathogenic fungi such as Cryptococcus and Aspergillus produce this class of ceramide31,259,260. Glycosylceramides are ceramides linked to a sugar polar head group, with glucose being the most common (glucosylceramide, known as GlcCer). GlcCer is an important virulence factor in pathogenic fungi31,242. The first step of GlcCer synthesis requires DHS converted by ceramide synthase to create α-hydroxy-ceramide. α-hydroxy-ceramide is then desaturated by ∆8 desaturase Sld8p to produce OH-Δ8-Cer261. C9-methyl transferase Smt1p then converts OH-Δ8-Cer into OH-Δ8, 9-Me-Cer261. Finally, the glucosylceramide synthase 1, Gcs1p adds a glucose moiety UDP-glucose to finalize GlcCer synthesis (α-hydroxy-Δ8, 9-methyl-glucosylceramide)261. The sphingolipid ∆8 desaturase (SLD1), the sphingolipid C9-methyltransferase (MTS1) and the glucosylceramide synthase (HSX11) in C. albicans are not essential for viability262,263. In C. neoformans, these genes are not essential for growth in vitro in standard laboratory conditions, but are necessary for full virulence in vivo31,264,265. It is unclear whether loss of SLD1, MTS1 or HSX11 in C. albicans affects virulence in vivo. The mammalian GlcCer synthase inhibitor D-threo-PDMP is effective at inhibiting growth in Aspergillus spp. The acylhydrazone BHBM inhibits GlcCer synthesis and is effective against Cryptococcus, Candida, and Aspergillus spp. in vitro, although further validation in vivo is needed to follow-up on these findings266. The ∆8 desaturase and C9-methyl transferase enzymes are exclusive to fungi, therefore targeted inhibition of these proteins could be an efficacious antifungal strategy in vivo .

Antifungal susceptibility is impacted by multiple lipid synthesis pathways. For example, ergosterol and sphingolipids are constituents of lipids rafts in the plasma membrane where they physically interact267. Research has observed that cells alter their sphingolipid composition in response to changes in sterol structures as a result of mutations in the ergosterol synthesis pathway in yeast137. NMR studies show that the type of sterol in artificial cell membranes affected the order of lipids and solubility of unsaturated fatty acids268. Functionally, in pathogenic fungi, there is evidence that altering the ergosterol synthesis pathway results in changes in lipid synthesis and composition in the cell membrane. In one study, C. auris clinical isolates that are triazole and amphotericin B resistant have divergent sphingolipid composition, supporting that cells can compensate for alterations in ergosterol content with changes in sphingolipid synthesis269. Additionally, sphingolipid synthesis pathway alterations impact susceptibility to triazoles270–272. One of the main regulators of sphingolipid synthesis, ormA (ORM1), has increased expression in response to triazole treatment and over-expression of ormA results in triazole resistance in A. fumigatus272. Furthermore, a C. albicans sphingoid long-chain kinase null mutant, ∆LCB4, is miconazole hyper-susceptible, and supplementation of its product PHS-1P increases miconazole resistance273. Phospholipid synthesis null mutant strains of PSD1, PSD2 (PE synthesis), CHO2, and OPI3 (PC synthesis) have increased fluconazole susceptibility in S. cerevisiae274. Altogether, these data suggest that altered ergosterol content results in changes in phospholipid/sphingolipid content and vice versa, which greatly affects cell membrane homeostasis. This close relationship between sterols and phospholipid/sphingolipids presents an opportunity to develop antifungals that target phospholipid or sphingolipid pathways to potentiate contemporary triazole antifungal activity.

Other ways to exploit sterol/phospholipid interactions are to target genes in fatty acid/sphingolipid synthesis pathways that exhibit genetic interactions with genes in the ergosterol synthesis pathway. This idea has been tested in S. cerevisiae and could be expanded to pathogenic fungi275–277. In S. cerevisiae, ERG11 exhibits synthetic lethal gene interactions with phospholipid methyltransferase OPI3 (PC synthesis) and with sphingolipid alpha-hydroxylase SCS7 (complex sphingolipid synthesis)278. Therefore, it is reasonable to hypothesize that a small molecule that inhibits Opi3p or Scs7p would synergize with triazoles. Erg11p is associated with multiple other lipid synthesis proteins in protein physical interaction experiments such as Cpt1p, Cho2p, Elo2p, Ept1p, Hnm1p, Lac1p, Ole1p, Orm1p, Scs7p, Slc1p, and Sur2p through PCA, but more work needs to be done to characterize these potential interactions and their biological implications in pathogenic fungi279,280. Supporting these protein interactions with Erg11p, loss and/or decreased function strains of most of the genes described above with genetic or physical interactions with ERG11/Erg11p have differential susceptibility to triazoles in S. cerevisiae281–284.

Multiple current antifungal molecules mentioned in this review support the feasibility of developing antifungal drugs that target FA or sphingolipid synthesis that show synergy with triazoles. The FAS inhibitors cerulenin and NPD6433, SPT inhibitor myriocin, ACS inhibitor AR-12, and UFA synthesis inhibitor MBX-7591 have been shown to enhance azole activity in different pathogenic fungi150,153,154,285. Furthermore, the GlcCer inhibitor BHMH has synergy with fluconazole and amphotericin B against C. neoformans266. Overall, exploiting the complex interplay between ergosterol synthesis and other lipid synthesis pathways will allow not only development of new antifungal drugs but an opportunity to consider novel combination therapies with triazole antifungals. Overall, research to date strongly suggests simultaneous inhibition of multiple pathways involved in the synthesis of key components of fungal cell membranes is a viable strategy for antifungal drug development. Opportunities exist for development of novel monotherapies as well as potent combination therapies with existing antifungal drugs. For example, based on their synthetic lethal interaction, we can predict that an inhibitor of the non-essential enzyme Scs7p (complex sphingolipid) would have synergy with an Erg11p inhibitor such as voriconazole. Moreover, outside of genes with established genetic interactions, the increased triazole susceptibility evident in null mutants of genes involved in lipid synthesis can be leveraged for increasing the efficacy of triazoles and amphotericin B. Further study into the interplay between ergosterol and lipid biosynthesis pathways is necessary for developing novel antifungal treatment strategies.

Limitations and challenges in this approach arise due to intangible and sometimes unexplored differences in lipid synthesis pathways between pathogenic and non-pathogenic fungi. The focus on yeast as a model organism allows us to describe pathways that are well defined, however a lack of complementary studies and differences in gene copy number in pathogenic fungi prevents us from fully predicting if a specific antifungal strategy would be successful in vivo. Finally, there is always the human host challenge, as most of the genes outlined in this review have human homologs. Therefore, careful consideration of drug target selection and subsequent fungal-specific drug design is imperative to overcome this challenge. That being said, both current antifungal therapies and cancer therapies demonstrate that new therapies with host toxicity can be effective at mitigating disease. As such, careful in vivo studies with the drug target and hit or lead molecules should be conducted to assess the risk-benefit for any new antifungal. Finally, increasing basic research on the fungal genes and pathways outlined in this review in pathogenic fungi to not only understand conserved target function and associated phenotypes but also to test function in vivo will be critical for prioritizing future drug development efforts.
