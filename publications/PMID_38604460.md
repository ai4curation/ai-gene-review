DEAD-box helicases are important players in mitochondrial gene expression, which is necessary for mitochon­ drial respiration. In this study, we characterized Schizosaccharomyces pombe Mss116 (spMss116), a member of the family of DEAD-box RNA helicases. Deletion of spmss116 in a mitochondrial intron-containing background significantly reduced the levels of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)-encoded cox1 and cob1 mRNAs and impaired mitochondrial translation, leading to a severe respiratory defect and a loss of cell viability during stationary phase. Deletion of mitochondrial introns restored the levels of cox1 and cob1 mRNAs to wide-type (WT) levels but could not restore mitochondrial translation and respiration in Δspmss116 cells. Furthermore, deletion of spmss116 in both mitochondrial intron-containing and intronless backgrounds impaired mitoribosome assembly and destabilization of mitoribosomal proteins. Our findings suggest that defective mitochondrial translation caused by deletion of spmss116 is most likely due to impaired mitoribosome assembly.

1. Introduction Mitochondria are semiautonomous eukaryotic organelles that are thought to be descendants of endosymbionts (Youle, 2019). They generate the biological energy currency ATP by oxidative phosphory­ lation (OXPHOS) (Vakifahmetoglu-Norberg et al., 2017). Mitochondria also participate in the metabolism of amino acids, nucleotides and lipids, and the synthesis of heme and iron–sulfur clusters (Rutter and Hughes, 2015; Vakifahmetoglu-Norberg et al., 2017). In mammals, mitochondria also act as a signaling organelle that controls cellular processes such as apoptosis and immune responses (Chandel, 2015). Mitochondria retain their own genomes (mtDNAs) and gene expression systems (Kummer and Ban, 2021). mtDNA primarily encodes core catalytic subunits of OXPHOS complexes, and a full set of tRNAs and two rRNAs. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe, mtDNA encodes three subunits of cytochrome c oxidase (Cox1, Cox2, and Cox3), apocytochrome b (Cob1, also called Cytb or COB), three subunits of the ATP synthase complex (Atp6, Atp8 and Atp9), a small mitochondrial ribosomal (mitoribosome) subunit (mtSSU) protein

Var1 (Anziano et al., 1983). Unlike mammalian mtDNAs, fungal mtDNAs generally contain introns. The mtDNA in the standard S. pombe WT strain 972 contains only three introns: two group I introns in cox1 (cox1-I1b and cox1-I2b) and one group II intron in cob1 (cob1-I1) (Schafer, 2005). cox1-I1b and cox1-I2b encode both a maturase and a DNA endonuclease, respectively (Pellenz et al., 2002), while cob1-I1 encodes both a reverse transcriptase and a maturase (Schäfer et al., 2003). These intron-encoded proteins are involved in the splicing of the introns that encode them. Translation of mitochondrial mRNAs (mt-mRNAs) relies mainly on nucleus-encoded proteins, such as pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) pro­ teins, DEAD-box proteins, and ribosomal proteins (Bohnsack et al., 2023; Herbert et al., 2013; Mays et al., 2019). It has been shown that mutations in nuclear genes encoding mitochondrial translational factors are linked to human diseases (Alston et al., 2021; Gorman et al., 2016; Perez-Martinez et al., 2008). In most cases, translation of mt-mRNAs in S. cerevisiae and S. pombe is regulated by multiple specific factors (Dinh and Bonnefoy, 2023; Her­ bert et al., 2013; Remes et al., 2023). Thus, it appears that a specific set of translational activators are involved in delivering of mtmRNAs to the mitoribosome through interacting with the 5′-untrans­ lated regions (UTRs) of mt-mRNAs. In addition, some of mRNA-specific factors are required for the stability and/or processing of mt-mRNAs, while others coordinate mitochondrial translation and the subsequent OXPHOS complex assembly. In S. cerevisiae, synthesis of Cob1 is dependent on the combined action of three translational activators (PPR protein Cbp1, Cbs1 and Cbs2) and two assembly factors (Cbp3 and Cbp6) (Gruschke et al., 2012; Salvatori et al., 2020), while translation of cox1 and cox2 mRNAs is controlled by the combined action of Pet309, Mss51, Mam33, and scMss116, and the Aim23-Pet111 complex, respectively (Chicherin et al., 2020; De Silva et al., 2017; Perez-Martinez et al., 2009; Roloff and Henry, 2015). In S. pombe, ten PPR proteins (Ppr1-10) were identified as regulators of the expression of mtDNAencoded genes (Kühl et al., 2011; Luo et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2017). Among these, Ppr1 and Ppr4 function as specific translation factors. Ppr1 stabilizes cox2 and cox3 mRNAs and plays a role in their translation (Kühl et al., 2011). Ppr4 forms a stable complex (Mrh5C) with DEADbox protein Mrh5, spMtf2, and spSls1 to activate the translation of cox1 mRNA (Herbert et al., 2021; Kühl et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022). In S. pombe, Cbp7 and Cbp8, which are homologs of S. cerevisiae Cbs2 and Cbp1, respectively, form a complex required for Cob1 synthesis (Herbert et al., 2021). Mammalian mtDNA-encoded mRNAs lack 5′-UTRs, hence their translation has been proposed to be regulated by different mechanisms. Consistent with this idea, TACO1, a translational activator for COX1 mRNA, is the only specific translational activator that has been identi­ fied in humans. Instead, many assembly factors for OXPHOS complexes have been identified to be required for mitochondrial translation, indi­ cating that coordinated regulation of mitochondrial translation and OXPHOS assembly. It has been shown that the two core components of the cytochrome c oxidase assembly complex MITRAC complex MITRAC12 and C12ORF62 are required for COX1 mRNA translation (Mick et al., 2012; Weraarpachai et al., 2012). Interestingly, the stability of TACO1 is dependent on the cytochrome c assembly factor PET117 and the two proteins form a complex (Richman et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2023; Weraarpachai et al., 2009). Recently, it has been shown that PREPL, a thioesterase, specifically regulates the translation of COX1 mRNA, sug­ gesting that PREPL may function as a link between mitochondrial lipid metabolism to mtDNA expression (Kramer et al., 2023). Mitochondrial translation is also globally controlled by translational factors. In S. cerevisiae, scMtf2, scSls1 and Mrh4 are required for mito­ chondrial translation (Asher et al., 1989; De Silva et al., 2013; Rode­ heffer and Shadel, 2003; Rouillard et al., 1996). In S. pombe, Ppr2 and the Ppr10-Mpa1 complex are required for overall mitochondrial protein synthesis (Kühl et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2017). In humans, PPR protein LRPPRC (Ruzzenente et al., 2012), IGF2BP1 (Cruz-Zaragoza et al., 2021), GTPBP10 (Lavdovskaia et al., 2018), METTL15 (Van Haute et al., 2019), DEAD-box protein DHX30 and DDX28 (Cruz-Zaragoza et al., 2021; Tu and Barrientos, 2015), and C6orf203 (Gopalakrishna et al., 2019) are required for mitochondrial translation. The precise roles of these proteins remain to be elucidated. The DEAD-box helicases are a family of RNA helicases that contain eight to nine conserved motifs. These proteins are highly conserved from bacteria to humans (Tanner and Linder, 2001). They virtually function in all processes of mitochondrial RNA metabolism such as mRNA turn­ over/surveillance, RNA splicing, translation, and mitoribosome biogenesis (Bourgeois et al., 2016; Jankowsky and Fairman, 2007; Szczesny et al., 2013). In S. cerevisiae, four mitochondrial DEAD-box proteins (Mrh4, Suv3, scMss116, and scIrc3) have been identified. Mrh4 and Suv3 are involved in mitoribosome biogenesis and RNA editing/turnover, respectively (De Silva et al., 2013; Dziembowski et al., 2003; Turk and Caprara, 2010). scMss116 plays a role in mitochondrial transcription elongation, splicing of mitochondrial introns, and

mitoribosome biogenesis and mRNA-specific translation (De Silva et al., 2017; Seraphin et al., 1989). scMss116 forms a complex with the PPR protein Pet309, a cox1 mRNA-specific translation factor, and protects it from degradation (De Silva et al., 2017). scIrc3 mediates mitochondrial translation elongation (Kaur and Datta, 2021). In S. pombe, there are four mitochondrial DEAD-box proteins: Mrh5, Rpm2, spMss116, and spIrc3. Rpm2, spMss116 and spIrc3 are homologous to S. cerevisiae Suv3, scMss116 and scIrc3, respectively. Mrh5 is involved in cox1 mRNA translation (Herbert et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024), while Rpm2 has been implicated in mitochondrial RNA 3′-end processing (Hoffmann et al., 2008). The functions of other two S. pombe DEAD-box proteins remain to be determined. In humans, the DEAD-box proteins DHX30 and DDX28 are required for mitoribosome biogenesis (Antonicka and Shoubridge, 2015; Tu and Barrientos, 2015), while SUPV3L1 is required for mt-RNA polyadenylation and turnover (Borowski et al., 2013). In the present study, we investigated the role of spMss116 and found that it is required for mitoribosome assembly and mitochondrial trans­ lation. 3. Results

Purification of mitochondria, protease protection, and isolation of soluble and membrane-bound proteins from mitochondria by sodium carbonate extraction were performed as previously described (Wang et al., 2017).

3.1. spMss116 and scMss116 are similar in structure We first performed sequence analysis and found that spMss116 possesses nine conserved motifs characteristic of the DEAD box family of RNA helicases (Fig. S1). spMss116 has sequence similarity to scMss116, and the two proteins share 35 % sequence identity and 59 % sequence similarity. We predicted the structures of spMss116 and scMss116 using AlphaFold (Jumper et al., 2021; Varadi et al., 2022). The results showed that spMss116 and scMss116 are composed of two domains. Domain 1 is located in the N-terminal of the protein and consists of motifs Q, I, Ia, Ib, II and III. Domain 2 is located in the C-terminal of the protein and consists of motifs IV, V and VI (Fig. S2A, B). As shown in Fig. S2C, the two predicted structures are aligned with a backbone RMSD = 3.8 Å and a TM-score of 0.80 if normalized by the length of spMss116 or a TMscore of 0.66 if normalized by the length of scMss116, suggesting that the two proteins have similar structures.

2.6. In vivo labeling of mitochondrial translation products S. pombe cells were grown to an early exponential phase in 50 mL rich medium containing 0.5 % yeast extract, 250 mg/L uracil, leucine, adenine and histidine, 5 % raffinose and 0.1 % glucose. Then cells were pre-incubated in 500 μL of reaction buffer (40 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 6.0, 50 g/L raffinose, 1 g/L glucose) containing 1 mg/mL anisomycin for 15 min. Mitochondrial translation products were labeled at 30 ◦ C with 176 μCi/ml [35S]-methionine/cysteine (NEG-072, PerkinElmer Life Science) for 3 h in reaction buffer containing 1 mg/mL ani­ somycin (Gouget et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2017). Cells were lysed in lysis buffer (1.8 M NaOH, 1 M β–mercaptoethanol and 10 mM PMSF) and precipitated by trichloroacetic acid (TCA). Proteins were separated on 16 % acrylamide–0.5 % bisacrylamide SDS-PAGE, blotted on nitrocel­ lulose membranes, and detected using the Cyclone Plus Storage Phos­ phor System (Perkin-Elmer).

3.2. spMss116 is localized in the mitochondria Previous genome-wide protein location analysis in S. pombe found that spMss116 is located in mitochondria (Matsuyama et al., 2006). To confirm and extend this result, we isolated mitochondria from WT S. pombe cells and prepared mitochondrial lysates for Western blot analysis. The results showed that spMss116 was enriched in the mito­ chondrial fraction (Fig. 1A). To further determine the submitochondrial localization of spMss116, we treated the isolated mitochondria with proteinase K alone or together with Triton X-100, a membranedisrupting detergent. spMss116 was protected from proteinase K digestion in the absence of Triton X-100 (Fig. 1B), demonstrating that spMss116 is localized inside the mitochondria. We further treated the mitochondria with Na2CO3 at pH 11.5, which separates soluble and peripheral membrane-associated proteins from integral membrane proteins. Most of spMss116 was found in the soluble fraction after treatment with Na2CO3, with a small amount in the pellet fraction (Fig. 1C). These results suggest that spMss116 might be mitochondrial peripheral membrane protein.

3.3. Deletion of spmss116 causes a respiratory growth defect To determine if spMss116 is important for mitochondrial function, we grew WT and Δspmss116 cells on rich medium containing glucose (YES; fermentation medium) or glycerol (respiration medium) as major carbon source. The results showed that Δspmss116 cells exhibited a slight growth defect on a glucose-containing medium (Fig. 2A). By contrast, like respiration-deficient mutants (Liu et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2017), Δspmss116 cells exhibited extremely poor growth on glycerol-containing rich medium (Fig. 2A). To confirm the slight growth retardation of Δspmss116 cells on YES medium, we examined the growth rate of WT and Δspmss116 cells in YES medium. Δspmss116 cells grew slightly slower than WT cells in YES medium (Fig. 2B). We next examined the viability of Δspmss116 cells by spotting assays. Consistent with other S. pombe respiratory deficient mutants (Liu et al., 2022; Su et al., 2017), Δspmss116 cells lost most of their viability after 48 h of incubation. (Fig. 2C). Altogether, these results suggest that spMss116 is required for mitochondrial respiration.

3.5. spMss116 is required for mitochondrial translation To determine whether spMss116 is required for mtDNA-encoded proteins synthesis, we performed in vivo [35S]-methionine/cysteine la­ beling of mitochondrial translation products in WT and Δspmss116 cells in the presence of anisomycin, a cytosolic translation inhibitor (Dujeancourt et al., 2013; Gouget et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2017). The labeled mtDNA-encoded proteins were identified based on their sizes and published results (Kühl et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2017). We found that compared to the WT cells, the levels of mtDNAencoded proteins were substantially decreased in Δspmss116 cells (Fig. 4A), indicating that spMss116 is required for the synthesis of mtDNA-encoded proteins. Western blot analysis revealed that the steady-state levels of Cox1, Cox2, Cox3, Cob1 and Atp6 were reduced dramatically in Δspmss116 cells (Fig. 4B). Altogether, these results showed that spMss116 is required for mitochondrial translation.

4. Discussion Our study demonstrates that spMss116 is required for global mito­ chondrial translation. While the exact role of spMss116 in mitochondrial translation still remains to be determined, our study provides clues regarding how spMss116 might be involved in mitochondrial protein synthesis. We show that deletion of spmss116 leads to destabilization of the mtSSU and mtLSU and a significant decrease in mitoribosome for­ mation, suggesting that spMss116 may be involved in mitoribosome assembly. It is very likely that impaired mitoribosome assembly caused by deletion of spmss116 resulted in a reduction in overall mitochondrial protein synthesis in the Δspmss116 cells. Given the fact that spMss116 belongs to the family of DEAD-box RNA helicases, we suggest that spMss116 may play a role in mitoribosome biogenesis via targeting of mt-rRNA(s). It remains to be determined how spMss116 recognizes and binds its target RNA(s). In this regard, it is worth noting that DEAD-box proteins were shown to have interaction partners (De Silva et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2024). This is due to the fact that DEAD-box proteins generally cannot recognize their targets directly, and are dependent on ancillary proteins for target recognition (Ozgur et al., 2015). It is possible that spMss116 has binding partner(s) that provide specific recognition of targets. spMss116 shares sequence and structural similarity with scMss116, suggesting that these proteins originate from a common ancestry.
