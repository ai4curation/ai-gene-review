The pathway for synthesis of proline in most forms of life produces a highly
unstable intermediate, γ-L-glutamyl 5-phosphate (GP). For nearly 70 y,
channeling of this intermediate from the active site of glutamate 5-kinase to
the active site of GP reductase has been believed to protect GP from cyclization
to a dead-end product. However, the evidence presented in support of this idea
is not conclusive. We show that changes in the structures of the kinase or
reductase that should preclude a protein-protein interaction do not compromise
proline synthesis in Escherichia coli, demonstrating that channeling does not
occur. We calculate that the half-life of GP is 320 ms. Although GP is indeed
unstable, it should diffuse the length of an E. coli cell in less than 3 ms.
Thus, most GP produced by glutamate 5-kinase should encounter the active site of
GP reductase before cyclization occurs.

## Full Text

Proline is an essential precursor for protein synthesis in all forms of life. As the only secondary amino acid in proteins, it plays an important role in both kinking and rigidifying polypeptide backbones (Fig. 1A). In addition, proline accumulation protects many organisms against osmotic, desiccation, and salt stress (1). The pathway for synthesis of proline in Escherichia coli begins with phosphorylation of L-glutamate to γ-L-glutamyl 5-phosphate (GP) by glutamate 5-kinase (GK, ProB in bacteria) (Fig. 1B). GP is subsequently reduced to L-glutamate 5-semialdehyde (GSA) by γ-glutamyl phosphate reductase (GPR, ProA in bacteria). GSA spontaneously cyclizes to (S)-1-pyrroline 5-carboxylate, which is reduced to proline by pyrroline 5-carboxylate reductase (ProC). This pathway had likely evolved by the last universal common ancestor (LUCA), as it is found in most bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes. A striking feature of this ancient pathway is that GP is unstable, rapidly cyclizing to 5-oxoproline (5-OP) (2).

The dogma in the literature for nearly 70 y has been that cyclization of GP is prevented by a physical interaction that channels GP between the active sites of ProB and ProA. The earliest recognition that GP is a problematic intermediate dates to a 1957 paper by Strecker, who stated that “Formation of free GP as an intermediate….seems unlikely” (3). In the ensuing decades, the idea that GP is channeled between the active sites of ProB and ProA has become generally accepted (2, 4–10). The finding that GK and GPR are fused into a bifunctional enzyme called Δ1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase (P5CS) in animals (11, 12) and plants (13) has been interpreted as evidence that the fusion might promote channeling between the two active sites (6). The recently published structures of Drosophila and Arabidopsis P5CS tetramers and filaments formed by stacking of these tetramers show that the two active sites are not directly juxtaposed (14, 15). However, they are close enough (within about 60 Å and possibly less due to motions of the GPR domain) that GP released from the GK active site is likely to be retained within the filament and rapidly captured by the GPR active site, a form of channeling that does not require a direct protein–protein interaction bridging the two active sites. However, the experimental evidence that has been presented to support the notion of channeling between ProB and ProA is not entirely convincing. Smith et al. (8) and Seddon et al. (4) reported that ProA stimulates the activity of ProB, suggesting a physical interaction between the two proteins. We found that addition of either ProA or bovine serum albumin (BSA) produced a modest stimulation of ProB activity (see below), suggesting that this may be a nonspecific effect. Finally, Seddon et al. reported that addition of ProA to ProB protected GP from cyclization, a finding that was interpreted as evidence of channeling (4). We also find that ProA protects GP from cyclization (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). While this finding is consistent with channeling, it is also consistent with release of GP into solution and capture by ProA before cyclization occurs without requiring channeling.

Here, we reexamine the long-standing assumption that GP is channeled between the active sites of ProB and ProA using a range of experimental and computational approaches. By changing the structures of either the kinase or the reductase, we show that channeling is not required to support proline synthesis in vivo. We calculate that the rate constant for release of GP from the active site of ProB rate is at least 10-fold faster than the rate constant for encounter of ProB and ProA in the cytoplasm. Finally, using model reactions in the literature, we calculate that the half-life of GP is on the order of 320 msec. Given that small molecules can diffuse the length of E. coli in 3 ms, there is ample time for GP to encounter the active site of ProA before it cyclizes.

DiscussionAlthough most of the thousands of metabolites in metabolic networks are reasonably well behaved, some pose challenges. Volatile metabolites such as ammonia and carbon monoxide can escape from cells, resulting in loss of valuable resources. Hydrophobic metabolites such as indole, an intermediate in tryptophan biosynthesis, can be lost by diffusion through cell membranes (33). Toxic metabolites such as tetrachlorobenzoquinone formed during degradation of pentachlorophenol (34) and aldehydes formed during degradation of 1,2-propanediol and ethanolamine (35–38) can damage cellular macromolecules. Nature has evolved diverse mechanisms to control problematic metabolic intermediates. Tunnels shepherd ammonia, carbon monoxide, and indole between active sites in multifunctional enzymes (39). Transient interactions between enzymes can channel intermediates between active sites (40). Bacteria sequester reactive aldehyde intermediates in microcompartments with impermeable protein shells (41). Tetrachlorobenzoquinone is sequestered at the active site of pentachlorophenol hydroxylase until it has been reduced to the less-toxic tetrachlorohydroquinone (36).The mechanism for control of GP, a notoriously unstable intermediate, has been assumed for decades to be channeling between the active sites of ProB and ProA, although careful analysis suggests that previous kinetic evidence has been inconclusive. Here, we employed methods that were not available to earlier researchers as well as classical enzyme kinetics to reexamine the long-standing belief that GP is channeled between the active sites of ProB and ProA.We found that altering the structure of either the kinase or the reductase in the proline synthesis pathway in ways that should preclude formation of a protein–protein interaction between the two proteins does not impair growth of E. coli, providing convincing evidence that channeling of GP between the active sites of ProB and ProA is not required. A similar observation was made by Orser et al. in 1988 (42); E. coli ProB was found to restore growth of a Saccharomyces cerevisiae mutant lacking GK (PRO1). The sequence of E. coli ProB was known (43), but the yeast PRO1 gene was not sequenced until 1992 (44) and there was no crystal structure for any GK. Similarly, overexpression of Corynebacterium glutamicum proA was found to support growth of an E. coli proA mutant (9). The first crystal structure of a ProA (from Thermotoga maritima) was not released until 2003 (45). Finally, our calculation of the half-life of GP suggests, contrary to previous belief, that it is stable enough to persist in the bacterial cytoplasm for long enough to encounter the active site of ProA.The lack of channeling between ProB and ProA makes sense in light of the evidence that the proline synthesis pathway shown in Fig. 1 dates back to the last universal common ancestor (LUCA). If GP were such a problematic intermediate, it seems unlikely that a pathway requiring a sophisticated protein–protein interaction would have emerged by the LUCA. However, the problem presented by cyclization of GP is clearly not irrelevant. Many bacteria, including E. coli, have a metabolite repair enzyme that converts 5-OP back to glutamate (46). This process requires expenditure of ATP but rescues the carbon and nitrogen invested in GP from ending up in a dead-end product.We suggest that an elaborate mechanism such as channeling is not necessary to protect the majority of the GP produced by ProB from cyclization in bacteria. However, the instability of GP might pose a bigger problem in larger cells. As described above, GK and GPR activities are fused in P5CS in animal and plant cells. P5CS forms puncta in the mitochondria of human cells (47); the mitochondrial localization could minimize the distance over which GP must diffuse between active sites. However, P5CS is found in the cytoplasm in plant cells (48), suggesting that targeting of the enzyme to a subcellular compartment is not necessary to sustain proline synthesis. Interestingly, recent studies have shown that Drosophila and Arabidopsis P5CSs form tetramers that assemble into filaments termed cytoophidia (cellular snakes) in the presence of substrates. The GK active sites in the center of the filaments are surrounded by the GPR active sites at the periphery of the filament (29, 31), an arrangement that should facilitate rapid capture of GP released from the active site of the kinase. The emergence of this elaborate mechanism for controlling the fate of GP suggests that fusion of GK and GPR and formation of some type of aggregated structure might have been a key evolutionary event enabling the dramatic increase in cell size between bacteria (0.5 to 1.5 µm3) and animal and plant cells (1,000 to 4,000 µm3).

The mechanism for control of GP, a notoriously unstable intermediate, has been assumed for decades to be channeling between the active sites of ProB and ProA, although careful analysis suggests that previous kinetic evidence has been inconclusive. Here, we employed methods that were not available to earlier researchers as well as classical enzyme kinetics to reexamine the long-standing belief that GP is channeled between the active sites of ProB and ProA.

We found that altering the structure of either the kinase or the reductase in the proline synthesis pathway in ways that should preclude formation of a protein–protein interaction between the two proteins does not impair growth of E. coli, providing convincing evidence that channeling of GP between the active sites of ProB and ProA is not required. A similar observation was made by Orser et al. in 1988 (42); E. coli ProB was found to restore growth of a Saccharomyces cerevisiae mutant lacking GK (PRO1). The sequence of E. coli ProB was known (43), but the yeast PRO1 gene was not sequenced until 1992 (44) and there was no crystal structure for any GK. Similarly, overexpression of Corynebacterium glutamicum proA was found to support growth of an E. coli proA mutant (9). The first crystal structure of a ProA (from Thermotoga maritima) was not released until 2003 (45). In both cases, the lack of structural information made it impossible to determine whether the residues required for channeling between GK and GPR are highly conserved or, at the other extreme, the two enzymes do not in fact interact to transfer GP.

Further support for the conclusion that GP is not channeled between ProB and ProA is provided by our calculation that the rate of dissociation of GP from the active site of ProB is at least an order of magnitude faster than the rate of encounter between ProB and ProA in the cytoplasm. Finally, our calculation of the half-life of GP suggests, contrary to previous belief, that it is stable enough to persist in the bacterial cytoplasm for long enough to encounter the active site of ProA.

The lack of channeling between ProB and ProA makes sense in light of the evidence that the proline synthesis pathway shown in Fig. 1 dates back to the last universal common ancestor (LUCA). If GP were such a problematic intermediate, it seems unlikely that a pathway requiring a sophisticated protein–protein interaction would have emerged by the LUCA. However, the problem presented by cyclization of GP is clearly not irrelevant. Many bacteria, including E. coli, have a metabolite repair enzyme that converts 5-OP back to glutamate (46). This process requires expenditure of ATP but rescues the carbon and nitrogen invested in GP from ending up in a dead-end product.

We suggest that an elaborate mechanism such as channeling is not necessary to protect the majority of the GP produced by ProB from cyclization in bacteria. However, the instability of GP might pose a bigger problem in larger cells. As described above, GK and GPR activities are fused in P5CS in animal and plant cells. P5CS forms puncta in the mitochondria of human cells (47); the mitochondrial localization could minimize the distance over which GP must diffuse between active sites. However, P5CS is found in the cytoplasm in plant cells (48), suggesting that targeting of the enzyme to a subcellular compartment is not necessary to sustain proline synthesis. Interestingly, recent studies have shown that Drosophila and Arabidopsis P5CSs form tetramers that assemble into filaments termed cytoophidia (cellular snakes) in the presence of substrates. The GK active sites in the center of the filaments are surrounded by the GPR active sites at the periphery of the filament (29, 31), an arrangement that should facilitate rapid capture of GP released from the active site of the kinase. The emergence of this elaborate mechanism for controlling the fate of GP suggests that fusion of GK and GPR and formation of some type of aggregated structure might have been a key evolutionary event enabling the dramatic increase in cell size between bacteria (0.5 to 1.5 µm3) and animal and plant cells (1,000 to 4,000 µm3).

Protein Purification.

His6-ProA, His6-ArgC, and His6-Asd were expressed from ASKA plasmids (20) in ΔargC::kanR ΔproA:cmR BL21 (DE3). Untagged ProB was prepared by expressing protein from two copies of proB on the same plasmid (pAM148), one encoding a His10-SUMO tag with a cleavage site for ubiquitin-like-specific protease 1 (Ulp1) and one lacking a tag sequence. The tagged and untagged proteins were coexpressed in ΔproBA::cmR E. coli BL21 (DE3) (AM483). Expressing mixed tetramers in which only some protomers were tagged improved yield after protease cleavage because cleavage of every His10-SUMO tag on the tetramer is required to enable separation of ProB from the cleaved His10-SUMO tag by immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC). (If a single protomer retains a His10-SUMO tag, the entire tetramer will be retained on the HisPur Ni-NTA resin used for IMAC.)

Tagged versions of ProB, ProA, ArgC, and Asd were purified using the following protocol with minor variations. Single colonies of E. coli strains carrying expression plasmids were inoculated into 10 mL of LB or TB plus kanamycin (30 µg/mL) or chloramphenicol (34 µg/mL) and the cultures were grown overnight at 37 °C with shaking. The cultures were inoculated into 1 L of LB or TB plus kanamycin (30 µg/mL) or chloramphenicol (34 µg/mL) and the flasks were incubated at 37 °C with shaking until the OD600 reached ≈0.6. IPTG was added to a final concentration of 500 µM and the temperature was decreased to 28 °C. After overnight growth, cells were harvested by centrifugation for 15 min at 5,000×g and 4 °C. The cell pellets were frozen at −20 °C.

After thawing, the cells were suspended in lysis buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 8.0, containing 300 mM NaCl and 10 mM imidazole; 40 mL/10 g wet cell weight). Lysozyme (final concentration 0.0 to 1.0 mg/mL), Benzonase nuclease (250 U/10 g wet cell weight), and protease inhibitor cocktail (Millipore Sigma, 50 µL/10 g wet cell weight) were added and the cells were rotated at 4 °C for at least 30 min. The cells were then disrupted by sonication on ice (repeated cycles of 10 s on and 20 s off for 5 min). Insoluble material was removed by centrifugation at 20,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C.

Proteins were purified via immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) using HisPur Ni-NTA resin (ThermoFisher). The resin was diluted into lysis buffer, and the resin was collected by centrifugation for 3 min at 700×g at 4 °C to remove the ethanol in the resin storage solution. The cell lysate was added to the resin and the slurry was rotated for 45 min at 4 °C. The solution was subjected to centrifugation for 3 min at 700×g at 4 °C and the supernatant was removed. Lysis buffer (5 to 10 mL) was added to the resin and the slurry was transferred to a 5 mL BioRad Poly-Prep column. The column was washed with 10 column volumes of chilled lysis buffer containing 20 mM imidazole. Protein fractions were eluted using five column volumes of chilled lysis buffer containing 200 mM imidazole. Fractions containing the purified protein were identified by SDS-PAGE and pooled. Proteins were concentrated and exchanged into storage buffer (50 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.5, containing 150 mM KCl) using 4 to 6 cycles of concentration and dilution in 10 kD MWCO 15 mL Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Units (MilliporeSigma) and then stored at −70 °C in 10% glycerol.
