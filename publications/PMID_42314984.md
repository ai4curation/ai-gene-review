Mitochondrial ATP synthase (mATPS) has the capacity to regulate the permeability
transition pore (mPTP) during Ca2⁺ fluctuations. Dysregulation of this mechanism
is implicated in neurodegenerative and other diseases; however, the endogenous
mechanisms that couple calcium sensing to mATPS function remain poorly defined.
We recently demonstrated that loss of TUSC2, a mitochondrial Ca2⁺-binding
protein, leads to mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload and sustained mPTP opening. The
oligomycin sensitivity-conferring protein (OSCP), a regulatory subunit of mATPS,
responds to Ca2⁺ and oxidative stress, positioning it as a potential integrator
of bioenergetic and stress signals. With age, the ability of OSCP to maintain
regulatory interactions and resist stress-induced conformational perturbations
declines, contributing to multiple pathologies. Here, we combine AI-assisted
structural modeling with phylogenetic analysis to nominate TUSC2 as a previously
unrecognized candidate OSCP-interacting partner. Across evolution-from basal
eukaryotes to higher metazoans-TUSC2 exhibits near-invariant conservation of its
central Ca2⁺-binding motif (CBM), in contrast to pronounced divergence of its
N-terminal region. AlphaFold3 modeling provides a structural framework for this
conservation, predicting Ca2⁺-dependent engagement of the CBM with a conserved
C-terminal region of OSCP with established regulatory function. The predicted
interface is preserved across phylogenetically distant species, indicating
co-conservation of interacting surfaces. The model further suggests coordinated
engagement of Ca2⁺ and a second metal ion by TUSC2 and OSCP, as well as
accommodation of a nucleotide. The deep conservation of these features across
eukaryotes is consistent with a functionally constrained, Ca2⁺-sensing
regulatory role for TUSC2 in mATPS function, with potential implications for
mPTP regulation.

INTRODUCTION

Calcium signaling—remarkably intricate and deeply conserved across evolution—governs ion homeostasis, cell growth and differentiation, immune responses, neuronal activity, muscle contraction, and numerous other essential physiological processes. Mitochondria, ancient endosymbionts of eukaryotic cells originating from ancestral α-proteobacteria, serve as fundamental regulators of intracellular calcium homeostasis [1, 2]. Disruption of mitochondrial calcium handling leads to a wide spectrum of congenital and acquired disorders, including metabolic syndrome, heart failure, stroke, neurodegeneration, diabetes, and cancer.

Mitochondrial Ca2+ is a central regulator of bioenergetics and cell fate, stimulating ATP production under physiological conditions while triggering mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening during overload. Although accumulating evidence implicates mitochondrial ATP synthase (mATPS) as a structural component of the mPTP, with the oligomycin sensitivity–conferring protein (OSCP) mediating cyclophilin D (CypD)–dependent pore sensitization [3], a fundamental question remains unresolved: how Ca2+ is sensed and translated into controlled regulation of ATP synthase activity versus pathological pore opening. Recent structural studies have suggested that Ca2+ may directly induce conformational changes within ATP synthase that promote permeability transition pore formation, potentially through structural rearrangements involving OSCP, the peripheral stalk, and the c-ring [4]. However, the endogenous molecular mechanisms that couple mitochondrial Ca2+ fluctuations to these structural transitions remain incompletely understood. Current models emphasize CypD binding to the C-terminus of OSCP and central role of Ca2+ as key to mPTP activation [4–6]; yet do not identify a dedicated Ca2+-sensing component capable of modulating ATP synthase function in response to physiological versus pathological Ca2+ signals. As a result, pharmacological strategies targeting the OSCP–CypD axis lack the ability to selectively modulate protective versus pathological mPTP activity [7], underscoring the need to identify endogenous Ca2+-sensing mechanisms that couple mitochondrial calcium dynamics to ATP synthase function and pore gating.

TUSC2/FUS1 is a 110–amino acid mitochondrial protein [8] with no recognizable homology to established protein families and no well-defined protein interaction partners. Using a genetically engineered Tusc2 knockout mouse model, we have demonstrated that Tusc2 deficiency results in dysregulated mitochondrial Ca2+ handling, including aberrant mPTP opening. These defects emerge in the setting of mitochondrial Ca2+ overload and are accompanied by features of premature aging [9–13] — processes associated with mPTP dysregulation [14].

However, despite this extensive study, the molecular mechanisms linking TUSC2 to mATPS, mPTP regulation, and its interaction network remain poorly defined. Here, phylogenetic analysis reveals a previously unrecognized TUSC2 motif under strong purifying selection and conserved across metazoan evolution. We then applied AI-based structural modeling to investigate how this motif may contribute to Ca2+-dependent regulation of ATP synthase functions.

Sequence Retrieval and Identification of TUSC2 Orthologs

Protein sequences of vertebrate TUSC2 orthologs were retrieved from NCBI Protein, UniProtKB, and the Ensembl genome browser using the human TUSC2 protein (accession: NP_009206.1) as the primary query. Orthologs were identified based on overall sequence homology and database orthology annotations. To evaluate evolutionary conservation of the proposed calcium-binding motif within the TUSC2 protein family, we inspected the curated Pfam/InterPro profile hidden Markov model (HMM) for TUSC2 (profile HMM logo for PF15000). Profile HMMs statistically encode residue conservation patterns derived from multiple aligned orthologous sequences and therefore provide a sensitive framework for assessing evolutionary constraint at specific amino acid positions across species.

To identify additional homologous proteins in more distant taxa, BLASTP searches were performed using full-length TUSC2 as the query sequence. Because TUSC2 is a small and otherwise weakly conserved protein, searches were guided by conservation of the acidic calcium-binding motif region, including the canonical DEDGDLAHEFYEE sequence and related D-x-D-x-D-containing variants, which represent the most evolutionarily conserved region of the protein family. Candidate sequences were additionally constrained by the characteristic TUSC2 protein length (~90–150 amino acids). Searches were performed using default BLOSUM62 scoring parameters with an E-value cutoff of 1e-5 for metazoan sequences and a relaxed threshold of 1e-3 for more divergent taxa to capture highly diverged candidate homologs.

All candidate sequences were subsequently subjected to multiple-sequence alignment and manual comparative analysis to confirm overall conservation patterns and establish orthology with the TUSC2 protein family. Candidate sequences were manually curated to exclude truncated entries, probable misannotations, low-confidence predicted proteins, and sequences lacking conservation of the characteristic acidic motif region. No high-similarity non-TUSC2 proteins containing the conserved acidic motif were identified during this analysis.

Evolutionary rate analysis of TUSC2

A phylogenetically balanced set of vertebrate TUSC2 protein sequences was assembled from NCBI RefSeq and GenBank, prioritizing high-confidence annotations. Sequences were aligned using standard protein multiple sequence alignment procedures, followed by manual inspection to ensure correct alignment of conserved motifs and removal of poorly aligned or truncated entries. Human TUSC2 was used as the positional reference for all subsequent analyses.

Raw Shannon entropy (H) was computed in bits and then normalized by dividing by log2(20), yielding values between 0 (fully conserved) and 1 (maximally variable across 20 amino acids). For each aligned position, entropy was calculated across all species while excluding gap characters.

Entropy values were plotted as a per-residue profile along the human TUSC2 sequence and displayed together with the aligned human amino acid sequence to enable direct visual comparison between sequence position and evolutionary constraint. No a priori regional partitioning was imposed on the rate profile; any regional patterns were inferred post hoc from the continuous per-residue distribution.

RESULTSNon-canonical features of the TUSC2 calcium-binding motif.Human TUSC2 protein (NP_009206.1) displays no obvious similarity with known proteins. To identify functionally constrained regions of TUSC2, we analyzed the distribution of evolutionary rates across the 110 amino acids of full-length TUSC2. Vertebrate TUSC2 orthologs were retrieved from NCBI and used to generate a phylogenetically balanced multiple sequence alignment comprising 40 species (Supplementary Table S1). Analysis of normalized Shannon entropy across aligned orthologs identified a 13 a.a. calcium-binding motif (CBM) containing a canonical DxDxD triad [13] of putative Ca2+-coordinating residues (FIG. 1A). The CBM exhibits exceptional conservation, remaining invariant across all 40 vertebrate orthologs analyzed, consistent with strong purifying selection. In contrast, other regions of TUSC2 show greater evolutionary variability: the C-terminal region is moderately conserved, whereas the N-terminus (residues 1–40) evolves most rapidly.Tertiary structure analysis indicated that the N-terminal region is intrinsically disordered and was therefore excluded from subsequent analyses. The resulting models reveal a distinctive structural feature conserved in all vertebrates and beyond: a chair-like fold in which a single Ca2+ ion is coordinated by three aspartate residues (Asp54, Asp56, and Asp58), forming the “seat” at the base of an extended β-hairpin (residues 63–82) that constitutes the “backrest” (Fig. 1B). This architecture defines a previously undescribed calcium-binding fold distinct from the canonical EF-hand, providing a mechanistic basis for the strong evolutionary conservation of the CBM.Tusc2 orthologs across metazoans.To assess the evolutionary depth of CBM conservation beyond vertebrates, we performed phylogenetic analysis of five basal chordate and ninety-five vertebrate TUSC2 orthologs. The resulting distance-based guide tree recapitulated established chordate phylogeny with high fidelity, reinforcing the evolutionary stability of TUSC2 across this lineage (FIG. 2A; Supplementary Table S2). Notably, robust TUSC2 orthologs were readily identified in early-diverging chordate groups, including tunicates and cephalochordates, indicating that the gene originated prior to the radiation of vertebrates and has been retained throughout chordate evolution.Outside vertebrates and other chordates, we found TUSC2 orthologs in 168 species spanning Cnidaria, Bryozoa, Brachiopoda (Lingula anatina), Nemertea (Lineus longissimus), Nematoda, Mollusca, Annelida, Arthropoda, Echinodermata, and Crustacea (Suppl. Table S3). The pattern of sequence variation mirrored that observed in vertebrates: although overall TUSC2 length remained relatively constrained (~100–125 aa), distinct regions of the protein exhibited markedly different evolutionary rates. Similar to vertebrates (FIG. 1A), the CBM and C-terminal TUSC2 areas in early metazoans were substantially more conserved than the N-terminal half of the protein, consistent with strong purifying selection acting early in evolution. Although substitutions were observed within the CBM in basal chordates (tunicates, lancelets) and across early-diverging lineages from choanoflagellates to echinoderms, these changes predominantly retained the acidic core of the motif (FIG. 2B).Evolutionary origin of TUSC2 and pan-metazoan conservation of its calcium-binding motif.Two of the earliest-branching eukaryotes containing Tusc2-like proteins were the choanoflagellate Salpingoeca rosetta, a unicellular protist considered the closest living relative of Metazoa, and the excavate Naegleria lovaniensis (XP_044542376), a more distantly related protist that nonetheless possesses a well-developed aerobic mitochondrial system resembling that of animals (FIG. 2B). No candidate TUSC2 orthologs were detected in the fungal or bacterial genomes examined. Together, these findings raise the possibility that Tusc2-like proteins emerged before the diversification of major eukaryotic lineages sampled here—an event estimated to predate 1 BYA [15].OSCP as a potential TUSC2 protein partner.Our previous studies in a mouse model showed that Tusc2 deficiency leads to mitochondrial calcium overload and promotes mPTP opening [16]. To identify candidate interaction partners that could mediate this effect, we modeled human protein–protein interfaces between TUSC2 and Ca2+-associated mitochondrial proteins, including all 20 subunits of mitochondrial ATP synthase (mATPS), a complex regulated by Ca2+ dynamics and implicated in mPTP assembly and function (Supplementary Table S4).Among these candidates, the OSCP subunit of mitochondrial ATP synthase (ATP5O) exhibited a strong predicted interaction with TUSC2, mediated by complementary electrostatic alignment of the TUSC2 CBM with a β-hairpin motif in OSCP (FIG. 3). The predicted TUSC2–OSCP complex was examined in the context of experimentally resolved ATP synthase structures, and no structural incompatibilities were observed. None of the other ATP synthase subunits exhibited a comparable interaction potential. AI-assisted structural modeling, yielding a high-confidence, evolutionary conserved TUSC2–OSCP complex, suggests that one function of TUSC2 may involve transient interaction with the C-terminal region of OSCP (residues 188–204 in human NP_001688.1). Their findings further suggest that the interface would need to become at least partially accessible to permit alternative regulatory interactions at this site. Accordingly, the TUSC2-OSCP interaction hypothesized in our study most likely occurs outside of the fully assembled ATP synthase complex, or during conformationally dynamic states in which the OSCP interface becomes transiently accessible for regulatory interactions. Our findings may complement recent structural models of ATP synthase–mediated permeability transition. Sazanov and colleagues proposed that Ca2+ can promote ATP synthase conformational changes associated with c-ring destabilization and pore opening, implicating OSCP and the peripheral stalk in transmission of these structural rearrangements [4].
