A key event in Wnt signaling is conversion of TCF/Lef from a transcriptional
repressor to an activator, yet how this switch occurs is not well understood.
Here, we report an unanticipated role for X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis (XIAP)
in regulating this critical Wnt signaling event that is independent of its
antiapoptotic function. We identified DIAP1 as a positive regulator of Wingless
signaling in a Drosophila S2 cell-based RNAi screen. XIAP, its vertebrate
homolog, is similarly required for Wnt signaling in cultured mammalian cells and
in Xenopus embryos, indicating evolutionary conservation of function. Upon Wnt
pathway activation, XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef where it monoubiquitylates
Groucho (Gro)/TLE. This modification decreases affinity of Gro/TLE for TCF/Lef.
Our data reveal a transcriptional switch involving XIAP-mediated ubiquitylation
of Gro/TLE that facilitates its removal from TCF/Lef, thus allowing
β-catenin-TCF/Lef complex assembly and initiation of a Wnt-specific
transcriptional program.

INTRODUCTION

The canonical Wnt signaling pathway is present in all metazoans and regulates many developmental processes (Logan and Nusse, 2004). Misregulation of the Wnt pathway results in several disease states in humans, including cancer. β-catenin is the main cytoplasmic effector in the Wnt pathway. In the absence of Wnt ligand, a β-catenin destruction complex, composed of Axin, glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3), casein kinase I α (CKIα), and the tumor suppressor adenomatous polyposis coli (APC), promotes phosphorylation of β-catenin, targeting it for ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation. Binding of Wnt to its co-receptors, Frizzled (Fz) and LDL receptor-related protein 5/6 (LRP5/6), results in inhibition of β-catenin phosphorylation and, thus, its stabilization. After its translocation to the nucleus, β-catenin binds TCF/Lef to activate a Wnt-specific transcriptional program.

β-catenin-mediated conversion of TCF/Lef from transcriptional repressor to activator is a critical nuclear event that occurs upon Wnt pathway activation. In the absence of Wnt ligand, TCF/Lef bound to Groucho (Gro)/TLE transcriptional co-repressors inhibits Wnt target gene transcription (Cavallo et al., 1998; Roose et al., 1998). This repression is thought to involve histone deacetylase recruitment by Gro/TLE to alter local chromatin structure and Gro/TLE oligomerization to promote long-range chromatin condensation (Buscarlet and Stifani, 2007; Jennings and Ish-Horowicz, 2008). According to a current model of the pathway, β-catenin that enters the nucleus upon Wnt signaling directly competes with Gro/TLE for TCF/Lef binding (Daniels and Weis, 2005). Once bound to TCF/Lef on chromatin, β-catenin recruits a co-activator complex, thereby converting TCF/Lef into a transcriptional activator.

Many proteins in the Wnt pathway are regulated by ubiquitylation. E3 ubiquitin ligases for β-catenin (Jiang and Struhl, 1998; Marikawa and Elinson, 1998) and Dishevelled (Angers et al., 2006) had been reported at the time we began our study, but a detailed understanding of how the ubiquitin system regulates the Wnt pathway was lacking. Thus, we sought to identify novel E3 ligases involved in Wnt signaling.

We performed a targeted RNAi screen in Drosophila S2 cells for E3 ubiquitin ligases involved in Wingless (Wg, the Drosophila homolog of Wnt) signal transduction that identified Drosophila
Inhibitor of Apoptosis 1 (DIAP1) as a critical regulator of Wg signaling. We demonstrate that the vertebrate homolog, X-linked Inhibitor of Apoptosis (XIAP), is similarly required for Wnt signaling in human cells and Xenopus embryos. In response to Wnt activation, we show that XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef transcriptional complexes where it ubiquitylates Gro/TLE, thereby decreasing affinity of Gro/TLE for TCF/Lef. Together, our data reveal a mechanism by which XIAP-mediated removal of Gro/TLE from TCF/Lef allows assembly of β-catenin-TCF/Lef complexes that initiate a Wnt-specific transcriptional program.

RESULTSDrosophila RNAi screen identifies DIAP1 as a critical component of Wg signalingTo identify novel E3 ubiquitin ligases involved in Wg signaling, we performed a genome-scale RNAi-based screen targeting E3 ubiquitin ligases in Drosophila S2 cells (Figure 1A). Plasmids encoding verified and predicted E3 ubiquitin ligases (122 clones) were obtained from the Drosophila Gene Collection Release 1 and 2, and a PCR approach was used to generate linear cDNA products for in vitro dsRNA synthesis. For the screen, dsRNA was added to a Drosophila S2R+ reporter cell line stably transfected with a Wg responsive TOPflash luciferase reporter (Korinek et al., 1997).Axin (negative regulator of Wg signaling) and Armadillo (Arm; Drosophila β-catenin homolog and positive regulator of Wg signaling) served as controls to confirm effective knockdown by dsRNA treatment in our screen (Figure 1B). Of the 122 E3 ubiquitin ligases screened, the knockdown of one (no. 112) was particularly potent in inhibiting Wg signaling, reducing TOPflash activity to a similar extent as knocking down Arm. This dsRNA targets Drosophila
Inhibitor of Apoptosis 1 (DIAP1), a well-characterized anti-apoptotic effector and member of the Inhibitor of Apoptosis protein (IAP) family (Srinivasula and Ashwell, 2008). We chose to pursue this hit further because it suggested an unexpected link between an IAP family member and regulation of the Wg pathway.XIAP is required for Wnt signaling in cultured mammalian cellsWe next sought to determine if mammalian XIAP, the homolog of DIAP1, is similarly required for Wnt signaling. We tested the effects of XIAP knockdown on Wnt3a-induced transcriptional activation using human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells stably transfected with TOPflash reporter (STF293) (Xu et al., 2004). XIAP knockdown with two independent short-interfering RNA (siRNA) constructs significantly blocked Wnt3a-induced TOPflash activation (Figure 2A) while having no effect on a constitutively active luciferase reporter (CMV-Luciferase) (Thorne et al., 2010) or Notch reporter (Minoguchi et al., 1997) (Figure S1A and B), indicating specific inhibition. XIAP knockdown also inhibited Wnt3a-induced expression of endogenous Wnt target genes (Figure S1C–F), providing further evidence that XIAP is required for Wnt-mediated transcriptional activation.XIAP knockdown did not alter cytoplasmic β-catenin levels, suggesting that XIAP functions downstream of the β-catenin destruction complex (Figure 2A). As further confirmation, we asked whether XIAP knockdown could inhibit Wnt signaling in cells treated with lithium, which results in elevated levels of β-catenin due to inhibition of the destruction complex kinase GSK3. We found that XIAP knockdown inhibited TOPflash activation in lithium-treated STF293 cells (Figure 2B).SW480 and HCT116 colon cancer cell lines have significantly decreased capacity to degrade β-catenin due to mutations in APC and β-catenin, respectively (Korinek et al., 1997; Morin et al., 1997). Both lines exhibit constitutively active, ligand-independent Wnt signaling. XIAP knockdown inhibited Wnt signaling in SW480 cells but had no effect on β-catenin levels; similar results were obtained using HCT116 cells deficient for XIAP (Figure 2C). Furthermore, we detected no change in β-catenin localization when XIAP was absent (Figure S1G) or downregulated (Figure S1H).In contrast to XIAP downregulation, its overexpression had no effect on Wnt signaling (Figure 2D). Thus, XIAP does not appear to be a limiting Wnt pathway component in the cell lines we tested. Together, these data indicate that XIAP is required for Wnt target gene activation in mammalian cells and that it likely functions downstream of β-catenin stabilization.XIAP is required for Wnt signaling in Xenopus embryosTo determine if XIAP is required for Wnt signaling during development, we investigated its role in dorsal-anterior structure formation in Xenopus laevis embryos, a process that is critically regulated by Wnt signaling (Heasman, 2006). Previous studies reported that dorsal injection of XIAP mRNA into Xenopus embryos synergized with BMP pathway components, TAB1/TAK1, to promote ventralization (Yamaguchi et al., 1999), consistent with a proposed role for XIAP in BMP signaling. This study, however, focused on XIAP overexpression in Xenopus embryos. We find that XIAP knockdown by dorsal injection of two independent morpholinos inhibited dorsal-anterior structure formation and resulted in severely ventralized embryos, consistent with Wnt pathway inhibition (Figure 3A).To more specifically assess whether the ventralized phenotype we observed was due to Wnt pathway inhibition, we tested the effect of XIAP knockdown on Xwnt8-induced secondary axes. There was a significant reduction in the number of embryos with secondary axes when Xwnt8 mRNA was co-injected ventrally with XIAP morpholino versus control morpholino (Figure 3B). Co-injection of XIAP morpholino and Xwnt8 mRNA into Xenopus animal caps significantly reduced Wnt target gene expression, further demonstrating that XIAP is required for Wnt signaling in Xenopus embryos (Figure 3C).Ventral injection of XIAP mRNA induced partial secondary axes, consistent with Wnt pathway activation (McMahon and Moon, 1989). In contrast to mammalian cells where XIAP overexpression had no apparent effect on Wnt signaling, XIAP overexpression in Xenopus embryos was sufficient to ectopically activate the Wnt pathway (Figure 3D). While XIAP may also regulate BMP signaling during Xenopus development, our data suggest that the Wnt pathway is a critical target of XIAP during early embryogenesis.XIAP binds and ubiquitylates Gro/TLEHaving demonstrated that XIAP is required for Wnt signaling across phyla, our next aim was to elucidate its molecular mechanism of action in the Wnt pathway. Our mammalian cell studies suggested that XIAP acts downstream of the β-catenin destruction complex. In a candidate approach, we performed co-immunoprecipitation experiments with tagged versions of XIAP and nuclear components of the Wnt pathway. We found that only one candidate co-immunoprecipitated with XIAP: the transcriptional co-repressor Transducin-like enhancer of split 3 (TLE3), the mammalian homolog of Drosophila Gro (Figure 4A and data not shown). Interaction between XIAP and TLE3 was further confirmed by demonstrating co-immunoprecipitation of the endogenous proteins from cells with or without Wnt stimulation (Figure 4B).These results prompted us to ask where XIAP and TLE3 interact in the cell. XIAP resides in the cytoplasm, although it can translocate to the nucleus under certain conditions (Liston et al., 2001; Russell et al., 2008), while TLE3 is almost exclusively nuclear. We analyzed the subcellular localization of both XIAP and TLE3 in the absence and presence of Wnt signaling and observed no Wnt-dependent changes (Figure S2A and data not shown). Although the majority of XIAP is in the cytoplasm, we detected a nuclear pool of XIAP that was dramatically enhanced by TLE3 overexpression (Figure S2B), suggesting these two proteins can interact within the nucleus and that TLE3 might regulate the nuclear pool of XIAP.XIAP contains three Baculovirus Inhibitor of apoptosis protein Repeat (BIR) domains and a C-terminal E3 ligase Really Interesing New Gene (RING) domain (Galban and Duckett, 2010). XIAP binds and inhibits caspases through its BIR domains, and much work has focused on the role of XIAP as an inhibitor of apoptosis. Only recently have E3 ligase roles for XIAP been revealed that involve both anti-apoptotic and non-apoptotic functions, including ubiquitylation of Copper metabolism (Murr1) domain containing 1 (COMMD1) (Burstein et al., 2004).We sought to determine if XIAP acts as an E3 ligase for Gro/TLE in the Wnt pathway using a previously described assay (Salghetti et al., 1999). Cells were transfected with myc-TLE3, His-Ubiquitin, and either Flag-XIAP or Flag-XIAP-ΔRING. His-ubiquitylated proteins were isolated under denaturing conditions by nickel affinity purification, and myc-TLE3 was detected by immunoblotting. In the absence of XIAP, we detected a band corresponding to mono-ubiquitylated TLE3; the signal was significantly enhanced when full-length XIAP, but not XIAP-ΔRING, was overexpressed, indicating the RING domain of XIAP is required for ubiquitylation of TLE3 (Figure 4C). Consistent with these results, higher molecular weight species of TLE3 were present in whole cell lysates in the presence of full-length XIAP, but not XIAP-ΔRING.To test whether the anti-apoptotic activity of XIAP is required for ubiquitylation of TLE3, we overexpressed a previously characterized form of XIAP with point mutations in each of its three BIR domains that abolish its capacity to bind and inactivate caspases (Lewis et al., 2004). Overexpression of this mutant form of XIAP enhanced TLE3 ubiquitylation to a similar extent as wild-type XIAP, indicating that its anti-apoptotic functions are not required TLE3 ubiquitylation (Figure 4D). Overexpression of Second mitochondria-derived activator of caspases (Smac), a peptide that binds to the BIR2 and BIR3 domains of XIAP and inhibits its anti-apoptotic activity (Huang et al., 2003), similarly had no effect on the capacity of XIAP to ubiquitylate TLE3 (Figure S2C). Thus, XIAP enhances TLE3 ubiquitylation in a manner that is independent of its anti-apoptotic functions.XIAP has two closely related homologs: cellular Inhibitor of Apoptosis 1 and 2 (cIAP1 and cIAP2). In some cases, cIAP1 and cIAP2 have redundant activity with XIAP and can compensate for its loss (Srinivasula 2008). To determine if XIAP is also a substrate of cIAP1 or cIAP2, we performed a cell-based ubiquitylation assay. We found that, in contrast to XIAP, neither cIAP1 nor cIAP2 promoted TLE3 ubiquitylation (Figure S2D). This finding suggests that ubiquitylation of TLE3 is not a general property of IAP family members, but is specific to XIAP.To test whether endogenous XIAP is required for TLE3 ubiquitylation, we knocked down XIAP by siRNA and performed an ubiquitylation assay as described above. We found that myc-TLE3 ubiquitylation was significantly decreased by XIAP knockdown (Figure 4E). We also observed significant reduction in the degree of ubiquitylation of endogenous TLE3 when XIAP was knocked down (Figure S2E), providing further evidence that Gro/TLE is an XIAP substrate.To more directly assess if XIAP can bind and ubiquitylate TLE3, we performed an in vitro ubiquitylation assay using recombinant XIAP (Figure 4F). We found that recombinant XIAP, but not XIAP-ΔRING, ubiquitylated TLE3 in vitro. Addition of wild-type ubiquitin and KO ubiquitin (in which all lysines have been changed to arginines to block polyubiquitin chain formation) resulted in identical patterns of ubiquitylated TLE3 species as detected by immunoblotting. The XIAP-mediated in vitro ubiquitylation pattern of TLE3 was essentially identical to the ubiquitylation pattern of TLE3 we observed when XIAP was overexpressed in cultured cells (Figure 4C). These results suggest that XIAP directly ubiquitylates TLE3 and that it likely conjugates multiple monoubiquitin moieties onto TLE3 (a prominent single mono-ubiquitin moiety as well as a less-abundant second mono-ubiquitin moiety).Gro/TLE ubiquitylation does not affect its turnover, localization, or tetramerizationHaving established that XIAP binds and ubiquitylates TLE3, we next asked how ubiquitylation of TLE3 might affect its function in the Wnt pathway. Ubiquitylation of proteins often serves as a signal for proteasomal degradation. Thus, we tested if gain or loss of XIAP has any effect on TLE3 levels. Neither overexpression nor knockdown of XIAP had any effect on steady-state levels of TLE3 in the presence or absence of Wnt stimulation (Figure 5A and B). Furthermore, overexpression or knockdown of XIAP had no effect on the turnover rate of TLE3 (Figure 5C and D). The stabilization of XIAP seen in Figure 5C was likely due to competition for the endogenous XIAP degradation machinery by overexpressed XIAP. We also note that XIAP turnover appears to be much more rapid than that of TLE3. These results indicate that ubiquitylation of TLE3 by XIAP does not affect its stability.Recent studies have shown a role for mono-ubiquitylation in regulating the subcellular localization of transcription factors (Dupont et al., 2009; Li et al., 2003; van der Horst et al., 2006). Thus, we tested whether XIAP has any effect on the nuclear localization of TLE3. We detected prominent nuclear staining of TLE3 in both control and XIAP-siRNA cells (Figure 5E), indicating loss of XIAP does not cause a shift in the subcellular localization of TLE3. XIAP overexpression similarly did not alter TLE3 localization (data not shown).TLE3 is a member of the highly conserved Gro/TLE family of proteins that function as transcriptional co-repressors in the Wnt pathway. Five isoforms of TLE proteins have been identified in humans (Gasperowicz and Otto, 2005). By performing ubiquitylation assays as described above, we showed that XIAP can ubiquitylate all human TLE isoforms, including the truncated isoform Amino-terminal enhancer of split (AES) (Figure S3A). AES contains only the first 197 amino acids found in full-length TLE isoforms (including the N-terminal glutamine-rich (Q) and Glycine-Proline rich (GP) domains), thus narrowing the potential target sites of XIAP-mediated ubiquitylation to this region (Figure S3B). We further defined the region by showng that recombinant XIAP can ubiquitylate recombinant Drosophila Gro Q domain in vitro (Figure S3C). The Q domains of Gro/TLE proteins are highly conserved and contain eight lysines that are present in Drosophila Gro and all human TLE isoforms (Figure S3B and D). Based on our results, XIAP likely targets one (or two) of the conserved lysines in the Q domain of the Gro/TLE proteins.Mapping the XIAP-mediated ubiquitylation sites on Gro/TLE to the Q domain provided clues regarding the functional consequences of this modification. Gro/TLE proteins homo-tetramerize through their Q-domains, a property that is required for TCF/Lef binding and mediating transcriptional repression (Gasperowicz and Otto, 2005; Jennings and Ish-Horowicz, 2008). Thus, we next tested if ubiquitylation of TLE3 by XIAP disrupts its capacity to tetramerize by assessing the hydrodynamic properties of in vitro-translated ubiquitylated and non-ubiquitylated species of TLE3. We found that the ubiquitylated forms of the N-terminal Q domain of TLE3 (TLE3-Q) and full-length TLE3 eluted in the same peak fractions as that of their non-ubiquitylated forms, indicating that TLE3 ubiquitylation does not disrupt its tetramerization (Figure 5F). The larger than expected apparent molecular weight of TLE3-Q and full-length TLE3 on gel filtration chromatography likely reflects an elongated tetrameric protein complex and has been previously reported (Kuo et al., 2010).Ubiquitylation of Gro/TLE by XIAP disrupts its binding to TCF/LefAlthough ubiquitylation of TLE3 did not interfere with its tetramerization in our hydrodynamic studies, we hypothesized ubiquitylation of TLE3 may prevent its interaction with TCF/Lef as Gro/TLE binds to TCF/Lef via its Q domain. To test this possibility, we ubiquitylated TLE3-Q and full-length TLE3 in vitro and assessed their capacity to bind TCF4. In contrast to their non-ubiquitylated forms, ubiquitylated TLE3-Q and full-length TLE3 were not pulled down by recombinant GST-TCF4, indicating that ubiquitylation of TLE3 inhibits its capacity to bind TCF4 (Figure 6A). A lighter exposure of the immunoblot showed equivalent amounts of non-ubiquitylated TLE3 in the input and pull-down lanes, indicating that absence of an ubiquitylated species in the pull-down lane was not due to under-loading (Figure S4A). We next asked if TCF/Lef has reduced affinity for ubiquitylated Gro/TLE in vivo. We expressed myc-TLE3 and HA-TCF4 in cells (with ubiquitin and XIAP to enhance TLE3 ubiquitylation) and assessed the presence of ubiquitylated or non-ubiquitylated forms of myc-TLE3 in HA-TCF4 immunoprecipitates (Figure 6B). In contrast to whole cell lysates, which contained a band representing ubiquitylated TLE3, only non-ubiquitylated TLE3 co-immunoprecipitated with TCF4, providing further evidence that ubiquitylation of Gro/TLE inhibits its capacity to bind TCF/Lef.These findings suggest that TLE3 ubiquitylation by XIAP may be required during Wnt pathway activation to facilitate its dissociation from TCF/Lef, allowing subsequent β-catenin binding and transcriptional activation. To determine if XIAP might also bind TCF/Lef in the process of binding and ubiquitylating Gro/TLE, we performed an in vitro binding assay with recombinant proteins. We found that XIAP is specifically pulled down by TCF4, indicating XIAP can directly bind TCF4 (Figure 6C). In contrast to Gro/TLE, however, we were unable to detect ubiquitylation of TCF4 by XIAP in an in vitro assay (Figure 6D), suggesting specificity of the E3 activity of XIAP for Gro/TLE.If XIAP ubiquitylates Gro/TLE in response to a Wnt signal to remove it from TCF/Lef, one might predict that Gro/TLE ubiquitylation would increase upon Wnt stimulation. We detected no change, however, in the degree of Gro/TLE ubiquitylation in response to Wnt ligand (Figure S4B). Because we also found that XIAP interacts with TLE3 in the absence or presence of Wnt signaling (Figure 4B), this would suggest that XIAP constitutively binds and ubiquitylates non-TCF-bound Gro/TLE in the nucleus. Thus, XIAP may regulate the nuclear pool of Gro/TLE available to bind TCF/Lef.Given that XIAP directly binds TCF4 in vitro, we hypothesized XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef complexes upon Wnt pathway activation to promote ubiquitylation and dissociation of TCF/Lef-bound Gro/TLE. To test this hypothesis, we assembled TLE3-TCF4 complexes in vitro followed by XIAP addition. We found that TLE3-TCF4 complexes were stable in the absence of XIAP with little dissociation of TLE3 from TCF4 beads over the course of the experiment (Figure S4C). Addition of XIAP, however, promoted dissociation of TLE3 from TCF4 (Figure S4D). We next tested whether XIAP-mediated ubiquitylation of full-length TLE3 or TLE3-Q in a complex with TCF4 increases their dissociation (Figure S4E). We found that XIAP ubiquitylated full-length TLE3 and TLE3-Q pre-bound to TCF4 beads, although we could not distinguish whether the Gro/TLE substrate was TCF4-bound or free. Ubiquitylated TLE3 was only detected in the fraction released from TCF4 beads, however, providing support for our model that ubiquitylated TLE3 has decreased affinity for TCF/Lef (Figure S4E).To test whether XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef transcriptional complexes in a Wnt-dependent manner, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays using an anti-TCF4 antibody (Figure 6E and F). In the absence of lithium (Figure 6E), XIAP was not detectable in TCF4 immunoprecipitates. Upon Wnt pathway stimulation by lithium, however, XIAP co-immunoprecipitated with TCF4. Wnt pathway activation normally leads to recruitment of β-catenin to TCF/Lef. Accordingly, we observed co-immunoprecipitation of endogenous β-catenin and TCF4 in response to lithium treatment. Significantly, XIAP knockdown reduced the amount of β-catenin that co-immunoprecipitated with TCF4 from lithium-treated cells. These results were confirmed using Wnt3a-conditioned media (Figure 6F). Specificity of the XIAP signal on immunoblots was confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation of TCF4 and XIAP from HCT116 versus HCT116 XIAP KO cells (Figure S4F).To confirm that XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef complexes in a Wnt-dependent manner, we performed a time-course experiment to analyze the kinetics of XIAP-TCF/Lef association upon Wnt stimulation of HEK293 cells. XIAP and TCF4 co-immunoprecipitated 30 minutes after Wnt3a treatment, but not at 0 or 60 minutes; β-catenin and TCF4 co-immunoprecipitated in a similar manner (Figure S4G). These findings further confirm that XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef upon Wnt stimulation and are consistent with a previous report showing that β-catenin transcriptional complexes cycle on and off TCF/Lef every 30–45 minutes (Sierra et al., 2006). These data indicate XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef transcriptional complexes in response to Wnt pathway activation with kinetics similar to that of β-catenin and that XIAP is required for efficient recruitment of β–catenin to TCF/Lef transcriptional complexes. As further confirmation of these results, we demonstrated by ChIP analysis that XIAP and β-catenin are bound to the AXIN2 promoter upon lithium-mediated activation of the Wnt pathway (Figure 6G).

We next asked if TCF/Lef has reduced affinity for ubiquitylated Gro/TLE in vivo. We expressed myc-TLE3 and HA-TCF4 in cells (with ubiquitin and XIAP to enhance TLE3 ubiquitylation) and assessed the presence of ubiquitylated or non-ubiquitylated forms of myc-TLE3 in HA-TCF4 immunoprecipitates (Figure 6B). In contrast to whole cell lysates, which contained a band representing ubiquitylated TLE3, only non-ubiquitylated TLE3 co-immunoprecipitated with TCF4, providing further evidence that ubiquitylation of Gro/TLE inhibits its capacity to bind TCF/Lef.

These findings suggest that TLE3 ubiquitylation by XIAP may be required during Wnt pathway activation to facilitate its dissociation from TCF/Lef, allowing subsequent β-catenin binding and transcriptional activation. To determine if XIAP might also bind TCF/Lef in the process of binding and ubiquitylating Gro/TLE, we performed an in vitro binding assay with recombinant proteins. We found that XIAP is specifically pulled down by TCF4, indicating XIAP can directly bind TCF4 (Figure 6C). In contrast to Gro/TLE, however, we were unable to detect ubiquitylation of TCF4 by XIAP in an in vitro assay (Figure 6D), suggesting specificity of the E3 activity of XIAP for Gro/TLE.

If XIAP ubiquitylates Gro/TLE in response to a Wnt signal to remove it from TCF/Lef, one might predict that Gro/TLE ubiquitylation would increase upon Wnt stimulation. We detected no change, however, in the degree of Gro/TLE ubiquitylation in response to Wnt ligand (Figure S4B). Because we also found that XIAP interacts with TLE3 in the absence or presence of Wnt signaling (Figure 4B), this would suggest that XIAP constitutively binds and ubiquitylates non-TCF-bound Gro/TLE in the nucleus. Thus, XIAP may regulate the nuclear pool of Gro/TLE available to bind TCF/Lef.

Given that XIAP directly binds TCF4 in vitro, we hypothesized XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef complexes upon Wnt pathway activation to promote ubiquitylation and dissociation of TCF/Lef-bound Gro/TLE. To test this hypothesis, we assembled TLE3-TCF4 complexes in vitro followed by XIAP addition. We found that TLE3-TCF4 complexes were stable in the absence of XIAP with little dissociation of TLE3 from TCF4 beads over the course of the experiment (Figure S4C). Addition of XIAP, however, promoted dissociation of TLE3 from TCF4 (Figure S4D). We next tested whether XIAP-mediated ubiquitylation of full-length TLE3 or TLE3-Q in a complex with TCF4 increases their dissociation (Figure S4E). We found that XIAP ubiquitylated full-length TLE3 and TLE3-Q pre-bound to TCF4 beads, although we could not distinguish whether the Gro/TLE substrate was TCF4-bound or free. Ubiquitylated TLE3 was only detected in the fraction released from TCF4 beads, however, providing support for our model that ubiquitylated TLE3 has decreased affinity for TCF/Lef (Figure S4E).

To test whether XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef transcriptional complexes in a Wnt-dependent manner, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays using an anti-TCF4 antibody (Figure 6E and F). In the absence of lithium (Figure 6E), XIAP was not detectable in TCF4 immunoprecipitates. Upon Wnt pathway stimulation by lithium, however, XIAP co-immunoprecipitated with TCF4. Wnt pathway activation normally leads to recruitment of β-catenin to TCF/Lef. Accordingly, we observed co-immunoprecipitation of endogenous β-catenin and TCF4 in response to lithium treatment. Significantly, XIAP knockdown reduced the amount of β-catenin that co-immunoprecipitated with TCF4 from lithium-treated cells. These results were confirmed using Wnt3a-conditioned media (Figure 6F). Specificity of the XIAP signal on immunoblots was confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation of TCF4 and XIAP from HCT116 versus HCT116 XIAP KO cells (Figure S4F).

To confirm that XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef complexes in a Wnt-dependent manner, we performed a time-course experiment to analyze the kinetics of XIAP-TCF/Lef association upon Wnt stimulation of HEK293 cells. XIAP and TCF4 co-immunoprecipitated 30 minutes after Wnt3a treatment, but not at 0 or 60 minutes; β-catenin and TCF4 co-immunoprecipitated in a similar manner (Figure S4G). These findings further confirm that XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef upon Wnt stimulation and are consistent with a previous report showing that β-catenin transcriptional complexes cycle on and off TCF/Lef every 30–45 minutes (Sierra et al., 2006). These data indicate XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef transcriptional complexes in response to Wnt pathway activation with kinetics similar to that of β-catenin and that XIAP is required for efficient recruitment of β–catenin to TCF/Lef transcriptional complexes. As further confirmation of these results, we demonstrated by ChIP analysis that XIAP and β-catenin are bound to the AXIN2 promoter upon lithium-mediated activation of the Wnt pathway (Figure 6G).

The current model, based primarily on reconstitution studies using purified proteins, proposes direct displacement of the transcriptional co-repressor Gro/TLE by the co-activator β-catenin through competition for overlapping binding sites on TCF/Lef (Daniels and Weis).

Our data suggest a model (Figure 7) in which XIAP constitutively binds and ubiquitylates non-TCF-bound Gro/TLE in the nucleus, thereby limiting the amount of Gro/TLE available to form co-repressor complexes with TCF/Lef. In the presence of a Wnt signal, XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef transcriptional complexes where it promotes dissociation of Gro/TLE. Our experiments were not able to distinguish whether XIAP ubiquitylates Gro/TLE bound to TCF/Lef to promote its dissociation or ubiquitylates dissociated Gro/TLE, thereby blocking its re-association. The mechanism by which XIAP is recruited to TCF/Lef transcriptional complexes is unknown, although our results demonstrating that lithium can also induce recruitment of XIAP to TCF/Lef suggest that GSK3 activity plays an important role in regulating this process.

Our proposed model for Wnt-mediated transcriptional activation parallels the findings of Sierra and colleagues who proposed that inactivation of Wnt target gene transcription similarly occurs as a multi-step process (Sierra et al., 2006). Their data suggest that APC and β-TRCP (an E3 ligase) mediate removal of β-catenin from Lef1 to allow for subsequent TLE1 binding. Together, these experiments and our current study have revealed that transcriptional activation and inactivation in the Wnt pathway are highly regulated processes.

β-catenin protein levels are tightly regulated in the cell via continual synthesis and degradation by the β-catenin destruction complex. Why, then, would a cell evolve an additional layer of regulation for Wnt transcriptional activation, as we propose here, as opposed to a simpler mechanism based solely on bimolecular association between β-catenin and TCF/Lef? We propose that this Wnt signaling circuitry provides a mechanism to dampen transcriptional noise without a corresponding loss in sensitivity. Binding of Gro/TLE to TCF/Lef allows the system to be resistant to stochastic fluxes in β-catenin levels in the absence of Wnt pathway activation. In the presence of a Wnt signal, a coincident circuit involving nuclear accumulation of β-catenin and recruitment of XIAP to TCF/Lef is established. Such circuitry ensures that transcriptional activation only occurs upon Wnt ligand binding and provides an additional mechanism for reducing spontaneous activity. Sensitivity to a Wnt signal is maintained by the facilitated removal of Gro/TLE from TCF/Lef, which ensures that even low levels of β-catenin would be sufficient to bind TCF/Lef and activate transcription.

Support for this model comes from a study showing that β-catenin levels change only modestly (~2–6-fold) upon Wnt signaling in human cells and Xenopus embryos (Goentoro and Kirschner, 2009). It is unlikely that this degree of nuclear β-catenin accumulation is sufficient to effectively displace Gro/TLE from TCF/Lef. This suggests that a facilitated mechanism for Gro/TLE removal is required prior to β-catenin-TCF/Lef complex formation.

Our data indicate that XIAP may also influence the nuclear pool of Gro/TLE that is available to form co-repressor complexes with TCF/Lef. Here, we find that XIAP is associated with Gro/TLE in the presence and absence of Wnt signaling. Additionally, whereas ubiquitylated Gro/TLE is readily observed in total cellular lysates, only the non-ubiquitylated form of Gro/TLE binds to TCF/Lef. This suggests a model in which XIAP functions to constitutively ubiquitylate free Gro/TLE to control the pool of Gro/TLE that can bind TCF/Lef. Our data also suggest the presence of an as yet unidentified de-ubiquitylase (DUB) that facilitates removal of ubiquitin from Gro/TLE, which would allow TCF/Lef binding (Figure 7). Cycles of mono-ubiquitylation and de-ubiquitylation have been shown to regulate activity of the transcriptional activators Smad4, p53, and FoxO (Dupont et al., 2009; Li et al., 2003; van der Horst et al., 2006). Our study provides evidence for a similar mode of regulation of a transcriptional repressor.

Until recently, most studies have focused on transcriptional co-activator activity because it was generally believed that co-repressors are abundant proteins subject to little regulation. It is becoming clear, however, that co-repressor activity is highly complex and can be controlled through a variety of mechanisms (Perissi et al., 2010). Here, we show that the co-repressor Gro/TLE is regulated by ubiquitylation in a manner that may be Wnt-pathway specific. Gro/TLE has been shown to participate in transcriptional repression of multiple signaling pathways (Buscarlet and Stifani, 2007). Our findings that XIAP ubiquitylates Gro/TLE on its N-terminal Q domain (which disrupts TCF/Lef binding) without disrupting its capacity to oligomerize suggest that XIAP modification of Gro/TLE may specifically affect its Wnt repressive function. This possibility is consistent with our observation that XIAP knockdown has no observable effect on Notch signaling. In the absence of Notch signaling, Gro/TLE normally binds to the Hairless protein to repress Notch target gene activation by the transcription factor, Suppressor of Hairless (Barolo et al., 2002). Binding to Hairless occurs via the C-terminal WD40 domain of Gro/TLE (Jennings et al., 2006). Thus, ubiquitin modifications of Gro/TLE on its N-terminal Q domain would not be expected to disrupt its interaction with Hairless in the Notch pathway or other pathways in which repression by Gro/TLE occurs via the WD40 domain or via Gro/TLE oligomerization.

Our identification of XIAP as a critical Wnt pathway component provides a link between apoptosis and Wnt signaling and represents a mechanism by which a cell could coordinate survival and proliferation. Wnt signaling has been shown to inhibit apoptosis and to be required for XIAP expression in cancer cells (Chen et al., 2001; Wang et al., 2010). Thus, XIAP may be part of a positive feedback loop involving Wnt pathway-induced proliferation and inhibition of apoptosis. Surprisingly, XIAP knock-out mice have no obvious apoptotic or Wnt phenotypes as would be expected given its important role in apoptotic inhibition and our findings that XIAP is required for Wnt signaling in cultured human cells and in Xenopus embryos. Only exon 1 of XIAP was deleted in the knockout mouse (Harlin et al., 2001). Thus, it is possible that there is read-through that permits expression of the C-terminal region of XIAP, which includes the RING domain. Alternatively, other IAP family members or E3 ligases might compensate for XIAP function when it is deleted in the mouse.

Our findings may have important clinical implications as XIAP is upregulated in a majority of human cancers, and inhibitors of XIAP are currently in clinical trials (LaCasse et al., 2008). Drug development has been largely focused on developing small molecule and peptide Smac mimetics that bind to the BIR domains of XIAP to inhibit its anti-apoptotic function. Here, we show that the critical role of XIAP in Wnt signaling depends on its E3 ligase RING domain and is distinct from its anti-apoptotic functions.
