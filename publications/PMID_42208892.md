P5A-ATPases constitute a distinct branch of the P-type ATPase superfamily
implicated in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) quality control through the removal or
reorientation of transmembrane segments. Although genetic and structural studies
have suggested a role for these enzymes in transmembrane-helix handling,
biochemical evidence linking substrate interaction to catalytic activity has
remained limited. Here, we show that purified Saccharomyces cerevisiae Spf1p, a
prototypical P5A-ATPase, is stimulated by C-terminal domains of tail-anchored
(TA) proteins. Fusion constructs containing the transmembrane and C-terminal
regions of either Fis1p (FIS) or the bacterial TA protein YgiM (YGIM) increased
ATP hydrolysis approximately twofold in a lipid-dependent and vanadate-sensitive
manner. Disruption of membrane-proximal basic residues, either by deletion of
the FIS C-terminal KKR motif or by alanine substitution in the YGIM C-terminal
tail, markedly reduced stimulation, indicating that C-terminal positive charges
contribute to activation. YGIM stimulated Spf1p ATPase activity in a
concentration-dependent and saturable manner (Km ≈ 0.12 μM) and increased the
relative contribution of a high-affinity component in vanadate inhibition. In
pulldown assays, binding of YGIM to Spf1p was enhanced when the enzyme was
stabilized in an E2P-like conformation using beryllium fluoride. Structural
inspection of the E2P state revealed a conserved acidic patch at the luminal
entrance of the central cavity, forming a vestibular ring positioned to interact
electrostatically with basic C-terminal tails of TA proteins. Altogether, these
results support the role of TA proteins as transported substrates of P5A-ATPases
and highlight charge-based interactions at the luminal cavity entrance as a key
determinant of substrate-dependent activation.

## Full Text

The P-type ATPase superfamily comprises a large and evolutionarily conserved group of primary active transporters that couple ATP hydrolysis to substrate translocation across biological membranes (1). Based on sequence and functional criteria, P-type ATPases are classified into five major subfamilies (P1–P5). While P1–P3 ATPases transport inorganic cations and P4 ATPases function as phospholipid flippases, the physiological substrates of the P5 subfamily remained elusive for many years. P5 ATPases are subdivided into two clades: P5A-ATPases, represented by Saccharomyces cerevisiae Spf1p and its human ortholog ATP13A1, and P5B ATPases (ATP13A2–ATP13A5), which mediate polyamine transport in vacuolar and lysosomal membranes (2).

A major conceptual advance occurred with the demonstration that P5A-ATPases act as transmembrane-helix dislocases in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), extracting mislocalized or aberrantly oriented membrane segments from the ER membrane (3). This finding shifted the view of P5A-ATPases away from classical small-molecule transporters toward enzymes specialized in membrane protein quality control. In this framework, substrates are not ions or metabolites, but membrane-embedded polypeptides whose topogenesis has failed or requires correction.

Genetic and cell biological studies in S. cerevisiae have implicated Spf1p in a wide range of ER-associated processes, including protein glycosylation, ion homeostasis, lipid organization, ER-associated degradation (ERAD), and membrane protein biogenesis (4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9). Rather than reflecting multiple independent functions, these phenotypes are consistent with a central role for Spf1p in safeguarding the fidelity of membrane protein insertion and topology within the ER. Cells lacking Spf1p accumulate mislocalized membrane proteins, supporting the idea that P5A-ATPases participate in surveillance pathways that recognize and resolve topogenic errors at the level of transmembrane segments.

Consistent with this role, P5A-ATPases function as ER-localized quality-control factors that promote correct membrane protein topology or remove proteins that misinsert into the ER membrane (10). Mitochondrial tail-anchored (TA) proteins such as OMP25 and BAK1, which can occasionally enter the ER via the EMC pathway, are selectively recognized and extracted by ATP13A1 (3). In addition, P5A-ATPases prevent topological inversion of signal-anchor type II membrane proteins during biogenesis, with loss of P5A function triggering distinct quality-control responses (11). Based on the structure of Spf1p and genetic analyses of membrane protein topology, it was proposed that P5A-ATPases may employ a negatively charged luminal pocket to recognize positively charged residues exposed by mis-oriented transmembrane helices or luminal loops of membrane proteins, thereby extending their substrate scope beyond tail-anchored proteins (1, 3, 9). This conceptual framework was further supported by recent studies on the human ortholog ATP13A1, which demonstrated charge-based recognition during the biogenesis of multi-spanning membrane proteins (12). However, direct biochemical evidence linking such charge-based recognition to stimulation of the ATPase catalytic cycle had not been experimentally addressed.

Despite this functional divergence from classical ion pumps, P5A-ATPases retain the core architecture and catalytic cycle of P-type ATPases and are thought to operate through a modified Post–Albers mechanism (13, 14). In the E1 state, the enzyme binds ATP at the cytosolic N domain and undergoes autophosphorylation at a conserved aspartate residue. Transition to the E2P state is accompanied by large conformational rearrangements that open a central cavity toward the ER lumen (3). Structural studies have revealed that this cavity can accommodate α-helical polypeptides, consistent with a transport cycle adapted for transmembrane-helix handling rather than ion translocation (3, 15).

Cryo-electron microscopy structures of P5A-ATPases have further identified distinctive architectural features, including an N-terminal extension and a large cytosolic insertion often referred to as the arm or plug domain (3, 15, 16). In structures of fungal Spf1p, densities corresponding to putative substrate helices have been observed within the central cavity, providing direct structural evidence for polypeptide engagement. Together, these studies support a model in which P5A-ATPases recognize transmembrane segments within a luminally accessible cavity and use ATP-driven conformational changes to reposition or extract them.

Even with these advances, the biochemical principles that govern substrate recognition and coupling to ATP hydrolysis remain poorly defined. In particular, direct evidence linking binding of transmembrane polypeptides to stimulation of P5A-ATPase activity has been lacking, largely due to the absence of tractable in vitro systems. To address this gap, we established a minimal biochemical assay using purified S. cerevisiae Spf1p and model substrates derived from tail-anchored proteins. Using ATP hydrolysis as a functional readout, we demonstrate that C-terminal domains of TA proteins stimulate Spf1p activity in a lipid-dependent and vanadate-sensitive manner. Our results provide direct biochemical evidence that substrate-derived polypeptides modulate the catalytic cycle of a P5A- ATPase, establishing a mechanistic link between substrate engagement and ATP hydrolysis in ER membrane protein quality control.

Moreover, it has previously been shown that the basal ATPase activity measured after expression and purification of a catalytically inactive Spf1p mutant is markedly lower than the basal activity of the wild-type enzyme (13), supporting the conclusion that the activity reported here reflects intrinsic turnover of Spf1p. All kinetic experiments assessing Spf1p ATPase activity were performed with purified Spf1p containing a C-terminal GFP fusion (Spf1p–GFP), whose ATPase activity is indistinguishable from that of non-tagged Spf1p under the assay conditions used. In the absence of YGIM, the majority of ATPase activity (∼90%) corresponded to a low-affinity, millimolar vanadate-sensitive component, indicating that Spf1p predominantly occupies conformations with limited access to transition-state–like intermediates. A similar low-affinity vanadate inhibition has been reported for the purified P4-ATPase Drs2p (40), consistent with the idea that basal ATPase activity can arise from enzyme populations that are catalytically active but poorly coupled to productive transport.

As expected for a substrate, addition of YGIM increased total ATPase activity and selectively enhanced the relative contribution of the high-affinity, micromolar vanadate-sensitive component. This shift is consistent with a redistribution of Spf1p toward conformations that more readily access dephosphorylation-competent states en route to an E2-like configuration. Importantly, the persistence of biphasic inhibition under all conditions indicates that YGIM does not eliminate the low-affinity population but instead increases the fraction of enzyme molecules sampling vanadate-sensitive conformations.

ATPase activity assay

All ATPase activity measurements reported in this study were performed using the Spf1p-GFPvariant, in which GFP is fused to the C terminus of Spf1p. This fusion protein exhibits ATPase activity indistinguishable from that of the wild-type enzyme under the assay conditions used (16, 23). ATPase activity was measured essentially as described previously (16), in a reaction medium containing 50 mM Hepes-K (pH 7.4 at 28 °C), 0.5 mM EGTA, 5 mM NaN3, and 5 mM MgCl2 (corresponding to 2 mM free Mg2+) in a final volume of 0.25 ml. Prior to initiating the reaction, a premix was prepared containing Spf1p, either peptide substrate or the corresponding volume of its elution buffer, and lipids. Purified and desalted Spf1p-GFP (15 μl; 1 μg protein) was mixed with 15 μl of substrate or elution buffer and 8 μl of a freshly sonicated lipid–detergent suspension containing 1% (w/v) soybean asolectin and 0.5% (w/v) C12E10. The mixture was preincubated for 8 min at 30 °C and then transferred to the reaction tube. Under these conditions the final concentrations were 420 μM asolectin and 0.016% (w/v) C12E10. After an additional 5 min equilibration, reactions were initiated by adding ATP to a final concentration of 3 mM and incubating for 20 min at 30 °C.

ATP hydrolysis was quantified by measuring the release of inorganic phosphate (Pi) using the Baginski method (47). One unit of ATPase activity (1 U) was defined as the hydrolysis of 1 μmol of ATP per minute under the assay conditions.

Basal ATPase activity of purified Spf1p (in the absence of peptide substrates) varied among enzyme preparations (0.5–1.3 U mg–1); all comparative experiments were performed using the same enzyme batch. Each data point was measured in duplicate, and assays were run in parallel with a blank containing the complete reaction mixture except for Spf1p.

Pull-down assay

YGIM lacking the His-tag was generated by digestion with TEV protease (Addgene plasmid #125194) purified following the Berglund laboratory protocol (48). Proteolysis was carried out overnight at 4 °C in 25 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.2, containing 125 mM NaCl and 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, using a 1:10 TEV:substrate molar ratio with gentle agitation. After digestion, the reaction mixture was applied to a Ni–NTA agarose column to remove His-tagged material, and the flow-through containing tag-free YGIM was collected for pull-down assays.

Microsomal membranes containing approximately 200 μg of the untagged Spf1p were solubilized and divided into two equal fractions. Each fraction was applied to a Ni–NTA agarose column (2-mL bed volume) and washed with purification buffer supplemented with 25 mM imidazole and 0.05% C12E10. A second wash was performed using the same buffer containing 10 mM MgCl2, and for one column the buffer additionally contained BeFx (0.05 mM BeSO4 and 3 mM NaF). Bound Spf1p was incubated with these wash solutions for 1 h at 8 °C to allow formation of the BeFx-stabilized complex.

Equivalent amounts of tag-free YGIM were then added to each column, and the unbound fractions were collected. Columns were subsequently washed with 10 ml of purification buffer containing 20 mM imidazole, with or without BeFx as appropriate. Bound proteins were eluted with purification buffer containing 300 mM imidazole under the same BeFx conditions.
