Rising drug resistance among pathogenic fungi, paired with a limited antifungal
arsenal, poses an increasing threat to human health. To identify antifungal
compounds, we screened the RIKEN natural product depository against
representative isolates of four major human fungal pathogens. This screen
identified NPD6433, a triazenyl indole with broad-spectrum activity against all
screening strains, as well as the filamentous mold Aspergillus fumigatus.
Mechanistic studies indicated that NPD6433 targets the enoyl reductase domain of
fatty acid synthase 1 (Fas1), covalently inhibiting its flavin
mononucleotide-dependent NADPH-oxidation activity and arresting essential fatty
acid biosynthesis. Robust Fas1 inhibition kills Candida albicans, while
sublethal inhibition impairs diverse virulence traits. At well-tolerated
exposures, NPD6433 extended the lifespan of nematodes infected with
azole-resistant C. albicans. Overall, identification of NPD6433 provides a tool
with which to explore lipid homeostasis as a therapeutic target in pathogenic
fungi and reveals a mechanism by which Fas1 function can be inhibited.

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, concern has been growing over the major threat fungal pathogens pose to human health.1–3 Fungi cause devastating diseases, killing in excess of 1.5 million people annually. Species of the genera Candida, Cryptococcus, and Aspergillus account for ~90% of these lethal infections.4 Despite the profound impact of fungal disease, only three major classes of antifungals are available for the treatment of systemic infections: azoles, echinocandins, and polyenes, all of which target the fungal cell wall or membrane. Each class suffers from limitations, such as inaccessibility in resource-poor areas of the world, host toxicity, poor pharmacology, or vulnerability to resistance development.5 Additionally, few antifungals exhibit broad-spectrum activity, which is problematic as it necessitates identification of the causative organism to tailor treatment.6 Clearly, strategies are needed for targeting as yet unexploited biological processes in pathogenic fungi.

Screening libraries to identify compounds with antifungal activity has the advantage of pre-selecting for bioactive molecules that already exhibit the desired property of inhibiting fungal growth. However, this approach has traditionally suffered from an inability to rapidly elucidate the molecule’s cellular target. Fortunately, advances in genetic and biochemical analyses have accelerated efforts to decipher the mechanism of action of bioactive compounds.7,8 Such advances enable the identification of vulnerabilities in pathogenic fungi, and ultimately expand the limited antifungal target space. For example, chemogenomic profiling facilitated the characterization of molecules such as A-12, a non-nucleoside acetyl CoA synthetase inhibitor with activity against Candida albicans and Cryptococcus neoformans9; benzothioureas, which target the cell wall integrity pathway in C. neoformans10; and GW461484A, which inhibits the casein kinase Yck2 in C. albicans.11 Additionally, screening extracts from marine microbes identified turbinmicin, a natural product that impedes vesicle-mediated trafficking to inhibit growth of drug-resistant fungi.12 Such findings coupled with the long history of successful antimicrobial drug discovery efforts using growth inhibition as endpoint, support the value of high-throughput screening of compound libraries to tap the wealth of molecules with antifungal activity remaining to be discovered.

Here, we identified compounds with broad spectrum antifungal activity by performing a high-throughput screen of the structurally-diverse RIKEN natural product depository (NPDepo) chemical library against four World Health Organization (WHO) critical priority human fungal pathogens.13 In follow-up, we defined the mechanism of action responsible for the antifungal activity of our most promising hit using genetic and biochemical techniques and characterized the impact of target inhibition by this compound on fungal biology and virulence traits.

RESULTSHigh-throughput screen identifies a broad-spectrum antifungal agentWe screened the RIKEN NPDepo14 library using isolates representative of four pathogenic yeast species: C. albicans, Candida glabrata, Candida auris, and C. neoformans. The library (19,840 compounds) included synthetic small molecules, as well as natural products and their derivatives. Hits were identified as compounds which reduced fungal growth by ≥ 50% and were subjected to verification under the same screening conditions against all four fungal pathogens to ensure reproducible bioactivity. Sixty-nine unique structures inhibited growth of at least one fungal pathogen (Figure 1A). Interestingly, the 10 compounds inhibiting at least three of the four fungal pathogens were all active against C. albicans, C. glabrata, and C. neoformans, but not C. auris. Notably, 20 compounds displayed activity against all four fungal pathogens (Figure 1A). Eleven were known antifungals, four were previously reported cytotoxic agents, and two were eliminated because they could not be purchased or showed no activity upon purchase (Figure 1A). This left three structurally unrelated compounds with previously unreported activity against all four fungal pathogens (Figure 1B).Compounds of interest were further examined for antifungal potency and toxicity to human cells (Figures 1B and 1C). Both NPD6433 and NPD8193 exhibited minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) below the initial screening concentration (10 μg/mL) against multiple species, while NPD5296 only showed this level of potency against C. auris (MIC90 = 5 μg/mL; Figure 1B). Cytotoxicity was profiled against the human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cancer cell line to assess fungal selectivity of each compound in culture (Figure 1C). Finally, given their broad anti-yeast spectrum, bioactivity was also examined against the mold Aspergillus fumigatus. NPD6433 was the only compound with bioactivity against A. fumigatus (Figure 1D). Given its promising combination of broad antifungal activity and human cell tolerability, NPD6433 was prioritized for further investigation.Chemogenomic profiling reveals Fas1 as the putative target of NPD6433To investigate NPD6433 mode-of-action, we performed haploinsufficiency profiling (HIP) in both the model yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae and the fungal pathogen, C. albicans. Pooled cultures of an S. cerevisiae barcoded essential gene heterozygous deletion collection of 968 strains constructed in a drug-sensitized genetic background15 were grown in the presence or absence of NPD6433. The relative abundance of each barcoded strain following NPD6433 treatment was determined by high-throughput sequencing of PCR-amplified barcodes followed by BEAN-counter analysis to generate a chemical-genetic interaction score reflecting enrichment or depletion of each strain in the presence of NPD6433 relative to solvent control.16 Analysis revealed a single gene, FAS1, for which reduced dosage conferred significant hypersensitivity to NPD6433; an extreme outlier interaction (Figure 2A). Growth curve experiments confirmed a reproducible defect for the FAS1 heterozygous strain relative to the parental control (Figure S1A). FAS1 encodes the β-subunit of FAS, a protein complex responsible for catalyzing the synthesis of long-chain saturated fatty acids in yeast. To complement HIP analysis, we leveraged a library of ~960 S cerevisiae strains,17 each overexpressing an essential gene to determine which gene(s), when overexpressed, would confer resistance. Overexpression of FAS1 conferred resistance to NPD6433 and was an outlier interaction based on Tukey’s criteria (Figure 2B).18 Three strains were identified and confirmed to be significantly hypersensitive (Figures 2B and S1B). Interestingly, OLE1, which encodes a fatty acid desaturase, is negatively regulated by fatty acid levels,19 suggesting NPD6433 may selectively stress cells overexpressing this desaturase, thus explaining the sensitivity phenotype observed when OLE1 was overexpressed (Figures 2B and S1B).To confirm results in a fungal pathogen, we adopted a similar approach using a library of heterozygous deletion strains covering ~90% of the C. albicans genome.20 Pools of double-barcoded mutants were grown in the absence or presence of NPD6433. The drug/solvent log2 ratio of barcode reads was used to identify outliers (Figure 2C). Consistent with S. cerevisiae data, FAS1 was identified as one of three top significant hits, along with orf19.3828 (an uncharacterized, C. albicans-specific ORF) and RPN9 (component of the proteasome regulatory particle).21 Hypersensitivity of strains was confirmed by testing each individual heterozygous deletion mutant (Figure S1C). Because FAS1 was identified as the only essential gene that altered NPD6433 susceptibility in both species, we pursued Fas1 as the most likely target of NPD6433.To begin investigating, we tested whether supplementing growth medium with essential fatty acids would reduce the growth inhibitory activity of the compound. lementation with a mixture of long chain fatty acids (0.01% each of stearic and myristic acid, 1% Tween 40) led to a >10-fold increase in the MIC90 for NPD6433 against S. cerevisiae (Figure 2D). This rescue is analogous to fatty acid rescue experiments reported for the known FAS inhibitor, cerulenin.22 The inability of fatty acid supplementation to fully rescue FAS inhibition is consistent with work in C. neoformans, which found that genetic depletion of FAS could not be fully rescued by exogenous fatty acids.23 Together, chemical genetic analyses in both S. cerevisiae and C. albicans support a model in which NPD6433 targets Fas1.NPD6433 inhibits Fas1 enoyl reductase activityIn fungi, FAS is a large, multi-enzyme complex comprised of six copies of eight independent functional domains (α6β6), encoded by FAS1 (β chain) and FAS2 (α chain). Together, these catalyze all reactions required for fatty acid synthesis.24 Given the numerous functionalities of this enzyme complex, we decided to select for NPD6433-resistant mutants to identify residues important for inhibition of FAS activity. Resistant lineages were evolved in two S. cerevisiae backgorunds, both of which had been drug-sensitized through the impairment of cellular drug efflux capabilities.15,25 In “Parent 1”, efflux was impaired via deletion of 16 genes involved in antifungal efflux.25 In “Parent 2”, efflux transporter gene SNQ2, and transcription factors PDR1 and PDR3, which enable drug resistance via efflux, were deleted.15 These strains enabled selection of resistant mutants at lower concentrations of compound and reduced the likelihood of efflux-related mechanisms of resistance arising. In three independent experiments, 1.5 × 105 cells were plated and 3 colonies, 2 colonies, and no colonies were isolated suggesting a low frequency for emergence of resistance in drug-sensitized S. cerevisiae. Isolates were verified to be resistant by dose-response testing in liquid medium (Figure 3A). Mutants were counter-screened for cross-resistance to cerulenin, a known Fas2 inhibitor.26,27 Whole genome-sequencing of drug-resistant mutants revealed four amino acid substitutions within the enoyl reductase (ER) domain at two distinct amino acid positions (Figure 3A). These positions localize to the periphery of the ER catalytic cavity and are adjacent to two loops of the ER domain of Fas1 where the mobile acyl carrier protein domain (ACP) docks.28,29The ER domain of Fas1 catalyzes the last step in the fatty acid elongation cycle by reducing the enoyl-ACP intermediate to yield a saturated acyl-ACP. This redox reaction utilizes NADPH, as does the ketoacyl reductase (KR) activity of the enzyme.24 To determine whether NPD6433 directly impairs FAS enzymatic activity, we assayed the activity of FAS complex, which had been affinity-purified from a functionally validated FAS1–3xFLAG/FAS1 C. albicans strain (Figure S2). The strain was grown in medium supplemented with fatty acids, which enabled growth in the presence of otherwise inhibitory concentrations of NPD6433 (Figures 3D and S2). As readout for reductive activity of the affinity-purified complex, NADPH consumption was monitored spectrophotometrically over time in the presence of malonyl-CoA.29 NPD6433 diminshed NAPDH consumption compared to solvent controls, as monitored by absorbance at 340 nm (Figure 3C). The two domains that use NADPH in their redox activity (ER and KR) can be distinguished because the ER domain in fungi (but not humans) also requires flavin mononucleotide (FMN) as a co-factor.24 To test if NPD6433 inhibits the ER domain of Fas1, we investigated whether supplementation of growth medium with FMN would antagonize NPD6433’s antifungal activity. While growth inhibition caused by either NPD6433 or cerulenin was rescued upon supplementation with fatty acids, addition of the co-factor FMN only rescued C. albicans growth inhibition caused by NPD6433 (Figure 3D). Collectively, results support a model in which NPD6433 inhibits fatty acid synthesis through disruption of FMN-dependent NADPH oxidation by the ER domain of Fas1.NPD6433-derived diazonium moiety exerts bioactivityTo gain insight into the chemical features necessary for bioactivity, a limited structure-activity relationship (SAR) study was undertaken by testing the activity of 10 commercially-available compounds that are structurally related to NPD6433 (Table S1). Potency of each compound was assessed by dose-response assays against C. albicans. Results indicated that modification of the indole ring in compounds 1 and 2 resulted in a two-fold increase in potency relative to NPD6433, and modification of the non-indole moiety and extension of the ester chain found in 3 and 4 also led to a two-fold increase in activity (Figures 4A and S3). All compounds displaying activity had 4-fold greater potency against the FAS1/fas1Δ heterozygous strain, as seen with NPD6433, suggesting on-target activity (Figure S3A). Importantly, compounds 5 and 6, which lack a triazene linkage between the two domains of the scaffold, did not show antifungal activity against either wild-type or FAS1/fas1Δ strains (Figures 4A and S3A), suggesting this linkage is essential for bioactivity. Finally, to investigate whether the increased antifungal potency of a representative analog was associated with increased cytotoxicity, we compared the inhibitory activity of NPD6433 to that of 1 against human 293T cancer cells in culture (Figure S3C). A similar ~two-fold increase in potency of 1 against the cancer cells was observed.Given the labile nature of the triazene linkage,30 we hypothesized that the major active species of NPD6433 and related compounds would likely be a product of triazene cleavage. Therefore, we evaluated the antifungal activity of the indole groups present in NPD6433, 1, and related analogs (Figures 4A and S3; 7–10). They were all inactive up to 100 μM, suggesting that a diazonium moiety generated by acid-mediated cleavage of the triazene linkage might be critical for bioactivity (Figure 4B). LC-UV-MS analysis of NPD6433 found that the major peak had an m/z ratio of 392 and eluted at 4.24 min, consistent with intact parent compound. However, a secondary peak was detected with an m/z ratio of 202, which eluted at 3.53 min, consistent with the diazonium moiety (Figure 4C). Both mass peaks produced distinct UV-visible spectra corresponding to the two distinct structures (Figure 4C).To determine what the bioactive component of the parent compound might be in aqueous solution, we performed UV-visible analysis and bioactivity assays in tandem (Figures 4D and 4E). The UV-visible spectrum of the compound at the start of incubation in buffer demonstrated absorption peaks at 294 and 370 nm as expected for parent compound based on previous LC-MS analysis (Figures 4C, top and 4D, top). After 60 min, the spectrum shifted to reveal absorption peaks at 262 and 358 nm corresponding to the 202 Da diazonium fragment (Figures 4C, bottom and 4D, bottom). The additional peak at 306 nm could be due to the presence of the other fragment generated by triazene cleavage. Importantly, despite the spectral evidence of rapid diazonium production, no difference was observed in the antifungal activity of NPD6433 or the structurally-related compound 1 before and after incubation (Figure 4E). Overall, findings indicate that triazene parent scaffolds are cleaved in aqueous solution, releasing diazonium products that are responsible for bioactivity.NPD6433-derived diazonium forms adduct within the ER domainThe known ability of diazonium ion to react with nucleophilic amino acid residues31 prompted us to investigate whether upon production, either extracellularly or intracellularly, the diazonium might adduct critical residues within the active site of the Fas1 ER domain. The FAS holo-complex was affinity purified from fungus grown in the presence of NPD6433, 1, or vehicle control, and eluted material was fractionated by SDS-PAGE. We grew fungus in the presence of compounds because adduct formation could be favored by the intracellular environment, and conditions that might not be adequately captured were compound added to affinity purified enzyme in buffer. Coomassie-stained bands containing the co-migrating Fas1 and Fas2 proteins, were excised, trypsin digested, and analyzed by LC-MS to identify peptide fragments. Using MSFragger software with an open search strategy, mass shifts consistent with diazonium adduct formation were detected (Figure 4F). Candidate masses for adduct formation were determined using all plausible degradation products and plausible reactions of degradation products with amino acid residues (i.e., ester hydrolysis, aromatic residue coupling,32 de-diazoniation,31,33,34 and amide formation).By comparing FAS complex LC-MS peptide spectra derived from vehicle control vs. NPD6433-or 1-treated samples, we identified mass shifts consistent with de-diazoniative adducts of both NPD6433 and 1 with the same 20 amino acid peptide fragment mapping to the enoyl reductase domain of Fas1 (Figure 4G). Despite the presence of a stoichiometric amount of Fas2, a protein of comparable size, no such mass shift was identified in the samples that were analyzed for peptides mapping to this protein. Importantly, the mass-shifted Fas1-derived peptide we identified encompasses the catalytic histidine 727 residue within the active site of the enoyl reductase domain required for its activity. Histidine 727 is conserved in fungal FAS proteins. In bacterial FAS, there are three distinct proteins (expressed via different genes) that act as enoyl reductases: FabI, FabK, FabL.35 FabK has a corresponding histidine as the catalytic residue.36 In human fatty acid synthase, a lysine and an aspartic acid enable NADPH-mediated reduction of the enoyl moiety of the acyl chain.37 These finding suggest that adduct formation with either histidine 727, or a neighboring residue, is responsible for the biochemical inhibition of FAS.Characterization of antifungal activityNext, we further characterized the antifungal activity of NPD6433 and analog 1 by testing them against a range of isolates of pathogenic yeast species under standard Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) conditions, rather than the rich-medium conditions used for screening. Changing culture conditions did not diminish compound activity. MIC80 values ranged from 1.25 to 10 μg/mL with the most sensitive organism being an isolate of C. neoformans (Figure S4A). MIC testing against clinical isolates of C. albicans with resistance to conventional antifungals was also performed under CLSI conditions and did not demonstrate cross-resistance (Figure S4B). Finally, we examined the potential for synergism between NPD6433 and fluconazole, which targets fungal sterol biosynthesis. In rich medium over the concentration ranges tested, NPD6433 did not sensitize azole-resistant isolates of C. albicans to fluconazole or synergize with fluconazole across the same panel of pathogenic yeasts used in our primary screen (Figure S4C).A desirable attribute for antimicrobials is the ability to kill organisms, rather than simply inhibit their growth.38 To determine whether FAS inhibition slowed the growth of C. albicans or killed it, dose-response assays in tandem with spotting assays were performed. After 24-h exposure to a gradient of NPD6433, cerulenin, or fluconazole, aliquots of cell suspension were spotted onto drug-free YPD agar and plates were incubated for 48 h. In contrast, to the known fungistatic activity of fluconazole, the FAS inhibitors NPD6433 and cerulenin demonstrated loss of colony-forming activity (Figure 5A). Consistent with spotting assays, time-kill assays in liquid culture demonstrated a >2-log reduction in viability after 48 h in NPD6433 (Figure 5B). To support the killing activity of FAS inhibition, we used a conditional expression strain in which one allele of the FAS1 gene is deleted and the other is under the control of a doxycycline (DOX)-repressible promoter (tetO-FAS1/fas1Δ). In this strain, culture in DOX significantly repressed FAS1 expression relative to the absence of DOX (Figure 5C). DOX-mediated depletion of FAS1 in the tetO-FAS1/fas1Δ strain also eliminated cell viability (Figure 5D).FAS inhibition impairs virulence traits in fungal pathogensGiven the importance of fatty acids for various cellular functions,39 we explored whether sub-lethal FAS inhibition by NPD6433 would perturb fungal virulence traits. In C. neoformans, the unique traits of capsule formation and melanin production were assessed. When cells were imaged in the presence of the dye India Ink, we observed a reduction in capsule thickness caused by a sub-MIC NPD6433 concentration as compared to untreated or fluconazole-treated conditions (Figure 6A). To quantify this observation, the % cell-area attributable to the capsule was quantified using CellProfiler, which confirmed a significant decrease in capsule size in cells treated with NPD6433 compared to untreated and fluconazole-treated cells (Figure 6B). The effect of NPD6433 at sub-MIC concentration on melanization, another C. neoformans trait required for pathogenicity, was also assessed. Cells were spotted onto medium containing the melanin precursor, L-DOPA, in the presence of various antifungals. After three days of incubation, cells treated with NPD6433, fluconazole, or amphotericin B remained lighter in pigmentation, whereas cells left untreated or those treated with an inhibitory concentration of the calcineurin inhibitor FK50640 had turned dark brown (Figure 6C).The ability of C. albicans to undergo morphological switching from yeast to filaments is a well-established virulence trait.41 To examine the effect of NPD6433 on this process, C. albicans was exposed to an array of filament-inducing cues,42 which as expected induced robust filamentation (Figure 6D). Treatment with fluconazole, a compound previously reported to block morphogenesis at sub-MIC concentrations,43 reduced filamentation in all conditions tested. Interestingly, treatment with NPD6433, even at concentrations that impeded growth, had no observable effect on filamentation (Figure 6D).Biofilms formed by Candida species on implanted medical devices are a frequent source of infection.44 To determine whether FAS1 is important for biofilm formation by C. albicans, we first pursued a genetic approach. The extent of adherent biofilm formed by a DOX-repressible tetO-FAS1/fas1Δ strain was measured using metabolic reduction of the dye XTT. In the absence of DOX, the strain showed no significant difference in the ability to form a biofilm compared to the parental strain. However, DOX-induced depletion of FAS1 blocked biofilm formation in the tetO-FAS1/fas1Δ mutant (Figure 6E). This block was comparable to that of the efg1Δ/efg1Δ control (Figure 6E), a strain defective in biofilm development.45 Next, using the same approach, we assessed whether NPD6433 could prevent biofilm formation by wild-type C. albicans or C. auris. While C. auris forms less robust biofilms compared to C. albicans,46 NPD6433 reduced biofilm formation in a concentration-dependent manner in both species (Figure 6F). Consistent with effects in liquid culture (Figure 6D), inhibition of biofilm formation by NPD6433 was not associated with loss of ability to filament. Residual small areas of biofilm formed in the presence of NPD6433 demonstrated filamentous morphology (Figures S5A and S5B).Given its activity in culture, we examined whether NPD6433 could sterilize catheters implanted in rats, a model that mimics central venous catheter-related infection in patients.47 Previously implanted catheters were infected with C. albicans by intraluminal instillation of fungal inoculum with or without NPD6433. Subsequent biofilm development was assessed by removing catheters and imaging the lumina of their tips. Under untreated conditions, robust biofilms were established, whereas with NPD6433 treatment, biofilm formation was markedly reduced (Figure 6G). Quantification demonstrated that NPD6433 reduced viable colony-forming units (CFUs) isolated from intraluminal catheter fluid by 100-fold compared to solvent control, down to the limit of detection for this assay (Figure 6H).To begin investigating therapeutic potential, we examined the ability of NPD6433 to rescue kidney cancer-derived cells (293T) when infected with C. albicans.11Using a marked-cell approach to distinguish the species in co-culture, we found that when untreated, fungal growth dominated the culture resulting in poor human cell survival (Figure 7A). However, addition of NPD6433 significantly reduced fungal growth in a concentration-dependent manner and rescued human cell survival to levels equivalent to treatment with the conventional antifungal fluconazole (Figure 7A). Finally, we examined the efficacy of NPD6433 in an infection model involving the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans. This invertebrate is frequently used as a model host animal due to ease of infection and functional similarity between the intestinal tract of C. elegans and mammals, a site from which C. albicans can invade to cause systemic infection.48 Worms were infected with azole-tolerant (Figure 7B) or azole-resistant (Figure 7C) clinical isolates of C. albicans, transferred to multi-well plates containing varying concentrations of NPD6433, and survival was monitored for up to 48 h. Treatment significantly extended the lifespan of worms infected with either isolate in a dose-dependent manner. Exposure of uninfected worms to the same concentrations of NPD6433 had no deleterious effect on survival (Figure 7D).

The library (19,840 compounds) included synthetic small molecules, as well as natural products and their derivatives. Hits were identified as compounds which reduced fungal growth by ≥ 50% and were subjected to verification under the same screening conditions against all four fungal pathogens to ensure reproducible bioactivity. Sixty-nine unique structures inhibited growth of at least one fungal pathogen (Figure 1A). Interestingly, the 10 compounds inhibiting at least three of the four fungal pathogens were all active against C. albicans, C. glabrata, and C. neoformans, but not C. auris. Notably, 20 compounds displayed activity against all four fungal pathogens (Figure 1A). Eleven were known antifungals, four were previously reported cytotoxic agents, and two were eliminated because they could not be purchased or showed no activity upon purchase (Figure 1A). This left three structurally unrelated compounds with previously unreported activity against all four fungal pathogens (Figure 1B).Compounds of interest were further examined for antifungal potency and toxicity to human cells (Figures 1B and 1C). Both NPD6433 and NPD8193 exhibited minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) below the initial screening concentration (10 μg/mL) against multiple species, while NPD5296 only showed this level of potency against C. auris (MIC90 = 5 μg/mL; Figure 1B). Cytotoxicity was profiled against the human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cancer cell line to assess fungal selectivity of each compound in culture (Figure 1C). Finally, given their broad anti-yeast spectrum, bioactivity was also examined against the mold Aspergillus fumigatus. NPD6433 was the only compound with bioactivity against A. fumigatus (Figure 1D). Given its promising combination of broad antifungal activity and human cell tolerability, NPD6433 was prioritized for further investigation.

NPD6433 inhibits Fas1 enoyl reductase activityIn fungi, FAS is a large, multi-enzyme complex comprised of six copies of eight independent functional domains (α6β6), encoded by FAS1 (β chain) and FAS2 (α chain). Together, these catalyze all reactions required for fatty acid synthesis.24 Given the numerous functionalities of this enzyme complex, we decided to select for NPD6433-resistant mutants to identify residues important for inhibition of FAS activity. Resistant lineages were evolved in two S. cerevisiae backgorunds, both of which had been drug-sensitized through the impairment of cellular drug efflux capabilities.15,25 In “Parent 1”, efflux was impaired via deletion of 16 genes involved in antifungal efflux.25 In “Parent 2”, efflux transporter gene SNQ2, and transcription factors PDR1 and PDR3, which enable drug resistance via efflux, were deleted.15 These strains enabled selection of resistant mutants at lower concentrations of compound and reduced the likelihood of efflux-related mechanisms of resistance arising. In three independent experiments, 1.5 × 105 cells were plated and 3 colonies, 2 colonies, and no colonies were isolated suggesting a low frequency for emergence of resistance in drug-sensitized S. cerevisiae. Isolates were verified to be resistant by dose-response testing in liquid medium (Figure 3A). Mutants were counter-screened for cross-resistance to cerulenin, a known Fas2 inhibitor.26,27 Whole genome-sequencing of drug-resistant mutants revealed four amino acid substitutions within the enoyl reductase (ER) domain at two distinct amino acid positions (Figure 3A). These positions localize to the periphery of the ER catalytic cavity and are adjacent to two loops of the ER domain of Fas1 where the mobile acyl carrier protein domain (ACP) docks.28,29The ER domain of Fas1 catalyzes the last step in the fatty acid elongation cycle by reducing the enoyl-ACP intermediate to yield a saturated acyl-ACP. This redox reaction utilizes NADPH, as does the ketoacyl reductase (KR) activity of the enzyme.24 To determine whether NPD6433 directly impairs FAS enzymatic activity, we assayed the activity of FAS complex, which had been affinity-purified from a functionally validated FAS1–3xFLAG/FAS1 C. albicans strain (Figure S2). The strain was grown in medium supplemented with fatty acids, which enabled growth in the presence of otherwise inhibitory concentrations of NPD6433 (Figures 3D and S2). As readout for reductive activity of the affinity-purified complex, NADPH consumption was monitored spectrophotometrically over time in the presence of malonyl-CoA.29 NPD6433 diminshed NAPDH consumption compared to solvent controls, as monitored by absorbance at 340 nm (Figure 3C). The two domains that use NADPH in their redox activity (ER and KR) can be distinguished because the ER domain in fungi (but not humans) also requires flavin mononucleotide (FMN) as a co-factor.24 To test if NPD6433 inhibits the ER domain of Fas1, we investigated whether supplementation of growth medium with FMN would antagonize NPD6433’s antifungal activity. While growth inhibition caused by either NPD6433 or cerulenin was rescued upon supplementation with fatty acids, addition of the co-factor FMN only rescued C. albicans growth inhibition caused by NPD6433 (Figure 3D). Collectively, results support a model in which NPD6433 inhibits fatty acid synthesis through disruption of FMN-dependent NADPH oxidation by the ER domain of Fas1.

NPD6433-derived diazonium moiety exerts bioactivityTo gain insight into the chemical features necessary for bioactivity, a limited structure-activity relationship (SAR) study was undertaken by testing the activity of 10 commercially-available compounds that are structurally related to NPD6433 (Table S1). Potency of each compound was assessed by dose-response assays against C. albicans. Results indicated that modification of the indole ring in compounds 1 and 2 resulted in a two-fold increase in potency relative to NPD6433, and modification of the non-indole moiety and extension of the ester chain found in 3 and 4 also led to a two-fold increase in activity (Figures 4A and S3). All compounds displaying activity had 4-fold greater potency against the FAS1/fas1Δ heterozygous strain, as seen with NPD6433, suggesting on-target activity (Figure S3A). Importantly, compounds 5 and 6, which lack a triazene linkage between the two domains of the scaffold, did not show antifungal activity against either wild-type or FAS1/fas1Δ strains (Figures 4A and S3A), suggesting this linkage is essential for bioactivity. Finally, to investigate whether the increased antifungal potency of a representative analog was associated with increased cytotoxicity, we compared the inhibitory activity of NPD6433 to that of 1 against human 293T cancer cells in culture (Figure S3C). A similar ~two-fold increase in potency of 1 against the cancer cells was observed.Given the labile nature of the triazene linkage,30 we hypothesized that the major active species of NPD6433 and related compounds would likely be a product of triazene cleavage. Therefore, we evaluated the antifungal activity of the indole groups present in NPD6433, 1, and related analogs (Figures 4A and S3; 7–10). They were all inactive up to 100 μM, suggesting that a diazonium moiety generated by acid-mediated cleavage of the triazene linkage might be critical for bioactivity (Figure 4B). LC-UV-MS analysis of NPD6433 found that the major peak had an m/z ratio of 392 and eluted at 4.24 min, consistent with intact parent compound. However, a secondary peak was detected with an m/z ratio of 202, which eluted at 3.53 min, consistent with the diazonium moiety (Figure 4C). Both mass peaks produced distinct UV-visible spectra corresponding to the two distinct structures (Figure 4C).To determine what the bioactive component of the parent compound might be in aqueous solution, we performed UV-visible analysis and bioactivity assays in tandem (Figures 4D and 4E). The UV-visible spectrum of the compound at the start of incubation in buffer demonstrated absorption peaks at 294 and 370 nm as expected for parent compound based on previous LC-MS analysis (Figures 4C, top and 4D, top). After 60 min, the spectrum shifted to reveal absorption peaks at 262 and 358 nm corresponding to the 202 Da diazonium fragment (Figures 4C, bottom and 4D, bottom). The additional peak at 306 nm could be due to the presence of the other fragment generated by triazene cleavage. Importantly, despite the spectral evidence of rapid diazonium production, no difference was observed in the antifungal activity of NPD6433 or the structurally-related compound 1 before and after incubation (Figure 4E). Overall, findings indicate that triazene parent scaffolds are cleaved in aqueous solution, releasing diazonium products that are responsible for bioactivity.

NPD6433-derived diazonium forms adduct within the ER domainThe known ability of diazonium ion to react with nucleophilic amino acid residues31 prompted us to investigate whether upon production, either extracellularly or intracellularly, the diazonium might adduct critical residues within the active site of the Fas1 ER domain. The FAS holo-complex was affinity purified from fungus grown in the presence of NPD6433, 1, or vehicle control, and eluted material was fractionated by SDS-PAGE. We grew fungus in the presence of compounds because adduct formation could be favored by the intracellular environment, and conditions that might not be adequately captured were compound added to affinity purified enzyme in buffer. Coomassie-stained bands containing the co-migrating Fas1 and Fas2 proteins, were excised, trypsin digested, and analyzed by LC-MS to identify peptide fragments. Using MSFragger software with an open search strategy, mass shifts consistent with diazonium adduct formation were detected (Figure 4F). Candidate masses for adduct formation were determined using all plausible degradation products and plausible reactions of degradation products with amino acid residues (i.e., ester hydrolysis, aromatic residue coupling,32 de-diazoniation,31,33,34 and amide formation).By comparing FAS complex LC-MS peptide spectra derived from vehicle control vs. NPD6433-or 1-treated samples, we identified mass shifts consistent with de-diazoniative adducts of both NPD6433 and 1 with the same 20 amino acid peptide fragment mapping to the enoyl reductase domain of Fas1 (Figure 4G). Despite the presence of a stoichiometric amount of Fas2, a protein of comparable size, no such mass shift was identified in the samples that were analyzed for peptides mapping to this protein. Importantly, the mass-shifted Fas1-derived peptide we identified encompasses the catalytic histidine 727 residue within the active site of the enoyl reductase domain required for its activity. Histidine 727 is conserved in fungal FAS proteins. In bacterial FAS, there are three distinct proteins (expressed via different genes) that act as enoyl reductases: FabI, FabK, FabL.35 FabK has a corresponding histidine as the catalytic residue.36 In human fatty acid synthase, a lysine and an aspartic acid enable NADPH-mediated reduction of the enoyl moiety of the acyl chain.37 These finding suggest that adduct formation with either histidine 727, or a neighboring residue, is responsible for the biochemical inhibition of FAS.

Characterization of antifungal activityNext, we further characterized the antifungal activity of NPD6433 and analog 1 by testing them against a range of isolates of pathogenic yeast species under standard Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) conditions, rather than the rich-medium conditions used for screening. Changing culture conditions did not diminish compound activity. MIC80 values ranged from 1.25 to 10 μg/mL with the most sensitive organism being an isolate of C. neoformans (Figure S4A). MIC testing against clinical isolates of C. albicans with resistance to conventional antifungals was also performed under CLSI conditions and did not demonstrate cross-resistance (Figure S4B). Finally, we examined the potential for synergism between NPD6433 and fluconazole, which targets fungal sterol biosynthesis. In rich medium over the concentration ranges tested, NPD6433 did not sensitize azole-resistant isolates of C. albicans to fluconazole or synergize with fluconazole across the same panel of pathogenic yeasts used in our primary screen (Figure S4C).A desirable attribute for antimicrobials is the ability to kill organisms, rather than simply inhibit their growth.38 To determine whether FAS inhibition slowed the growth of C. albicans or killed it, dose-response assays in tandem with spotting assays were performed. After 24-h exposure to a gradient of NPD6433, cerulenin, or fluconazole, aliquots of cell suspension were spotted onto drug-free YPD agar and plates were incubated for 48 h. In contrast, to the known fungistatic activity of fluconazole, the FAS inhibitors NPD6433 and cerulenin demonstrated loss of colony-forming activity (Figure 5A). Consistent with spotting assays, time-kill assays in liquid culture demonstrated a >2-log reduction in viability after 48 h in NPD6433 (Figure 5B). To support the killing activity of FAS inhibition, we used a conditional expression strain in which one allele of the FAS1 gene is deleted and the other is under the control of a doxycycline (DOX)-repressible promoter (tetO-FAS1/fas1Δ). In this strain, culture in DOX significantly repressed FAS1 expression relative to the absence of DOX (Figure 5C). DOX-mediated depletion of FAS1 in the tetO-FAS1/fas1Δ strain also eliminated cell viability (Figure 5D).

FAS inhibition impairs virulence traits in fungal pathogensGiven the importance of fatty acids for various cellular functions,39 we explored whether sub-lethal FAS inhibition by NPD6433 would perturb fungal virulence traits. In C. neoformans, the unique traits of capsule formation and melanin production were assessed. When cells were imaged in the presence of the dye India Ink, we observed a reduction in capsule thickness caused by a sub-MIC NPD6433 concentration as compared to untreated or fluconazole-treated conditions (Figure 6A). To quantify this observation, the % cell-area attributable to the capsule was quantified using CellProfiler, which confirmed a significant decrease in capsule size in cells treated with NPD6433 compared to untreated and fluconazole-treated cells (Figure 6B). The effect of NPD6433 at sub-MIC concentration on melanization, another C. neoformans trait required for pathogenicity, was also assessed. This study leveraged a large repository of structurally-diverse natural products and their derivatives to reveal NPD6433 as a broad-spectrum antifungal compound with activity against four WHO critical priority fungal pathogens.13 Through chemical-genetic and biochemical analyses, we found that NPD6433 inhibits fatty acid synthesis by covalently adducting Fas1, within the essential heterododecameric FAS complex. Our findings expand the current understanding of the role of fatty acid biosynthesis in supporting fungal growth and virulence21,49 and provide a tool with which to further investigate FAS as an antifungal target.

Fatty acids are the building blocks of complex lipids, including phospholipids and sphingolipids, which have diverse roles in energy storage, transcriptional regulation, cellular signaling, and post-translational modification of proteins.50 In the context of fungal pathogenesis, depletion of FAS1 or FAS2 using conditional-expression strains in C. albicans and C. neoformans has been shown to attenuate virulence in mouse models of systemic candidiasis and pulmonary cryptococcosis, respectively.23,51 In C. albicans, however, we did not find that modulation of FAS2 expression significantly altered sensitivity to NPD6433. Based on previous work in S. cerevisiae,52 the most likely explanation is that FAS2 expression is regulated in a Fas1-dependent manner to control the stoichiometry of the FAS complex. Results from Xu et al. with the known FAS inhibitor cerulenin suggest that a similar regulatory mechanism exists in C. albicans21, as heterozygous deletion of FAS1, but not FAS2, caused hypersensitivity to cerulenin, even though the compound directly targets Fas2.27 Such findings underscore a limitation of chemogenomic approaches in identifying drug targets that act as part of protein complexes due to the potential for confounding effects on regulation of subunit stoichiometry, complex assembly, or complex activation.

In C. neoformans, fatty acid-derived lipids are major constituents of key virulence factors. For example, 3-hydroxy fatty acids have been found within the polysaccharide capsule of C. neoformans,53,54 and large phospholipid-bound vesicles, called melanosomes are involved in the production and deposition of melanin in the C. neoformans cell wall.55 Whether the inhibition of capsule formation and melanization we report for NPD6433 represents a distinct biological property of NPD6433, or a manifestation of toxicity at concentrations nearing the compound’s MIC remains to be determined. We also found that pharmacological and genetic inhibition of FAS prevented the formation of biofilms by Candida species, consistent with literature that fatty acid biosynthesis genes are up-regulated during biofilm growth.56–58 Deletion of FAS2 in Candida parapsilosis, a frequent cause of nosocomial infections, has also been shown to prevent biofilm formation.59 Interestingly, despite the importance of morphogenesis for biofilm formation,60 NPD6433 did not block the ability of C. albicans to transition from yeast to hyphae. This supports growing evidence that filamentation and biofilm development are not inextricably linked.61–63 Whether a consequence of partially impairing fatty acid biosynthesis or a more generic manifestation of compromised fitness prior to death, our results indicate that targeting Fas1 modulates virulence traits and could have beneficial effects in vivo, even when complete target inhibition is not achieved.

Given the importance of fatty acid synthesis across all life forms, FAS has emerged as an important target for the development of compounds with a range of antimicrobial and antineo-plastic activities.64–66 In contrast to all previous agents, NPD6433 targets the ER domain of Fas1, the most structurally divergent domain of the FAS complex.24 In addition, this compound inhibits the catalytic activity of the domain in an FMN-sensitive manner, an important observation as this co-factor is not required for FAS activity in humans.24 Importantly, the antifungal activity of the compound was not rescued by flavins and fatty acids provided by 10% serum supplementation of culture medium. However, NPD6433 demonstrated a narrow window between concentrations required for antifungal activity and concentrations causing toxicity to cancer cells in culture. This may be attributable to disruption of other flavin-dependent enzymes within human cells67 or other non-flavin-related, off-target effects that remain to be defined. Nevertheless, findings confirm the potential of targeting fatty acid biosynthesis as a broad-spectrum antifungal strategy, and they provide a useful point of departure for developing more fungal-specific compounds in future work.

RIKEN NPDepo screening conditionsA ~20,000 compound subset of the Natural Products Depository (NPDepo) library from the RIKEN Institute in Wako, Japan was used to identify compounds with antifungal activity against four fungal pathogens: C. albicans (CaSS1), C. glabrata (BG2), C. auris (Ci 6684), and C. neoformans (H99). A BioTech ADS-384–8 compound dispenser was used to transfer 0.2 μL of 1 mg/mL compound stocks in DMSO (FUJIFILM, Wako) from each mother plate into four 384-well (Corning) daughter plates. The dispenser cartridges were washed with DMSO and dried between each mother plate. YPD medium was inoculated with ~1×103 cells/mL of a given species from saturated YPD overnight cultures. Then, 20 μL of this cell dilution was added per well to each plate using a Rainin Liquidator™ 96. Plates were incubated for 24 hours at 30°C for all Candida species and 48 h for C. neoformans. Plates were mixed using an Eppendorf MixMate™ (Fisher Scientific) for 15 s and optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was read (Molecular Devices SpectraMax M2) as a measure of cell growth. Percent growth of each species in a given compound was calculated relative to the average growth in the DMSO controls wells on each plate. Compounds demonstrating ≥50% growth inhibition were then cherry picked to produce a new mother plate, which was re-screened under the same conditions to validate bioactivity. Those compounds that showed reproducible activity against any pathogens were re-ordered from an external supplier and used for all subsequent analyses.

S. cerevisiae chemical-genetic data processingBarcode amplicon sequencing data was processed using the BEAN-counter ((barcoded experiment analysis from next-generation sequencing) ) software pipeline.16 The detailed scoring method can be found in the Supplementary Note of Piotrowski et al. 2017.15 For each mutant library, screens were performed in separate batches, and each batch was processed and analyzed independently using BEAN-counter. Chemical-genetic (CG) interaction z-scores were calculated as the standard deviation of each strain in the compound-treated profile with respect to its counterpart strain in the reference DMSO profile. Strains with less than 20 barcode read counts were excluded from the calculation. The log read count profile for each condition was LOWESS-normalized with respect to the reference DMSO profile. Because CG profiles do not follow a normal distribution, Tukey’s method18 was used to determine extreme outliers in a profile based on the interquartile range of the distribution of CG z-scores. Profiles at concentrations that showed the best separation between outlier chemical genetic interactions and the mean z-score for all interactions in the profile were selected as the representative profiles for each mutant pool.

If a specific UP-TAG or DOWN-TAG had more than one of its triplicate samples in the solvent control condition with read counts < 20% of the median read per million mapped, these reads were filtered and omitted from further analysis. Log2 fold differences for each strain’s UP-TAG and DOWN-TAG were calculated. Then in CellProfiler 3, Watershed was used to identify the outer borders of the envelope on micrographs. Masks of cells identified using the approach described above were imported in CellProfiler as objects. Imported masks were then used as primary objects to identify a secondary object around each using propagation to the outer borders of the envelope. MeasureObjectSizeShape was used to extract area and mean radius measurements for each type of object (primary = cells excluding envelope; secondary = cells including envelope). The area of the envelope expressed as percent of the total area was calculated as: (secondary_object_area − primary_object_area)/secondary_object_area and when the difference between the secondary and primary object was negative, the number was set to zero. The average percent total area was calculated for >100 cells and an unpaired Student’s t-test was used to determine significance between the treated and untreated sample, which was plotted in GraphPad Prism.

LC-MS detection of Fas1-derived peptide mass shiftThe FAS holocomplex was affinity purified from fungus grown in the presence of NPD6433, 1 or vehicle control, as described for the NADPH assay above. The eluted material was fractionated by SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad), and Coomassie-stained bands containing the co-migrating Fas1 and Fas2 proteins which make up the complex were excised. Gel bands were reduced and alkylated using DTT and iodoacetamide, digested with trypsin overnight, and tryptic peptides were analyzed by nano-LC-MS/MS. Samples were loaded at 400 nL/min and eluted at 200 nL/min, with peptides separated over a 60 min gradient (2% acetonitrile to 35% acetonitrile) using a 15 cm, 75 μm packed tip column with 3 μm C18 (Reprosil-Aq, Dr. Maisch) and an Eksigent 425 nanoLC. A Fusion Lumos Orbitrap (Thermo Fisher) measured peptides using a data dependent acquisition (DDA) program consisting of 1 MS scan followed by 3 s of MS/MS scans where peptides were dynamically excluded for 9 s after MS/MS. MS1 scans were performed at 120,000 resolution and MS/MS scans at 7500 resolution following HCD fragmentation. Data was analyzed by MSFragger Open Search80,81 using the FragPipe software package version 16.0 (Fragpipe.nesvilab.org). Default parameters for Open Search were used, including precursor mass tolerance −150–500 Da, fragment mass tolerance of 20 ppm, trypsin cleavage with 2 missed cut sites, fixed modification for cysteine (carbamidomethyl) and variable modification for methionine (oxidation). Data was searched against a C. albicans protein database from Uniprot with FragPipe contaminant protein entries included and reversed sequences used as decoys (Uniprot taxonomy 5476 reviewed sequences downloaded Oct 8, 2021; total of 2802 entries in the Fasta database). The Crystal-C and PTM Sheppard82 modules from FragPipe were used to filter results to a 1% FDR level, to identify peptide spectra from FAS1 and FAS2 proteins exhibiting a mass shift consistent with diazonium adduct formation. Candidate masses for adduct formation were determined using all plausible degradation products and plausible reactions of degradation products with amino acid residues.
