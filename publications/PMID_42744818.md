Alanyl-tRNA synthetase 1 (AARS1) was recently identified as a lactyltransferase
responsible for protein lactylation, a modification associated with epigenetic
regulation and metabolic adaptation. Here, we report compound XY353, which
covalently binds C184 of AARS1, inducing steric clashes with F175 and triggering
structural rearrangements in the region that displace W176, a key residue for
lactate binding. By means of a combination of a variety of biophysical and
enzymatic assays with MD simulations and structural analysis, we show that XY353
and its derivative XY353-1 inhibit AARS1 by competing with lactate via the
C184-F175-W176 relay, supporting a covalent allosteric mechanism of inhibition,
which is further confirmed by our cellular data that XY353-1 reduces lactylation
of the AARS1 substrate YAP and suppresses the proliferation of HGC-27 cells.
Collectively, these findings identify a covalent allosteric mechanism for AARS1
inhibition and provide chemical tools to explore its biological functions.

Introduction

Most cancer cells preferentially rely on aerobic glycolysis to generate lactate for energy production rather than utilizing mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, which is known as “Warburg effect”1. Recent evidence indicates that lactate can covalently modify lysine residues on proteins, regulating their functions and challenging the traditional view of lactate as merely a metabolic waste product2,3. AARS1 and AARS2 function as critical lactyltransferases that regulate diverse pathological landscapes through protein lactylation14,15. In tumorigenesis and malignant proliferation, AARS1-mediated lactylation of the YAP–TEAD complex, ASH2L, and p53 promotes gastric and hepatocellular carcinoma progression11,16,17. Collectively, these findings underscore AARS1/2 as pivotal therapeutic targets for modulating the lactylation landscape in diverse pathological conditions. Currently, only the lactate analogs β-alanine and L-alanine have been identified to bind to the substrate site and inhibit lactylation23. The limited availability of specific or efficient chemical inhibitors of AARS1/2 have hampered their chemical biology research.

In this work, we construct and synthesize a covalent library comprising 381 compounds containing either a chloroacetamide or acrylamide warhead for screening potential covalent ligands. Compound XY353 was identified as a binder to AARS1 by differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) and protein crystallography. Structural analysis reveals that XY353 covalently binds to C184 of AARS1, causing steric clashes with F175 and displacing W176, a critical residue for lactate binding. Competition assays further demonstrate that XY353 and its derivatives inhibit AARS1 activity by competing with lactate for binding. Pull-down and western blot assays confirm that XY353 derivative XY353-1 bind to AARS1 in cells and reduce global lactylation levels. Functionally, XY353-1 decreases lactylation of the AARS1 substrate YAP and suppresses the proliferation of HGC-27 cells. Together, these findings elucidate the covalent allosteric C184-F175-W176 relay pathway and provide chemical tools for targeting AARS1.

ResultsCovalent compound screening against AARS1Covalent drugs, which target specific protein residues (e.g., cysteine), often offer stronger binding affinity and higher selectivity compared to traditional non-covalent drugs, thereby attracting widespread attention24. Targeting cysteine residues has become a mainstream strategy in covalent drug discovery, and several cysteine-targeting drugs (such as afatinib and sotorasib) have already been clinically approved25. In this study, we aimed to apply this covalent strategy to identify covalent inhibitors of AARS1. AARS1 is a multi-domain protein comprising an N-terminal catalytic domain, an editing domain, and a C-terminal domain (Fig. 1a)10. The catalytic domain, responsible for the enzyme’s activity, contains five cysteine residues that may serve as potential covalent binding sites. To facilitate screening, we synthesized a library of 381 covalent compounds containing either a chloroacetamide or an acrylamide warhead, both of which are commonly used in covalent drug design (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data 1)26. Covalent compounds inhibit AARS1 via the C184–F175–W176 allosteric relay pathway

AARS1 catalyzes the reaction between lactate and ATP to generate lactatyl-AMP and PPi (Fig. 4a). Given the structural similarity between glycine and lactate (Fig. 4b), and previous reports identifying 5’-O-(N-(glycyl)sulfamoyl)adenosine (Gly-SA) as a substrate analog of alanyl-tRNA synthetase in E. coli27, we employed Gly-SA as an analog of lactatyl-AMP to investigate the inhibitory mechanism of XY353 against AARS1. Co-crystallization of Gly-SA with AARS1 yielded a structure in which the adenine and furanose rings of Gly-SA closely overlapped with those of AMP-PCP (root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) = 0.36 Å), an ATP analog, bound to an E. coli alanyl-tRNA synthetase (PDB ID: 3HXY), indicating that Gly-SA occupied the substrate-binding site of AARS1 (Supplementary Fig. 4). Further structural analysis revealed that W176, which is adjacent to C184, engaged in hydrophobic interactions with the glycine moiety of Gly-SA (Fig. 4c). W176 has been previously identified as a critical residue for the binding of both lactate and alanine, and its mutation to alanine (W176A) disrupts AARS1 activity13. We then compared the structure of AARS1–Gly-SA with that of AARS1–XY353, and found that XY353 would clash with F175 of AARS1 in the AARS1–Gly-SA structure, which might displace W176 and inhibited substrate binding (Fig. 4d, e). Remarkably, in the AARS1–XY353 structure, the residues in the D173–D180 loop including F175 and W176 were disordered (Fig. 4e). This disorder exposes C184 and creates a new pocket that is occluded in the Gly-SA-bound structure, suggesting an allosteric mechanism of inhibition (Fig. 4f). We also compared our structures with the complex structure of AARS1 with 5’-O-(N-(alanyl)sulfamoyl)adenosine (Ala-SA)10. Consistent with the Gly-SA-bound structure, W176 forms hydrophobic interactions with the alanyl moiety (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Structural alignment further reveals a similar steric clash between XY353 and F175 of the Ala-SA-bound AARS1, reinforcing the proposed allosteric inhibition mechanism (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Collectively, these structural and comparative analyses support a model in which covalent modification of C184 by XY353 induces local conformational disorder in the D173–D180 region (including F175 and W176), thereby disrupting substrate recognition and leading to allosteric inhibition of AARS1.

To further investigate the inhibition mechanism, we crystallized the AARS1–XY353–Gly-SA ternary complex (Fig. 5a). Notably, residues D173–D180, which were unresolved in the AARS1–XY353 structure, were clearly visible in the ternary complex, similar to that in the AARS1–Gly-SA structure (Supplementary Fig. 6), suggesting that Gly-SA stabilized this region. However, the electron density for the side chain of F175 remained missing, indicating that XY353 disrupted the stabilization of this key residue (Fig. 5b). The B-factor (temperature factor) in crystallography describes atomic displacement or thermal motion from its average position, offering a measure of protein flexibility and stability. Accordingly, we examined the B-factor profiles of the AARS1–XY353–Gly-SA and AARS1–Gly-SA structures to evaluate local conformational dynamics. Overall, the average B-factors of the two complexes were comparable (~24 Å²), but substantial differences were observed in residues N170–T181, a region essential for lactate binding (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Fig. 5c, d). Within this segment, the average B-factor of AARS1–XY353–Gly-SA (38.4 Å²) was notably higher than that of AARS1–Gly-SA (28.2 Å²) (Fig. 5e). In particular, the B-factor of F175 increased from 21.9 to 34.5 Å², and that of W176 increases from 21.0 to 27.2 Å² (Fig. 5e). These results suggested that XY353 binding increased the flexibility of residues N170–T181, especially F175 and W176, thereby perturbing lactate binding. Although XY353-1 inhibited global lactylation, it remains unclear whether this compound affects the lactylation of the physiological substrates of AARS1 and their associated functions. Yes-associated protein (YAP), one of the earliest identified lactylation substrates of AARS1, has been shown to promote gastric cancer cell proliferation upon lactylation16. Therefore, we investigated whether XY353-1 affects YAP lactylation and its downstream functions. First, we examined whether XY353-1 inhibits YAP lactylation at the in vitro enzymatic level. Enzymatic assays demonstrated that incubation of AARS1 with 10–20 μM XY353-1 effectively reduced the lactylation level of the YAP protein (Fig. 8c). Mass spectrometry analysis further confirmed that XY353-1 inhibited lactylation of the YAP K90 peptide (Fig. 8d). After confirming that XY353-1 inhibits YAP lactylation in vitro, we next validated this effect at the cellular level. Western blot analysis showed that 10–40 μM XY353-1 significantly decreased YAP K90 lactylation in both HEK293FT and HGC-27 cells (Fig. 8e, f). Notably, in HEK293FT cells co-overexpressing YAP and AARS1, treatment with 20 and 40 μM XY353-1 also reduced YAP K90 lactylation (Fig. 8g). In contrast, treatment with XY353-1 at 10 or 20 μM had no detectable effect on the lactylation of the YAP K90R mutant (Supplementary Fig. 19). We aligned sequences flanking the modified lysine (K) residues across known AARS1 substrates and found they are not conserved, indicating that AARS1 does not recognize a specific linear motif for protein lactylation (Supplementary Fig. 20). Further functional analyses revealed that XY353-1 decreased CTGF mRNA levels, suppressed TEAD transcriptional activity, and inhibited the proliferation of HGC-27 cells (Fig. 8h–j). Consistently, proliferation assays in gastric cancer tumor organoids demonstrated that XY353-1 suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 8k). Collectively, these findings indicate that XY353-1 inhibits YAP lactylation at the cellular level and further confirm its inhibitory effect on AARS1.

Primary evaluation of the selectivity of XY353-1We next assessed the selectivity of XY353-1 against functionally related enzymes, as well as its proteome-wide selectivity. Specifically, its inhibitory activity was evaluated toward AARS2, a homologous enzyme belonging to the same family as AARS1, as well as the lactylation-associated enzyme LDHA and the cysteine-dependent enzymes YOD1 and OTUD328. XY353-1 exhibited an IC50 value of 9.9 μM against AARS2, corresponding to approximately 9-fold selectivity over AARS1 (IC50 = 1.1 μM) (Supplementary Fig. 21a). In enzymatic assays, XY353-1 did not significantly affect the activity of LDHA, YOD1, or OTUD3 within the concentration range of 5–20 μM (Supplementary Fig. 21b–d). In addition, as KAT2A is also a lactylation-associated enzyme, we further evaluated its interaction with XY353-2 using binding assays7. The results showed that XY353-2 did not exhibit apparently binding to KAT2A compared with AARS1 (Supplementary Fig. 21e, f), further supporting the selectivity of this compound series. Additionally, we performed a preliminary evaluation of the proteome-wide selectivity of XY353-1 using competitive cysteine-reactivity profiling in HEK293T cell lysates with an IA-alkyne probe, which was subsequently conjugated to a rhodamine fluorophore for SDS–PAGE-based detection. XY353-1 labeled 17.8–21.7% of cysteine-containing proteins across 10–40 μM, substantially below the 44.9% labeling achieved by the non-selective cysteine-reactive probe IA at 40 μM (Supplementary Fig. 22). These findings suggest that XY353-1 possesses some degree of selectivity in cell lysates and provide additional confidence that the observed cellular effects of XY353 derivatives are primarily driven by engagement of AARS1.We then investigated the effect of XY353-1 on the canonical activity of AARS1 (alanyl-tRNA synthetase) and its impact on protein synthesis. Notably, XY353-1 did not significantly affect AARS1 activity when alanine was used as the substrate at concentrations of 5–20 μM, while partial inhibition (33.5%) was observed at 40 μM (Supplementary Fig. 23a). Consistently, XY353-1 did not inhibit protein synthesis at 10 μM, whereas inhibition was observed at 20 and 40 μM (Supplementary Fig. 23b). Importantly, XY353-1 reduced global lactylation with an IC50 of 12.9 μM, whereas inhibition of protein synthesis occurred at a higher IC50 of 34.7 μM in U2OS cells (Supplementary Fig. 24), further demonstrating its functional selectivity. These results indicate that XY353-1 can inhibit the canonical alanyl-tRNA synthetase activity of AARS1, but requires higher concentrations compared to its inhibition of lactyltransferase activity. Mechanistically, XY353-1 covalently modifies C184, inducing disorder in the D173–D180 region. This shift affects residue W176, which is critical for substrate recognition. While this structural perturbation likely interferes with both lactate and alanine binding, alanine (Kd = 1.9 μM) possesses a significantly higher affinity for AARS1 than lactate (Kd = 14 μM)13. Consequently, this induced conformational disorder preferentially impairs the lower-affinity lactate binding, rendering lactyltransferase activity more susceptible to inhibition than canonical alanyl-tRNA synthetase.

DiscussionAARS1 has recently been identified as a lactyltransferase11,13,16; however, to the best of our knowledge, no specific inhibitor for inhibiting this enzyme has been reported to date. Given the emerging significance of protein lactylation in metabolic reprogramming and disease progression23,29, the development of potent AARS1 inhibitors is highly warranted. In this study, we employed a covalent screening strategy combined with protein crystallography to identify compound XY353, which covalently binds to C184 of AARS1. LC-MS/MS and enzymatic assays demonstrated that XY353 modifies C184 to inhibit AARS1 activity, with an IC50 value of 5.3 μM. Structural analysis, XL-MS, and MD revealed that C184 is located near the lactate-binding site and that its modification by XY353 induces steric clashes with F175, triggering conformational rearrangements in surrounding residues and altering the W176 conformation essential for lactate binding. ITC, SPR, FP, lead optimization, and enzymatic assays further demonstrated that XY353 and its derivatives inhibit AARS1 through the C184–F175–W176 allosteric relay pathway by competing with lactate for binding. Cellular experiments also demonstrated that XY353-1 inhibits AARS1, thereby reducing lactylation of its substrate YAP and suppressing the proliferation of HGC-27 cells. This observation aligns with recent studies highlighting how aberrant lactylation of the Hippo-YAP pathway drives oncogenic signaling16. Preliminary compound optimization improved potency (e.g., XY353-1, IC50 = 1.1 μM), but the inhibitory activity of the current compounds remains moderate, and effective cellular activity requires relatively high concentrations (20–40 μM). Moreover, potential off-target reactivity cannot be fully excluded, warranting further investigation. Consequently, additional optimization is necessary to enhance potency, selectivity, and cellular efficacy, as well as to assess pharmacological properties. At this stage, XY353 and its derivatives are best regarded as mechanistic chemical probes rather than fully optimized lead compounds. Based on structural and biochemical evidence, we propose a C184–F175–W176 allosteric relay pathway, which may guide the development of more potent AARS1 inhibitors. Given the critical role of W176 in lactate binding, this allosteric mechanism may also offer insights into the catalytic process of AARS1. Overall, the discovery of XY353 and its derivatives, together with the proposed C184–F175–W176 allosteric relay pathway, provides valuable chemical tools and mechanistic insights for advancing research on AARS1-mediated protein lactylation.
