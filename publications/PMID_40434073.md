SUMMARY

The bacterial cytoplasmic membrane, consisting of roughly equal proportions of proteins and lipids, plays a crucial role in cellular growth, metabolism, and maintaining the cytoplasmic boundary. It is a dynamic, fluid matrix that separates intracellular compartments, where lipids and proteins coexist in a highly organized yet flexible arrangement. Membrane fluidity, defined as the inverse of viscosity, determines how rapidly molecules diffuse within the membrane at a given temperature. This property is vital for protein mobility and biomolecular interactions. Structurally, the membrane primarily comprises a lamellar lipid bilayer, with glycerophospholipids and fatty acids forming its core framework. In Bacillus subtilis , a key model organism for studying gram-positive bacterial physiology, major membrane lipids include phospholipids, glycolipids, and lipoteichoic acids, the latter anchored to diacylglycerol glycolipids. This review examines the synthesis and regulation of membrane lipids in B. subtilis , with a focus on fatty acid biosynthesis, its diversification, and post-synthetic modifications such as desaturation. It also explores the production of phosphatidic acid and the integration of fatty acid and phospholipid biosynthesis. We review the well-characterized pathway of cold-induced membrane lipid modification in B. subtilis , arguably the best-studied model system for temperature sensing. This pathway is tightly linked to transcriptional responses triggered by changes in bilayer viscosity, detected by a membrane-associated thermosensor. Finally, this review highlights the importance of fatty acid biosynthesis in B. subtilis differentiation and its contributions to the production of biotin and lipoic acid, two universal cofactors essential for fatty acid synthesis and intermediary metabolism.

KEYWORDS: fatty acids, phospholipids, desaturase, membrane fluidity, FapR, sporulation, biotin, lipoic acid

INTRODUCTION

The subject of Type II fatty acid synthase (FASII) in bacteria has been extensively examined in outstanding previous reviews ( 1 – 4 ). Here, we will briefly address the topic and focus on the aspects that differentiate this process in B. subtilis from the well-studied E. coli model.

The FASII initiation module

The initiation module of the bacterial fatty acid synthesis pathway (FASII) is responsible for generating both the primer and the essential building blocks required by the elongation module ( Fig. 1 ). The first committed step in bacterial phospholipid synthesis is catalyzed by acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), which converts acetyl-CoA into malonyl-CoA via carboxylation ( Fig. 2 ; for a recent review, see 5 ). To become a suitable substrate for FASII enzymes, the malonate group from malonyl-CoA must be transferred to the acyl carrier protein (ACP) by the enzyme FabD ( 3 , 6 , 7 ). The elongation cycle is then initiated when FabH condenses malonyl-ACP with a short-chain acyl-CoA (typically C2–C5) ( 8 ). The malonyl-ACP produced by ACC and FabD also serves as the primary substrate for chain extension during fatty acid elongation, emphasizing the central role of ACC in maintaining optimal rates of membrane phospholipid synthesis ( Fig. 1 ). Each condensation reaction catalyzed by FabH results in a new fatty acid molecule, contributing to membrane biogenesis. As such, the initiation module serves as a critical control point for both the quantity and rate of fatty acid production.

Acetyl-CoA carboxylase

In bacteria, acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) is a multi-subunit enzyme complex that relies on biotin, a sulfur-containing cofactor (see below). It comprises three primary components: biotin carboxylase (AccC), biotin carboxyl carrier protein (AccB, also known as BCCP), and carboxyltransferase (AccAD). Although AccC and AccB form homodimers, AccAD functions as a heterotetramer ( 5 , 9 ). In vivo , ACC likely exists as a multimeric complex of all four subunits, but cell disruption leads to dissociation, allowing the two half-reactions to be studied independently. The first half-reaction involves the ATP-dependent carboxylation of biotin on AccB by AccC ( Fig. 2 ). This reaction proceeds efficiently only when biotin is covalently linked to a lysine residue on AccB ( 10 ). In the second half-reaction, AccAD transfers the carboxyl group from carboxylated AccB to acetyl-CoA. Although each half-reaction can be assayed separately, reconstituting the full ACC activity in vitro is more complex ( 11 ), requiring high concentrations of each purified subunit to reach the threshold necessary for catalytic activity ( 12 , 13 ). The genetic organization of acc genes varies across species. In E. coli , B. subtilis , and Staphylococcus aureus , the accAD and accBC genes are organized into separate operons located at distinct chromosomal sites ( 4 , 14 , 15 ). In contrast, in Streptococcus pneumoniae , all four acc genes are clustered within a single transcriptional unit alongside other FASII genes ( 16 ).

Evidence supporting the role of ACC in regulating fatty acid production comes from a study investigating the overexpression of accABCD in E. coli ( 17 ). Maintaining a balanced lipid-to-protein ratio is essential for cellular homeostasis. To achieve this, the authors developed a method to decouple FASII from phospholipid synthesis, allowing excess fatty acids to be excreted. Overexpression of the acc genes, together with a soluble acyl-ACP thioesterase that uncoupled FASII from membrane synthesis, resulted in a 5-fold to 6-fold increase in fatty acid production ( 17 ). As discussed later, the upregulation of ACC in B. subtilis also enhances malonyl-CoA synthesis and fatty acid overproduction, mediated by the transcription factor FapR, which regulates fatty acid and phospholipid metabolism ( 18 , 19 ). These findings reveal distinct regulatory mechanisms for malonyl-CoA-driven fatty acid synthesis in E. coli and B. subtilis . In E. coli , ACC overproduction necessitates the presence of a metabolic sink to accommodate increased fatty acid synthesis ( 17 ). Conversely, B. subtilis integrates ACC overproduction with a coordinated upregulation of genes involved in lipid synthesis ( 18 , 19 ).

It has been reported that in B. subtilis , ACC is partially regulated by YqhY, a conserved DUF322/Asp23 protein that is highly expressed and often encoded together with ACC subunits as part of an accB-accC-yqhY operon ( 20 ). In S. aureus , Asp23 is a membrane-associated protein originally associated with alkaline shock ( 21 ). Loss of Asp23/YqhY leads to cell wall stress and poor growth ( 20 , 22 ). In B. subtilis, yqhY null mutants acquire suppressor mutations in the accDA and accB genes, enhancing the growth of the yqhY mutants. However, this selective pressure is relieved in a medium supplemented with acetate ( 20 ). Based on these results, it has been suggested that YqhY regulates the activity of ACC, leading to the inhibition of ACC activity ( 20 ). Nevertheless, there is a lack of biochemical experiments to substantiate this hypothesis.

Diversification of the pathway: role of BkdH and FabH in determining fatty acid structure

Many gram-positive bacteria, such as B. subtilis ( 6 , 7 ) and S. aureus ( 23 ), produce predominantly odd-numbered branched-chain fatty acids (BCFAs). At 37°C, B. subtilis membranes contain around 83% of BCFAs, both saturated and unsaturated, being odd-numbered 76% of them ( 24 ). The branched/straight and even/odd characteristics are determined by the substrate specificity of FabH, the initiation condensing enzyme, that condenses a branched-chain acyl-CoA with malonyl-ACP ( 25 , 26 ). Extensive studies, mostly with B. subtilis , have been done to elucidate that the “primer” carbons for the synthesis of BCFAs are α-keto acids derived from the branched-chain amino acids valine, leucine, and isoleucine ( 27 , 28 ). Although isoleucine is the precursor of anteiso-BCFAs (a-BCFAs), leucine and valine give rise to the primers for iso-BCFAs (i-BCFAs) ( Fig. 3A ). A soluble branched-chain amino acid transferase (BCAT) converts these amino acids to their corresponding α-ketoacids ( 29 ). Then, these intermediates are converted to acyl-CoAs by branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase (BKDH), a lipoate-dependent enzyme ( 30 ). In B. subtilis , these two groups of enzymes (BCAT and BKDH) are encoded by the bkd operon, consisting of seven genes responsible for the synthesis of BCFAs through the utilization of the respective amino acids ( 31 ). These genes include ptb , bcd , buk , lpd , bkdA1 , bkdA2 , and bkdB , encoding phosphate butyryl coenzyme A transferase, valine, isoleucine and L-leucine dehydrogenase, butyrate kinase, and four components of the BKDH complex: E3 (dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase), E1α (dehydrogenase), E1β (decarboxylase), and E2 (dihydrolipoamide acyltransferase), respectively ( 31 ) ( Fig. 3B ).

Fig 3.

After transamination by BCAT and oxidative decarboxylation by BKDH, Val gives rise to isobutyryl CoA (iC4-CoA), Leu is converted to isovaleryl-CoA (i5-CoA), and Ile becomes 2-methyl-butyryl-CoA (aC5-CoA) ( Fig. 3A ). Although fatty acid synthesis initiation with an amino acid-derived acyl-CoA results in BCFA synthesis, initiation with acetyl-CoA gives rise to straight-chain fatty acids ( 25 ). The absence of BKDH activity in B. subtilis , whether due to deletion of the entire bkd operon ( 34 ), disruption of the bkdB gene encoding the E2 subunit (Mansilla, unpublished), or impairment of E2 post-translational modification with lipoic acid (LA) ( 35 ), can be rescued by supplementation with extracellular short-chain fatty acids, such as 2-methylbutyric or isobutyric acid. These experiments suggest the presence of an activation pathway to form short-chain acyl-CoAs. It has been reported that L. monocytogenes has a butyrate kinase/phosphotransbutyrylase (Buk/Ptb) that is responsible for the two-step activation of short-chain carboxylic acids ( 32 , 33 ). Since the genes encoding Buk and Ptb are located adjacent to the bkd operon in Listeria and Bacilli, it is possible that this alternative pathway may also be functional in B. subtilis ( Fig. 3 ).

In S. aureus , BCFAs are also the most abundant fatty acids in membrane phospholipids (around 70%, 36 ), and bkd strains cannot grow in a defined medium unless it is supplemented with short carboxylic acids. However, in this bacterium, the genes coding for Buk/Ptb are not present. Recent findings showed that a short-chain acyl-CoA synthetase, named MbcS, bypasses S. aureus BKDH deficiency when 2-methylbutyric and isobutyric acids are present in the environment ( 23 , 37 ). This enzyme converts these short carboxylic acids to their respective CoA thioesters, directly providing substrates for FabH to initiate fatty acid biosynthesis.

In summary, the alternative pathways for synthesizing BCFAs in some gram-positive bacteria do not seem to involve biosynthetic enzymes that bypass BKDH. Rather, they appear to function as C5/iC4-CoA synthetases, scavenging exogenous short-chain acids from the environment for use in BCFA synthesis.

B. subtilis possesses two FabH isoenzymes, FabHA and FabHB, both of which preferentially utilize branched-chain acyl-CoA primers ( 25 ). Additionally, although the E. coli FabH is incapable of using primers with a chain length greater than four carbons, the B. subtilis FabHA and FabHB enzymes are active with straight-chain 4-, 5-, and 6-carbon primers ( 25 ). A fabHA deletion strain shows an increased abundance of straight-chain fatty acids and a decrease in i-BCFAs compared with the parent B. subtilis strain ( 38 ). Conversely, a fabHB deletion strain exhibits the opposite pattern, with a slight reduction in straight-chain fatty acids and an increase in i-BCFAs relative to the parent strain ( 38 ). The substrate-free structures of FabHA and FabHB show that both enzymes have similar residues that line the active site, but they differ in the architecture surrounding the catalytic residue ( 39 ). Branching in the FabHB active site may better accommodate the structure of an iso-branched acyl-CoA molecule and thus confer superior utilization than FabHA for this primer site. In contrast to B. subtilis , which synthesizes two FabH enzymes, other organisms with type II FAS possess only one. For instance, in S. aureus , there is a single FabH that utilizes the short-chain acyl-CoA primer pool to initiate FASII ( 4 ). The selectivity of S. aureus FabH (SaFabH) for branched-chain acyl-CoAs is attributed to structural differences in the hydrophobic pocket adjacent to the active site, accommodating the acyl-enzyme intermediate. The organization of the residues in this pocket determines which acyl-CoA primers can be accommodated by the enzyme ( 4 , 26 ). In the E. coli FabH structure, the binding pocket is only large enough to fit acetyl or propionyl-CoA ( 26 , 40 ). Although the sequence of the residues throughout the tunnel is almost identical, in S. aureus FabH, the side chains of the residues are oriented to create space for bulkier branched-chain acyl-CoAs. The shape of this pocket is considered a major determinant of the substrate specificity of FabHs ( 4 ). Although SaFabH prefers aC5-CoA, the fatty acid composition in S. aureus is predominantly determined by the composition of primers available to FabH, as determined by metabolomics measurements ( 41 ).

The elongation module

The elongation module forms the core of the bacterial FASII pathway, consisting of four enzymatic steps that act sequentially to extend acyl-ACP molecules by two carbon units with each cycle, ultimately producing long-chain acyl-ACPs ( Fig. 1 ). This module begins with the product of the FabH-catalyzed condensation, which is reduced by the NADPH-dependent β-ketoacyl-ACP reductase (FabG) to yield β-hydroxyacyl-ACP. The next step involves the dehydration of this intermediate to form trans-2-enoyl-ACP, a reaction catalyzed by FabZ in gram-positive bacteria that primarily synthesize saturated fatty acids (SFAs) ( 4 ). Because the dehydration step is reversible, the elongation cycle is driven forward by the enoyl-ACP reductase, typically FabI in many bacterial species. In E. coli , FabI preferentially uses NADH, whereas in S. aureus , it favors NADPH. Interestingly, B. subtilis encodes two enoyl-ACP reductases: FabI and FabL ( 4 , 42 ). Although neither enzyme is individually essential, the inability to generate a double knockout suggests that FabI and FabL can compensate for each other and perform redundant functions within the FASII pathway ( 42 ). The growing acyl chain is extended by β-ketoacyl synthase I or II (FabB or FabF), which condenses malonyl-ACP with the acyl-ACP to form a new β-ketoacyl-ACP that is two carbons longer ( 43 , 44 ; for a review, see 2). Although E. coli produces both types of condensing enzymes, B. subtilis relies solely on FabF to catalyze the elongation steps in fatty acid synthesis ( 7 , 45 , 46 ). Targeting this key reaction in the FASII pathway has been a focus of therapeutic research for conditions such as obesity and diabetes ( 47 ), cancer ( 48 ), and bacterial infections ( 4 ). Several inhibitors have been discovered that specifically block the bacterial condensing reaction, leading to the development of antibiotic compounds like thiolactomycin ( 49 ), platensimycin ( 50 ), and platencin ( 51 ). Cerulenin was the first known inhibitor of FabB/FabF ( 52 ). It acts by undergoing epoxide ring opening catalyzed by the condensing enzyme, resulting in a covalent bond with the active site cysteine residue. Cerulenin’s 12-carbon acyl chain fits into the enzyme’s hydrophobic channel, which normally accommodates the hydrocarbon chain of the acyl-enzyme intermediate ( 53 ). Structural studies of B. subtilis FabF, both in its native form and bound to cerulenin, along with analysis of the antibiotic-resistant FabF[I108F] mutant, have shed light on the enzyme’s catalytic mechanism and how resistance to cerulenin arises ( 46 ). Inhibition of FASII in B. subtilis with cerulenin was also a remarkable experimental device to study the regulation of lipid synthesis ( 18 , 45 , 54 ), the L form proliferation ( 55 ), the coupling of fatty acid to phospholipid synthesis ( 56 ), the cell envelope synthesis ( 57 , 58 ), and the role of lipids in differentiation ( 59 , 60 ).

Interestingly, an extracytoplasmic σ factor (σW) within the B. subtilis fabHA-fabF operon is activated by cell envelope active compounds, including detergents ( 38 ). Activation leads to a decrease in the amount of FabHA coupled with an increase in FabF, respectively. Downregulation of FabHA results in an increased reliance on the FabHB paralog, leading to a greater proportion of straight-chain fatty acids in the membrane, and the upregulation of FabF increases the average fatty acid chain length. The combined effect is a membrane with longer acyl chains and fewer BCFAs. This increased membrane rigidity serves to protect cells against detergents and antimicrobial peptides ( 38 ).

Several intermediates generated in the elongation module are also required for other essential cellular processes. For instance, LA is synthesized from octanoyl-ACP produced by the elongation cycle ( 61 ) (see below) and is essential for BCFA synthesis. Another essential enzyme cofactor derived from FASII is biotin, synthesized from pimeloyl-ACP ( 62 ). The importance of biotin as a cofactor for ACC has been discussed above.

Introduction of the double bond by the oxygen-dependent desaturation pathway

Konrad Bloch, a Nobel Laureate chemist, and his coworkers discovered that unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) are synthesized through two distinct pathways. The first occurs post-biosynthetically in aerobic organisms and requires molecular oxygen ( Fig. 4 ). The second pathway, used under anaerobic conditions, takes place during the fatty acid synthesis cycle at the stage of a ten-carbon intermediate ( 63 ). The anaerobic pathway of UFA biosynthesis was extensively studied in the E. coli model system and has been covered in several comprehensive reviews ( 1 , 2 , 64 ). Here, we briefly review the aerobic pathway of UFA synthesis, which functions in all eukaryotic forms of life and certain groups of bacteria ( 64 – 66 ), such as Bacilli , Pseudomonads, and Cyanobacteria . In this pathway, the double bond is introduced postbiosynthetically into SFAs by desaturase enzymes ( 24 ) ( Fig. 4 ).

Bacillus desaturase

Desaturase enzymes catalyze dehydrogenation reactions that introduce double bonds into fatty acids. This process begins with the energetically demanding abstraction of a hydrogen atom from a methylene group ( Fig. 4 ) ( 67 ). To facilitate this reaction, desaturases utilize a diiron cluster at their active site to recruit and activate molecular oxygen ( 64 , 67 , 68 ). Membrane-bound desaturases are broadly distributed across different organisms and belong to a large, functionally diverse enzyme family that includes alkane hydroxylases, xylene monooxygenases, carotene ketolases, sterol, and sphingolipid hydroxylases ( 69 ), as well as ornithine lipid hydroxylases ( 70 ). Based on their substrate preferences, desaturases are categorized into two main classes: acyl-CoA desaturases and acyl-lipid desaturases ( 69 ). These integral membrane proteins share a conserved active site architecture, typically featuring a tripartite motif composed of eight histidine residues arranged as HX (3, 4)H, HX (2, 3)HH, and HX (2, 3)HH. These motifs occupy conserved positions in the amino acid sequence. Site-directed mutagenesis studies have shown that substituting any of these conserved histidines with other amino acids results in a complete loss of enzymatic activity—likely due to the disrupted ability to coordinate iron at the active site ( 71 ).

In B. subtilis , the Δ5 desaturase (Des) introduces a cis-double bond at the Δ5 position of a broad range of SFAs ( 24 , Fig. 4 ). Because of its key role in remodeling membrane lipids in response to environmental temperature (see below), this membrane-bound fatty acid desaturase was extensively characterized (for reviews, see 65 , 66 ). Studies using systematic mutagenesis of conserved residues have clearly demonstrated that the His clusters are essential for Des activity ( 68 ). Furthermore, genetic analysis and in vivo assays established that Des is an acyl-lipid desaturase, specific to phospholipid substrates ( 72 ), utilizing both ferredoxins and flavodoxins as electron donors for the desaturation reaction ( 73 ). More recently, a domain swapping strategy using Δ5 desaturases from several Bacilli revealed that residues of the N-terminus, including the two first transmembrane segments region, play a crucial role in the activity of this class of enzymes ( 74 ). These findings are significant as the structure-function relationship of membrane-bound lipid desaturases remains poorly understood.

PHOSPHATIDIC ACID SYNTHESIS

In all organisms, phospholipid biosynthesis begins with two steps in which glycerol 3-phosphate (G3P) is acylated to form 1,2-diacylglycerol-3P (phosphatidic acid, PA) ( 2 ). This reaction is catalyzed by G3P-acyltransferases, which transfer the acyl chain from acyl-ACPS, formed by the elongation cycle, to either the sn-1 and sn-2 carbons of G3P ( 75 , Fig. 1 ). It has been accepted for many years that the acyl donors for PA synthesis are fatty acyl chain intermediates bound through a thioester linkage to the sulfhydryl group present in coenzyme A (acyl-CoAs) or in the prosthetic group of acyl carrier protein (acyl-ACPs). In E. coli , for which genetic approaches in concert with biochemical studies have resulted in a detailed understanding of phospholipid synthesis, the acylation of the 1-position of G3P is catalyzed by the protein PlsB. The synthesis of PA is then completed by PlsC, by transferring a fatty acid to the 2-position of acyl-G3P ( 2 ). Although understanding the E. coli phospholipid synthetic pathway has been useful in the study of other organisms, this falls short of being a generally applicable paradigm. In fact, analysis of multiple genomes indicates that most bacteria lack a homolog of the plsB gene, strongly suggesting that an alternate biochemical step exists for the attachment of the first fatty acid.

For more than 20 years, the role of PlsX in E. coli remained elusive. The story began with the isolation of E. coli G3P auxotrophs with defective PlsB acyltransferase activity by Bell’s group. These mutants showed an increased Michaelis constant (Km) for G3P in in vitro acyltransferase assays ( 76 ), caused by a single missense mutation in the open reading frame. The resulting plsB mutants required an artificially high intracellular concentration of G3P for activity. However, the interpretation of the phenotype of these mutants was complicated by the discovery that the growth defect of plsB strains required a second mutation in the unlinked plsX gene. Complementation of plsB plsX strains with the wild-type version of either gene circumvented the dependence on G3P supplementation ( 76 ). Despite extensive studies, the role of PlsX in phospholipid biosynthesis remained an enigma until Lu and coworkers ( 77 ) were able to predict its role in the formation of acyl-phosphate from acyl-ACP. A biochemical characterization of PlsX and the structural identification of the reaction products confirmed the proposed activity of the enzyme as a phosphate:acyl-ACP acyltransferase. However, how was this new lipid intermediate incorporated into G3P? In vitro assays proved that membranes of S. pneumoniae were proficient in the use of acyl-PO 4 , but not acyl-CoAs or acyl-ACPs, as acyl donors for G3P acylation. Bioinformatic analysis pointed to PlsY, a putative membrane protein, as the enzyme responsible for the incorporation of acyl-PO 4 into G3P ( Fig. 1 ). The activity of PlsY as the acyl-PO 4 -G3P acyltransferase partner of PlsX was confirmed in vivo and in vitro ( 77 , 78 ; Fig. 1 ). plsX and plsY are present in most of the bacterial genomes sequenced so far, and in most bacteria, including the major gram-positive pathogens, the PlsX-PlsY pathway seems to be the sole pathway involved in the first acylation of G3P. In contrast, PlsB is much less ubiquitous: homologs of the enzyme encoded by the E. coli plsB gene are mostly restricted to the γ-proteobacteria, providing these organisms with two alternative pathways for the initiation of phospholipids biosynthesis. In all cases, the final step is completed by the universally expressed PlsC ( Fig. 1 ). The picture that emerges from these studies is that bacteria lacking PlsB homologs should require the PlsX and PlsY enzymes for cell growth ( 78 ).

Control of membrane lipid biosynthesis in B. subtilis by the FapR transcriptional repressor

The fatty acid biosynthetic pathway is essential and energetically expensive, prompting bacteria to develop various homeostatic mechanisms to maintain optimal lipid levels. In other words, these cells are never overly fat or thin ( 79 ). This tight regulation is achieved through a diverse range of transcriptional regulators working in concert with biochemical processes ( 4 ). The first global transcriptional regulator of lipid synthesis discovered in bacteria was the FapR repressor of B. subtilis ( 18 ). FapR is largely conserved in gram-positive bacteria, including Staphylococci, Clostridia, and Listeria. Unlike several specific gene regulators identified in bacteria ( 4 ), FapR regulates both FASII and genes involved in phospholipid synthesis. The fap regulon of B. subtilis consists of the genes encoding the condensing enzymes ( fabHAB and fabF ), fabI , fabD , plsX , and plsC ( Fig. 1 ) and a non-characterized membrane protein ( yhfC ) ( 18 ). A central question in the regulation of the fap regulon by FapR was how the status of fatty acid synthesis controls the activity of the repressor. The first clue to answering this question came from the discovery that decreasing levels of malonyl-CoA, the building block for fatty acid synthesis, inhibited the expression of the fapR regulon, suggesting that this molecule might be the regulatory ligand ( 18 ). This possibility was directly confirmed by in vitro transcription experiments using several promoters of the fap regulon, which showed that FapR is unable to repress transcription in the presence of malonyl-CoA ( 19 ). The same direct and specific role of malonyl-CoA as the effector molecule was shown for FapR of S. aureus ( 80 ).

The first molecular insights into how FapR activity is regulated came from crystallographic studies of the B. subtilis repressor ( 19 ). These revealed that the effector-binding domain of FapR forms a homodimer with a thioesterase-like “hot-dog” fold—structurally similar to that of other thioesterases that act on acyl-CoA substrates ( 19 ). Upon binding malonyl-CoA, the protein undergoes a disorder-to-order transition, converting an open ligand-binding groove into a narrow tunnel that accommodates the effector molecule ( 19 ). Further mechanistic understanding of FapR function was gained from structural studies of the full-length repressor from S. aureus (SaFapR). Crystal structures were obtained for both the unbound form (apo-SaFapR) and SaFapR in complex with its operator DNA and the effector malonyl-CoA ( 80 ) ( Fig. 5A ). These structures revealed that the operator binds two SaFapR homodimers with different affinities, with helix-turn-helix motifs making specific contacts within both the major and minor grooves of the DNA ( Fig. 5A ). Interestingly, although the DNA-bound SaFapR adopts an elongated conformation, malonyl-CoA binding induces a shift to a more compact quaternary structure. In this conformation, the DNA-binding domains are repositioned on opposite sides of the central thioesterase-like core, rendering the complex incapable of binding DNA ( Fig. 5B and C ). Crucially, disrupting the interaction between FapR and malonyl-CoA through site-directed mutagenesis impairs cell growth in S. aureus , highlighting the importance of membrane lipid homeostasis for bacterial viability and pointing to new targets for antibiotic development against gram-positive pathogens ( 80 ).

Fig 5.

The response of FapR to increased malonyl-CoA levels allows monitoring lipid metabolism and function to trigger appropriate genetic and biochemical adjustments to relieve dysfunctions of this essential metabolic pathway ( 81 ). Additionally, malonyl-ACP binds to FapR with an affinity comparable to that of its precursor, malonyl-CoA, effectively disrupting the FapR-operator complex ( 82 ). NMR studies indicate that there is no direct protein-protein interaction between ACP and FapR ( 82 ). This observation aligns with the crystal structure of malonyl-ACP, which reveals that the malonyl-phosphopantetheine group extends outward from the ACP core, allowing it to function as an effector ligand ( 82 ). As a result, FapR modulates the expression of the fap regulon in response to the presence of malonyl-phosphopantetheine. This regulatory mechanism enables B. subtilis to tightly control fatty acid metabolism at the transcriptional level by sensing the levels of the two initial intermediates in fatty acid synthesis, malonyl-CoA and malonyl-ACP, thereby maintaining balanced membrane phospholipid production.

COUPLING OF FATTY ACID SYNTHESIS TO PHOSPHOLIPID SYNTHESIS IN B. SUBTILIS

In growing cultures of E. coli , fatty acid synthesis is closely linked to phospholipid synthesis, leading to small intracellular pools of intermediates. This suggests that the regulation of fatty acid synthesis is coordinated with phospholipid synthesis ( 2 ). Early experiments in B. subtilis conducted by Paoletti et al. ( 83 ), analyzing conditional knockout mutants, revealed that the depletion of PlsY or PlsC impeded phospholipid synthesis while leaving fatty acid synthesis unaffected, resulting in the accumulation of free fatty acids (FFA). Conversely, depletion of PlsX halted both fatty acid and phospholipid synthesis, indicating PlsX’s pivotal role in coordinating both biosynthetic pathways ( 83 ). As PlsX generates acyl-PO 4 from long-chain acyl-ACP, it was proposed that the inhibition of fatty acid synthesis upon PlsX depletion would cause the buildup of acyl-ACPs, subsequently inhibiting crucial fatty acid biosynthetic enzymes ( 83 ). This hypothesis stemmed from a model proposed for E. coli , wherein long-chain acyl-ACPs act as feedback regulators of the fatty acid biosynthetic pathway ( 3 ).

The significance of acyl-ACP in feedback control in B. subtilis was investigated by conditionally blocking plsX expression and overexpressing a cytosolic form of the type I thioesterase of E. coli (TesA) ( 56 ). Inhibition of phospholipid synthesis through PlsX depletion led to halted cell growth, accumulation of long-chain acyl-ACP, and downregulation of fatty acid biosynthesis. The expression of TesA alleviated the inhibition of fatty acid synthesis induced by PlsX depletion. As TesA catalyzed the hydrolysis of accumulated acyl-ACP and restored FASII activity, it can be concluded that the inhibition of fatty acid synthesis is indeed triggered by the accumulation of acyl-ACPs ( 56 ). Interestingly, the expression of TesA not only reactivates FASII but also rescues the growth of a strain depleted of PlsX, indicating that the FFA liberated from the hydrolysis of acyl-ACP by TesA bypasses the PlsX-catalyzed step to be incorporated into phospholipids. The pathway that is involved in the bypass of B. subtilis PlsX has been identified as the fatty acid kinase system FakA/B for the incorporation of FFA in gram-positive bacteria ( 56 ). In this pathway, FFA binds to the fatty acid binding protein FakB to be subsequently phosphorylated by the kinase FakA ( 84 , Fig. 1 ). FakB then delivers the resulting acyl-PO 4 to PlsY to initiate phospholipid synthesis, rescuing the growth of PlsX-deprived cells ( Fig. 1 ).

The identity of the fatty acid synthetic enzyme(s) that are inhibited by the putative inhibitory acyl-ACP in B. subtili s is not clear. In previous work, it was suggested that ACC, the enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of acetyl-CoA into malonyl-CoA, was likely a candidate for downregulation of fatty acid synthesis following the accumulation of acyl-ACP in PlsX-deprived B. subtilis cells ( 4 , 83 ). This hypothesis was then extended to other gram-positive bacteria. However, it was recently found that in B. subtilis, PlsX depletion triggered a 20-fold increase of malonyl-CoA ( 56 ). Clearly, these data are not compatible with feedback inhibition of ACC by acyl-ACP after arrest of both fatty acid and phospholipid synthesis. Thus, it remains possible that fatty acid inhibition by acyl ACP is due to shutdown of one or more enzymes of the FASII pathway, rather than targeting the conversion of acetyl-CoA into malonyl-CoA. However, at this stage, nothing is known about the identity of the FASII enzyme(s) modulated by long-chain acyl-ACPs in B. subtilis .

PlsX plays both catalytic and channeling roles in phospholipid synthesis

A key feature of phospholipid biosynthesis is the spatial organization of the process. Due to the amphipathic and compartmentalized nature of the membrane bilayer, synthesizing its components is more complex than typical metabolic reactions involving soluble intermediates. Membrane expansion occurs through the localized synthesis of new phospholipid molecules using soluble precursors, such as glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) and acyl-ACP, that are generated in the cytoplasm ( 4 ). Until recently, it was unclear how these precursors are efficiently directed into membrane-associated pathways or how acyltransferase enzymes simultaneously access both membrane-bound and cytosolic substrates. The resolution of the crystal structures of PlsY ( 85 ) and PlsC ( 86 ) has provided significant insights into these mechanisms. PlsY is an integral membrane protein with seven transmembrane segments that form a V-shaped active site extending deep into the bilayer. Its substrate, acyl-P, appears to access the active site from within the membrane through a proposed lateral gate between transmembrane helices 2 and 5, whereas G3P enters via the cytoplasmic opening ( 85 ). In contrast, the crystal structure of PlsC ( 86 ) revealed that this protein lacks transmembrane segments and is instead anchored to the cytoplasmic leaflet of the bilayer through a novel motif composed of two alpha helices lying parallel to the membrane. This mode of anchoring places the active site of PlsC above the bilayer in a way that it can simultaneously access lysophosphatidic acid residing within the membrane and acyl-ACP from the cytoplasm. In contrast to PlsY and PlsC, PlsX is a peripheral protein that is loosely associated with the membrane ( 83 , 87 ) and whose available crystal structures provided little clue to the mechanism of membrane association ( 88 , 89 ). It was recently determined that the membrane anchoring motif of PlsX, consisting of a hydrophobic loop localized at the tip of two amphipathic dimerization helices, is responsible for peripheral localization of PlsX ( 90 ). Interestingly, the synthesis of phospholipids was significantly impaired in cells where PlsX was detached from the membrane, leading to an accumulation of FFA as revealed by metabolic labeling ( 90 ). Although these mutations did not affect PlsX transacylase activity in vitro , it can be inferred that membrane association plays a crucial role in efficiently delivering acyl-PO 4 to PlsY, the subsequent enzyme in the pathway. This model suggests a dual function for PlsX in phospholipid synthesis, functioning both as a catalyst and as a chaperone protein that facilitates the transfer of its products to or positions them in close proximity to PlsY. This mechanism could potentially prevent the hydrolysis of acyl-PO 4 , which is an unstable intermediate in the pathway ( 90 ).

To unravel the molecular basis of the channeling mechanism, a series of experiments with model membranes using the Langmuir monolayer technique were conducted to delineate the binding and insertion events of PlsX into the membrane, as well as its subsequent catalytic activity ( 91 ). These experiments showed that membrane binding is a process mostly mediated by phospholipid charge, whereas fatty acid saturation and membrane fluidity remarkably influence the membrane insertion step ( 91 ). Strikingly, a PlsX derivative containing a single point mutation in the membrane-interacting domain, whose biological functionality was severely compromised in vivo but remains catalytically active in vitro , was able to specifically bind to phospholipid vesicles. However, this mutant protein loses the insertion capacity, strongly supporting the importance of membrane insertion in acyl-PO 4 delivery. Thus, a mechanism was proposed wherein membrane fluidity governs PlsX insertion, thereby regulating phospholipid biosynthesis in Gram-positive bacteria ( 91 ).

COORDINATION OF PHOSPHOLIPID AND MACROMOLECULAR SYNTHESIS

An important question related to the regulation of membrane biogenesis is how fatty acid and phospholipid syntheses are kept in synchrony with the biosynthesis of other macromolecules (protein, RNA, DNA, and peptidoglycan). A key player in the coupling of biosynthetic processes is the stringent response mediated by the alarmone ppGpp, whose properties were extensively reviewed (for a recent review, see 92 ). Fatty acid starvation is a well-known trigger of the stringent response in gram-negative bacteria ( 2 , 92 ). ppGpp production during fatty acid starvation inhibits many metabolic activities, ensuring that the cell volume does not outstrip the cell envelope capacity, thus maintaining the integrity of the envelope ( 93 ).

Pulschen et al. ( 94 ) showed that the stringent response also plays a crucial role in the adaptation of B. subtilis to fatty acid starvation. Inhibition of fatty acid synthesis with cerulenin in wild-type B. subtilis led to a quick growth arrest, but the cells remained viable ( 45 , 94 ). In contrast, B. subtilis strains incapable of activating the stringent response undergo massive death when treated with this inhibitor of fatty acid synthesis. Loss of viability is paralleled by perturbation of membrane integrity and function, with collapse of membrane potential as the likely cause of death ( 94 ). How does fatty acid starvation compromise membrane function in B. subtilis cells that cannot activate the stringent response? One simple possibility is that the absence of ppGpp signaling uncouples lipid and protein synthesis in cerulenin-treated cells, which try to continue growing, although they cannot make more membrane. The increment of cell volume in the absence of new membrane will eventually impose a physical stress on the membrane that should increase its permeability to molecules and ions, thus disrupting membrane potential ( 94 ).

Although no increment of ppGpp could be detected in lipid-starved B. subtilis , a substantial increase was observed in the GTP/ATP ratio of strains incapable of synthesizing ppGpp ( 94 ). In gram-positive bacteria, many of the changes promoted by the stringent response result from a reduction in intracellular GTP rather than being a direct effect of ppGpp on protein targets ( 95 ). Indeed, artificially lowering GTP with decoyinine rescued the viability of B. subtilis lacking ppGpp synthases during lipid starvation, confirming observations that low intracellular GTP is important for survival of nutritional stresses ( 94 ). Thus, it was proposed that cells that fail to lower the GTP/ATP ratio on fatty acid starvation will continue to synthesize proteins and grow until the membranes become compromised. A follow-up question to this work is how ppGpp synthesis is activated by fatty acid starvation in B. subtilis ? Evidence suggested that in E. coli, an interaction between unacylated-ACP, which accumulates in the absence of lipid synthesis, and SpoT couples fatty acid synthesis to ppGpp levels ( 96 ). However, it has been demonstrated that ACP does not interact with B. subtilis or S. pneumoniae ppGpp synthases, suggesting that such a coupling mechanism does not operate in gram-positive bacteria to trigger the stringent response during fatty acid starvation ( 96 ).

Interestingly, when fatty acids are scarce, cellular lysine decreases, leading to the accumulation of uncharged lysine tRNA, which directly activates ppGpp synthesis in E. coli ( 97 ). This reduction in lysine levels is likely to be the result of depletion of its precursor pyruvate, brought about by fatty acid starvation. Thus, it is possible that fatty acid starvation triggers the stringent response in gram-positive bacteria through the depletion of lysine or other amino acids that use pyruvate as a precursor (such as branched-chain amino acids), but has not yet been investigated.

Coordination of cell wall and membrane synthesis

During bacterial growth, the coordinated synthesis of envelope components is essential for maintaining cell envelope integrity; however, our understanding of this process remains incomplete. In E. coli , a molecular cross-talk between phospholipid synthesis and peptidoglycan sacculus expansion has been proposed ( 98 ). This model suggests that the availability of cellular phospholipids regulates the stability of the hydrolytic enzyme MepS, thereby facilitating peptidoglycan sacculus expansion. In contrast, the coordination between membrane and peptidoglycan layer synthesis in B. subtilis is less well understood. During cell elongation, synthesis of this macromolecule is mediated by the elongasome complex in cooperation with class A penicillin-binding proteins (aPBPs). Cells deficient in aPBP activity and unable to upregulate the elongasome via the σI pathway cannot compensate by enhancing elongasome function and fail to grow in LB medium unless supplemented with high magnesium levels ( 58 ). A spontaneous suppressor mutation that restores the viability of these mutants involves an altered function repressor, FapR, which acts as a super-repressor (FapR*) and reduces the transcription of fatty acid synthesis genes. Supporting the link between fatty acid limitation and cell wall synthesis, inhibition of fatty acid synthesis by cerulenin restored the growth of peptidoglycan-limited cells ( 58 ). As discussed above, YqhY has been implicated as a possible negative regulator of ACC ( 20 ). However, the induction of yqhY did not rescue the growth of peptidoglycan-limited strains, suggesting that simply overexpressing the already abundant YqhY protein may not be sufficient to downregulate ACC activity ( 58 ).

In summary, the findings of Wildding et al. ( 58 ) indicate that B. subtilis lacks an effective mechanism to downregulate membrane synthesis in response to a slowdown in peptidoglycan synthesis. This conclusion is supported by quantitative phase microscopy studies, which show that membrane synthesis in B. subtilis drives surface expansion independently of peptidoglycan insertion ( 57 ). It also aligns with earlier findings by Mindich ( 99 ), who demonstrated that cell wall synthesis in B. subtilis persists even when membrane synthesis is inhibited by glycerol starvation, as evidenced by the incorporation of radioactive alanine.

The synthesis of the cell membrane and the peptidoglycan layer must be uncoupled under certain conditions. For instance, many bacteria can transition from walled growth to an L-form state (cell wall-deficient variants) in response to antibiotic treatment or through relatively simple genetic changes ( 100 , 101 ). In this state, the cell membrane is essential, as L-form proliferation depends on the upregulation of membrane synthesis ( 55 ). In B. subtilis , a single cytosine-to-adenine substitution (accDA*) in the 5' untranslated region (UTR) of the operon encoding the accD and accA genes significantly enhances the production of AccDA subunits, thereby promoting L-form proliferation ( 55 ). This point mutation occurs within a stem-loop structure just upstream of the accD coding region, suggesting a regulatory mechanism in B. subtilis that modulates AccDA synthesis ( 55 ). The upregulation of ACC results in increased synthesis of malonyl-CoA, inducing the FASII enzymes that drive fatty acid synthesis, via the FapR regulator. This presumably leads to heightened lipid synthesis and, consequently, increased membrane surface area. It is noteworthy that an unbiased genetic screen identified mutants in the lpd gene ( Fig. 3B ) that can grow in the walled state but fail to proliferate as L-forms ( 102 ). This defect was traced to a specific deficiency in a-BCFAs, with the mutation affecting membrane fluidity. Although the mutant L-forms could grow and undergo shape changes similar to wild-type L-forms, they were unable to complete the final scission event required to produce separated sister cells ( 102 ). Collectively, studies on L-forms have unexpectedly emerged as a valuable tool for exploring the role of fatty acids in the structure, function, and regulation of membrane biogenesis in B. subtilis .

WHY DO BACTERIA REQUIRE UNSATURATED OR METHYL-BRANCHED CHAIN FATTY ACIDS?

The bacterial cytoplasmic membrane is built on the familiar lamellar phospholipid bilayer, an extended two-dimensional surface of two opposed monolayers. Although there is considerable diversity of membrane lipid structures in the bacterial world, most of them are glycerophospholipids that contain two fatty acid chains. Phospholipid bilayers composed of only saturated acyl chains of chain length C14 to C18 are rigid (non-ﬂuid) at physiological temperatures ( 103 ). The linear acyl chains pack together tightly to produce a bilayer that has a high phase transition temperature, high viscosity, and low permeability ( 103 ). The phase transition is a melting of the chains at higher temperatures where the bilayer acyl chains become fluid. Proper physiological function requires membranes to be permeable, allowing small molecules to enter and exit the cell. A non-fluid membrane impedes diffusion of proteins and other molecules (e.g., enzyme substrates, quinones) within the membrane, slowing protein conformational changes. In fact, high bilayer viscosity has been shown to inhibit cellular respiration by reducing diffusion between quinone electron carriers and transmembrane oxidoreductases ( 104 ).

Cells cope with decreased membrane fluidity by modulating the packing of acyl chains ( 105 ). In bacteria, the usual modulation of packing is done by cis UFAs that have a single cis double bond, generally located in the central portion of the chain. The double bond results in a kink in the chain, which disrupts packing ( Fig. 4 ). This results in lower phase transition temperatures, decreased bilayer rigidity, and higher permeability ( 3 , 106 ). In a sense, membranes with UFA-containing phospholipids are partially melted relative to those containing SFA-containing phospholipids. The composition of the BCFA also affects membrane fluidity owing to the disruptive effect of the methyl group on acyl chain packing ( 3 , 105 , 106 ). The a-BCFAs, which are derived from isoleucine ( Fig. 3 ), promote greater membrane fluidity than i-BCFAs, as their methyl branch is positioned farther from the fatty acid’s end ( 3 , 28 , 105 , 106 ). As discussed below, B. subtilis can use either a specific branched acyl chain or introduce a double bond in either straight or BCFAs ( 24 ) to form membranes functional at low temperatures. The absence of both acyl chain species blocks growth at low temperatures, and the cells lyse and die ( 35 , 107 ).

THERMAL REGULATION OF LIPID SYNTHESIS

As previously noted, B. subtilis synthesizes a mixture of terminally BCFAs that are incorporated into membrane phospholipids. Among these, iso- and a-BCFAs, particularly a-BCFAs, enhance membrane fluidity. At 37°C, the iso/anteiso-BCFA ratio of B. subtilis membranes is around 1.5 and drops to 1.13 at 25°C ( 24 ). The synthesis of a-BCFAs increases at lower temperatures through a mechanism that remains unidentified. Beyond modulating lipid fluidity via BCFAs, B. subtilis employs regulatory mechanisms to sense environmental temperature changes and adjust the expression of the lipid desaturase enzyme (Des). The biochemical properties of this enzyme were discussed earlier (see above). Below, we examine the system regulating lipid desaturation in response to temperature reduction, as studied in B. subtilis .

Transcriptional regulation of the des gene encoding a phospholipid desaturase

Early studies by Fulco and Bloch ( 108 – 111 ) revealed fatty acid desaturation in several bacteria, including Bacillus megaterium . Among their notable findings was the temperature-dependent desaturation of palmitate to Δ⁵-hexadecenoate in B. megaterium , occurring only at low temperatures ( Fig. 4 ; for historical perspectives, see 106 , 112 , 113 ). To explore the molecular mechanisms of cold-induced UFA synthesis and its temperature-dependent regulation, we investigated this phenomenon in B. subtilis due to its genetic tractability. Like B. megaterium, B. subtilis predominantly synthesizes SFAs at 37°C ( 72 ). However, when cultures grown at 37°C are shifted to 20°C, UFA synthesis is induced ( 72 ). This desaturation process requires de novo RNA and protein synthesis, as evidenced by its complete inhibition when rifampin or chloramphenicol is added before the temperature shift ( 72 ).

The des gene, encoding the sole desaturase of B. subtilis , was isolated prior to the organism’s genome sequencing through complementation of E. coli strains harboring mutations in fabA or fabB, genes essential for UFA synthesis ( 114 ). The des gene encodes a membrane-bound desaturase that introduces a cis double bond at the Δ⁵ position of various SFAs and BCFAs ( 24 ). Operon fusion and transcriptional analysis have shown that des expression is regulated by environmental temperature ( 114 , 115 ). At 37°C, des transcript levels are minimal, but they are transiently induced following a temperature downshift. This derepression occurs exclusively at the transcriptional level in a promoter-dependent manner and does not require de novo protein synthesis ( 115 ). Furthermore, when the wild-type des promoter was replaced with a non-cold shock synthetic promoter, transcript levels did not decrease after extended growth at 20°C ( 115 ). This indicates that the transient induction of the wild-type des gene at low temperatures results from the cessation of transcription following initial induction, rather than transcript instability. It explains why UFA levels in B. subtilis are higher during the first growth cycle at 20°C compared with subsequent generations ( 72 ). This phenomenon also parallels observations by Fulco and colleagues in B. megaterium , where rapid desaturation following a temperature shift was described as "hyper-induction" ( 116 ). Hyper-induction of desaturation likely facilitates the rapid adaptation of membrane phospholipids to new temperatures, followed by the maintenance of this adaptation.

A dedicated two-component system regulates B. subtilis des gene

Targeted mutagenesis of a two-gene operon of previously unknown function situated directly upstream of the des gene led to the discovery of a histidine kinase (DesK) and a response regulator (DesR). These proteins play a role in upregulating des expression in response to low temperatures in B. subtilis cells ( 117 ). DesK contains an N-terminal sensor domain of approximately 150 amino acids consisting of five transmembrane (TM) segments, followed by a C-terminal cytoplasmic domain (DesKC) of about 220 residues. In the presence of ATP, DesKC autophosphorylates at a conserved histidine residue (His-188), which serves as the site of autokinase activity ( 118 ). Autophosphorylated DesKC transfers a phosphoryl group to the effector protein DesR, which becomes phosphorylated at the predicted Asp-54 residue ( 118 ). Phosphorylated DesR (DesR-P) is the active form of the protein that binds to DNA, promoting the expression of the des gene ( 119 ). The resulting des mRNA is translated into desaturase, which inserts Δ5 double bonds into the acyl chains of saturated phospholipids. Once a sufficient accumulation of Δ5 unsaturated chains has adapted the cells to low temperature, DesK dephosphorylates DesR, leading to a decrease in transcription to a maintenance level. Therefore, the hyper-induction and modulation phases of UFA synthesis are determined by the phosphorylation level of DesR, which, in turn, is controlled by the switching of DesK between its kinase- and phosphatase-competent forms ( 106 ). This regulatory circuit is referred to as the Des pathway ( Fig. 6 ).

In vivo evidence that membrane fluidity, rather than growth temperature, controls the transcription of the des gene was obtained by experiments in which the proportion of a-BCFAs was varied, controlling the provision of exogenous fatty acid precursors, in the commonly used B. subtilis strain JH642 ( 120 ). Although this strain was chosen due to its extensively studied genetic manipulation, this was serendipitous because JH642 turned out to be defective in the biosynthesis of isoleucine, the precursor of a-BCFA ( 121 ). Limiting the supply of exogenous isoleucine in JH642 dramatically reduces the amounts of a-BCFA into phospholipids ( 121 ). Growth of cells in defined medium in the absence of isoleucine results in activation of des transcription at 37°C, using a DesK/DesR-dependent mechanism. Thus, a decrease in the content of membrane isoleucine-derived fatty acids at 37°C mimics a drop in the growth temperature, and both stimuli can induce UFA synthesis ( 120 ).

In line with this evidence, in lipoate synthase mutants ( lipA ), which are unable to synthesize BCFA precursors because of BKDH’s lack of function in the absence of lipoate synthesis ( 35 ), transcription of the des gene reaches levels about four-fold greater than those observed in wild-type strains growing in an isoleucine-free medium at 37°C. The most direct interpretation of these experiments suggests that the induction of des transcription is triggered by DesK’s ability to detect a decrease in membrane lipid fluidity. This interpretation was supported by fatty acid analysis ( 24 ) and DPH anisotropy ( 122 ), although Laurdan Generalized Polarization (GP) measurements suggested that phospholipid order did not significantly change in B. subtilis after a temperature downshift ( 123 ). Recently, a quantitative assay based on total internal reflection fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (TIRF-FCS) was developed to directly measure membrane fluidity in live bacteria by tracking the diffusivity of fluorescent membrane markers ( 124 ). TIRF-FCS analysis confirmed that upon cold shock, B. subtilis cells initially exhibit a decrease in membrane fluidity, followed by a rapid increase ( 124 ). This trend aligns with modifications in plasma membrane composition, specifically the increased incorporation of UFAs and BCFAs, as discussed above. However, rather than being essential ( 114 ), Des appears to act to optimize the membrane phospholipid bilayer, enhancing its function at the new temperature. In this regard, B. subtilis ' response to a temperature decrease parallels that of the condensing enzyme FabF in E. coli ( 43 ) and Enterococcus faecalis ( 125 ). Although this enzyme is not essential, it exhibits heightened activity at lower temperatures, leading to increased incorporation of 18:1 Δ11 (cis-vaccenic acid) to replace saturated acyl chains.

Mechanism of DesK sensing

Genetic and biochemical studies that led to the identification of the Des pathway in B. subtilis suggested that DesK can adopt different signaling states, depending on growth temperature ( 105 , 117 , 118 ). Subsequent in vivo and in vitro investigations using both full-length DesK and truncated versions of its TM domain indicated that DesK senses membrane thickness as a proxy for detecting cold ( 54 , 126 ). Progressive truncation of DesK’s TM helices revealed that both TM1 and TM5 are essential for the temperature-dependent regulation of kinase activity ( 126 ). Constructs containing only TM1 or TM5 failed to sense the cold signal, whereas a chimeric minimal sensor construct combining TM1 and TM5 (MS-DesK) retained cold sensitivity comparable with that of full-length DesK ( 126 ). Using MS-DesK, researchers explored how amino acid substitutions and insertions within the TM helix affected function, concluding that parameters such as hydration, packing, and hydrophobic matching to the membrane fine-tune its signaling output. Notably, altering the hydrophobic length of the TM1/5 chimeric helix affected signaling, suggesting it functions as a sensor, or “ruler,” for membrane thickness ( 54 , 126 ). Further experiments with MS-DesK reconstituted into liposomes of varying membrane thickness showed enhanced and more rapid kinase activation in liposomes composed of lipids with longer acyl chains ( 126 ). Similarly, both in vitro and in vivo studies with full-length DesK confirmed that lipids with longer acyl chains promote activation of its kinase function ( 54 , 127 ). Together, these findings support the idea that DesK and MS-DesK respond to membrane thickness by adjusting their catalytic activity. However, it remains unclear whether membrane thickness itself serves as the physiological signal in vivo . Notably, the increase in membrane thickness in B. subtilis phospholipids when the temperature drops from 37°C to 25°C is modest, only about 1 to 1.1 Å ( 128 ), which is less than the thickness difference observed in the artificial proteoliposomes that stimulated DesK and MS-DesK activity in vitro ( 126 , 127 ).

It is necessary to consider that membrane properties other than thickness are affected by temperature and acyl chain lengths, including permeability to water, membrane curvature, and fluidity/rigidity. In any case, it is clear that DesK senses membrane status as a proxy for cold, through an unknown mechanism that further experiments and modeling studies attempted to unveil, but that is presumably rooted in the internal mechanics of the TM bundle.

Interestingly, recent observations indicate that DesK reconstituted in Brij58 micelles behaves similarly to its behavior in liposomes. Specifically, DesK shows higher autokinase activity at lower temperatures compared with higher ones, with activity being five times greater at 25°C than at 37°C ( 129 ). This suggests that DesK might sense changes in the physical properties of the detergent micelles in response to temperature variations or that direct temperature sensing by DesK could be another factor influencing its kinase/phosphatase activity. Further research is needed to clarify this potential mechanism.

Biochemical and genetic research, coupled with X-ray structures and computational models, has unveiled structural features of transmembrane and cytoplasmic regions of DesK kinase and phosphatase-competent states (for reviews, see 106 , 130 – 132 ). Moreover, biochemical experiments and molecular simulations have demonstrated that the reversible formation of a two-helix coiled-coil (2-HCC) structure in the fifth transmembrane segment and the N-terminus of the cytoplasmic domain is crucial for the sensing and signal transduction mechanisms ( 130 , 133 – 135 ). However, further research is imperative to precisely elucidate how the DesK helix bundle perceives membrane biophysical properties and transmits the signal to the transcriptional apparatus.

Histidine kinases have been the subject of study for decades; however, a clear understanding of how they generate and transmit signals to the downstream catalytic domain remains elusive. Recent advances in cryogenic electron microscopy, particularly in lipid nanodisc technology ( 136 ), offer promising avenues to unveil new mechanistic insights at atomic resolution regarding signal sensing and transduction of histidine kinases that respond to bulk properties of membranes.

ROLE OF DE NOVO LIPID SYNTHESIS IN SPORE FORMATION IN B. SUBTILIS

Endospore formation in B. subtilis is a tightly regulated developmental process initiated in response to nutrient deprivation. This complex process involves asymmetric cell division, the engulfment of the forespore by the mother cell, and spore maturation, ultimately leading to the release of a highly resilient, dormant spore ( 137 – 140 ). Although B. subtilis has long served as a model system for studying cellular differentiation, the regulation, synthesis, and functional roles of lipids during this process remain underexplored. Here, we review evidence highlighting the importance of de novo lipid synthesis in driving spore development by contributing to key processes such as gene regulation, protein activation, and membrane biogenesis.

B. subtilis sporulation begins with the formation of a septum close to one cell pole (polar septation) that gives rise to a sporangium consisting of two cells of different sizes: the smaller forespore and the larger mother cell. This polar division event primes the sporangia to activate forespore- and mother cell-specific transcriptional regulators that control the expression of genes necessary for subsequent events in spore development (reviewed in 141 ). Early studies demonstrated that spore formation relies on de novo fatty acid biosynthesis ( 59 ). Although fatty acid synthesis peaks during the mid-logarithmic phase of growth, it declines significantly by the end of vegetative growth ( 142 ). In sporulating cells, this process is reactivated ( 142 ) under the control of the master sporulation regulator Spo0A∼P ( 143 ; for a comprehensive review, refer to 139 ). This regulator governs a broad regulon of genes associated with asymmetric cell division and the activation of sporulation-specific sigma factors. Spo0A∼P directly binds to the promoter of the accDA operon ( 142 ), which encodes two essential subunits of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) ( Fig. 1 ). The transcriptional activation of accDA is proposed to elevate malonyl-CoA levels, facilitating fatty acid synthesis and relieving FapR-mediated repression of the fap regulon. This reactivation of fatty acid synthesis is crucial for the mother cell-specific activation of σE, a key sigma factor regulating early mother cell gene expression. Initially synthesized as an inactive precursor, pro-σE, its processing depends on the lipid-driven regulatory network established during sporulation ( 59 ).

However, how and why does de novo lipid synthesis play a key role in pro-σE processing? Although it was established early on that SpoIIGA is required for the processing of pro-σE to its mature form ( 144 , 145 ), it was only more recently that its product, SpoIIGA, was identified as a novel type of aspartic protease ( 146 ). Modeling and mutational evidence suggest that SpoIIGA forms dimers similar to the HIV-1 protease and support a model in which this protein exists in an inactive state that is then activated through a conformational change induced by association with the signaling protein SpoIIR ( 146 – 148 ). SpoIIR is produced in the forespore under σF control and is then secreted into the intermembrane space of the septum that separates the mother cell and forespore, where it activates the membrane-bound SpoIIGA protease. Biochemical and cell biology experiments have shown that SpoIIR’s ability to activate SpoIIGA is dependent on the acylation of its threonine residue (T27) and requires de novo fatty acid synthesis ( 149 , Fig. 7 ). SpoIIR is synthesized in very limited amounts; thus, its modification with a fatty acid may serve to concentrate the acylated protein within a membrane microdomain containing both SpoIIGA and the signal peptidase. This could facilitate the enzymatic cleavage of the pre-protein signal peptide and the release of mature SpoIIR into the interseptal space ( 149 , Fig. 7 ). The activation of σE then enables the transcription of the σE regulon, which includes genes essential for engulfment. Although this model is appealing, the nature of the activated acyl donor and the signal peptidase responsible for processing pro-SpoIIR remain unidentified ( Fig. 7 ). The connection between fatty acid synthesis and σE activation seems to be designed to ensure the localized transmission of a weak signal across the bilayer, thereby controlling cell-cell communication during development.

After asymmetric division, the polar septum curves around the forespore as the mother cell swallows it, resulting in a double membrane-bound forespore in the mother cell cytosol that requires new membrane lipid synthesis ( 150 ). Curiously, engulfment is successful in a B. subtilis mutant in the SpoIIIE translocase, where most of the chromosome of the forespore remains trapped on the mother cell side, including many genes involved in phospholipid synthesis ( 151 ). Thus, it is possible that in such a strain, phospholipids are provided by the mother cell and then reach the forespore inner membrane. In B. subtilis , the accBC operon and the yqhY gene, genetically associated with the ACC complex, are located downstream of the spoIIIA locus, although the exact nature of this relationship remains unclear (see above). The spoIIIA locus encodes proteins that, together with SpoIIQ proteins, form a channel connecting the mother cell and the forespore. Interestingly, this genomic organization is highly conserved among several sporulating bacteria ( 151 ). Additionally, in Paraclostridium sordellii , spoIIIA is immediately preceded by an 8-gene operon encoding most of the enzymes from the pathway leading to PA, the precursor of membrane phospholipids ( 151 ). Thus, a physiological relationship between the SpoIIIA proteins and phospholipid synthesis might be worth investigating ( 151 ).

At the end of the process, the engulfing membranes must undergo fission to pinch off and release the forespore. FisB is the only protein identified to date with a dedicated role in membrane fission in bacteria. Produced in the mother cell, it is essential for releasing the developing spore into the mother cell’s cytoplasm ( 60 , 152 , 153 ). Recent experiments using cerulenin showed that de novo lipid synthesis is required to create the engulfing membrane surrounding the forespore and to support the increase in surface area of the forespore due to inflation caused by the translocation of DNA inside it ( 60 ). It was hypothesized that lipid flow from the mother cell to the engulfing and forespore membranes follows a tension gradient, with lipids moving towards areas of higher tension (the mother cell membrane around the forespore and the forespore membrane) ( 60 ). FisB then oligomerizes at the membrane fission site, blocking this lipid flux, increasing the tension to the point of lysis, and causing the membrane to break to release the forespore ( 60 ).

COFACTOR FORMATION FROM FATTY ACID PRECURSORS IN B. SUBTILIS

Biotin and lipoate are enzyme cofactors derived from fatty acids, present in all domains of life. Enzymes that require them for their activity play crucial roles in metabolism, including lipid biosynthesis. They are required for malonyl-CoA and branched-chain ketoacids production, the primers for FASII (see above). The de novo synthesis of these cofactors has been most extensively studied in E. coli and Bacillus species and depends on the production of fatty acid precursors. In both the BioC-BioH pathway in E. coli and the BioW pathway in B. subtilis , FASII enzymes are essential for producing the pimeloyl thioester, a precursor of biotin. Also, the octanoyl-ACP precursor for LA is synthesized through reactions catalyzed by FASII enzymes. This overview examines the biosynthetic pathways for biotin and LA acid in B. subtilis , which differ from the established model described for E. coli .

Synthesis of biotin in B. subtilis

Biotin, also known as vitamin H or B7, is an essential sulfur cofactor necessary for numerous carboxylases, such as the BBCP subunit of ACC ( Fig. 2 ) and a putative pyruvate carboxylase of B. subtilis ( 14 ). There are many similarities between biotin synthesis in E. coli and B. subtilis ; however, there are also several striking differences (reviewed in 62 , 154 , 155 ). The chemical structure of biotin consists of a tetrahydroimidizalone ring fused with an organosulfur-containing tetrahydrothiophene ring that bears valeric acid ( Fig. 8A ). In B. subtilis, biotin must be covalently attached to lysine 122 of the BBCP subunit of ACC ( 14 ). This reaction is performed by the protein BirA, a biotin ligase, that catalyzes the adenylylation of biotin and subsequent transfer of the biotinyl group of biotinyl-5-AMP to the conserved lysine of BCCP ( 156 , Fig. 8B ).

Fig 8.

The biotin biosynthetic genes of B. subtilis, bioWAFDBI, are contained in a single operon containing seven genes ( 154 , Fig. 8C ). The bioA , bioF , bioB, and bioD genes are homologous to related genes in E. coli , and their products have been shown to catalyze equivalent biosynthetic steps in B. subtilis ( 154 , Fig. 8A ). The bio genes are transcribed from a promoter that is regulated in a biotin-dependent manner by BirA, a biotin protein ligase/repressor, which binds to bioO , the operator sequence located upstream of bioW ( 154 , 156 ) ( Fig. 8B and C ). The biotin biosynthetic pathway can be divided into two stages, synthesis of a pimelate moiety, a seven-carbon α-ω-dicarboxylic acid, and assembly of the bicyclic rings ( Fig. 8A ). Until recently, little was known about pimelate synthesis, whereas the enzymes of ring assembly have been well characterized ( 157 ). The best-characterized pathway of pimelate synthesis is that of E. coli, which proceeds by a modified fatty acid synthetic pathway, altered by methylation and demethylation enzymes called BioC and BioH ( 158 , Fig. 8A ). B. cereus synthesizes biotin via the BioC-BioH pathway, but interestingly, B. subtilis lacks the genes encoding those proteins. Instead, it has two novel genes in its biotin operon, bioW and bioI , which flank the conserved ring assembly genes ( 159 , Fig. 8A ). It was previously accepted that BioI, a cytochrome P450 enzyme, produces trace amounts of pimeloyl-ACP by cleaving fatty acids and acyl-ACPs ( 157 , 160 , 161 ). In addition, it was proposed that BioW, originally thought to be a pimeloyl-CoA synthetase, played a secondary role, likely in scavenging pimelic acid from the environment. However, genetic and biochemical studies revealed that this prediction was incorrect: bioW is an essential pimeloyl-CoA synthetase, whereas bioI is not essential, and its physiological function remains unknown ( 62 , 162 – 164 ). NMR studies using cells labeled with 13 C-acetate suggested the involvement of fatty acid biosynthesis in pimelate production ( 164 ). This hypothesis was supported by the observation that inhibiting B. subtilis FASII with cerulenin reduced biotin synthesis ( 164 ). It has been proposed that pimelate biosynthesis might involve the use of malonyl-ACP as a primer rather than acetyl-CoA. After two elongation cycles, pimeloyl-ACP would be cleaved by a thioesterase to release free pimelate, which would then be converted to pimeloyl-CoA, the substrate for BioF, by BioW ( Fig. 8A ). However, the identity of this thioesterase is unknown, and the overall pathway for pimelate biosynthesis remains to be elucidated.

Lipoic acid biosynthesis

LA (6,8-dithiooctanoic acid) is a sulfur derivative of octanoate, which acts as an essential cofactor for several multi-enzyme complexes ( 61 ). In B. subtilis , covalently attached LA plays an essential role in the function of dehydrogenase complexes involved in oxidative metabolism: pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), α-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase (ODH), BKDH, and acetoin dehydrogenase (ADH). These complexes are composed of many copies of three catalytic subunits, known as E1, E2, and E3. The core of each complex is formed by the E2 subunits, which contain lipoylable domains with conserved lysines that link the cofactor through an amide bond. These flexible arms allow the transfer of reaction intermediates between the multiple active sites of the enzyme complexes ( 30 ). Furthermore, LA is covalently attached to the H-protein (GcvH), an essential component of the glycine cleavage system (GCS), responsible for the reversible decarboxylation of glycine ( 61 ).

Bacteria acquire LA for protein modification by either de novo biosynthesis or salvage from the environment. Notably, LA is assembled on its apoproteins, unlike other covalently linked cofactors which are attached after being synthesized separately, such as that described for biotin. E. coli has the simplest LA biosynthetic pathway ( 165 ). In the first step, the N 6 amino group of a conserved lysine in the lipoyl domain of GcvH or E2 subunits of dehydrogenases is acylated by an octanoyltransferase, LipB ( 166 ). This enzyme uses octanoyl-ACP, a transient intermediate of fatty acid biosynthesis, as a donor, highlighting the essentiality of an active FASII for LA production. After that, lipoate synthase (LipA) replaces two hydrogen atoms of the octanoamide moiety with sulfur atoms, via a radical S -adenosylmethionine mechanism ( 167 ). This pathway is mainly present in proteobacteria and mycobacteria ( 168 ).

B. subtilis possesses a more complex pathway for LA synthesis, involving four proteins instead of the two-protein model (LipB and LipA) found in E. coli . It comprises three enzymes, termed LipM, LipA, and LipL, and a lipoyl transporter protein, GcvH. LipM is an octanoyl transferase unrelated to LipB that, in contrast to this last enzyme, transfers endogenously produced octanoate exclusively to GcvH ( 169 , 170 ). Then, LipA introduces two sulfur atoms on octanoyl-GcvH, resulting in lipoyl-GcvH ( 35 , Fig. 9 ). To modify the rest of the lipoyl-dependent enzymes, B. subtilis requires a new enzymatic activity: amidotransferase, accomplished by LipL ( 170 , 171 ). This process by which LA is synthesized on GcvH and then is distributed to the E2 subunits of the target enzymes was termed the "lipoyl-relay pathway" ( 172 ) ( Fig. 9 ).

Fig 9.

B. subtilis can also scavenge LA from the environment through a lipoate-protein ligase, LplJ, which activates free LA and transfers the lipoyl moieties to the lipoyl domains of apoproteins. In bacteria like E. coli , which use the LipB-LipA pathway, lipoate ligases can lipoylate or octanoylate all apoproteins. However, in B. subtilis, LplJ only modifies GcvH and the E2 subunit of ODH (E2o) with LA or octanoate ( 173 ). In the salvage pathway, the LipL amidotransferase plays a crucial role in modifying the E2 components of PDH and BKDH. Notably, LipL is indispensable for both LA synthesis and scavenging in B. subtilis , highlighting that the protein lipoylation pathways in this organism are non-redundant, unlike in E. coli .

The lipoyl-relay pathway was also found in gram-positive pathogens like Listeria monocytogenes ( 174 ), B. anthracis (Mansilla, unpublished) and S. aureus ( 175 ). This mechanism of protein lipoylation is conserved in eukaryotes like Saccharomyces cerevisiae ( 176 ), trypanosomatids ( 177 ), Filasterea ( 178 ), Apicomplexa ( 178 ), Caenorhabditis elegans ( 179 ), and Homo sapiens ( 172 ), suggesting that the lipoyl-relay, described for the first time in B. subtilis , may represent the most distributed pathway across diverse clades.

PERSPECTIVES

B. subtilis has become a key model for studying fatty acid synthesis and regulation in both vegetative and sporulating cells, offering insights into membrane biogenesis and differentiation. A major unresolved question is how phospholipid synthesis is tightly regulated to maintain the appropriate membrane-to-cell size ratio. Given the high energy demands of fatty acid biosynthesis, its stringent control is essential. The transcriptional regulator FapR plays a central role by sensing malonyl-CoA levels and regulating genes involved in fatty acid and early phospholipid synthesis. Beyond its function as a biosynthetic precursor, malonyl-CoA also acts as a regulatory molecule, suggesting the existence of an unknown mechanism governing its homeostasis in FapR-containing organisms. This regulation may involve feedback inhibition by acyl-ACPs on key enzymes in the FASII pathway rather than direct control of acetyl-CoA conversion into malonyl-CoA by ACC.

The coordination of fatty acid and phospholipid synthesis depends critically on PlsX, positioning it as a key regulatory node in membrane biogenesis and cellular growth. However, how PlsX activity is modulated by its interaction with lipid bilayers remains unclear. In gram-positive bacteria, recent studies have revealed intricate cross-talk between fatty acid and peptidoglycan biosynthesis, broadening our understanding of cell envelope growth beyond the traditional focus on peptidoglycan alone. These findings will guide future research on bacterial membrane regulation.

As a gram-positive bacterium lacking an outer membrane, B. subtilis serves as an excellent model for in vivo studies of membrane lipid biophysics. Recent TIRF-FCS experiments have shown that cold shock initially reduces membrane fluidity, followed by rapid recovery. Future quantitative studies using fatty acid desaturation mutants will be essential for clarifying the role of UFAs in this process and uncovering bacterial strategies for maintaining membrane homeostasis. Additionally, the absence of an outer membrane necessitates a unique balance of fatty acid production, phospholipid synthesis, and cofactor availability in B. subtilis , offering insights into the evolutionary retention or loss of biosynthetic pathways.
