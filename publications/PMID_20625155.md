Abstract

Purified Candida albicans sterol 14-α demethylase (CaCYP51) bound the CYP51 substrates lanosterol and eburicol, producing type I binding spectra with K s values of 11 and 25 μM, respectively, and a K m value of 6 μM for lanosterol. Azole binding to CaCYP51 was “tight” with both the type II spectral intensity (Δ A max ) and the azole concentration required to obtain a half-Δ A max being proportional to the CaCYP51 concentration. Tight binding of fluconazole and itraconazole was confirmed by 50% inhibitory concentration determinations from CYP51 reconstitution assays. CaCYP51 had similar affinities for clotrimazole, econazole, itraconazole, ketoconazole, miconazole, and voriconazole, with K d values of 10 to 26 μM under oxidative conditions, compared with 47 μM for fluconazole. The affinities of CaCYP51 for fluconazole and itraconazole appeared to be 4- and 2-fold lower based on CO displacement studies than those when using direct ligand binding under oxidative conditions. Econazole and miconazole were most readily displaced by carbon monoxide, followed by clotrimazole, ketoconazole, and fluconazole, and then voriconazole (7.8 pmol min −1 ), but itraconzole could not be displaced by carbon monoxide. This work reports in depth the characterization of the azole binding properties of wild-type C. albicans CYP51, including that of voriconazole, and will contribute to effective screening of new therapeutic azole antifungal agents. Preliminary comparative studies with the I471T CaCYP51 protein suggested that fluconazole resistance conferred by this mutation was through a combination of increased turnover, increased affinity for substrate, and a reduced affinity for fluconazole in the presence of substrate, allowing the enzyme to remain functionally active, albeit at reduced velocity, at higher fluconazole concentrations.

Fungal sterol 14-α demethylase (CYP51) is required for ergosterol biosynthesis, an ancestral activity in the cytochrome P450 (CYP) superfamily of hemoproteins, and is the main target for azole antifungal drugs ( 15 ). CYP51 has been shown to be essential for viability in Saccharomyces cerevisiae ( 14 ). Azole inhibitors that are selective for the fungal enzyme over the human homologue have been developed and are commonly used to treat fungal infections, including those caused by Candida albicans ( 23 , 39 ). The mode of action of azole antifungal drugs involves the selective inhibition of the fungal CYP51, involving the nucleophilic nitrogen of the azole heterocyclic ring coordinating as the sixth ligand of the heme iron in the ferric state and the azole drug side chains interacting with the polypeptide structure ( 12 , 57 ). However, due to prolonged and prophylactic use of azole drugs in the clinic, the emergence of azole-resistant C. albicans strains and other Candida species has become an increasing problem, especially among hospitalized immunocompromised patients, such as HIV and AIDS, cancer, and transplant patients, leading to a growing need to develop new effective antifungal strategies against drug-resistant strains ( 41 , 45 , 49 ).

In this study we expressed wild-type CaCYP51 in E scherichia coli and purified the protein to homogeneity from the membrane fraction. We performed detailed azole binding studies by using a combination of UV-Vis spectroscopy under oxidative conditions and displacement of bound azole by carbon monoxide under reductive conditions. Azole resistance in C. albicans has been associated with amino acid substitutions in CYP51, besides other mechanisms, such as altered drug efflux and an erg3 mutation that reduced production of 14-α methylergosta-3,6-diol after azole exposure ( 16 , 32 , 54 ). We have also partially characterized the I471T CaCYP51 mutant protein, first described by Kakeya et al. ( 13 ), in terms of substrate binding, fluconazole resistance ( K d and 50% inhibitory concentration [IC 50 ]), and sensitivity to fluconazole ( K d ) in the presence of substrate in comparison to the wild-type CaCYP51 protein. This combination of spectral techniques and IC 50 CYP51 reconstitution assays can be used as a preliminary screen for potential in silico -designed drug candidates for therapeutic use against C. albicans infection and has not been undertaken previously with these comparative methods or with the azole antifungal collection evaluated here. Similar screens for affinities of CYP inhibitors are drawing attention in a number of potential therapeutic areas besides antifungal applications, for instance, screening for antituberculosis agents based on M ycobacterium tuberculosis CYP130 and CYP51 ( 42 , 43 ) and screening for antitrypanosomal agents based on T rypanosoma brucei and T rypanosoma cruzi CYP51 proteins ( 18 , 25 ) to identify new therapeutic compounds to treat trypanosomal infections, with both applications using automated UV-Vis spectral analysis. In addition, Ekins et al. ( 7 ) screened human CYP51 inhibitors based on lanosterol demethylase IC 50 determinations, and Peng et al. ( 40 ) screened for CYP2C9 drug interactions with quinoline-4-carboxamide analogues by using both UV-Vis spectral analysis and lanosterol demethylase IC 50 determinations. The methodology we describe here facilitates screening among antifungal CYP51 inhibitors, besides assays using target organisms, and we also provide insight into the biochemical basis of azole antifungal resistance.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cloning CaCYP51.

The CaCYP51 gene (accession number P10613 ) was isolated by PCR from the CYP51 cDNA in YEp51 ( 48 ) and, as for the first CaCYP51 yeast expression to elucidate the reaction mechanism of this enzyme, it contained an altered triplet for Ser263 from CTG (translated by C. albicans as Ser but by E. coli as Leu) to TCA to ensure correct expression in E. coli . The CaCYP51 gene, including a four-codon extension (CATCACCATCAC) encoding four histidine residues prior to the stop codon, was expressed using pCWori + ( 27 ).

Heterologous expression in E. coli and isolation of recombinant CaCYP51 protein.

The pC Wori + -Ca C YP51 construct was transformed into competent E. coli DH5α cells, and transformants were selected using ampicillin. Overnight cultures (10 ml) of transformants were used to inoculate 1-liter volumes of Terrific broth supplemented with 20 g·liter −1 peptone and 0.1 mg·ml −1 sodium ampicillin. Cultures were grown at 37°C and 230 rpm for 8 h prior to induction with 1 mM isopropyl-β- d -thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and expression at 29°C and 190 rpm for 18 h in the presence of 1 mM 5-aminolevulenic acid. Recombinant CaCYP51 protein was isolated according to the method of Arase et al. ( 3 ) except that the sonication buffer contained 2% (wt/vol) sodium cholate and no Tween 20. The solubilized CaCYP51 was purified by affinity chromatography using Ni 2+ -nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) agarose as previously described ( 5 ) with the modification that 0.1% (wt/vol) l -histidine in 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.1) and 25% (wt/vol) glycerol was used to elute nonspecifically bound E. coli proteins after the salt washes and elution of CaCYP51 protein was achieved with 1% (wt/vol) l -histidine in 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.1) and 25% (wt/vol) glycerol. The CaCYP51-containing eluent was dialyzed against 60 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.1) and 10% (wt/vol) glycerol prior to use in CYP51 reconstitution assays. Protein purity was assessed by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis followed by staining intensity analysis using the UTHSCSA ImageTool 3.0 ( http://ddsdx.uthscsa.edu/dig/itdesc.html ).

Determination of cytochrome P450 protein concentrations.

Reduced carbon monoxide difference spectra ( 8 ) were used to determine native cytochrome P450 concentrations based on an extinction coefficient of 91 mM −1 cm −1 ( 38 ) from the difference in absorbance between 446 and 490 nm. Absolute spectra were determined between 700 and 300 nm by using 4.75 μM CaCYP51 in 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.1) and 25% (wt/vol) glycerol for the oxidized protein, the 10 mM sodium dithionite-reduced protein, and the reduced carbon monoxide-P450 complex as previously described ( 5 ). All spectral determinations were made using quartz semimicrocuvettes with a light path of 4.5 mm and with a Hitachi U-3310 UV/VIS spectrophotometer (San Jose, California).

Substrate binding spectral determinations.

Lanosterol and eburicol (0.05%, wt/vol) were solubilized in a 1:10 mixture of chloroform-acetone containing 0.5% (vol/vol) Tween 80 prior to evaporation to dryness under nitrogen. The Tween 80-sterol residue was then dissolved in water to produce a stock 0.05% (wt/vol) aqueous sterol solution used for substrate binding experiments. Eburicol (24-methylene-24,25-dihydrolanosterol) is an alternative CYP51 substrate in Aspergillus fumigatus ( 1 ) and as the major sterol CYP51 substrate seen under azole inhibition of C. albicans may reflect the preferred metabolic route to ergosterol ( 46 ). Absolute spectra were determined using 10 μM CaCYP51 in 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.1) and 25% (wt/vol) glycerol between 700 and 300 nm in the presence of lanosterol or eburicol concentrations from 0 to 88 μM. The absorbance difference spectra were derived by subtracting the absolute spectrum of CaCYP51 in the absence of sterol from all subsequent spectra. The sterol saturation curves were constructed from the Δ A peak-trough derived from the difference spectra, including corrections for changes in sample volume. The substrate binding constant ( K s ) was determined by nonlinear regression (Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm) using the Hill equation, Δ A = Δ A max /(1 + K s /[sterol] n ), where n is the apparent Hill number, and the Michaelis-Menten equation, Δ A = (Δ A max × [sterol])/( K s + [sterol]). Spin state change was calculated from the Δ A value of the difference spectra by using an extinction coefficient of 118 mM −1 cm −1 derived for CYP164A2, which can be modulated from 100% low spin to nearly 100% high spin by physico-chemical means ( 53 ).

CYP51 reconstitution assays and IC 50 determinations.

Lanosterol (2 to 60 μg) and dilaurylphosphatidylcholine (DLPC; 100 μg) were dissolved in 0.25 ml of chloroform prior to the addition of 0.2 ml water. The mixture was vortexed to form a white emulsion while simultaneously evaporating off the chloroform under nitrogen to produce aqueous sterol-DLPC micelles. The CaCYP51 reconstitution system consisted of 0.2 ml of sterol-DLPC micelles diluted to 0.8 ml to contain 2.3 nmol CaCYP51, 9 nmol truncated Δ33 yeast cytochrome P450 reductase ( 52 ), 3 U yeast glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, 2 μmol disodium glucose-6-phosphate, 100 μmol potassium phosphate (pH 6.6). The mixture was preincubated at 37°C for 5 min prior to reaction initiation by the addition of 0.2 ml 10 mM β-NADPHNa 4 in 0.2% (wt/vol) NaHCO 3 , followed by further incubation at 37°C for 60 min with shaking. The final pH of the CaCYP51 reconstitution assay mixture was 7.0 to 7.2. The reaction was terminated by the addition of 2 ml of 15% (wt/vol) KOH in ethanol followed by incubation at 85°C for 90 min to saponify the lipid fraction. Sterol metabolites were recovered by extraction with n -hexane followed by derivatization with N , O -bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide and tetramethylsilane prior to analysis by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry ( 51 ). IC 50 determinations were performed using 15 μg lanosterol per assay mixture with various fluconazole and itraconazole concentrations in 5 μl of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) added prior to incubation at 37°C and addition of β-NADPHNa 4 .

Azole binding spectra determinations.

Binding of azole antifungal agents to CaCYP51 was performed as previously described ( 23 ) with DMSO also added to the cytochrome P450-containing compartment of the reference cuvette. Stock 0.5-, 0.1-, and 0.05-mg ml −1 solutions of clotrimazole, econazole, fluconazole, itraconazole, ketoconazole, miconazole, and voriconazole were prepared in DMSO. The chemical structures of the azole antifungal agents used in this study are shown in Fig. 1 . Fluconazole was progressively titrated against five different CaCYP51 concentrations (4.75, 3.56, 2.38, 1.19, and 0.59 μM) in 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.1) and 25% (wt/vol) glycerol, with the spectral difference determined after each incremental addition of fluconazole. The other six azoles were individually titrated against 4.75 μM CaCYP51. The azole-induced red shift of the heme Soret peak was determined by measuring the absolute spectra of 4.75 μM CaCYP51 between 700 and 300 nm in the presence of 10 μM azole. The dissociation constant ( K d ) of the enzyme-azole complex for each azole was determined by nonlinear regression (Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm) of the Δ A peak-trough against the azole concentration by using a rearrangement of the Morrison equation: Δ A = Δ A max × {( E t + azole concentration + K d ) − [( E t + azole concentration + K d ) 2 − (4 × E t × azole concentration)] 0.5 }/(2 × E t ) ( 33 ), where E t is the total amount of CaCYP51 available to bind azole. The [azole] 0.5 value was also determined directly from the azole saturation curves and is defined here as the azole concentration required to obtain half the Δ A max value for a stated concentration of CaCYP51.

CO displacement of bound azoles.

Solutions of 2.5 μM CaCYP51 were prepared in 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.5) and 10% (wt/vol) glycerol, including 10 mM sodium dithionite and fluconazole (0.5 to 25 μM). The reduced fluconazole-CaCYP51 solution was divided equally between two quartz cuvettes, and baseline spectra between 500 and 400 nm were recorded. CO gas was then gently bubbled through the sample cuvette for 90 s, and sequential spectra between 500 and 400 nm were recorded at 45-s intervals. A control determination in the absence of azole was also performed. This experiment was repeated using itraconazole (0.5 to 25 μM). CO displacement rates in the presence of 3 μM solutions of clotrimazole, econazole, ketoconazole, miconazole, and voriconazole were also determined. Where the rates of [CO-P450] red formation against time were hyperbolic, as measured by the increase in the A CO-Soret peak, half-lives ( t 1/2 ) of [CO-P450] red formation were calculated using the following equation: Δ A = (Δ A max × t )/( t + t 1/2 ). Linear regression was used to determine rates of [CO-P450] red formation against time for azole concentrations of 2 μM or higher. Determination of K d values from the fall in A CO-Soret with increasing azole concentration was performed by nonlinear regression (Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm) using the Morrison equation: Δ A = Δ A max × {1 − ( E t + azole concentration + K d ) − [( E t + azole concentration + K d ) 2 − (4 × E t × azole concentration)] 0.5 }/(2 × E t ) ( 37 ) at time points of 2 and 14 min.

Preliminary characterization of the mutant I471T CaCYP51 protein.

The I471T CaCYP51 point mutant was synthesized by Genecust (Dudelange, Luxembourg) from supplied wild-type pCWori + Ca CYP51 plasmid, altering the triplet at amino acid position 471 from ATT to ACT. Gene integrity was confirmed by DNA sequencing. The same expression and purification conditions were used for I471T CaCYP51 as described for the wild-type protein. The lanosterol binding properties and IC 50 determination for fluconazole were performed as previously described using a 2 μM concentration of the I471T and wild-type CaCYP51 proteins. In addition, the binding of fluconazole to 2 μM I471T and wild-type CaCYP51 was examined in the absence and presence of 70 μM lanosterol to establish whether the displacement of bound substrate by fluconazole differed between wild-type and mutant proteins. The lanosterol was mixed with the CaCYP51 proteins 5 min prior to commencing fluconazole binding. In the control experiment an equivalent volume of 0.5% (vol/vol) Tween 80 replaced the stock lanosterol solution.

Data analysis.

Experiments were performed in triplicate, with the mean data sets subjected to further analyses. The standard deviations obtained were less than 10% unless stated. Curve fitting of numerical data was performed using the computer program ProFit 5.0.1 (QuantumSoft, Zurich, Switzerland).

Chemicals.

All chemicals, including azole antifungals except for voriconazole, were obtained from Sigma Chemical Company (Poole, United Kingdom). Voriconazole was supplied by Discovery Fine Chemicals (Bournemouth, United Kingdom). Growth media, sodium ampicillin, IPTG, and 5-aminolevulenic acid were obtained from Foremedium Ltd. (Hunstanton, United Kingdom). Ni 2+ -NTA-agarose affinity chromatography matrix was obtained from Qiagen (Crawley, United Kingdom). Eburicol was synthesized and purified by W. David Nes (Texas Tech University).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Heterologous expression and purification of recombinant CaCYP51 protein.

The azole antifungal target CaCYP51, an endoplasmic reticulum-associated C. albicans protein, was expressed in E. coli , where the protein localized in the membrane fraction. The reduced carbon monoxide difference spectral assay ( 8 ), an archetypal assay for the cytochrome P450 superfamily, was performed using the membrane suspension giving a characteristic peak at 447 nm and at 446 nm after solubilization and purification of CaCYP51. Protein isolation by cholate extraction using sonication ( 3 ) yielded 0.15 μmol per liter culture. This was comparable to expression levels obtained previously ( 4 , 27 ). Purification by Ni 2+ -NTA-agarose chromatography resulted in a 25.4-fold increase in purity compared with the solubilized cellular fraction and a 54% recovery of native CaCYP51. SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis confirmed CaCYP51 purity to be greater than 95% when assessed by staining intensity. An apparent molecular mass of 60.9 kDa was obtained, which was close to the predicted value of 61,221 Da for the protein including the four-histidine C-terminal extension. The use of C-terminal histidine extensions to facilitate protein purification by Ni 2+ -NTA-agarose is commonplace among published cytochrome P450 protein studies, with the C-terminal histidine extension considered not to alter the properties of the protein or structure ( 3 , 26 , 27 , 35 ).

Spectral properties of recombinant CaCYP51 protein.

Absolute spectra (Fig. 2 ) and the reduced CO-P450 difference spectrum (Fig. 2 , inset) of CaCYP51 were characteristic of a cytochrome P450 protein ( 5 , 11 ), indicating that the enzyme was expressed in the native form. CaCYP51 displayed the typical spectral properties of a ferric P450 with a low-spin-state heme iron Soret (γ) band at 417 nm in addition to α, β, and δ bands at 569, 538, and 354 nm, respectively. Dithionite one-electron reduction did not alter the wavelength of the Soret or α bands but did cause a small blue shift of the β band, from 569 to 562 nm. Binding carbon monoxide to dithionite-reduced ferrous CaCYP51 resulted in a typical red shift of the Soret band from 417 to 446 nm in the formation of the CO-ferrous P450 complex and a smaller amount of hemoprotein with a maximum at 420 nm (an inactivated form).

Sterol binding affinity.

Progressive titration of CaCYP51 with lanosterol and eburicol gave type I difference spectra (Fig. 3 ). Type I binding spectra occur when the substrate (or other molecules) displaces the water molecule coordinated as the sixth ligand to the low-spin hexa-coordinated heme prosthetic group, causing the heme to adopt the high-spin penta-coordinated conformation ( 11 ). These spectra are useful for investigating substrate affinity with the CYP51 protein. In this study the spectral intensity was 6-fold higher with lanosterol (Fig. 3A ) than with eburicol (Fig. 3B ), indicating that the spin state change obtained with lanosterol was 6-fold greater than with eburicol. Addition of 88 μM lanosterol caused a 14.5% change in spin state of CaCYP51, based on an extinction coefficient of 118 mM −1 cm −1 for the difference spectrum (Δ A ) with CYP164A2 ( 53 ), while addition of 88 μM eburicol induced a spin state change of 2.3%. The relatively small change in spin state caused by the binding of lanosterol was previously observed for other CYP51 enzymes ( 2 , 5 , 28 ), with spin state changes generally not exceeding 10%. Recently Lepesheva et al. ( 26 ) reported that the water molecule coordinated as the sixth ligand to the heme iron in Trypanosoma brucei CYP51 also forms direct contact with the I-helix via a network of hydrogen bonds with several I-helix residues. This network would strengthen the water-iron coordination, explaining why CYP51 proteins are found predominantly in the low-spin form ( 29 ) and why CYP51 proteins undergo only a partial low- to high-spin transition during substrate binding. The water molecule may not be expelled from CYP51 proteins by the substrate but only slightly shifted to one side, as observed in CYP46A1 ( 35 ). Titration of CaCYP51 with 0.5% (vol/vol) Tween 80, equivalent to that present in the stock lanosterol and eburicol solutions, caused no discernible change in the CaCYP51 absorbance spectrum. The Hill equation best fit the sterol saturation curves (Fig. 3C ), with K s values of 11.4 (±3.1) μM for lanosterol and 25.1 (±7.1) μM for eburicol, in comparison to 15.5 (±1.0) μM and 28.2 (±3.5) μM, respectively, using the Michaelis-Menten equation, indicating similar affinities for both substrates. The apparent Hill numbers were 0.6 to 0.8, suggesting mild negative cooperative allosterism for substrate binding. However, the apparent allosterism of substrate binding to CaCYP51 in free solution was not observed when CaCYP51 was reconstituted in DLPC micelles in the CYP51 assay, with the velocity curve obtained (Fig. 3C ) obeying Michaelis-Menten kinetics and yielding a K m value for lanosterol of 5.6 (±0.5) μM. This was similar to that previously reported (7.4 μM) ( 50 ) but 4-fold less than with 3β-hydroxylanost-7- en -32-ol (20 μM) ( 23 ). In comparison, the K m values previously reported for S. cerevisiae and human CYP51 were 5 μM ( 17 ) and 30 μM ( 7 ), respectively. The K s value for lanosterol of CaCYP51 (11.4 μM) was 11-fold greater than that obtained for M. tuberculosis CYP51 (1 μM) ( 5 ) and 23-fold greater than for human CYP51 (0.5 μM) ( 24 ). It is possible that the substrate binding properties of CaCYP51 in free solution differ from those within the lipid bilayer of the endoplasmic reticulum membrane, and this requires further investigation. Overall, the ability of lanosterol to exert a larger spin state change than eburicol indicated different physical properties but no conclusive evidence regarding the physiological CaCYP51 substrate preference.

FIG. 3.

Azole binding studies.

Fluconazole bound to CaCYP51 produced a type II difference spectrum (Fig. 4A ) characteristic of azole-inhibiting drugs. This was characterized by a peak at 429 nm and a trough at 411 nm, caused by the triazole ring N-4 nitrogen coordinating as the sixth ligand of the heme iron ( 12 ). The absolute spectrum of the resultant CaCYP51-fluconazole complex (data not shown) indicated a red shift of the Soret peak from 417 to 421 nm, with α, β, and δ bands centered at 573, 538, and 356 nm, respectively. Fluconazole binding studies performed with five different CaCYP51 concentrations revealed fluconazole binding to be tight (Fig. 4B ), with both the [fluconazole] 0.5 and Δ A max being proportional to the CaCYP51 concentration (Table 1 ). Tight-binding ligands are those for which the observed K d is similar to or below the enzyme concentration present ( 37 ). The high affinity for the ligand effectively means that fluconazole can be regarded as directly titrating the CaCYP51 protein. This was confirmed in the fluconazole saturation curves (Fig. 4B ), where the Morrison equation gave the best fit to the data, with the increase in Δ A being almost linear with respect to fluconazole concentration until 90% of the Δ A max was reached. The observed tight binding of fluconazole explained why the Hill and Michaelis-Menten equations could not be used to satisfactorily fit the data. The K d value of CaCYP51 for fluconazole was determined to be 46 to 59 nM based on the Morrison equation (Table 1 ). The cellular system that defines sensitivity to antifungals includes a number of factors, such as the drug affinity of the target, as well as other properties, such as lipophilicity and drug uptake/efflux, but not metabolism by the fungus in the case of azoles. C. albicans also has other CYPs that can bind antifungal azoles within the cell ( 36 ). Hence, K d values are of interest in terms of explaining part of the efficacy of antifungal drugs in arresting growth.

The saturation curves obtained with clotrimazole, econazole, itraconazole, ketoconazole, miconazole, and voriconazole (Fig. 4C ) indicated that all six azole drugs bound tightly to CaCYP51, with the Morrison equation providing the closest fit to the data. This was confirmed by performing saturation experiments using a 4-fold-lower CaCYP51 concentration (data not shown), which gave [azole] 0.5 values that were 3.3- to 4.2-fold lower. The [azole] 0.5 values were similar (Table 2 ) for all seven azoles; however, the K d values indicated that CaCYP51 had a 2- to 4-fold-lower affinity for fluconazole than the other six azoles, again indicating fluconazole as the weaker inhibitor. The spectral peaks (λ max ) and troughs (λ min ) were similar for all seven azoles studied (Table 2 ). However, the imidazole-based antifungals (clotrimazole, econazole, ketoconazole, and miconazole) gave more intense type II binding spectra, with approximately 40% larger Δ A max values than the triazole-based antifungals (fluconazole, itraconazole, and voriconazole). A lower Δ A max value suggests suboptimal coordination orientation or hindered binding ( 31 ) of the triazoles relative to the imidazole antifungals, although the inherent field strength of the ligand will also affect the observed intensity and wavelength of the spectrum. All seven azole antifungal drugs examined caused a red shift of between 4 and 6 nm of the CaCYP51 heme Soret band from 417 nm to 421 to 423 nm (Table 2 ) when bound under oxidative conditions.

Most previous azole binding studies with CaCYP51 have used microsomal preparations ( 22 , 23 , 50 ), making comparison of our results with previously published work on CaCYP51 difficult. Even where purified C. albicans CYP51 protein was used ( 4 , 19 ), analysis of the azole binding data was by the Michaelis-Menten or Hill equations, which is applicable for systems where the K d for the ligand is at least 5-fold higher than the concentration of enzyme ( 6 ). Previous work with S. cerevisiae microsomes containing wild-type C. albicans CYP51 gave [azole] 0.5 values approximately half the P450 concentration used, which is compatible with the binding observed being “tight” for fluconazole, itraconazole, and ketoconazole ( 22 , 23 ).

Azole K d values obtained in this study (10 to 50 nM) were comparable to the IC 50 s derived by Trösken et al. ( 50 ) from inhibition studies of CYP51 enzyme reactions (39 to 91 nM), the IC 50 s of 7.6 and 8 nM determined by Lamb et al. ( 21 ) for itraconazole and ketoconazole, the IC 50 of 30 nM determined for fluconazole by Kudo et al. ( 19 ), and the K i constants derived for the inhibition of the sterol demethylase reaction ( 22 ) of 20 and 30 nM for ketoconazole and fluconazole. However, the fluconazole K d values determined in this study were 80-fold lower than the 4 μM K d value determined previously by Bellamine et al. for fluconazole ( 4 ). As indicated above, the differences may have been due to the use of Michaelis-Menten or Hill equations to fit a tight ligand binding data set instead of the Morrison equation. Previous work ( 4 , 27 ) also used a four-histidine tag.

IC 50 experiments using fluconazole and itraconazole (Fig. 5 ) confirmed the tight azole binding previously observed during spectral titration of purified CaCYP51 protein with azoles. IC 50 s of 1.2 to 1.3 μM for both fluconazole and itraconazole (half of the CaCYP51 concentration present) were obtained, with total inhibition at 2.5 μM azole. These results are in agreement with previous in vitro CYP51 activity studies using purified CaCYP51 and microsomal protein fractions ( 4 , 19 , 22 , 23 , 50 ). However, MIC values for CYP51 activity using C. albicans cell extracts ( 51 ) previously indicated that itraconazole was 4-fold more effective as an inhibitor of CYP51 activity than fluconazole, in contrast to the 2-fold differences in K d values (Table 2 ) and the similar IC 50 s from the CYP51 reconstitution assays (Fig. 5 ). These discrepancies suggest that in vitro CYP51 enzyme assay systems using purified proteins in artificial DLPC micelles may not accurately mimic the in vivo structural arrangements of CaCYP51 molecules and partner cytochrome P450 reductase molecules/sterol biosynthesis system in the endoplasmic reticulum. This limits the usefulness of the in vitro CYP51 reconstitution assay system to a preliminary screening role in combination with direct ligand binding studies. Even cell-free systems may not be accurate predictors of the magnitude of in vivo efficacy of azole antifungal activity. Recently, we showed the value of humanized yeast strains in ranking the efficacy of azole inhibition of CYP51 ( 39 ).

FIG. 5.

CO displacement of bound azoles from CaCYP51.

We also examined the ability of CO to displace azoles from CaCYP51 to ascertain whether azoles can be ranked by this method. Formation of the [CO-P450] red complex with time ( A 446 ) at low azole concentrations (0 to 1.5 μM) was hyperbolic (data not shown), with CO preferentially binding to free CaCYP51 instead of displacing azole from CaCYP51-azole complexes. The t 1/2 values obtained during this hyperbolic phase were fairly constant at 1.3 to 2.3 min −1 for fluconazole but decreased from 1.3 to 0.22 min −1 for itraconazole. Formation of the [CO-P450] red complex at higher azole concentrations (2 μM and above) was almost entirely due to displacement of bound azole from CaCYP51 by CO (Fig. 6A ). The displacement of fluconazole by CO was linear against time over the first 14 min, remaining constant at 16.3, 16.9, 16.2, and 15.6 pmol min −1 for fluconazole concentrations of 2, 2.5, 3, and 4 μM, respectively. At higher fluconazole concentrations the rate of [CO-P450] red formation fell to 12.6, 10.5, and 7.0 pmol min −1 at 5, 10, and 25 μM fluconazole, respectively, probably due to the perturbation of the chemical equilibrium leading to increased flux through the reverse reaction of fluconazole displacing CO from CaCYP51. In contrast, no formation of [CO-P450] red was observed with itraconazole concentrations of 2 μM or above (Fig. 6A ). Therefore, CO was unable to displace itraconazole from CaCYP51, suggesting that CaCYP51 had a significantly higher affinity for itraconazole than fluconazole.

FIG. 6.

Analysis of [CO-P450] red produced after 14 min versus fluconazole and itraconazole concentration (Fig. 6B ) using the Morrison equation ( 37 ) gave K d values for fluconazole and itraconazole of 236 ± 75 nM and 36 ± 9 nM, respectively. Analysis of the [CO-P450] red produced after 2 min (data not shown) gave K d values of 187 ± 42 nM and 28 ± 9 nM for fluconazole and itraconazole, respectively. The affinity of CaCYP51 for fluconazole under reducing conditions appeared to be 7-fold less than for itraconazole, in comparison to 2-fold less under oxidative conditions.

CO displacement rates for 3 μM clotrimazole, fluconazole, and ketoconazole from CaCYP51 were similar, at 22.3, 16.2, and 20.1 pmol min −1 , respectively, whereas displacement of 3 μM econazole and miconazole was more rapid at 37.3 and 40.0 pmol min −1 . Voriconazole displacement was significantly lower, at just 7.8 pmol min −1 , and 3 μM itraconazole could not be displaced from CaCYP51 under the assay conditions. The CO displacement results suggest that itraconazole binds to CaCYP51 with the highest affinity of all seven azoles examined.

Expression levels of I471T CaCYP51 protein were 5-fold lower than the wild-type enzyme at just 27 (±3) nmol per liter of culture, limiting the scope of the initial characterization studies performed to substrate binding, fluconazole binding, and IC 50 determinations with fluconazole. Purification by Ni 2+ -NTA-agarose chromatography resulted in over 90% recovery of active protein and a 31-fold increase in purity compared with the solubilized cellular fraction. The absolute spectrum of the purified I471T CaCYP51 protein (data not shown) was indistinguishable from the wild-type protein, except the formation of the reduced CO complex was appreciably slower than the wild-type protein.

Progressive titration of I471T CaCYP51 with lanosterol gave a type I difference spectrum with a peak at 385 nm and a trough at 419 nm (data not shown) that was 3-fold more intense than for the wild-type protein (Fig. 7A ), indicating a 3-fold greater induced spin state change. I471T CaCYP51 had a 2.4-fold higher affinity for lanosterol ( K s , 4.8 ± 0.2 μM) than the wild-type protein ( K s , 11.6 ± 1.1 μM), suggesting that the I471T mutation conferred a modest increase in binding affinity for the CaCYP51 substrate. The IC 50 data (Fig. 7B ) confirmed that the I471T mutation conferred a catalytic advantage over the wild-type protein, with a 2-fold-higher lanosterol demethylase reaction velocity observed at fluconazole concentrations up to 2 μM. Trace amounts of catalytic product were detected with I471T CaCYP51 at fluconazole concentrations of 2 to 3 μM, in contrast to no detectable product observed with the wild-type CaCYP51. No product was detectable at 4 μM fluconazole with either the I471T or wild-type CaCYP51 proteins. The resulting IC 50 s were similar at 1.28 and 1.16 μM for the I471T and wild-type CaCYP51 proteins, respectively, confirming that both CaCYP51 proteins bound fluconazole tightly. Kakeya et al. ( 13 ) demonstrated that expression of I471T CaCYP51 in Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain DKY1 resulted in a modest 4-fold increase in MIC value toward fluconazole over the wild-type CaCYP51 protein, from 1.5 to 6 μg ml −1 . This 4-fold difference in MIC values ( 13 ) was not reflected in the IC 50 results per se (Fig. 7B ), suggesting that the small detectable CYP51 activity at higher fluconazole concentrations of I471T CaCYP51 is sufficient to support cell division at higher fluconazole concentrations.

FIG. 7.

Fluconazole bound strongly to both I471T and wild-type CaCYP51 proteins in the absence of lanosterol, producing type II binding spectra (Fig. 8A and B ) with a peak at 429 nm and a trough at 412 nm. In the presence of 70 μM lanosterol, strong type II fluconazole binding spectra were obtained, especially with I471T CaCYP51, except that the spectral troughs and peaks had undergone blue shifts to 390 nm and 424 nm, respectively. These spectral changes in wavelength and intensity in the presence of lanosterol were due to the increased spin state change caused by fluconazole, changing the high-spin CaCYP51-substrate complex (Soret peak at 393 nm) to form a low-spin CaCYP51-fluconazole complex (Soret peak at 421 nm).

FIG. 8.

Fluconazole saturation curves (Fig. 8C and D ) in the absence of lanosterol were best fit using the Morrison equation ( 33 ), indicating that the observed binding was tight. I471T CaCYP51 had a 2-fold-lower affinity for fluconazole ( K d , 84 ± 17 nM) in the absence of lanosterol than with the wild-type protein ( K d , 38 ± 14 nM). Therefore, the I471T point mutation did not confer fluconazole resistance by significantly altering the affinity of the free CaCYP51 protein for fluconazole. However, the presence of 70 μM lanosterol caused a change in the mode of fluconazole binding to I471T CaCYP51 from tight to normal hyperbolic ligand binding that could be described by the Michaelis-Menten equation and resulted in a 72-fold increase in the apparent K d value for fluconazole (6,079 ± 318 nM) compared to the wild-type protein (61 ± 14 nM). The I471T residue lies in the cysteine pocket behind the plane of the heme and therefore cannot directly interact with the substrate or inhibitor ( 44 ). Cysteine pocket residues provide contacts between β-sheet and α-helical domains, with mutations in this region inducing conformational changes between the domains relative to the heme ( 44 ) and so affecting substrate and inhibitor binding affinities. Spatial alterations caused by the I471T mutation appear to both restrict fluconazole access to the heme and alter fluconazole binding affinity when lanosterol is already bound to the I471T CaCYP51 protein rather than to wild-type CaCYP51. Control fluconazole binding determinations for both CaCYP51 proteins in the presence of 0.3% (vol/vol) Tween 80 (equivalent to that present in the 70 μM lanosterol experiments) gave no significant changes in observed K d values for fluconoazole, with the binding remaining tight.

Our preliminary characterization of the I471T CaCYP51 protein suggested that the increased resistance to fluconazole conferred by the I471T mutation results from a combination of increased catalytic turnover, increased affinity for substrate, and a reduced affinity for fluconazole in the presence of substrate in comparison to wild-type CaCYP51, allowing the I471T enzyme to remain functionally active, albeit at a much reduced velocity, at higher fluconazole concentrations. Similar studies may assist in understanding other point mutations associated with antifungal azole resistance and demonstrate mechanistically how these mutations cause resistance. Performing direct ligand binding studies using several different azole antifungals with mutant CaCYP51 proteins in combination with IC 50 sterol demethylase activity assays would establish whether a given point mutation confers azole resistance by altering the affinity of the CaCYP51 protein for the ligand. In addition, IC 50 studies would also establish whether azole resistance was conferred by increasing the turnover number of the CaCYP51 protein. Likewise, direct substrate binding studies using mutant CaCYP51 proteins would establish whether resistance was conferred by increasing the affinity of CaCYP51 for substrate.

As a screening tool, the studies presented demonstrate useful approaches for distinguishing between antifungal CYP51 inhibitors. Azole antifungal drugs consist of three main structural components: first, the imidazole or triazole aromatic ring, which is responsible for coordination to the heme iron atom through the N-3 atom of imidazole or N-4 atom of triazole; second, the halogenated aromatic constituent on the C-2 aliphatic carbon interacts with hydrophobic residues within the active site of the CaCYP51 apoprotein, such as the flat aromatic ring of Tyr132; third, the secondary aromatic side chain on the C-2 aliphatic carbon, which is the most variable constituent of azole antifungal drugs, ranging from another triazole ring in the case of fluconazole to the long aromatic side chains of itraconazole and posaconazole. These long secondary side chains interact with the hydrophobic amino acid residues lining the CaCYP51 substrate access channel, and such interactions have been modeled ( 20 , 55 ).

Our findings suggest that under oxidative conditions the affinities of purified wild-type C. albicans CYP51 for azole antifungals were similar, with K d values of 10 to 26 nM with the exception of fluconazole ( K d , 47 nM). Previously, the increased affinity of Candida species CYP51 for voriconazole over fluconazole was attributed to the extra methyl group present on the C-1 aliphatic carbon in voriconazole (Fig. 1 ), which results in stronger hydrophobic interactions with the aromatic amino acids lining the substrate binding pocket ( 10 ). The similar binding affinities observed for clotrimazole and itraconazole suggest that under oxidative conditions the side chain of an azole antifungal is not a major determinant of the exhibited binding affinity as measured by changes induced in the UV-Vis spectrum of the heme environment. However, our CO displacement experiments indicated that CaCYP51 had a 7-fold increased affinity for itraconazole over fluconazole, compared to only a 2-fold difference under oxidative conditions, suggesting a more prominent influence of the azole side chain in determining azole binding affinity in the CO displacement studies.

UV-Vis ligand binding and CO displacement studies can therefore be used in tandem to provide a preliminary screening mechanism for in silico -designed azole antifungal drug candidates of therapeutic interest prior to direct efficacy studies with the pathogen and in host-pathogen animal models. By building on this comprehensive investigation of clinically relevant azoles and their binding properties to purified wild-type CaCYP51, we are currently investigating the effects of CaCYP51 point mutations associated with azole resistance ( 9 , 30 , 32 , 34 , 47 , 54 , 56 ).
