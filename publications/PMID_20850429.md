Extracellular Hedgehog (Hh) proteins alter cellular behaviours from flies to man
by regulating the activities of Gli/Ci family transcription factors. A major
component of this response in Drosophila is the inhibition of proteolytic
processing of the latent transcriptional activator Ci-155 to a shorter Ci-75
repressor form. Processing is thought to rely on binding of the kinesin-family
protein Cos2 directly to Ci-155 domains known as CDN and CORD, allowing
Cos2-associated protein kinases to phosphorylate Ci-155 efficiently and create a
binding site for an E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. Here we show that the last
three zinc fingers of Ci-155 also bind Cos2 in vitro and that the zinc finger
region, rather than the CDN domain, functions redundantly with the CORD domain
to promote Hh-regulated Ci-155 proteolysis in wing discs. We also find evidence
for a unique function of Cos2 binding to CORD. Cos2 binding to CORD, but not to
other regions of Ci, is potentiated by nucleotides and abrogated by the
nucleotide binding variant Cos2 S182N. Removal of the CORD region alone enhances
processing under a variety of conditions. Most strikingly, CORD region deletion
allows Cos2 S182N to stimulate efficient Ci processing. We deduce that the CORD
region has a second function distinct from Cos2 binding that inhibits Ci
processing, and that Cos2 binding to CORD relieves this inhibition. We suggest
that this regulatory activity of Cos2 depends on a specific nucleotide-bound
conformation that may be regulated by Hh.

Introduction

Extracellular Hedgehog (Hh) family proteins regulate cell fates and proliferation in many species from flies to humans through a core, partially conserved signal transduction pathway (Ingham and McMahon, 2009; Jiang and Hui, 2008). In all well-studied examples, Hh proteins bind to a Patched receptor and co-receptors to activate a seven trans-membrane domain Smoothened (Smo) protein and, ultimately, alter the transcriptional activity of Gli/Ci family proteins. In Drosophila, full-length Ci (Ci-155) is held inactive in the cytoplasm by protein partners as it is phosphorylated and subsequently processed by the proteasome to a transcriptional repressor, Ci-75 in the absence of Hh (Jiang and Hui, 2008; Smelkinson et al., 2007; Tian et al., 2005). Hh leads to inhibition of Ci-155 processing and transcriptional activation of target genes by Ci-155. In mammals, Gli2 and Gli3 are regulated similarly to Ci, while Gli1, which is generally induced transcriptionally by Hh pathway activity, acts only as a regulated activator (Pan and Wang, 2007). Processing of Ci, Gli2 and Gli3 depends upon a similar set of Protein Kinase A (PKA), Casein Kinase 1 (CK1) and Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3 (GSK3) sites, whose phosphorylation promotes binding to a Slimb/β-TRCP E3 ubiquitin ligase (Smelkinson et al., 2007; Tempe et al., 2006; Wang and Li, 2006). In Drosophila Ci-155 that is protected from processing can be activated further by the action of the Fused (Fu) protein kinase or by removal of Suppressor of Fused (Su(fu)) (Ohlmeyer and Kalderon, 1998). Gli activators are also limited by Su(fu) in mammals but no role for a Fu-like protein has yet been demonstrated (Svard et al., 2006; Wilson et al., 2009).

Drosophila has provided the most sophisticated understanding to date of how Ci/Gli processing is accomplished and how it is blocked by Hh signaling. The kinesin-family protein Costal 2 (Cos2) is essential for Ci-155 processing (Methot and Basler, 2000; Sisson et al., 1997), while the Cos2 ortholog, Kif-7, is required for normal processing of mammalian and zebrafish Gli proteins (Ingham and McMahon, 2009). Cos2 was found to bind to Ci, PKA, CK1, and GSK3 and to promote Ci-155 phosphorylation, suggesting that a scaffolding function is important to ensure efficient Ci-155 phosphorylation and processing (Zhang et al., 2005). Furthermore, partial dissociation of Cos2 complexes has been inferred to mediate inhibition of Ci-155 processing by Hh (Ruel et al., 2003; Zhang et al., 2005). Whether Cos2 acts only to bring protein kinases to Ci-155 and how interactions among Ci-155, Cos2 and the three protein kinases are altered by Smo activation remain important outstanding questions likely to be pertinent to Hh signaling in all organisms.

Here we have examined the interactions between Ci-155 and Cos2 to discover that the zinc fingers of Ci-155 can substitute for the previously defined CORD domain (Wang et al., 2000) to promote Cos2-Ci association sufficient for normal, Hh-regulated Ci-155 processing. We also find that Cos2 binding to CORD depends on a nucleotide-bound conformation of Cos2, which has a unique function in promoting Ci-155 processing, countering an inhibitory action of the CORD region.

ResultsCi CDN and CORD domains are dispensable for Ci proteolysisCos2 is thought to promote Ci-155 phosphorylation and consequent processing by binding simultaneously to Ci-155 and the protein kinases, PKA, CK1 and GSK3 (Zhang et al., 2005). Two regions of Ci-155 (Fig. 1A), known as CDN (346-440) and CORD (941-1065), are individually sufficient to bind Cos2 in yeast two hybrid assays and removal of both regions was found to abrogate cytoplasmic retention of Ci by over-expressed Cos2 in wing discs and Ci-155 processing in tissue culture cells (Monnier et al., 2002; Wang et al., 2000; Wang and Jiang, 2004; Wang and Price, 2008). However, the requirement for these two domains in a physiological assay of Ci-155 processing in the intact organism has not been reported.We therefore generated transgenes encoding the appropriate Ci variants with C-terminal Myc-epitope tags to allow visualization of full-length protein levels in Drosophila wing discs, where Hh signaling has been studied most thoroughly. Here, Ci is normally expressed only in anterior compartment cells. Hh, emanating from posterior cells, inhibits Ci-155 processing in a band of anterior “AP border” cells, where elevated Ci-155 and induction of target genes, including the ptc-lacZ reporter, are readily observed (Fig. 1B). Ci-155 processing and its inhibition by Hh can be assayed in several ways for Ci transgene products.First, when UAS-Ci-Myc is expressed evenly throughout wing discs by using the C765-GAL4 driver at levels slightly lower (18C) or higher (25C) than endogenous Ci, Myc staining is lowest in anterior wing disc cells (Fig. 1C). Myc staining is higher in AP border and posterior cells because Hh inhibits Ci-155 processing (Smelkinson et al., 2007). The difference between anterior and posterior Myc levels is, however, smaller than the drastic underlying change in processing because Hh also promotes complete degradation of activated Ci-155 through an unrelated proteolytic mechanism (Jiang and Hui, 2008). Reduced anterior Myc staining is only observed for Ci transgenes subject to efficient Ci-155 processing (Smelkinson et al., 2007). To our surprise, this pattern was seen also for Ci variants lacking CDN, CORD or both CDN and CORD regions (CiΔCDNΔCORD) (Fig. 1D-F). Indeed, the reduced Myc staining of anterior cells for these variants was more pronounced than for wild-type Ci, especially for CΔCDNΔCORD, implying very efficient processing in anterior cells. Loss of smo activity in posterior clones also reduced Myc staining for CiΔCDNΔCORD (Fig. 1H), just as for Ci-WT (Smelkinson et al., 2007), confirming efficient reduction of full-length Ci levels in the absence of Hh signaling. Furthermore, CiΔCDN, CiΔCORD and CiΔCDNΔCORD each induced the Hh target gene ptc-lacZ ectopically only in posterior cells, just like Ci WT (Fig. 1B-F). Ci transgene products that are not processed efficiently generally induce ectopic ptc-lacZ expression in both anterior and posterior cells under these conditions (Smelkinson et al., 2007).Second, reduction of full-length Ci explicitly by processing it to Ci-75 repressor can be assayed directly either by Western blots using an N-terminal epitope tag or by repression of posterior hh-lacZ expression, which can be assayed in situ in smo mutant clones (to block Hh signaling) in posterior wing disc cells (where Ci derives only from the transgene). CiΔCDN, CiΔCORD and CiΔCDNΔCORD all strongly repressed hh-lacZ in posterior smo mutant clones (Fig. S1A-C). Furthermore, a Western blot of wing disc extracts expressing CiΔCDNΔCORD revealed a processed derivative which was slightly smaller than Ci-75, as expected because it lacks the CDN region, (Fig. 1J).Third, processing of wild-type Ci-155 depends on Cos2 activity and on phosphorylation of several defined sites in Ci, including S849 (Smelkinson et al., 2007). CiΔCDNΔCORD containing an S849A substitution, just like Ci-S849A (Smelkinson et al., 2007), exhibited equally strong Myc staining of full-length Ci protein in anterior and posterior cells and induced ptc-lacZ strongly in both compartments, indicating blocked processing (Fig. 1G). Myc staining of CiΔCDNΔCORD was also clearly elevated in anterior smo cos2 mutant clones compared to surrounding anterior cells (Fig. 1I arrowhead); it was also much higher in posterior smo cos2 mutant clones than in posterior smo mutant clones (Fig. 1H, I arrows). Thus, Ci lacking CDN and CORD regions is clearly processed very efficiently in a manner that depends on Cos2 activity and Ci phosphorylation, just as for wild-type Ci. A previous study of Ci processing in Kc tissue culture cells reached a different conclusion that efficient processing required the presence of either CDN or CORD, but the CDN deletion employed (1-440) in that study also removed more N-terminal residues (Wang and Price, 2008).A new Cos2 binding region on Ci: zinc fingersSince Cos2 is thought to promote Ci-155 processing through direct association we searched for additional Ci domains that might bind Cos2 by using a variety of purified GST-tagged Ci fragments to pull down Cos2 from extracts of Kc cells expressing HA-Cos2. HA-Cos2 bound strongly to GST-Ci 346-620 but not to GST alone (Fig. 2A). Surprisingly, the CDN region alone in GST-Ci 346-440 or in the larger GST-Ci 1-440 polypeptide did not bind HA-Cos2, whereas GST-Ci 440-620, containing only the zinc finger domain of Ci, pulled down HA-Cos2 very well. GST-Ci 346-620 still bound to HA-Cos2 when the first two zinc fingers (residues 440-506) were deleted but not when the last three zinc fingers (residues 506-613) were deleted (Fig. 2A). Thus, the last three zinc fingers in Ci, which mediate DNA binding (Pavletich and Pabo, 1993), can also bind Cos2 in vitro. Given prior evidence for CDN-Cos2 interactions (Monnier et al., 2002; Wang and Jiang, 2004), our failure to see Cos2 binding by the CDN domain alone might result from aberrant folding of the two Ci fragments tested. We can, however, be certain that the five Ci zinc fingers suffice for Cos2 binding.We deleted the last three zinc fingers in the context of a transgene encoding full-length Myc-tagged Ci to test the consequence of removing this newly discovered Cos2 binding domain in vivo. CiΔZn expressed evenly in wing discs using C765-GAL4 showed slightly lower Myc staining in anterior cells than in posterior cells (Fig. 2C,D). To be certain that Hh regulated the levels of full-length Ci lacking zinc fingers we looked at posterior smo mutant clones in wing discs expressing CiΔZn and saw a clear reduction in Myc staining (Fig. 2B). Ci lacking both CDN and zinc fingers showed an extremely clear pattern of reduced Myc staining in anterior cells (Fig. 2F), confirming efficient, Hh-regulated Ci proteolysis in the absence of Ci zinc fingers. By contrast, when Ci lacked both the CORD domain and zinc fingers, Myc staining was equally high in anterior and posterior cells, indicating impaired Ci proteolysis (Fig. 2E). The effect of deleting CORD in addition to zinc fingers was seen most clearly in Ci variants that also lacked the CDN domain. Myc staining was much lower in anterior cells than posterior cells for CiΔZnΔCDN, but was uniform throughout the wing disc for CiΔZnΔCDNΔCORD (Fig. 2F, G). Each pattern of Myc tag staining described for Ci variants lacking CDN, CORD and zinc finger domains, alone or in combination, was reproducible for all transgene insertion sites tested (see Methods). We could not use the more sensitive hh-lacZ repression assay to test whether Ci variants lacking zinc fingers might be processed to a small degree because those variants cannot bind DNA to effect hh-lacZ repression. We conclude that efficient Ci-155 proteolysis does not require the CDN region but does require the presence of either the last three zinc fingers or the CORD region.N- and C-terminal regions of Ci contribute to proteolysisWe investigated whether any other regions of Ci-155 were important for processing in wing discs. We found that Myc-tagged Ci lacking residues 1161-1370 or 1286-1370 exhibited equally high Myc staining in anterior and posterior wing disc cells, indicating defective processing, whereas processing appeared normal for Ci lacking residues 1201-1286 (Fig. 3A and data not shown). Consistently, CiΔC (1286-1370 deleted) induced ptc-lacZ in anterior as well as posterior cells (Fig. 3A), and failed to repress hh-lacZ in posterior smo mutant clones (Fig. S1H), indicating that no Ci-75 repressor was produced. The C-terminal region of Ci did not bind Cos2 in vitro as a GST-Ci 1286-1397 fusion protein (data not shown). Thus, the C-terminus of Ci has an essential role in Ci-155 processing that does not appear to be related to direct binding of Cos2.We also found that the N-terminus of Ci is required for normal regulation of full-length Ci levels and activity. Myc-tagged CiΔN (lacking residues 6-339) showed similar Myc staining in anterior and posterior wing disc cells, strong induction of anterior, as well as posterior, ptc-lacZ and no repression of hh-lacZ in posterior smo mutant clones (Fig. 3B and Fig. S1G). Failure to repress hh-lacZ normally indicates complete loss of Ci-155 processing (Smelkinson et al., 2007). However, for CiΔN it is possible that residues 6-339 are essential for repressor activity rather than for initiating proteolysis. Indeed, we believe that CiΔN is proteolyzed to some degree because, unlike CiΔC, ptc-lacZ induction is increased in posterior smo pka mutant clones compared to smo mutant clones (Fig. S1K-N), presumably because loss of PKA blocks residual proteolysis of CiΔN. We conclude that CiΔN is targeted inefficiently for proteolysis and that its proteolysis does not produce an active Ci repressor form.The N-terminus of Ci is known to include a binding site for Su(fu) (Monnier et al., 1998) but Su(fu) is known principally for restricting the activity of Ci-155 spared from proteolysis (Ohlmeyer and Kalderon, 1998). Loss of Su(fu) did not impair hh-lacZ repression by wild-type Ci in posterior smo mutant clones (Fig. S1J), confirming prior evidence that PKA-dependent Ci-155 processing can proceed without Su(fu) (Ohlmeyer and Kalderon, 1998). However, because Ci-155 and Ci-75 levels are both altered by Su(fu) (Ohlmeyer and Kalderon, 1998), a contribution of Su(fu) to the efficiency of Ci-155 processing cannot readily be ruled out. Thus, the inefficient proteolysis of Ci lacking residues 6-339 might be connected to loss of robust Su(fu) association.A novel function for CDN and CORD regions: opposing efficient Ci proteolysisIf the CORD region only functions redundantly with the zinc fingers of Ci to bind Cos2 and CDN has no function in Ci-155 processing, we would expect wild-type Ci and CiΔCDNΔCORD to be processed with equal efficiency. However, Myc staining in anterior wing disc cells was clearly much lower (compared to posterior cells) for CiΔCDNΔCORD than Ci-WT for all transgenic lines we examined, implying enhanced processing in the absence of Hh (Fig. 1C, F). We tested the inference that CDN and CORD normally reduce the efficiency of Ci-155 processing further by removing these domains from Ci in two situations where processing was impaired by additional changes.First, we used CiΔN to impair Ci proteolysis. Remarkably, removing CDN and CORD domains from Myc-tagged CiΔN reduced anterior Myc levels to below that of posterior wing disc cells in one of two transgenic lines expressing CiΔNΔCDNΔCORD (Fig. 3C). Thus, the C-terminus of Ci has an essential role in Ci-155 processing that does not appear to be related to direct binding of Cos2.

We also found that the N-terminus of Ci is required for normal regulation of full-length Ci levels and activity. Myc-tagged CiΔN (lacking residues 6-339) showed similar Myc staining in anterior and posterior wing disc cells, strong induction of anterior, as well as posterior, ptc-lacZ and no repression of hh-lacZ in posterior smo mutant clones (Fig. 3B and Fig. S1G). Failure to repress hh-lacZ normally indicates complete loss of Ci-155 processing (Smelkinson et al., 2007). However, for CiΔN it is possible that residues 6-339 are essential for repressor activity rather than for initiating proteolysis. Indeed, we believe that CiΔN is proteolyzed to some degree because, unlike CiΔC, ptc-lacZ induction is increased in posterior smo pka mutant clones compared to smo mutant clones (Fig. S1K-N), presumably because loss of PKA blocks residual proteolysis of CiΔN. We conclude that CiΔN is targeted inefficiently for proteolysis and that its proteolysis does not produce an active Ci repressor form.

The N-terminus of Ci is known to include a binding site for Su(fu) (Monnier et al., 1998) but Su(fu) is known principally for restricting the activity of Ci-155 spared from proteolysis (Ohlmeyer and Kalderon, 1998). Loss of Su(fu) did not impair hh-lacZ repression by wild-type Ci in posterior smo mutant clones (Fig. S1J), confirming prior evidence that PKA-dependent Ci-155 processing can proceed without Su(fu) (Ohlmeyer and Kalderon, 1998). However, because Ci-155 and Ci-75 levels are both altered by Su(fu) (Ohlmeyer and Kalderon, 1998), a contribution of Su(fu) to the efficiency of Ci-155 processing cannot readily be ruled out. Thus, the inefficient proteolysis of Ci lacking residues 6-339 might be connected to loss of robust Su(fu) association.

Regulation of Cos2 binding to the CORD region of Ci by nucleotidesPlus-end directed kinesins bind and hydrolyze ATP within a globular N-terminal motor domain that also binds directly to microtubules. Cycles of ATP binding, hydrolysis and dissociation induce a series of conformational changes that are coupled to movement along microtubules (Hirokawa et al., 2009). Cargo, typically bound to the C-terminal domain, is separated from the motor domain by a flexible hinge (or neck) region and an elongated coiled coil domain. Nucleotide-dependent kinesin conformational changes affect binding to microtubules and folding back of the motor domain on to the hinge region, both of which are maximal when ATP, rather than ADP is bound. Cos2 has been shown to move along microtubules and this activity is perturbed by alterations, including S182N, expected to block nucleotide binding or hydrolysis (Farzan et al., 2008). Furthermore, the regions of Cos2 implicated in binding to the CDN and CORD regions of Ci include the conformationally dynamic hinge region (Monnier et al., 2002; Wang and Jiang, 2004). We therefore investigated whether nucleotides affect the binding of Cos2 to Ci in vitro.We found that GST-Ci 620-1065 and GST-Ci 934-1065, which both include the CORD domain, bound moderately well to HA-Cos2 from Kc cell extracts but binding was greatly enhanced when ATP, ADP, AMP or AMP-PNP was added to the extracts prior to binding (Fig. 4). Binding of GST-Ci 346-620, which includes CDN and the zinc fingers, to HA-Cos2 was not increased by addition of the same nucleotides; in fact, ATP reproducibly reduced binding slightly (Fig. 4). Strikingly, Cos2 S182N showed no binding to GST-Ci 620-1065 or 934-1065, whether nucleotides were added or not, while binding to GST-Ci 346-620 was just as strong for Cos2 S182N as for wild-type Cos2 (Fig. 4 and Fig. S2B). Cos2 binding of a Ci fragment (GST-Ci 346-1065) containing all three potential Cos2 binding domains was not significantly stimulated by nucleotides and was not significantly impaired by the S182N substitution in Cos2 (Fig. S2C), in keeping with the idea that only binding of Cos2 to the CORD region is regulated by nucleotides. A second Cos2 variant, G175A, also expected to be deficient for binding the β-phosphate of nucleotides, showed a similar loss of ATP-stimulated binding to GST-Ci 934-1065 (Fig. 4). We deduce that the high affinity state of Cos2 for the CORD domain requires nucleotide binding and that the failure of Cos2 S182N to adopt a normal nucleotide-bound conformation prevents it from binding to the CORD domain. In the absence of normal nucleotide binding, as in Cos2 S182N, Cos2 still binds well to the N-terminus of Ci, which we now believe to be mediated principally by the last three zinc fingers of Ci.

DiscussionCos2 is a major regulator of Ci activity. It is absolutely required for Ci-155 processing and Ci targets are ectopically activated in the absence of Cos2 (Jiang and Hui, 2008). Prior to this study it was thought that Cos2 regulates Ci by binding to specific protein kinases and directly to Ci-155 via two regions, CDN and CORD to promote Ci-155 phosphorylation. Experiments with tissue culture cells suggested that CDN and CORD regions of Ci-155 act largely redundantly to promote Ci-155 processing (Wang and Price, 2008). Our investigations have modified these views in two significant ways. First, our studies in the physiological setting of Drosophila wing discs confirm some functional redundancy of two Cos2-binding regions on Ci to promote Ci proteolysis, but the region acting together with the CORD binding site comprises the last three zinc fingers of Ci, not the CDN region. Second, we found that the CORD region has an additional unique function of inhibiting Ci proteolysis unless it binds to Cos2. Cos2 is a major regulator of Ci activity. It is absolutely required for Ci-155 processing and Ci targets are ectopically activated in the absence of Cos2 (Jiang and Hui, 2008). Prior to this study it was thought that Cos2 regulates Ci by binding to specific protein kinases and directly to Ci-155 via two regions, CDN and CORD to promote Ci-155 phosphorylation. Experiments with tissue culture cells suggested that CDN and CORD regions of Ci-155 act largely redundantly to promote Ci-155 processing (Wang and Price, 2008). Furthermore, we uncovered the potential for Cos2-CORD association to be regulated by nucleotide binding to Cos2 and evidence that Hh signaling may modulate Cos2 function in at least two ways to regulate its interaction with Ci.

Other modulators of Ci proteolysis

We found that phosphorylation of Ci at specific sites in the CORD domain (PKA site S962) and the Slimb-binding region (especially GSK3 sites primed by PKA site S892) reduced binding of the CORD region to Cos2 in vitro. That investigation was prompted by prior knowledge that loss of PKA sites in the CORD region appeared to enhance Ci activity (Price and Kalderon, 1999). However, that observation would more readily be explained by increased, rather than decreased, Cos2-Ci binding in response to Ci phosphorylation. An alternative hypothesis is that the observed dependence of Cos2-CORD binding on Ci phosphorylation might contribute to extending a graded Hh response. Where Hh levels are high, Ci-155 will be less phosphorylated and would bind Cos2 more readily, requiring a strong Hh signal to disrupt Cos2-CORD association. At the edge of Hh signaling territory Ci-155 will be more highly phosphorylated, would bind Cos2 less readily and hence allow only a very low level of Hh to disrupt Cos2-CORD association and inhibit Ci-155 processing. We do not, however, currently have any in vivo evidence testing that hypothesis.

While large regions of Ci (CDN and CORD) could be deleted without impairing proteolysis, implying that Ci is composed largely of independently folding domains, we identified two other deletions (of residues 6-339 and 1286-1377) with significant effects on proteolysis. Ci lacking C-terminal residues did not generate any detectable Ci-75 repressor. CiΔC strongly induced ptc-lacZ, implying that binding to CBP, which has been mapped to an adjacent region of Ci and is required for Ci-155 activity (Akimaru et al., 1997), was not affected. How the C-terminus of Ci contributes to proteolysis remains a mystery since we have no evidence from binding assays or co-localization studies in tissue culture cells showing association with Cos2 or Cos2-associated factors.

A study using Kc tissue culture cells previously identified the extreme C-terminus of Ci as essential for Ci processing (Wang and Price, 2008). That study also found that the zinc fingers of Ci were not essential for processing, provided they were substituted by a stably folded domain that contributes to the arrest of proteasome digestion. That result is consistent with our observation that CiΔZn is efficiently proteolyzed in wing discs.
