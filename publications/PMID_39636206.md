PURPOSE: Although the B-cell receptor (BCR) signal plays a critical role in
chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) cell survival and a target of current
therapies (ibrutinib targets Bruton's tyrosine kinase; idelalisib targets
PI3Kδ), contribution of the cytokine-driven JAK2 pathway to the "CLL
cell-survival signaling network" is largely undefined.
EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN: Patients with CLL were enrolled to investigate
expression/activation of JAK2 and acylglycerol kinase (AGK), and their
functional implication in primary CLL cell survival. A series of biochemical and
molecular biology assays were employed to uncover the underlying mechanism.
RESULTS: We detected that compared with normal B cells, CLL cells aberrantly
express constitutively active JAK2. Mechanistically, HSP90 forms a chaperoning
complex with JAK2, resulting in its aberrant accumulation in CLL cells. We also
discovered aberrant upregulation of a novel mitochondrial lipid kinase, AGK,
which remains complexed with HSP90 in CLL cells activating JAK2. Although AGK is
typically mitochondrial, we detected its nuclear localization in association
with JAK2 in some CLL cells. Functionally, JAK2 phosphorylates its noncanonical
substrate, histone H3(Y41), but not STAT3, activating transcription of diverse
sets of genes in a patient-specific manner. Additionally, JAK2 activates the BCR
signal in CLL cells via LYN/Bruton's tyrosine kinase axis. Targeted inhibition
of JAK2 as monotherapy, or in combination with the BCR inhibitors or venetoclax
(a BCL2 inhibitor), induced apoptosis synergistically in CLL cells.
CONCLUSIONS: These findings suggest that aberrantly expressed AGK activates
JAK2, independent of cytokine, leading to activation of diverse sets of gene
transcription in CLL cells. Combined targeting of JAK2 and BCR signals or BCL2
may be effective in some patients with CLL.

To achieve such a goal, it is critical to identify other druggable targets, which are constitutively expressed/activated to influence the clinical outcome. Although the Janus kinase (JAK)-pathway is known to be associated with other hematologic neoplasia, its role in CLL cell signaling and therapy is not clearly defined.

Deregulated protein kinases play a pivotal role in cancer progression via cell proliferation, survival, invasiveness, and therapeutic resistance(8, 9). JAKs are a family of non-receptor tyrosine kinases (JAK1, JAK2, JAK3 and Tyk2), that participate in signaling from a range of cell-surface receptors, particularly members of the cytokine receptor superfamily, which lack intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity. JAKs are nearly ubiquitously expressed, except for JAK3, which is only expressed in hematopoietic, vascular smooth muscle, and endothelial cells(10). JAK2 signaling among other members of the JAK-family is important in both physiology and pathophysiology, as it plays prominent roles in embryonic development and hematopoiesis, as well as in cancer and inflammation(11, 12). JAK2 is typically located in the cytoplasm, binds to the membrane proximal proline-rich region of ligand-dependent receptors(13, 14), and acts as a well-known positive regulator of transcription of STAT-targeted genes, such as BCL2, BCL-xL, survivin, VEGF, MYC and cyclin D1(15). These reports justify why JAK2 emerged as an attractive therapeutic target for many cancer types including myeloid malignancies(16), B cell lymphomas(17, 18), and breast cancers(19).

In this study, we discovered aberrant expression of a novel mitochondrial lipid kinase AGK, and constitutively active JAK2 in CLL cells. We showed that AGK activates JAK2 in CLL cells, independent of cytokine-involvement and co-migrates into the nucleus of CLL cells. In addition to our finding that JAK2 regulates the BCR signal, it also phosphorylates its non-canonical target histone H3 activating a diverse set of genes in CLL cells. Our study also suggests that JAK2-inhibition in combination with ibrutinib/idelalisib or venetoclax can be capable of inducing synergistic leukemic cell death in CLL patients.

DiscussionAberrantly activated JAK2/STAT3 is frequently involved in oncogenesis, angiogenesis, and metastasis of many cancers including hematologic malignancies(14, 15, 51–53) that are usually refractory to standard chemotherapy(54). In this study, we have detected aberrant expression of JAK2 in CLL cells, which remains catalytically active. Myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) cells express JAK2 bearing V617F activating mutation and are reported to be physically associated with HSP90(55). However, analysis of the publicly available dataset (https://www.cbioportal.org/) from a total 1308 CLL patients did not reveal V617F mutation in JAK2(56–59), indicating that constitutive activation of JAK2 in CLL cells is not due to V617F mutation.Previously, we have shown that HSP90 is overexpressed in CLL cells, forming a multi-molecular complex with the BCR signal mediators(25). Here, our findings add important biologic information that HSP90 can chaperon JAK2 as well; resulting in aberrant accumulation of the kinase in CLL cells and provides novel information in a growing list of “client proteins” of HSP90. Cancer cells use the evolutionarily conserved HSP90 chaperone machinery to protect an array of mutated and overexpressed oncoproteins, kinases from misfolding and rapid degradation(60). Our work shows that HSP90 may likely be a crucial facilitator of kinase addiction and cell survival in CLL cells.Additionally, we also discovered aberrant expression of a novel mitochondrial lipid kinase, AGK, in CLL cells. Interestingly, we found that the aberrant accumulation of AGK was, at least in part, due to its complex formation with HSP90 in CLL cells. Functionally, this multi-substrate lipid kinase catalyzes the production of lysophosphatidic acid and phosphatidic acid from monoacylglycerol and diacylglycerol(61–63). Earlier studies reported that overexpression of AGK leads to activation of EGFR and promotes proliferation and migration of prostate cancer cells; suggesting that AGK might act as a potent oncogene(61, 64). Aberrantly activated JAK2/STAT3 is frequently involved in oncogenesis, angiogenesis, and metastasis of many cancers including hematologic malignancies(14, 15, 51–53) that are usually refractory to standard chemotherapy(54). In this study, we have detected aberrant expression of JAK2 in CLL cells, which remains catalytically active. Myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) cells express JAK2 bearing V617F activating mutation and are reported to be physically associated with HSP90(55). However, analysis of the publicly available dataset (https://www.cbioportal.org/) from a total 1308 CLL patients did not reveal V617F mutation in JAK2(56–59), indicating that constitutive activation of JAK2 in CLL cells is not due to V617F mutation.

Previously, we have shown that HSP90 is overexpressed in CLL cells, forming a multi-molecular complex with the BCR signal mediators(25). Here, our findings add important biologic information that HSP90 can chaperon JAK2 as well; resulting in aberrant accumulation of the kinase in CLL cells and provides novel information in a growing list of “client proteins” of HSP90. Cancer cells use the evolutionarily conserved HSP90 chaperone machinery to protect an array of mutated and overexpressed oncoproteins, kinases from misfolding and rapid degradation(60). Our work shows that HSP90 may likely be a crucial facilitator of kinase addiction and cell survival in CLL cells.

Additionally, we also discovered aberrant expression of a novel mitochondrial lipid kinase, AGK, in CLL cells. Interestingly, we found that the aberrant accumulation of AGK was, at least in part, due to its complex formation with HSP90 in CLL cells. Functionally, this multi-substrate lipid kinase catalyzes the production of lysophosphatidic acid and phosphatidic acid from monoacylglycerol and diacylglycerol(61–63). Earlier studies reported that overexpression of AGK leads to activation of EGFR and promotes proliferation and migration of prostate cancer cells; suggesting that AGK might act as a potent oncogene(61, 64). Indeed, high AGK levels in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma(65), and with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma(66) are reported to be associated with poor prognosis. We found that AGK does translocate into the nucleus of CLL cells, although this discovery in terms of its overall functional implication remains elusive and is the subject of our future work. Interestingly, we found that partial depletion of AGK in CLL cells resulted in reduced catalytic activity of JAK2, while it did not show any inhibitory effect on P-STAT3-Y705 levels. Our findings also demonstrate that AGK can activate JAK2 via a protein-protein interaction and co-localize in both cytoplasm and nucleus of CLL cells. Translocation of JAK2, a cytoplasmic non-RTK, in the nucleus of leukemic cells is of specific interest, as it is known to regulate downstream signaling of multiple cytokine receptors(36). To our knowledge, our data are the first to demonstrate that JAK2 and AGK can co-migrate into the nucleus of CLL cells while the exact mechanism of this translocation remains to be elucidated.

Accumulation of CLL cells in the typical homing sites (lymph nodes, spleen, bone marrow) consisting of resident myeloid cells, mesenchymal stromal cells, T- and NK-cells, create a leukemia cell survival-supportive niche(67). The interaction between CLL cells and various resident cells in tissue microenvironments cooperatively generates a chronic inflammatory and chemokine milieu which may include IL1α/β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, TNF-α, CXCL4, CXCL10, and CXCL12, and activate a signaling network within CLL cells, promoting their survival, migration and proliferation(67). Thus, enhanced activation of JAK2 in CLL cells may be achieved via interaction with specific cytokines that are produced in CLL tumor microenvironment.

BTK regulates the BCR pathway, which plays a central role in CLL pathogenesis. Interestingly, an early study suggested that heterotrimeric G-proteins and JAK2 are both necessary for CXCR4-induced BTK activation(41). However, through a series of experiments, we have demonstrated that JAK2 can directly regulate the BCR signal via LYN/BTK and PI3K/AKT signaling axes thus maintaining an uninterrupted BCR survival signal in CLL cells.

Furthermore, our experimental evidence indicates that constitutively active JAK2 is not involved in regulating its canonical target STAT3 in steady-state CLL cells. Instead, we found JAK2-mediated phosphorylation of its non-canonical nuclear substrate, histone H3(Y41) in CLL cells. Phosphorylation of H3Y41 by JAK2 results in release of the transcriptional repressor heterochromatin protein 1α (HP1α) from chromatin, resulting in transcription of genes repressed by HP1α(39). While cytoplasmic JAK2 primarily regulates gene expression through activation of STATs(38), it adds evidence that the function of nuclear-JAK2 is not the same as that of cytoplasmic-JAK2. Given that the histone H3(Y41) lies within a region known to affect nucleosome-remodeling(68), JAK2-mediated phosphorylation of H3(Y41) may regulate chromatin architecture around specific gene promoters. The JAK2-H3Y41-HP1α signaling specifically targets thousands of loci, many of which lack identifiable STAT-binding elements(17). Thus, the signaling axis is likely to activate an additional set of target genes distinct from those regulated by JAK-STAT pathways. Indeed, our RNA-seq data supports this concept, revealing a patient-specific regulation of unique sets of genes in JAK2-depleted CLL cells, which is likely due to the extensive biologic heterogeneity of the CLL clones. In line with this concept, we found significant alteration of 1152 and 848 gene transcripts in CLL cells from the patients, P23 and P53 respectively upon JAK2-depletion, while about 2000 genes were differentially expressed in JAK2-knockdown leukemic cells of P21, P22 and P63. Further analysis identified several transcripts known to be the downstream targets of the BCR signal (KEGG database) and histone H3(Y41) axis according to a ChIP-seq analysis(48). The later study(48) identified 1841 putative target genes of the P-histone H3(Y41)/JAK2 axis in a human erythroid leukemia cell line. Given that we identified altered expressions of many other coding/non-coding or pseudogene transcripts in JAK2-depleted CLL cells which are not listed in the above study or any earlier reports, these transcripts may likely be the new targets of the JAK2/histone H3(Y41) pathway which warrant further in-depth investigation. It is also tempting to speculate that these putative targets of the JAK2-signaling at the steady-state level may have functional implications in CLL pathobiology, as indicated by the fact that P-H3(Y41)-mediated HP1α-displacement can occur at loci of proto-oncogenes(39). Thus, a better understanding of nuclear JAK2 pathways, including discovery of new substrates and target genes for nuclear-JAK2, that are druggable will provide a broader and better basis for designing new therapeutic strategies targeting JAK2 in CLL and other human cancers.

Pathway analysis using all the significantly altered genes indicates enrichment of several signaling axes relevant in cancer cell growth, proliferation, and chemotaxis (Supplementary Table S8). Thus, to further interrogate the impact of JAK2-signaling on chemotaxis, for example, JAK2 in CLL cells was targeted using fedratinib or a specific-siRNA, followed by induction of migration by adding the chemokine CXCL12 in a Boyden chamber. We found that inhibition of JAK2 reduced spontaneous migration of steady-state CLL cells by ~60–95%. However, migration of CLL cells was increased by about 7–14-fold in response to CXCL12, which was reduced substantially even below the steady-state level upon JAK2-inhibition (Supplementary Fig. S9, panels A–B). A partial depletion of JAK2 also inhibited CXCL12-induced migration of leukemic cells (panels C–D), supporting the above findings. Therefore, these results are in agreement with the pathway analysis prediction, at least in part, that JAK2 does regulate CLL cell migration both at the steady-state and chemokine-induced levels.

Given our findings that aberrantly active JAK2 regulates multiple signals, we hypothesized that JAK2-inhibition would induce apoptosis in CLL cells. This is supported by the observation that CLL cells from 5 of 7 patients tested were responsive to JAK2-inhibition showing apoptosis-induction. Of interest, we also found that some CLL cells (3 of 6) responded synergistically to combined treatment with fedratinib and ibrutinib or idelalisib. It is tempting to speculate that patient-specific regulation of diverse sets of genes by the activated JAK2/BCR/H3(Y41) signaling axes may result in such a heterogeneous response. An earlier study(31) showed significant CLL cell death when a JAK2-inhibitor (AG490) was combined with ibrutinib in vitro, albeit at much higher doses (50μM and 2.5μM, respectively). Our findings indicate that combination of fedratinib with ibrutinib or idelalisib can be beneficial for some CLL patients at much lower doses. This is important information, as side effects of these targeted-agents can be significantly lowered thus likely more tolerable; which is the most crucial factor for the older CLL patients under the burden of other co-morbid ailments. However, it is of specific interest that our in vitro evidence indicates that fedratinib can act better with venetoclax, rather with ibrutinib/idelalisib, in inducing a robust level of apoptosis in CLL cells synergistically. The failure of targeting BCR signaling in combination with JAK2 inhibitor to generate consistent synergistic apoptotic responses in CLL cells can be explained with our findings that JAK2 and BTK/PI3K can act on the same pathways. Conversely, fedratinib and venetoclax target different pathways, thus producing a consistent robust cytotoxic effect on leukemic B-cells, suggesting that this combination can be a more effective strategy in treating CLL patients, particularly those who become refractory to BTK-inhibitors or relapsed.

In summary, we, for the first time, showed that an aberrantly expressed mitochondrial lipid kinase AGK regulates the basal catalytic activity of JAK2 in CLL cells. Importantly, we found that HSP90 forms a chaperoning complex with both JAK2 and AGK in CLL cells resulting in aberrant accumulation of these kinases in CLL cells. Therapeutically, our study indicates that while combination of fedratinib with ibrutinib/idelalisib augmented apoptosis levels in some CLL cells, administration of fedratinib with venetoclax can exert a more pronounced, synergistic cytotoxic effects on leukemic B-cells. Thus, the latter approach appears to be promising and of great importance in conducting a phase-1 clinical trial to treat high-risk CLL patients who become resistant to ibrutinib and/or venetoclax therapy or relapsed. However, future studies are also needed to understand more completely the AGK-JAK2-BCR-H3(Y41) functional loop in regulating survival of drug-resistant/relapsed CLL clones.
