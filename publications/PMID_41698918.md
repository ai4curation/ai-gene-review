Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) are key regulators of cellular signaling and
are often co-opted in cancer. ROS1 is an orphan RTK aberrantly expressed in
multiple tumors, yet no approved biologic therapies target it, and its
activation mechanism remains unknown. Here, we present Cryo-EM structures of
mammalian ROS1 in ligand-free and NELL2-bound states, revealing how trimeric
NELL2 induces both receptor clustering and a conformational switch that relieves
receptor autoinhibition - both mechanisms are required for ROS1 activation.
These structures, along with biochemical characterization, reflect a striking
evolutionary divergence in regulatory logic compared to the invertebrate
ortholog Sevenless (dROS1), highlighting how conserved RTKs can adopt
fundamentally different activation strategies. Guided by these structural
insights, we develop monoclonal antibodies that either block ligand binding or
trap ROS1 in an inactive conformation. These agents potently suppress ROS1
signaling, representing distinct mechanistic classes of biologics that directly
target ROS1 activity. Our findings elucidate a distinct mode of RTK regulation
and establish a therapeutic framework for cancers driven by ROS1.

Under normal physiological conditions, ROS1 is essential for the development of the epididymis11,12. In ROS1 knock-out mice, the epididymis is poorly differentiated, resulting in infertility due to impaired sperm maturation12. In cancer, ROS1 is often associated with gene fusion-driven (ligand-independent) activation13, as seen in ~1% of glioblastomas14,15 and <2% of non-small-cell lung cancers16–18. However, full-length (non-fusion) ROS1 has been reported to be expressed in ~30% of primary brain tumors19,20, 30% of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)21, and 80–100% of oral squamous cell carcinomas22. Additionally, full-length ROS1 is upregulated in E-cadherin-deficient breast cancers, where inhibition of ROS1 with tyrosine kinase inhibitors leads to synthetic lethality23. Consequently, targeting the extracellular region of ROS1 presents a promising strategy for controlling multiple types of cancer. Despite this potential, the absence of experimental evidence regarding the structure and regulation of ROS1’s extracellular region (ECR) has hindered the development of therapeutic biologics.

Recently, NELL2 (Neuronal EGF-Like-Like 2) has been identified as a possible agonist for mammalian ROS124. NELL2 is a multimeric, multidomain secreted glycoprotein composed of an N-terminal laminin-G domain, a coiled-coil domain, five von Willebrand factor type C (VWC) domains, and six epidermal growth factor (EGF) repeats25,26 (Fig. 1a). Previous studies have shown that NELL2 plays a role in axon guidance by interacting with ROBO327,28. However, the mechanism by which NELL2 regulates ROS1 activation remains unclear. In this study, we demonstrate that NELL2 binds directly to ROS1 with high affinity and enhances the receptor’s intracellular kinase activity. Using cryo-electron microscopy (Cryo-EM), we determine the structures of ROS1’s extracellular region (ECR) both in the absence and presence of NELL2, revealing significant ligand-induced conformational changes essential for ROS1 activation. The activation of ROS1 integrates two distinct regulatory mechanisms, where NELL2’s oligomerization and its induction of a ROS1 conformational change are required for activation. Furthermore, we identify two inhibitory antibodies that target ROS1’s critical regulatory mechanisms. Our findings provide a molecular basis for the regulation of ROS1 activity, highlight the pivotal role of NELL2 in inducing these structural transitions, and open avenues for therapeutic interventions targeting ROS1-expressing cancers.

ResultsNELL2 is a direct agonist for ROS1Previous studies have established that the lumicrine factor NELL2 plays a role in modulating ROS1-related cell differentiation within the mouse epididymis24. Our research aimed to further delineate this pathway by investigating whether NELL2 could directly interact with and influence ROS1 activation. We engineered expression constructs for the entire extracellular region (ECR) of murine ROS1 and full-length human NELL2 (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a–d). Through biolayer interferometry (BLI) analysis, we demonstrate that NELL2 binds directly to the N-terminal ECR of ROS1 with high affinity, yielding an equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of 116 nM (Fig. 1b). This affinity is similar to other soluble growth factors binding to their receptors (e.g., the KD of EGF binding to EGFR is 175 nM)29.Having established direct binding, we sought to determine if NELL2 alone could stimulate ROS1 phosphorylation in cells. A significant challenge for studying ROS1 activity in cells is that no cell lines are known to endogenously express full-length ROS1. Moreover, repeated attempts to generate stable ROS1-expressing heterologous cell lines by transfection have failed to yield detectable protein.9. To overcome this challenge, we devised a chimeric construct combining the ECR of ROS1 with the kinase domain of ALK, resulting in an NIH/3T3 stable cell line that expresses ROS1’s ECR with ALK’s kinase domain (designated ROS1AK). This design allowed the use of well-established phospho-ALK antibodies for detection and is particularly relevant since ROS1 and ALK’s kinase domains are phylogenetically closest to each other9,13,30 (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). Utilizing this ROS1AK stable cell line, we demonstrate that NELL2 triggers robust receptor and ERK phosphorylation (Fig. 1c) in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 2d), a response not observed in the control ALK-expressing cell line (Supplementary Fig. 2c).RTK signaling is typically stimulated by clustering receptors along with their intracellular tyrosine kinase domains. Consequently, oligomeric ligands commonly mediate activation of different RTKs1,31. Therefore, we investigated the role of NELL2’s oligomerization on ROS1 activity by generating a deletion mutant that removes NELL2’s coiled-coil domain (NELL2ΔCC). NELL2ΔCC expressed well and purified as a monomer (Supplementary Fig. 3a). NELL2ΔCC was able to bind with the same affinity as wild-type NELL2 to ROS1 as determined by BLI (Fig. 1c). However, NELL2ΔCC was unable to stimulate ROS1 and ERK phosphorylation in ROS1AK stable cells (Fig. 1d). Taken together, these findings establish NELL2 as a direct agonist for ROS1 and show that ligand oligomerization is necessary for inducing ROS1 activation.To further define the stoichiometry of the NELL2–ROS1 receptor interaction, we performed size-exclusion chromatography coupled with multi-angle light scattering (SEC–MALS) (Supplementary Fig. 3b). NELL2 alone eluted at its expected trimeric mass (276.9 kDa). Titrating increasing amounts of ROS1 into a fixed concentration of trimeric NELL2 revealed robust formation of 1:1 (trimeric NELL2:ROS1) and 1:2 complexes. Further increasing ROS1 to a 1:3 ratio produced a species of ~711.8 kDa, smaller than the predicted mass of a fully saturated NELL2–ROS1 complex, consistent with incomplete occupancy of NELL2’s third binding site. Model of ROS1 activationOur finding that mutations that disrupt NELL2 binding to the FNIII domains (site 2) substantially impair signaling (Fig. 2e–h) indicates that receptor clustering alone is insufficient and that the conformational switch is required for activation. To investigate this requirement, we modeled an encounter complex (site 1 only) in which ROS1 adopts its rigid bent-over conformation and is docked onto all three binding sites of an AlphaFold-modeled NELL2 trimer. In this configuration, the extracellular juxtamembrane regions remain widely spaced, suggesting that ligand-mediated clustering of bent-over ROS1 does not position the kinase domains close enough for productive signaling (Fig. 3f). This observation led us to hypothesize that the bent-over conformation corresponds to an auto-inhibited state. In this model, the N-terminal “arm” region—composed of the CATCH domain and the first two FNIII domains—engages the long distal leg region. This stabilizes both the leg and arm regions together into a relatively rigid structure. Subsequent binding of NELL2 to ROS1’s arm, FNIII domains (site 2), breaks these interactions. Without the restraints between the arm and leg, the leg region becomes decoupled from the ligand-binding domains. While we do not observe a defined new conformation for the released leg, its increased mobility may allow the transmembrane helices to reposition into closer proximity, facilitating activation of the intracellular kinase domains.

As they share a similar binding footprint, we predict that binding of Fab-RX5 to ROS1 would sterically block NELL2 binding to site 1.

To determine how Fab-RX5 impacts NELL2 binding, we used surface plasmon resonance (SPR, Biacore) to monitor ROS1’s ability to bind ligand in the presence of Fab. In the absence of Fab-RX5, ROS1 binds readily to a surface covalently coupled with NELL2 (Fig. 4g). However, when ROS1 is preincubated with an excess of Fab-RX5 (1:10), ROS1 is prevented from binding to the NELL2 coated surface (Fig. 4g). Thus, RX5 binding to Site 1 on ROS1 is sufficient to block the receptor’s ability to engage ligand— similar to the impact of Site 1 mutations.

To further test if Fab-RX5 can inhibit ROS1 cellular signaling, we pre-incubated ROS1AK cells with Fab-RX5 prior to stimulation by NELL2. In this context, Fab-RX5 was able to inhibit ROS1 signaling by NELL2 in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 4h). Additionally, a full antibody based on Fab-RX5 (IgG-RX5) robustly inhibits ROS1 signaling at low nM concentrations (Fig. 4i). We next asked whether the bivalent IgG-RX5 is capable of clustering ROS1 receptors using SEC–MALS. Addition of ROS1 at a concentration sufficient to fully saturate IgG–RX5 yielded a predominant 1:2 (IgG–RX5:ROS1) complex, with an apparent molecular weight of ~537.2 kDa (Supplementary Fig. 10b). These data indicate that an antibody recognizing the ligand-binding epitope (site 1) can cluster ROS1 in a manner analogous to NELL2. Taken together, these results demonstrate that an antibody that blocks ligand binding can prevent ligand-induced ROS1 activation. And importantly, that simply cross-linking receptors via a bivalent antibody is insufficient to induce receptor activation—consistent with the need for a conformational change to overcome auto-inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 10c).

Unexpectedly, we find that despite being structurally different receptor tyrosine kinases, ROS1 and EGFR share commonalities in their regulation. Firstly, both receptors interact with the EGF domains of their cognate ligands. Secondly, EGF domain binding to ROS1 stabilizes a large conformational change (~130° rotation) that is reminiscent of those orchestrated by EGF domain binding to EGFR and other ErbB receptors43 (Supplementary Fig. 15). In each case, EGF domain binding relieves intramolecular auto-inhibitory constraints. However, unlike EGFR/ErbB receptors, where ligand binding exposes a dimerization arm that forms a direct receptor–receptor interface, NELL2-bound ROS1 does not generate an equivalent ROS1–ROS1 contact; instead, activation depends on NELL2-mediated clustering of conformationally unlocked receptors (Supplementary Fig. 15).

A recent study demonstrated that truncation of the ROS1 extracellular region (ECR) can lead to ligand-independent activation of the receptor44. This observation is consistent with our structural findings that the N-terminal domains of ROS1 mediate an auto-inhibited conformation. Removal of this regulatory segment likely destabilizes the autoinhibited state, promoting receptor activation. Similarly, mutations or truncations of EGFR’s bent-over tether have shown a higher affinity to ligand or ligand-independent activation29,45.

Typically, the structure of ligands for RTKs is maintained throughout evolution. However, ROS1 appears to diverge from this pattern as NELL2 is absent in invertebrates. The ligand for Drosophila Sevenless (dROS1) is the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) BOSS (Bride of Sevenless), which is expressed on adjacent cells and interacts with dROS1 on R7 photoreceptor cells5,46,47. This suggests that ROS1 has evolved different ligands and regulatory mechanisms in invertebrates and vertebrates to accommodate their distinct biological roles.

The dose rate was set to 19.03 electrons per physical pixel per second. The exposure time of each movie was 1.964 s, leading to a total dose of 50 electrons per Å2, fractionated into 50 frames. Images of the complexes of ROS1[N-term]–NELL2, ROS1[N-term]–Fab-RX5, and ROS1[N-term]–Fab-CT4 were recorded using EPU (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in super-resolution mode at a nominal magnification of ×105,000, corresponding to a calibrated super-resolution pixel size of 0.413 Å with a preset defocus range from −0.8 to −2.0 µm. The dose rate was set to 15 electrons per physical pixel per second. The exposure time of each movie was 2.3 s, leading to a total dose of 50 electrons per Å2, fractionated into 40 frames. For the ROS1ECD and ROS1ECD–NELL2 structures, a total of 8320 and 14,568 images were collected, respectively. For the ROS1[N-term]–NELL2 complex, a total of 17,000 images were collected. For the ROS1[N-term]–Fab-RX5 complex, a total of 8548 images were collected. For the ROS1[N-term]–Fab-CT4 complex, a total of 7936 images were collected.

Cryo-EM data processingThe datasets were processed with cryoSPARC v4.3.050. Motion correction and CTF estimation were first carried out for all images. Micrographs with a CTF estimation resolution worse than 6.0 Å were excluded. Next, blob pick was used for the first round of particle picking. After several rounds of 2D classification, the selected good 2D classes were then used as templates for the second round of template picking from all images. Several rounds of 2D classification were performed, and the well-aligned 2D classes showing clear 3D projections were chosen for 3D reconstruction. Ab initio model reconstruction was used to generate initial 3D models. Particles corresponding to different classes were selected and optimized through iterative rounds of heterogeneous refinement in cryoSPARC. The best models were refined using non-uniform51 refinement and local refinement. The overall resolutions were estimated based on the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) = 0.143 criteria. Local resolution was estimated from two half data maps under cryoSPARC.

Phage display for fragment antigen-binding (Fab) selectionPhage display was performed to select ROS1-specific antibody as described previously58,59. In brief, the Fab library (library F)59 was first added to a 96-well plate Nunc MaxiSorp (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coated with streptavidin and incubated at room temperature for 2 h to reduce non-specific phage binders. The phage solution was then transferred into another Nunc MaxiSorp plate coated with ROS1 protein and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. The wells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) containing 0.05% Tween-20. Phages were eluted with 100 mM HCl and neutralized with 1 M Tris (pH 11.0), then the eluted phages were amplified and subjected to a second round of selection. Five rounds of phage display were performed. The phages from rounds three to five were diluted and used for bacteria omnimax infection. Single colonies were picked and cultured overnight. The media containing phages were directly used in subsequent enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).
