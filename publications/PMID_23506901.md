# The heteromeric organic solute transporter, OSTα-OSTβ/SLC51: a transporter for steroid-derived molecules.
**Authors:** Ballatori N, Christian WV, Wheeler SG, Hammond CL
**Journal:** Mol Aspects Med (2013)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.mam.2012.11.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mam.2012.11.005)
**PMC:** [PMC3827772](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3827772/)

## Abstract

1. Mol Aspects Med. 2013 Apr-Jun;34(2-3):683-92. doi: 10.1016/j.mam.2012.11.005.

The heteromeric organic solute transporter, OSTα-OSTβ/SLC51: a transporter for
steroid-derived molecules.

Ballatori N(1), Christian WV, Wheeler SG, Hammond CL.

Author information:
(1)Department of Environmental Medicine, University of Rochester School of
Medicine, Rochester, NY 14642, United States.

The organic solute transporter alpha-beta (OSTα-OSTβ) is one of the newest
members of the solute carrier family, designated as SLC51, and arguably one of
the most unique. The transporter is composed of two gene products encoded by
SLC51A and SLC51B that heterodimerize to form the functional transporter
complex. SLC51A encodes OSTα, a predicted 340-amino acid, 7-transmembrane (TM)
domain protein, whereas SLC51B encodes OSTβ, a putative 128-amino acid,
single-TM domain polypeptide. Heterodimerization of the two subunits increases
the stability of the individual proteins, facilitates their post-translational
modification, and is required for delivery of the functional transporter complex
to the plasma membrane. There are no paralogues for SLC51A or SLC51B in any
genome that has been examined. The transporter functions via a facilitated
diffusion mechanism, and can mediate either efflux or uptake depending on the
electrochemical gradient of its substrates. Overall, characterization of the
transporter's substrate specificity, transport mechanism, tissue distribution,
subcellular localization, and transcriptional regulation as well as the
phenotype of the recently generated Slc51a-deficient mice have revealed that
OSTα-OSTβ plays a central role in the transport of bile acids, conjugated
steroids, and structurally-related molecules across the basolateral membrane of
many epithelial cells. In particular, OSTα-OSTβ appears to be essential for
intestinal bile acid absorption, and thus for dietary lipid absorption.

1. Introduction

OSTα–OSTβ was identified in 2001 using a comparative animal model, the little skate Leucoraja erinacea, and a unique expression cloning strategy (Wang et al., 2001). This study by Wang et al. (2001) provides the only example to date of the successful use of expression cloning to simultaneously identify two interacting gene products. The mouse and human orthologues were subsequently identified, cloned and characterized by Seward and coworkers in 2003. OSTα and OSTβ are encoded by the SLC51A and SLC51B genes on different chromosomes, exhibit no sequence identity with each other, and appear to lack paralogues (Table 1) (Ballatori et al., 2009). Heterodimerization of OSTα and OSTβ is required for the delivery of the functional transporter complex to the plasma membrane (Li et al., 2007). Interestingly, the human and mouse proteins are able to complement each other, as well as those from the little skate; that is, all nine OSTα–OSTβ pairs made using the human, mouse, and skate genes generate robust transport activity (Seward et al., 2003). This high degree of functional conservation suggests that these proteins evolved from an evolutionarily ancient common ancestor. A phylogenetic analysis of human OSTα and OSTβ is shown in Fig. 1; however, this does not include the skate proteins due to the restrictions placed on the analysis. Human OSTα shares 45% and 83% amino acid identity with skate and mouse OSTα, respectively. Human OSTβ shares less similarity with skate and mouse OSTβ, exhibiting only 25% and 62% amino acid identity, respectively.

Strong evidence for a role of OSTα–OSTβ in sterol homeostasis was provided by recent studies in Slc51a-deficient mice (Ballatori et al., 2008; Rao et al., 2008). These mice display a defect in intestinal bile acid and conjugated steroid absorption; a decrease in bile acid pool size and serum bile acid levels; altered intestinal, hepatic and renal disposition of known substrates of the transporter; and altered serum triglyceride, cholesterol, and glucose levels. Taken together, these observations indicate that OSTα–OSTβ is essential for bile acid and sterol disposition, and confirm its critical role in the enterohepatic circulation of bile acids (Fig. 2A).

In the enterohepatic circulation, hepatocytes metabolize cholesterol to bile acids, which are subsequently transported across the canalicular membrane into bile by the bile salt export pump, BSEP/ABCB11, with a minor contribution from the multidrug resistance-associated protein-2, MRP2/ABCC2 (Fig. 2B). Bile acids travel to the gallbladder during the interdigestive phase for storage, and to the descending part of the duodenum via the common bile duct during digestion. Bile acids facilitate biliary secretion of endogenous metabolites and xenobiotics, and are required for the emulsification and intestinal absorption of dietary lipids. Approximately 90–95% of the bile acids delivered into the duodenum are actively reabsorbed from the small intestine, with most of the absorption occurring in the ileum, the distal portion of the small intestine. Bile acid uptake across the apical membrane of ileal enterocytes is mediated mainly by the apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporter, ASBT/SLC10A2, whereas basolateral efflux is mediated largely by OSTα–OSTβ/SLC51 (Fig. 2B). Venous blood from the ileum goes directly into the portal vein and then into the liver sinusoids, where the bile acids are very efficiently extracted by the hepatocytes. Hepatocellular uptake of bile acids, which completes the cycle, is primarily carried out by the sodium taurocholate co-transporting polypeptide, NTCP/SLC10A1 (Fig. 2B).

2. Tissue distribution and cellular localizationOSTα and OSTβ are expressed in nearly all tissues, but are most abundant in tissues involved in bile acid and steroid homeostasis (Ballatori et al., 2009). In general, tissues with high levels of OSTα also have high levels of OSTβ, indicating co-expression of these genes. In humans, SLC51A and SLC51B mRNA levels are highest in the small intestine, liver, colon, kidney, testes, ovary, and adrenal gland, and are also detected in mammary gland, uterus, prostate, thyroid, and brain (Seward et al., 2003), consistent with a role in the disposition of steroid-derived compounds. In the brain, SLC51A and SLC51B are expressed in Purkinje cells and cells in the CA region of the hippocampus (Fang et al., 2010). In support of a role for OSTα– OSTβ in basolateral efflux, both OST proteins are localized to the basolateral membrane of key epithelial cells, including enterocytes, renal tubular cells, hepatocytes, and cholangiocytes (Ballatori et al., 2005; Dawson et al., 2005).Note that hepatic expression exhibits significant species differences: SLC51A and SLC51B mRNA are relatively abundant in human liver, whereas mouse and rat liver have very low levels of the corresponding transcripts (Seward et al., 2003; Ballatori et al., 2005). Human OSTα protein is detected in hepatocytes and cholangiocytes, whereas mouse hepatocytes have no detectable OSTα protein and mouse cholangiocytes have only moderate expression of this protein (Ballatori et al., 2005). In mice, relatively high levels of Slc51a and Slc51b message and protein are found in the small intestine, colon, and kidney, and the expression is especially high in the ileum (Dawson et al., 2005). Slc51a and Slc51b tissue distribution also parallels that of Slc10a2 (encoding ASBT), consistent with its role in bile acid homeostasis (Dawson et al., 2005).Additional evidence for a role of the transporter in bile acid disposition comes from the observation that OSTα–OSTβ tissue distribution pattern overlaps that of the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and of TGR5/GPBAR1, both bile acid receptors (Ballatori, 2011). FXR expressed sequence tag counts are quite abundant in human adrenal gland, are expressed at relatively high levels in the intestine, kidney, liver, and mammary gland, and are also detected in ovary, blood, brain, and connective tissue (Ballatori, 2011).

3. Functional studiesStudies on the mechanism of OSTα–OSTβ transport in Xenopus laevis oocytes revealed that the transporter can mediate either cellular efflux or uptake depending on the particular substrate's electrochemical gradient (Ballatori et al., 2005). OSTα–OSTβ-mediated transport in the oocytes was unaffected by depletion of intracellular ATP, alterations in transmembrane electrolyte concentration gradients, or changes in the pH gradient, and was trans-stimulated by known substrates (Ballatori et al., 2005), consistent with a facilitated diffusion transport mechanism.Evidence that OSTα–OSTβ is a multispecific transporter came from the initial characterization of the skate genes in Xenopus oocytes (Seward et al., 2003; Wang et al., 2001). Oocytes injected with skate slc51a and slc51b cRNA were able to transport taurocholate, estrone 3-sulfate, digoxin, and prostaglandin E2, but not p-aminohippurate or S-dinitrophenyl glutathione (Wang et al., 2001), indicating that this transport system may participate in cellular uptake of bile acids, some endogenous and exogenous steroids, and eicosanoids. In support of this hypothesis, transport was cis-inhibited by a variety of bile salts, steroids, and other organic anions. Although inhibition of transport does not necessarily establish that these compounds are transported substrates, it does provide insight into possible substrates. A comparison of the inhibitory potency of bile salts indicates that taurine-conjugated bile salts were in general more effective than the corresponding glycine-conjugated compounds, and unconjugated bile salts were the least effective inhibitors (Wang et al., 2001). The addition of a sulfate group further enhanced inhibitory potency, such that bile salts containing both taurine and sulfate modifications were strong cis-inhibitors of taurocholate and estrone 3-sulfate transport. Spironolactone was also an inhibitor of transport, as were sulfobromophthalein, probenecid and indomethecin. Mouse and human OSTα–OSTβ generally exhibited a similar substrate and inhibitor profile (Seward et al., 2003).Studies in MDCK cells triply transfected with mouse OSTα, mouse OSTβ, and human ASBT demonstrated that OSTα–OSTβ is able to transport a variety of taurine- and glycine-conjugated bile acids (Ballatori et al., 2005; Dawson et al., 2005). The MDCK/ASBT cells expressing both OST subunits mediated significant apical to basolateral taurocholate transport, with minimal transport in the opposite direction (i.e., basolateral to apical), reflecting the appropriate sorting of the proteins and the unidirectional nature of bile acid transport within these polarized cells (Dawson et al., 2005). For each bile acid species, the taurine-conjugated bile acid appeared to be transported more efficiently than the glycine-conjugated bile acid across the basolateral membrane by OSTα–OSTβ (Ballatori et al., 2005).Additional studies have demonstrated that OSTα–OSTβ also transports dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS) (Ballatori et al., 2005), and pregnenolone sulfate (PREGS) (Fang et al., 2010), and that both of these compounds are high affinity substrates for the transporter (Fang et al., 2010). Because DHEAS and PREGS are also major excitatory neurosteroids, these observations suggest a possible function for OSTα–OSTβ in the brain. The studies of Fang et al. (2010) also demonstrate that a variety of sulfate- and glucuronic acid-conjugates of steroids can inhibit OSTα–OSTβ, suggesting that these conjugates are also substrates.

4. RegulationBile acids regulate their own synthesis and transport (Eloranta and Kullak-Ublick, 2008), and thus the expression of OSTα and OSTβ was predicted to be regulated by FXR (encoded by the NR1H4 gene), the major bile acid-activated transcription factor. Indeed, there is now strong evidence for this hypothesis, including the observations that: (a) Nr1h4-null mice have lower ileal expression levels of OSTα and OSTβ (Zollner et al., 2006); (b) Chenodeoxycholate (CDCA), a natural agonist of FXR, and GW4064, a synthetic agonist, induce expression of Slc51a and Slc51b mRNA in liver, adrenal, and intestinal cell lines (Boyer et al., 2006; Frankenberg et al., 2006; Landrier et al., 2006; Lee et al., 2006); (c) The bile acid- and GW4064-mediated induction of Slc51a and Slc51b can be abolished via siRNA targeted knock-down of Nr1h4 (Landrier et al., 2006); (d) Treatment of ex vivo adrenal gland cultures with GW4064 induces expression of Slc51a and Slc51b in wild type adrenal glands but not in organs from Nr1h4-deficient mice (Zollner et al., 2006); and (e) FXR elements are found in the promoters of the SLC51 genes: two in human SLC51A and one in human SLC51B, mouse Slc51a, and mouse Slc51b (Frankenberg et al., 2006; Landrier et al., 2006; Lee et al., 2006).Dynamic regulation of OSTα and OSTβ is also achieved through other transcription factors, namely the small heterodimer partner (SHP, encoded by NR0B2) and the liver receptor homolog-1 (LRH-1, encoded by NR5A2), both orphan nuclear receptors (Fig. 3). Bile acids bind FXR, which in turn dimerizes with the retinoid X receptor-α (RXRα) and translocates to the nucleus to induce the expression of Slc51a, Slc51b and Nr0b2 (Goodwin et al., 2000; Lu et al., 2000; Boyer et al., 2006; Frankenberg et al., 2006; Landrier et al., 2006; Lee et al., 2006) (Fig. 3B). SHP interacts with LRH-1 forming a transcriptional repressor that downregulates OSTα and OSTβ expression after binding to LRH-1 elements in the promoters of Slc51a and Slc51b (Fig. 3C) (Frankenberg et al., 2006). One functional LRH-1 response element is present in mouse OSTα and two in mouse OSTβ (Frankenberg et al., 2006). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that FXR is a master transcriptional regulator involved in both the induction and repression of OSTα and OSTβ (Fig. 3).Although FXR, SHP, and LRH-1 appear to be the dominant transcription factors, other nuclear receptors involved in bile acid homeostasis also regulate Slc51a and Slc51b. These include transcriptional activators such as the liver X receptor-α and the hepatic nuclear factor-4α (Okuwaki et al., 2007), and repressors, like the pregnane X receptor (Teng and Piquette-Miller, 2007).Slc51a and Slc51b expression is also regulated via the FXR-FGF15/19-FGFR4 pathway (Fig. 4). In this pathway, bile acids bind FXR in ileal enterocytes and induce the expression of fibroblast growth factor 15/19 (FGF15 in mice and FGF19 in humans) (Holt et al., 2003; Inagaki et al., 2005). FGF15 can act as a hormone and signals at the liver upon binding the fibroblast growth factor receptor 4 (FGFR4), stimulating the downregulation of Cyp7a1 in a predominant manner over the FXR-SHP-LRH-1 pathway, which also downregulates Cyp7a1 (Goodwin et al., 2000; Holt et al., 2003; Inagaki et al., 2005; Kim et al., 2007). Reductions in CYP7A1, the rate-limiting enzyme for the classical pathway of bile acid synthesis, lead to a decrease in the bile acid pool size (Fig. 4) (Ballatori et al., 2008; Rao et al., 2008). The lower bile acid concentrations lead to a decrease in the expression of bile acid efflux transporters, including Slc51a and Slc51b through reduced FXR signaling (Fig. 3). In human hepatocytes, OSTα–OSTβ is localized on the basolateral plasma membrane, and is thought to play a role in the sinusoidal efflux of bile acids during conditions of cholestasis (Ballatori et al., 2005; Boyer et al., 2006). Elevated levels of SLC51A and SLC51B mRNA and protein are found in livers of patients with primary biliary cirrhosis (PBC), a cholestatic disease (Boyer et al., 2006), indicating that SLC51A and SLC51B may undergo adaptive regulation to protect the liver from accumulating hepatotoxic bile acids. In Slc10a2-deficient mice, a model for bile acid malabsorption, Slc51a and Slc51b are upregulated in the cecum and proximal colon yet downregulated in the ileum where Slc10a2 is normally expressed (Dawson et al., 2005). The diminished uptake of bile acids into ileocytes is most likely responsible for the decreased expression of Slc51a and Slc51b in the ileum. The inability of bile acids to be absorbed via ASBT may also promote their conversion from primary to secondary bile acids within the lumen of the small intestine, thus rendering them less water-soluble and in turn more susceptible to apical, passive diffusion in the large intestine. These changes may explain the elevated levels of Slc51a and Slc51b in the colon of these animals (Dawson et al., 2005).

4. Regulation

Bile acids regulate their own synthesis and transport (Eloranta and Kullak-Ublick, 2008), and thus the expression of OSTα and OSTβ was predicted to be regulated by FXR (encoded by the NR1H4 gene), the major bile acid-activated transcription factor. Dynamic regulation of OSTα and OSTβ is also achieved through other transcription factors, namely the small heterodimer partner (SHP, encoded by NR0B2) and the liver receptor homolog-1 (LRH-1, encoded by NR5A2), both orphan nuclear receptors (Fig. 3). Bile acids bind FXR, which in turn dimerizes with the retinoid X receptor-α (RXRα) and translocates to the nucleus to induce the expression of Slc51a, Slc51b and Nr0b2 (Goodwin et al., 2000; Lu et al., 2000; Boyer et al., 2006; Frankenberg et al., 2006; Landrier et al., 2006; Lee et al., 2006) (Fig. 3B). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that FXR is a master transcriptional regulator involved in both the induction and repression of OSTα and OSTβ (Fig. 3).

Although FXR, SHP, and LRH-1 appear to be the dominant transcription factors, other nuclear receptors involved in bile acid homeostasis also regulate Slc51a and Slc51b. These include transcriptional activators such as the liver X receptor-α and the hepatic nuclear factor-4α (Okuwaki et al., 2007), and repressors, like the pregnane X receptor (Teng and Piquette-Miller, 2007).

Slc51a and Slc51b expression is also regulated via the FXR-FGF15/19-FGFR4 pathway (Fig. 4). In this pathway, bile acids bind FXR in ileal enterocytes and induce the expression of fibroblast growth factor 15/19 (FGF15 in mice and FGF19 in humans) (Holt et al., 2003; Inagaki et al., 2005). The lower bile acid concentrations lead to a decrease in the expression of bile acid efflux transporters, including Slc51a and Slc51b through reduced FXR signaling (Fig. 3). Although a complete understanding of the role of these transcription factors in the overall regulatory process has not yet been established, these findings reveal that Slc51a and Slc51b are regulated through complex interconnected pathways.

These changes may explain the elevated levels of Slc51a and Slc51b in the colon of these animals (Dawson et al., 2005).

6. Pharmaceutical aspectsThe observation that Slc51a-deficient mice have a significantly smaller bile acid pool size (Ballatori et al., 2008; Rao et al., 2008) makes OSTα–OSTβ an attractive therapeutic target for diseases associated with increased bile acid levels. As discussed above, the elevated bile acid concentrations in cholestatic liver diseases can have serious adverse consequences. OSTα–OSTβ inhibition could theoretically lower intestinal and renal bile acid reabsorption, as well as diminish hepatic bile acid synthesis, thereby decreasing systemic and hepatic bile acid levels and protecting against cholestatic liver injury (Soroka et al., 2010).Slc51a-deficient mice also excrete more cholesterol in feces (Rao et al., 2008) and have lower serum cholesterol and triglyceride levels (Ballatori et al., 2008), suggesting that OSTα–OSTβ may also be an attractive therapeutic target for diseases associated with high lipid levels. Commonly prescribed drugs for dyslipidemia treatment include statins and ezetimibe, which inhibit cholesterol synthesis and dietary cholesterol uptake, respectively, and are often prescribed together (Toth, 2010). An additional treatment for dyslipidemia includes bile acid sequestrants, which bind to and sequester bile acids in the small intestine, therefore rendering them unable to facilitate the emulsification and absorption of dietary lipids (Staels and Kuipers, 2007). However, bile acid sequestrants have been found to increase triglyceride levels, and thus their use is often limited (Scaldaferri et al., 2011). In the Slc51a-deficient mouse model, both serum cholesterol and triglycerides are lowered (Ballatori et al., 2008). While the mechanism for this is not yet known, it is likely that the reduced bile acid pool size results in lower bile acid levels in the lumen of the small intestine, thus causing impaired dietary lipid absorption.Even though Slc51a-deficient mice have a much smaller bile acid pool size and lower serum bile acid levels, they have higher expression of Nr1h4 in the small intestine and liver, and of Fgf15 in the small intestine (Ballatori et al., 2008; Rao et al., 2008). Although not yet established experimentally, the higher FGF15 levels may increase FGFR4 signaling, and thus contribute to the decreased Cyp7a1 expression that has been observed in these mice (Ballatori et al., 2008; Rao et al., 2008). Based on these findings, it can be predicted that a putative OSTα–OSTβ inhibitor would produce some of the same effects as either an FXR agonist, administration of FGF15/FGF19, or as an agonist to FGFR4. In addition to altering bile acid levels, FXR activation has also been implicated in lowering triglyceride levels, improving insulin sensitivity, and in modulating gluconeogenic genes (Cariou et al., 2006; Lefebvre et al., 2009; Zhang et al., 2006). Transgenic mice expressing FGF19, as well as mice injected with FGF19 have decreased adiposity, resistance to high fat diet-induced weight gain, and improved glucose tolerance in diabetic mouse models (Tomlinson et al., 2002; Fu et al., 2004). Studies in Fgfr4-deficient mice suggest that FGFR4 is important for maintaining cholesterol and triglyceride levels, and insulin sensitivity (Huang et al., 2007). The alterations in these signaling pathways may contribute to the phenotype of the Slc51a-deficient mouse, and suggest that OSTα–OSTβ may be a valuable therapeutic target for the treatment of insulin resistance, diabetes, and the metabolic syndrome.
