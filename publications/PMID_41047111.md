Ribosome biogenesis is critical for postembryonic development progression in
Caenorhabditis elegans. Although maternally supplied ribosomes allow null
mutants of ribosomal protein genes to complete embryogenesis, subsequent larval
stages arrest if de novo ribosome production is compromised. Here, we compared
null mutants in large (rpl-5, rpl-33) and small (rps-10, rps-23) ribosomal
subunit genes with mutants defective in rRNA synthesis (rpoa-2 and rDNA loci).
By tracking divisions of the mesoblast (M) cell, we discovered that large
subunit mutations cause a stringent arrest in M cell proliferation, distinctly
more severe than the partial arrests observed in small subunit and rRNA
synthesis mutants. Unlike nutrient-deprived (starvation) L1 diapause, this
arrest does not activate the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor CKI-1, suggesting
a CKI-1-independent checkpoint. Gene expression analyses revealed that rpl-5(0)
and rDNA(0) mutants share overexpression of genes involved in ribosomal RNA
processing and ribosome assembly, whereas larvae depleted of the RNA polymerase
I subunit RPOA-2 uniquely overexpress lipid metabolism genes. Tissue-specific
manipulations previously confirmed that ribosomal insufficiency in a single
tissue can impose a whole-organism developmental block. Genetic analyses further
implicated the gap junction protein INX-14 and the TORC2 component SINH-1 as
partial suppressors of the M cell arrest in small ribosomal subunit mutants
(rps-23(0)), but not in large ribosomal subunit mutants (rpl-5(0)). Introducing
null mutations in downstream TORC1/TORC2 kinases to a tissue-specific RPOA-2
depletion background similarly modulated growth arrest, suggesting that gap
junction communication and TOR pathways converge upon a ribosomal stress
checkpoint. Collectively, our findings highlight a unique, CKI-1-independent
arrest driven by large ribosomal subunit gene loss and reveal how distinct
signaling pathways coordinate postembryonic development in response to ribosome
biogenesis defects.

Introduction

Mutations in ribosomal protein genes and associated translation factors frequently yield profound developmental defects across diverse organisms (Surya and Sarinay-Cenik, 2022). In Drosophila melanogaster, heterozygous loss-of-function in many ribosomal protein (RP) genes causes the classic Minute syndrome, characterized by slower development, small body size, and thin bristles (Marygold et al., 2007; Kongsuwan et al., 1985; Farnsworth, 1957; Andersson et al., 1994; Cramton and Laski, 1994). In vertebrates, partial ribosomal insufficiency underlies human ribosomopathies such as Diamond–Blackfan anemia, which manifests with bone marrow failure and developmental abnormalities (Da Costa et al., 2001; Panici et al., 2021; Boria et al., 2010; Ellis and Lipton, 2008). Model organism studies recapitulate these effects: for example, zebrafish carrying RP gene mutations (e.g., in rps19 or rpl11) exhibit widespread developmental perturbations in hematopoietic and neural tissues associated with activation of the p53 stress response and intriguingly increased tumors (Amsterdam et al., 2004; Danilova et al., 2011; Chakraborty et al., 2009; Jia et al., 2013). Similarly in mice, several RP haploinsufficiency triggers p53 dependent checkpoints that cause growth arrest and tissue-specific phenotypes (Morgado-Palacin et al., 2015; Jaako et al., 2014; Watkins-Chow et al., 2013; Devlin et al., 2010). In Caenorhabditis elegans, the essential role of ribosomal components in development is also evident due to haploinsufficient phenotypes (Surya et al., 2025).

A striking feature of ribosomal protein gene haploinsufficiency is the variability in phenotypic severity depending on which specific ribosomal protein is affected. Different RP gene mutations produce a spectrum of developmental outcomes and tissue-specific defects, highlighting gene-specific impacts on organismal development and physiology (Carlston et al., 2017; Ulirsch et al., 2019; Panici et al., 2021). For instance, mutations in ribosomal protein genes such as RPS19 or RPS7, associated with Diamond-Blackfan anemia (DBA), exhibit diverse clinical manifestations, including anemia, growth retardation, and congenital and heart abnormalities (Gazda et al., 2008; Watkins-Chow et al., 2013). Similarly, in animal models, phenotypes differ notably depending on the ribosomal protein gene affected. RPS19-deficient mouse models exhibit severe hematological abnormalities and bone marrow failure (Jaako et al., 2014), whereas RPL11 haploinsufficiency in zebrafish affects hematopoietic and metabolic processes including suppression of glycolysis (Danilova et al., 2011). This variability suggests the complexity of ribosomal protein functions beyond translation, affecting various aspects of cellular growth, differentiation, metabolism and organismal development.

C. elegans provides a powerful model system for dissecting the developmental consequences of ribosomal protein deficiencies due to the presence of maternal ribosome contributions, which can compensate for early zygotic loss. Specifically, embryos entirely lacking zygotic expression of certain ribosomal protein genes can still develop into morphologically normal first-stage larvae (L1), indicating that maternal ribosomal loading is sufficient for initial embryonic development (Cenik et al., 2019). This maternal contribution allows embryos to reach the first larval stage even in the complete absence of zygotic ribosomal protein expression. Interestingly, even when only a subset of embryonic cells (such as AB or P1 lineages) lacks sufficient ribosomes, mosaic embryos still undergo global developmental arrest at the larval stage. This suggests that ribosomal insufficiency in a subset of cells triggers a systemic signal that inhibits development at a whole-organism level, suggesting a global regulatory response that coordinates developmental progression based on ribosomal capacity (Cenik et al., 2019).

Developmental quiescence in C. elegans is traditionally studied in the context of environmental stress, such as starvation or overcrowding. Under nutrient deprivation, newly hatched L1 larvae enter a reversible arrest (L1 diapause) until food becomes available (Baugh, 2013; Baugh et al., 2009). This starvation-induced quiescence is an active, regulated state controlled by endocrine signaling pathways: the FOXO transcription factor DAF-16 promotes arrest by downregulating pro-growth signals, including TGF-β (DBL-1) and steroid hormone (DAF-12) pathways, in a cell-nonautonomous manner (Kaplan and Baugh, 2016; Kaplan et al., 2015; Gumienny and Savage-Dunn, 2013; Aghayeva et al., 2021).

Another well-known quiescent state is the dauer diapause at the L3 stage, triggered by high population density and low food, which involves insulin/IGF, TGF-β, and nuclear hormone signaling (Fielenbach and Antebi, 2008). These examples demonstrate that C. elegans has evolved systemic signaling mechanisms to coordinate developmental arrest in response to external cues. By contrast, much less is known about how developmental quiescence can be induced by internal dysfunction such as ribosomal component loss (“atypical” quiescence not caused by external starvation or crowding). The evidence from ribosomal protein mutants suggests that C. elegans possesses a surveillance system that responds to internal ribosomal defects with a whole-organism arrest, but the nature of this system remains to be elucidated (Cenik et al., 2019).

Recent work has shown that tissue-specific ribosome insufficiency in the C. elegans epidermis elicits an organism-wide growth arrest independent of nutritional status (Zhao et al., 2023). Specifically, larvae with epidermis-specific depletion of an essential ribosome biogenesis factor ceased development at the mid-larval stage (L3), entering a quiescent state distinct from starvation-induced L1 arrest or dauer diapause with a unique gene expression signature that differed from those states. Further analysis pointed to a non-autonomous mechanism involving secreted factors: a set of secreted proteins were upregulated under ribosome-biogenesis inhibition, and disruption of a dense-core vesicle release pathway (UNC-31/CAPS and IDA-1) in the epidermis alleviated the global arrest (Zhao et al., 2023). These findings support the model that ribosomal stress in one tissue triggers endocrine-like signals that impose developmental quiescence organism-wide, independent of nutritional status.

While secreted peptides and hormonal signals are clear mediators of growth coordination in C. elegans, other routes of intercellular communication may also contribute to sensing and responding to ribosomal defects. For instance, the Target of Rapamycin (TOR) kinase pathway is a central regulator of cell growth that exists in two complexes, TORC1 and TORC2. TORC1 (LET-363 kinase with DAF-15/Raptor) links nutrient availability to protein synthesis and developmental progression. TORC2, on the other hand, has a more enigmatic role in development: loss of rict-1 (encoding an essential TORC2 subunit) in C. elegans causes slowed development, smaller body size, reduced fertility, and altered metabolism, suggesting TORC2 provides signals for proper growth even when nutrients are ample (Soukas et al., 2009). This raises the question of whether TORC might be involved in the organism’s response to ribosomal component loss-for example, by sensing cellular stress or metabolic changes downstream of ribosome dysfunction. Additionally, direct cell-cell communication via gap junctions could plausibly synchronize developmental responses. C. elegans cells are extensively connected by innexin-based gap junction channels, allowing small molecules and ions to diffuse between neighboring cells and tissues (Hall, 2017; Starich et al., 2001; Altun et al., 2009). Such coupling might enable a tissue experiencing ribosomal stress to rapidly influence the physiology of adjacent tissues without requiring secreted factors.

In this study, we analyzed homozygous null mutants in large (rpl-5 (0), rpl-33(0)) and small (rps-10(0), rps-23(0)) ribosomal subunit genes, as well as impairments in rRNA synthesis (rDNA(0) and RNA polymerase I (RPOA-2) depletion). Despite these disruptions all compromising ribosome production, we observed markedly different severities of developmental arrest. Strikingly, large ribosomal subunit mutants (rpl-5 (0), rpl-33(0)) exhibited a stringent block of M cell divisions—a phenotype absent in small ribosomal subunit and rRNA synthesis mutants—and this strict arrest diverged from starvation-induced L1 diapause by its independence from CKI-1. Transcriptomic analyses revealed overlapping as well as condition-specific gene expression changes across these genotypes. We further showed that reducing expression of the gap junction gene inx-14 or the TORC2 component sinh-1 partially rescued the developmental arrest in the small ribosomal subunit mutant rps-23(0) but not in the large ribosomal subunit mutant rpl-5(0), highlighting gene-specific roles within the ribosome. Finally, loss of TORC downstream kinases genes or inx-14 also suppressed the epidermis-specific ribosome depletion-induced larval quiescence, emphasizing that multiple communication pathways converge upon ribosomal integrity to dictate developmental progression. Our findings establish C. elegans as a valuable model for uncovering how distinct ribosomal defects trigger unique, cell-nonautonomous checkpoints regulating postembryonic development.

2.Results2.1.Distinct developmental arrest phenotypes in large and small ribosomal subunit mutants versus rRNA synthesis defects revealed by M cell divisions in C. elegansThe mesoblast (M) cell is a convenient marker of somatic developmental quiescence in L1 (Fig. 1A). It is generated early during embryonic development and remains quiescent until midway through L1 stage (Sulston et al., 1983). During the L1 stage, it is the only cell expressing the Twist ortholog HLH-8, making it straightforward to visualize via phlh-8::GFP (Harfe et al., 1998). Under normal conditions, the M cell undergoes multiple divisions before the L2 stage, and it remains undivided in nutrient-deprived L1 larvae (Baugh, 2013; Fukuyama et al., 2012; Kasuga et al., 2013; Kniazeva et al., 2004; Cenik et al., 2019).Here, we introduced a phlh-8::GFP transgene into four homozygous null ribosomal protein (RP) gene mutants: rpl-5(0), rpl-33(0), rps-10(0), and rps-23(0). We tracked M cell divisions in arrested homozygous animals over 3 day at 16 ◦C. In large subunit RP mutants rpl-5(0) and rpl-33 (0), essentially no M cell divisions were detected, 0 % of larvae (n > 50) showed any M descendants beyond the M founder cell (Fig. 1B). Small subunit RP mutants rps-23(0) and rps-10(0) exhibited a less extreme phenotype: a small fraction of these larvae initiated one or two M cell divisions (e.g., producing 2–4 M descendants) before arresting, whereas the majority still showed no M cell divisions (Fig. 1B).Interestingly, arrested larvae remain viable for at least a week, suggesting that maternal ribosomes are highly stable. To determine if postembryonic development progresses further over an extended period, we observed larvae for 7 day at 16 ◦C. Notably, the rps-23(0) mutant exhibited additional M cell divisions after seven days, while the large subunit RP mutants rpl-5(0) and rpl-33(0) remained arrested without division (Fig. 1C). After 7 days, M cells in rps-10(0) animals likely divided to 18 cells and were difficult to observe, thus not reported. Consistent with this partial progression in small subunit RP mutants, raising the temperature to 20 ◦C increased the frequency of M cell divisions in both rps-23(0) and rps-10(0) animals, with at least 50 % of larvae undergoing divisions (Fig. 1D). In contrast, rpl-5(0) and rpl-33(0) mutants maintained stringent arrest without further M cell divisions under these conditions (Fig. 1D). Based on the role of 26S rRNA processing in nucleolar vacuole formation, Class I and Class II large subunit RPs have distinct functions in regulating nucleolar vacuoles (Xu et al., 2023). RPL-5 and RPL-33 belong to different large subunit RP classes with respect to 26S rRNA processing and nucleolar structure maintenance, yet their mutants exhibited the same M cell division phenotype. This suggests that M cell division is independent of 26S rRNA processing and nucleolar structure maintenance. These data suggest that the large ribosomal subunit genes rpl-5 and rpl-33 confer more severe developmental arrest phenotypes compared to small subunit genes rps-23 and rps-10.To determine if disrupting ribosomal RNA production yields similar developmental arrest, we examined mutants defective in rRNA synthesis. The rpoa-2(0) mutation (a null allele of rpoa-2, encoding the second-largest subunit of RNA polymerase I-POL1RB) and a complete loss of ribosomal DNA (rDNA(0)) both abrogate transcription of the 45S pre-rRNA, effectively preventing new ribosome assembly. As expected, these ribosome biogenesis mutants caused arrested development at an early larval stage.Interestingly both rpoa-2(0) and rDNA(0) mutant animals were arrested at a later stage than RP null mutants. In both rpoa-2(0) and rDNA(0) L1 larvae, the M cell was able to execute its normal division program – producing the full complement of 18 descendants – indicating that development progressed through at least till the end of the L1 stage in spite of lack of ribosomal RNA production (Fig. 1E–Zhao et al., 2023). Given lack of a similar arrest phenotype in comparison to RP gene null mutants, we wondered if these larvae also changed their body size, which would indicate if further growth can happen even in the absence of additional zygotic ribosome synthesis. Interestingly both rpoa-2(0) and rDNA(0) mutants progressed to a stage that significantly exceeded typical L1 after two to 3 day at 16 ◦C and 20 ◦C (Fig. 1F and G). Thus, these findings suggest that disruptions in rRNA synthesis via rpoa-2(0) or complete rDNA deletion do not stop M cell divisions, in contrast to mutations in large ribosomal subunit protein genes, rpl-5 and rpl-33. While all of these mutations compromise ribosome production, our data suggest that rpl-5(0) and rpl-33(0) cause a specific, severe developmental arrest, which is not solely attributable to global ribosome insufficiency.2.2.Ribosomal protein gene mutants undergo a distinct, CKI-1-independent M cell arrest uncoupled from starvation-induced quiescenceGiven the full M cell division arrest in rpl-5(0) and rpl-33(0) mutants, we asked whether the developmental arrest caused by the loss of these genes engages the same molecular pathways as nutrient-dependent L1 diapause observed in starved larvae. Starvation-induced L1 arrest is a well-characterized quiescent state. When C. elegans hatch in the absence of food, all somatic blast cells (including the M cell, somatic gonad precursors, etc.) remain in G1 and do not divide. This starvation arrest requires the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor CKI-1 (homolog of P27 cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor in the Cip/Kip family), which is upregulated in arrested L1 blast cells to enforce cell cycle quiescence (Hong et al., 1998). We tested whether the arrest caused by loss of ribosomal protein genes occurs through a CKI-1–dependent pathway similar to starvation. To this end, we introduced the extrachromosomal array kqEx73, which carries cki-1::GFP and rab-3::RFP (Kihira et al., 2012), into three heterozygous RP gene mutants (rpl-5(0)/+, rpl-33 (0)/+, and rps-10(0)/+) to assess cki-1::GFP expression in homozygous null animals. As RP null mutants are arrested at L1 stage (Cenik et al., 2019), we examined cki-1::GFP in L1 animals. cki-1::GFP is not expressed in wild-type L1 animals, but it is strongly induced after 3 days of starvation from hatching (Fig. 2A). In contrast, 3-day-old RP null mutants grown on food did not show CKI-1::GFP expressed (Fig. 2A), indicating that the developmental arrest by RP null mutants does not rely on the CKI-1-mediated pathway and diverges from starvation induced quiescence.Because heterozygous rpl-33(0)/+ and rpl-5(0)/+ animals exhibit notable developmental delays and stress responses (Surya et al., 2025), we sought to determine whether maternal contributions (i.e., maternal dowry) could influence the M cell arrest in homozygous offspring. To achieve this, we used a rescued rpl-33 duplication strain that otherwise appears wild type and produces rpl-33(0) homozygotes (Fig. 2B). The resulting F1 rpl-33(0) larvae still displayed undivided M cells at high penetrance, indicating the M cell arrest does not stem from parental haploinsufficiency (Fig. 2C).To further test for potential “positive” maternal effects, we crossed ncl-1(e1942) into rpl-5(0)/+. ncl-1 encodes a zinc finger protein that represses RNA polymerases I and III and, when mutated, increases global ribosome production (Frank and Roth, 1998; Tiku et al., 2017). However, this cross also failed to rescue the M cell division phenotype, again implying that these arrests in rpl-5(0) and rpl-33(0) animals are not driven by maternal factors (Fig. 2D).2.3.Comparative gene expression analyses reveal overlapping and distinct responses in rpl-5(0), rDNA(0), and RPOA-2 depleted larvaeTo understand which pathways underlying developmental arrest caused by ribosome biogenesis disruption, we compared gene expression profiles of arrested larvae lacking rpl-5 gene, rDNA loci, or with depletion of the RNA polymerase I subunit RPOA-2 (Zhao et al., 2023; Cenik et al., 2019). Specifically, we analyzed RNA-seq datasets from larvae in two independent strains with complete deletion of rDNA loci (Cenik et al., 2019) and compared these to previously published datasets from rpl-5(0) mutants and from organism-wide RPOA-2 depletion experiments in stage-matched larvae (Zhao et al., 2023; Cenik et al., 2019). Because the wild-type controls for each experiment were collected separately, we employed batch correction (see Methods) to directly compare all datasets. The reliability of these datasets is supported by experimental evidence: PHA-4 is overexpressed in epidermal RPOA-2–depleted animals, consistent with its role as a sensor of nucleolar stress that can trigger lipid accumulation (Zhao et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2018). Genes upregulated by SKN-1 gain-of-function are also mildly overexpressed in ribosomal protein haploinsufficient mutants, and SKN-1 is required for oxidative stress resistance in rpl-5(0)/+ mutants (Surya et al., 2025).Gene expression analysis revealed that loss of rpl-5 resulted in significant overexpression of 394 genes and underexpression of 32 genes compared to wild-type larvae (Data S1). In contrast, deletion of rDNA loci caused overexpression of 70 genes and underexpression of 206 genes, while RPOA-2 depletion led to overexpression of 102 genes and underexpression of only 14 genes (Data S1). Notably, among the 70 genes overexpressed upon rDNA deletion, 68 overlapped significantly with those overexpressed in rpl-5(0) mutants (Data S1, Fisher’s exact test, p = 1.3e-117). These shared genes were enriched in functional categories such as noncoding RNA metabolism, ribosomal RNA processing, and ribosome assembly (Fig. 3A). Conversely, RPOA-2 depletion uniquely led to overexpression of genes involved in lipid metabolic processes (Fig. 3B). This difference may reflect that rpl-5(0) and rDNA (0) are genetic null, while RPOA-2 depletion results from inducible proteasome-mediated degradation (Zhao et al., 2023). Alternatively, direct impairment of RNA polymerase I activity may trigger distinct regulatory responses compared to loss of rDNA copies or ribosomal protein genes.Among genes significantly underexpressed upon rpl-5 or rDNA loss, only 11 were shared; however, this overlap remained statistically significant (Data S1, Fisher’s exact test, p = 6.95e-20). Due to the limited number of overlapping genes, we conducted separate GO enrichment analyses for the underexpressed genes in each condition independently (Fig. 3C). No significant overlaps were found between RPOA-2 depleted larvae and either rpl-5(0) or rDNA-deleted datasets for over- or underexpressed genes (Data S1, Fisher’s exact test, p > 0.05).Given that genes within the lipid metabolic process (GO:0044255) were underexpressed in response to rDNA loss but overexpressed in RPOA-2 depleted larvae, we plotted the average log CPM counts for these genes across all datasets (Fig. 3D). 3.DiscussionOur study establishes distinct severities of postembryonic developmental arrest associated with ribosome biogenesis defects and highlights the involvement of TORC2 and gap junction signaling.
