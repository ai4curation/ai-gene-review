Metalloenzymes couple substrate binding and formation of oxidative intermediates
to minimize unwanted side reactions. However, the molecular details of such
coupling frequently remain ambiguous. Radical S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM)
enzymes constitute one of the largest groups of metalloenzymes and catalyze
various radical-mediated reactions. While radical SAM enzymes significantly
accelerate the conserved radical initiation reaction, the reductive cleavage of
SAM into 5'-deoxyadenosyl radical (5'-dA•), the molecular mechanism of this rate
acceleration is largely unexplored. Here, using MoaA, aradical SAM enzyme in the
molybdenum cofactor (Moco) biosynthesis, as a model, we reveal the mechanism of
substrate-triggered radical initiation. We first elucidated the intact active
site structure of MoaA using solution NMR characterization of the C-terminal
tail, which was disordered in the reported crystal structures, and its
computational docking into the MoaA structure. Together with the comprehensive
functional validation, we show that MoaA uses its conformationally flexible
C-terminal tail with two conserved Gly residues (GG motif) at the C-terminus as
a sensor to detect substrate guanosine 5'-triphosphate (GTP) binding and trigger
reductive SAM cleavage. Importantly, mutations that disrupt this regulatory
mechanism cause Moco deficiency disease in humans. Comparison of these
observations with other radical SAM enzymes provides insight into the general
mechanism of substrate-triggered radical initiation in radical SAM enzymes.

## Full Text

Redox-active metalloenzymes catalyze chemically challenging reactions using highly oxidative metal complexes or organic radicals. Frequently, the formation of these oxidative intermediates is coupled with substrate binding to avoid damage to the enzymes or surrounding molecules. For example, in P450 oxygenases, the oxygen activation is strictly regulated by the binding of the substrate and reductase (1). In ribonucleotide reductase, the initiation of radical catalysis is conformationally gated in response to the binding of the substrate and allosteric effectors (2). However, for most other metalloenzymes, significant ambiguity remains about the structural basis of controlled radical initiation due to the transient nature of the catalytically relevant enzyme conformation.

Radical SAM enzymes (3) form one of the largest enzyme superfamilies that catalyze radical-mediated reactions. These enzymes initiate radical chemistry by reductive cleavage of SAM using a 4Fe-4S cluster ([4Fe-4S]RS, Fig. 1A) (4). The rate of SAM cleavage is controlled in response to substrate binding (5). However, the actual mechanism of regulation remains largely uncharacterized (6). Here, we report the elucidation of an elaborate regulatory mechanism of radical initiation in the MoaA radical SAM enzyme, the most widely conserved enzyme in this enzyme superfamily.

MoaA catalyzes a unique 3′,8-cyclization of GTP (Fig. 1B) as the initial and rate-limiting step of molybdenum cofactor (Moco) biosynthesis (9). Moco is an organometallic cofactor found in all molybdenum-dependent enzymes except for nitrogenase (10) and is widely conserved in all kingdoms of life. In humans, mutations in Moco biosynthetic enzymes cause the fatal metabolic disorder, Moco deficiency (MoCD) disease (11). More than 50% of human MoCD patients carry mutations in MOCS1A, a human homolog of MoaA (12). Many of these mutations are in one of the three conserved Arg residues in the GTP binding site (Arg17, Arg266, and Arg268; numberings based on Staphylococcus aureus MoaA, Fig. 1C), or two conserved Gly residues at the C-terminus (GG-motif; Gly339 and Gly340, SI Appendix, Fig. S1). However, the mechanism by which these mutations inactivate the enzyme is largely unknown, mainly due to the lack of structural information on the catalytically relevant state of the active site.

In the reported crystal structures of MoaA, the C-terminal 11 amino acid residues were disordered (7, 8), and the active site was exposed to solvent (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Consequently, the catalytically relevant structure of the active site has remained elusive. Since the C-terminal tail was essential for SAM binding and was found to interact with Asp198 adjacent to the SAM binding site, the C-terminal tail was proposed to form part of the active site (13). The main challenge in the structural characterization of the C-terminal tail is the transient nature of its interaction with the active site, which cannot be captured by X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy (EM). Here, we report the successful structural characterization of the C-terminal tail and the active site of MoaA using transferred nuclear Overhauser effect (NOE) spectroscopy of a peptide surrogate of the C-terminal tail and its comprehensive functional characterization. These results have revealed a regulatory mechanism by which MoaA uses its C-terminal tail to detect GTP binding and trigger the SAM cleavage. Disruption of this regulatory mechanism, as observed in disease-causing mutations in human MOCS1A, inactivates the enzyme. A comparison with other radical SAM enzymes suggests a general mechanism of regulation of SAM reductive cleavage through substrate-induced interaction with the SAM methyl group.

Results and DiscussionNMR Characterization of the C-Terminal Tail Peptide in Its Bound State.A synthetic 11mer peptide with the amino acid sequence identical to the C-terminal tail of S. aureus MoaA (RKKINMNYIGG) serves as a surrogate of the C-terminal tail and restores the catalytic function of GG-motif mutants that are otherwise inactive (13). However, the weak binding affinity of this peptide to MoaA (Kd = 50 to 150 μM) (13) made it difficult for structural characterization by X-ray crystallography or cryo-EM. Therefore, we employed transferred NOE (14, 15) to determine the 11mer peptide structure in complex with MoaA. This NMR technique harnesses the property of NOE signals being enhanced when a small ligand is bound to a macromolecule (>~35 kDa) (14–16). This approach is applicable to ligands with a fast exchange rate between the bound and free states, typically with Kd > 10 μM, where NOE signals from the bound state are carried over after the ligand’s dissociation from the receptor (14–16). In the presence of ∆330-340-MoaA lacking the C-terminal tail, the synthetic 11mer peptide exhibited NOE signals, most of which were not observed in a control experiment in the absence of ∆330-340-MoaA (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Importantly, all the interresidue NOEs were observed only in the presence of ∆330-340-MoaA. The same results were obtained when the experiment was performed using G340A-MoaA, suggesting that the mutated C-terminal tail does not significantly interfere with the peptide binding (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). These observations suggest a successful detection of NOE signals of the 11mer peptide in the conformation bound to G340A-MoaA.To assign the NOE signals, we performed a series of multiresonance experiments (SI Appendix, Figs. S4–S7) using a uniformly 13C- and 15N-labeled 11mer peptide ([U-13C,15N]11mer). To distinguish Ile4 vs. Ile9 and Asn5 vs. Asn7 with very close 1H and 13C chemical shifts, we used an 11mer peptide in which the Met6, Asn7, and Ile9 residues were labeled with 13C and 15N ([M6, N7, I9-13C,15N]11mer). These experiments allowed us to unambiguously assign all the interresidue NOEs (SI Appendix, Figs. S8–S10).We observed a total of 59 interresidue NOE correlations (SI Appendix, Table S1). Extensive NOEs were observed between Tyr8 and Ile4, Met6, Ile9, Gly10, and Gly11 (Fig. 2 A–D and SI Appendix, Fig. S9), suggesting a structure compactly folded around Tyr8. Most importantly, NOEs between Hα of two Gly residues (Hα2 and Hα3), and Hδ and Hε of Tyr8 (Fig. 2D) suggested an ordered structure of the C-terminal GG-motif. Since most of the observed interresidue NOE signals (56 of 59) were among the C-terminal eight amino acid residues (4INMNYIGG11) and the 8-mer peptide could still restore the activity of the GG-motif mutants of MoaA (13), we computed the three-dimensional structures of the 8-mer peptide (Fig. 2E). The computation yielded conformers with similar overall structures, in which the peptide folded around Tyr8 surrounded by Ile4, Met6, Ile9, Gly10, and Gly11. Error bars represent one SD from three replicates.Conformational Change upon C-Terminal Tail Binding.To model the peptide structures into a catalytically relevant form of MoaA, we investigated a potential protein conformational change upon the C-terminal tail binding by modeling the full-length MoaA structure using AlphaFold2 (17). While MoaA was reported as a dimer in the crystal structures (7, 8), for this analysis, we consider only the monomer unit because the C-terminal tail was proposed to interact with the active site of the same subunit (13). No evidence suggests that the active site structure is affected by the presence of the other subunit. Of the five predicted structures (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), the top four ranked structures have the C-terminal tail inserted into the active site, which was made possible by the break of the C-terminal α-helix in the crystal structures (Try316-Arg328, Fig. 1D) into two short helices (Fig. 1E). The fifth model has the C-terminal α-helix similar to the crystal structures, where the C-terminus is far from the active site or any other part of the enzyme (SI Appendix, Fig. S11E). This conformation is inconsistent with the catalytically essential role of the GG motif and, therefore, catalytically irrelevant. A comparison of the predicted structures and the crystal structures (Fig. 1 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S11) suggested that the C-terminal tail binding to the active site could induce the closure of the loop consisting of Phe196-Asp206 (Gating loop, compare Fig. 1 D and E). Asp198 in this loop was previously reported to interact with one of the three cationic residues (Arg330, Lys331, and Lys332) in the C-terminal tail (13). Consistently, in the AlphaFold models with C-terminal tail insertion (Fig. 1E), Lys332 is H-bonded to D198.The closed Gating loop conformation is also consistent with the SAM adenine binding mode conserved among radical SAM enzymes. In general, radical SAM enzymes interact with SAM adenine through several hydrophobic amino acid side chains and H-bonding interactions between N1 and 6-NH2 of SAM adenine and the enzyme’s main-chain amides (SI Appendix, Fig. S12) (18). Consistently, in the MoaA AlphaFold model, the mainchain nitrogen and oxygen of Met197 are positioned close to N1 and 6-NH2 of SAM adenine, and Phe196, Met197, and Val199 form the hydrophobic pocket (Fig. 1 F and G). Mutation of the amino acid side chains close to the adenine base (M197A/N203A-MoaA) significantly increased Km for SAM (78-fold relative to wt, SI Appendix, Fig. S13). These observations suggest that the Gating loop closes upon the C-terminal tail insertion and forms the SAM binding site.On the other hand, the structure of the C-terminal eight amino acid residues in the AlphaFold model is unlikely to be catalytically relevant. In this model, the C-terminal carboxylate interacts with Arg17 and is very close to C3′ of GTP (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A and B), which would interfere with the H3′ abstraction or the subsequent 3′,8-cyclization (19, 20). Met335 clashes with SAM in the active site (SI Appendix, Fig. S14A). Finally, the per-residue measure of local confidence (pLDDT) of the C-terminal tail is low (<70, SI Appendix, Fig. S14 C and D), and the relative orientation of Ile338, Ile333, and Tyr337 (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A and B) is inconsistent with the NOE correlations among these residues described above. Therefore, this part of the protein must be remodeled using our NMR structure.Modeling the MoaA•C-Terminal Tail Peptide Complex Structure.To model the MoaA•C-terminal tail peptide complex structure, we prepared the receptor structure by removing the C-terminal 11 amino acid residues from the AlphaFold model and adding 4Fe-4S clusters, GTP, and SAM based on the crystal structures (7, 8). The side chain conformations of Arg17, Arg266, and Arg268 that interact with GTP were corrected based on the crystal structure in complex with GTP (7) (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Using the resulting structure, we performed a docking analysis of the C-terminal tail peptide NMR structures using AutoDock Vina (21). All the 20 NMR conformers were successfully docked with the C-terminal carboxylate inserted into the active site with excellent shape complementarity (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S16). Of these, eight conformers with the most favorable binding energy (−8.3 ~ −11.8 kcal/mol) were docked in a very similar orientation with a conserved H-bonding interaction between the C-terminal carboxylate of the peptide and Arg268 of MoaA (Fig. 2 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S16 and Table S3). This interaction was not observed in the other 12 conformers with less favorable binding energies (−2.3 ~ −7.9 kcal/mol, SI Appendix, Fig. S16 and Table S3), suggesting its importance in the peptide–MoaA interaction. All eight high-affinity conformers showed the same Ile9 side chain rotamer (trans gauche plus, SI Appendix, Fig. S17 and Table S4) bound in the hydrophobic pocket formed by Thr73, Ser19, Arg17, and Arg268 (Fig. 2 G and H and SI Appendix, Figs. S16 and S18). Also conserved were the H-bond interactions through the amino acid residues in the MoaA active site cavity (Ser19 and Leu279 backbone carbonyl) and the Gating loop (Asn201, Fig. 2G and SI Appendix, S16 and Table S3). As described below, we found that the interactions conserved among the high-affinity conformers are critical for the MoaA catalytic function, suggesting that these high-affinity conformers are likely the catalytically relevant conformation.Unique Conformation and Functional Significance of Ile9.In all the high-affinity conformers, Ile9 adopts a conformation at the boundary of favored and disallowed regions of the Ramachandran plot (Φ +177 ~ 179° and Ψ −45 ~ −49°, SI Appendix, Table S4). While this conformation is unusual and likely has a relatively high free energy (22), a series of evidence supports its existence and functional significance. First, the conformation of Ile9 is based on the numerous interresidue NOEs between Ile9 and Met6, Tyr8, Gly10, and Gly11 (SI Appendix, Table S1). Removing any of these NOEs from the structural calculation does not significantly alter the Ile9 conformation, suggesting that the multitude of NOE signals supports this conformation. Second, while some of the computed peptide conformers had Ile9 in the favorable region of the Ramachandran plot (conformers 2-4, 6-8, and 19 with Φ ~ −140° and Ψ −70 ~ −80°, SI Appendix, Table S4) (22), these conformers show ~3 to 6 kcal/mol less favorable theoretical binding energy than the representative high-affinity conformer (Fig. 2 F–J). Thus, the favorable binding energy of the high-affinity conformers likely compensates for the less favorable free energy of the unusual conformation of Ile9.We also found a very strong correlation between the Ile9 conformation and the interaction between the C-terminal carboxylate and Arg268. As described above, this H-bond interaction was observed with all the high-affinity conformers, while it was not observed with other conformers, including those with Ile9 in the favorable region of the Ramachandran plot (conformers 2-4, 6-8, and 19, SI Appendix, Table S3). Since the results described in the subsequent section suggest that this H-bonding interaction is essential for triggering SAM cleavage, these observations indicate that the unique conformation of Ile9 is likely crucial for MoaA catalysis.To obtain insights into the structure–function relationship, we synthesized 11mer peptide variants with Ile9 replaced with Val, Ala, Leu, L-norvaline (Nva), or Phe, and characterized their ability to restore the activity of G340A-MoaA. As a result, even the modest change in the side chain size by the I9Nva and I9V mutations resulted in a significant increase (>1,000-fold) in apparent Kd (KdApp, SI Appendix, Figs. S19 and S20 A–C). The variants with a significantly smaller (Ala) or larger (Phe) amino acid showed minimal to no activity (<1% of wt activity, SI Appendix, Fig. S19). These observations suggest the importance of the Ile9 side chain in the 11mer–MoaA interaction. In the MoaA•11mer complex model, the Ile9 side chain fills the hydrophobic pocket consisting of Thr73, Ser19, Arg268, and Arg17 in MoaA (Fig. 2 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S18), which likely limits the conformation of Ile9 to those observed in the high-affinity conformers. Based on all of these observations, we propose that MoaA uses the unique conformation of Ile9 to direct the GG-motif and the C-terminal carboxylate toward Arg268. In fact, Ile9 is highly conserved among MoaA homologs (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). These observations collectively support our structural model and the functional significance of Ile9.The GG Motif Bridges the GTP Binding Site and SAM.In all eight high-affinity conformers, the GG motif filled the space between GTP and SAM (Fig. 2I). The positions of the two Gly residues are consistent with the reported inactivation of MoaA by GG-motif mutations (13). Gly11 Cα is in a crowded area between Arg268 and Arg17, and adding any side chain to this residue would cause a steric clash. Gly10 is located close to the methyl group of SAM, and any side chain on this residue would interfere with SAM binding. These structural observations provide the molecular insights into the mechanism by which the GG-motif mutations inactivate MoaA and cause human MoCD disease.The observed binding mode of the GG motif allows the peptide to surround the SAM methyl group (Fig. 2 I and J). The SAM methyl is within 3.4 to 3.8 Å from the Gly10-Cα and carbonyl oxygens of Gly10 and Ile9 (SI Appendix, Table S5). The interactions through the SAM methyl would sterically push the SAM sulfonium toward [4Fe-4S]RS or limit the motion of the SAM sulfonium away from [4Fe-4S]RS. In the proposed mechanism of SAM cleavage through a direct inner sphere electron transfer from [4Fe-4S]RS to the antibonding orbital of the C-S bond (5, 23, 24), the distance between the sulfonium sulfur of SAM and the unique Fe of [4Fe-4S]RS shortens as the C5′-S bond of SAM is cleaved (25, 26). For MoaA, it is important not to initiate the radical chemistry by ATP that exists at concentrations higher than GTP (28). Therefore, we characterized MoaA’s activity toward ATP. The binding of ATP to MoaA was confirmed by its ability to inhibit the GTP-3′,8-cyclase activity with Ki of 106 ± 8 µM (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). However, ATP did not facilitate the reductive SAM cleavage (SI Appendix, Fig. S27), nor did it coordinate the [4Fe-4S]AUX cluster (SI Appendix, Fig. S28). In this model, GTP first coordinates to [4Fe-4S]AUX with its keto-tautomer stabilized by Arg266 (Step 1 in Fig. 4). This GTP binding mode stabilizes the conformation of Arg268 and creates the binding site for the C-terminal carboxylate. To insert the C-terminal tail, the C-terminal α helix breaks into two short helices and folds back toward the active site (Step 2). Consequently, the N-terminal region of the C-terminal tail (331KKIN334) interacts with the Gating loop and induces its conformational change to form the SAM adenine binding site. Once the C-terminal tail is inserted into the active site, the GG motif binds between Arg268 and the SAM methyl group. This interaction facilitates the motion of sulfonium toward [4Fe-4S]RS (Step 3), and catalyzes the reductive cleavage of SAM. Most notably, in anaerobic sulfatase maturase (AtsB) (33) and spore photoproduct lyase (SPL), upon substrate binding, an active site amino acid side chain (Q64 in AtsB and Y98 in SPL) moved toward SAM methyl and/or Cγ in a way that would facilitate the motion of the SAM sulfonium toward [4Fe-4S]RS (SI Appendix, Fig. S31 A and B). Thus, we propose that these interactions through SAM methyl or Cγ are important for coupling the substrate binding and reductive SAM cleavage. These analyses and the results described in this manuscript together provide a testable hypothesis for studying the general mechanism of controlled radical initiation in radical SAM enzymes.

Conformational Change upon C-Terminal Tail Binding.To model the peptide structures into a catalytically relevant form of MoaA, we investigated a potential protein conformational change upon the C-terminal tail binding by modeling the full-length MoaA structure using AlphaFold2 (17). While MoaA was reported as a dimer in the crystal structures (7, 8), for this analysis, we consider only the monomer unit because the C-terminal tail was proposed to interact with the active site of the same subunit (13). No evidence suggests that the active site structure is affected by the presence of the other subunit. Of the five predicted structures (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), the top four ranked structures have the C-terminal tail inserted into the active site, which was made possible by the break of the C-terminal α-helix in the crystal structures (Try316-Arg328, Fig. 1D) into two short helices (Fig. 1E). The fifth model has the C-terminal α-helix similar to the crystal structures, where the C-terminus is far from the active site or any other part of the enzyme (SI Appendix, Fig. S11E). This conformation is inconsistent with the catalytically essential role of the GG motif and, therefore, catalytically irrelevant. A comparison of the predicted structures and the crystal structures (Fig. 1 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S11) suggested that the C-terminal tail binding to the active site could induce the closure of the loop consisting of Phe196-Asp206 (Gating loop, compare Fig. 1 D and E). Asp198 in this loop was previously reported to interact with one of the three cationic residues (Arg330, Lys331, and Lys332) in the C-terminal tail (13). In general, radical SAM enzymes interact with SAM adenine through several hydrophobic amino acid side chains and H-bonding interactions between N1 and 6-NH2 of SAM adenine and the enzyme’s main-chain amides (SI Appendix, Fig. S12) (18). Consistently, in the MoaA AlphaFold model, the mainchain nitrogen and oxygen of Met197 are positioned close to N1 and 6-NH2 of SAM adenine, and Phe196, Met197, and Val199 form the hydrophobic pocket (Fig. 1 F and G). Mutation of the amino acid side chains close to the adenine base (M197A/N203A-MoaA) significantly increased Km for SAM (78-fold relative to wt, SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Met335 clashes with SAM in the active site (SI Appendix, Fig. S14A). Distinction of ATP and GTP.The coupling of substrate binding and SAM cleavage is important to minimize unwanted radical formation in the complex cellular environment. For MoaA, it is important not to initiate the radical chemistry by ATP that exists at concentrations higher than GTP (28). Therefore, we characterized MoaA’s activity toward ATP. The binding of ATP to MoaA was confirmed by its ability to inhibit the GTP-3′,8-cyclase activity with Ki of 106 ± 8 µM (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). However, ATP did not facilitate the reductive SAM cleavage (SI Appendix, Fig. S27), nor did it coordinate the [4Fe-4S]AUX cluster (SI Appendix, Fig. S28). On the other hand, inosine 5′-triphosphate (ITP) was able to trigger SAM cleavage with an efficiency comparable to that with GTP (SI Appendix, Fig. S27). Furthermore, ITP was converted into the corresponding cyclic product, 3′,8-cyclo-7,8-dihydro-ITP (3′,8-cH2ITP, SI Appendix, Fig. S29). These observations suggest that the O6 of guanine and inosine base is essential and sufficient for the coordination of N1 of purine to the [4Fe-4S]AUX cluster and the subsequent SAM cleavage. These results are consistent with the proposed function of Arg266 in stabilization of the keto-tautomer and facilitating N1 coordination to the [4Fe-4S]AUX cluster. Therefore, this substrate recognition mechanism is likely used to distinguish GTP and ATP and prevent radical initiation with the highly abundant ATP.

General Mechanism of Coupling the SAM Cleavage and Substrate Binding.To assess the generality of the substrate-induced interaction with SAM, we analyzed reported structures of radical SAM enzymes in complexes with substrate and SAM or methionine (SI Appendix, Fig. S31). In all of these structures, we identified interactions through the methionine part of SAM, either the methyl or Cγ. Most notably, in anaerobic sulfatase maturase (AtsB) (33) and spore photoproduct lyase (SPL), upon substrate binding, an active site amino acid side chain (Q64 in AtsB and Y98 in SPL) moved toward SAM methyl and/or Cγ in a way that would facilitate the motion of the SAM sulfonium toward [4Fe-4S]RS (SI Appendix, Fig. S31 A and B). Especially in the SPL–substrate complex, SAM methyl moved closer to [4Fe-4S]RS compared to the structure without the substrate (SI Appendix, Fig. S31B). In PFL-AE (34), one of the best characterized radical SAM enzymes, the carbonyl oxygen of the substrate Gly residue is positioned close to the SAM methyl (SI Appendix, Fig. S31C). In all the structures we analyzed (SI Appendix, Fig. S31), we identified interactions that would facilitate the motion of the methionine moiety of SAM toward [4Fe-4S]RS while leaving the ribose moiety relatively flexible for the migration of 5′-dA• to the substrate. Thus, we propose that these interactions through SAM methyl or Cγ are important for coupling the substrate binding and reductive SAM cleavage. These analyses and the results described in this manuscript together provide a testable hypothesis for studying the general mechanism of controlled radical initiation in radical SAM enzymes.

The resultant solution was acidified by the addition of 1 M HCl to a pH of 2. After removing the precipitated protein by centrifugation at 5,000× g for 10 min, the supernatant was concentrated in vacuo to <10 mL using a rotary evaporator, followed by neutralization back to neutral pH and lyophilization. The lyophilized peptide was dissolved in ~2 mL of 50% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA)/H2O, and any undissolved precipitations were removed by centrifugation. The resultant supernatant was injected into HPLC equipped with an ODS Hypersil column (3 μm, 4.6 × 150 mm, ThermoFisher Scientific). Chromatography was performed by a linear gradient of 0 to 100% 0.1% TFA/MeCN in 0.1% TFA/H2O (16 min, 1.5 mL/min, retention time: 7.6 min for 11mer peptide) and monitored by UV absorption at 280 nm. The eluate containing [U-13C,15N]11mer peptide was fractionated, combined, and concentrated in vacuo by a rotary evaporator. The resultant solution was then lyophilized, brought into the glove box, and redissolved in anaerobic H2O. The final labeled peptide was analyzed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry, which was conducted on a 6,224 TOF LC/MS system equipped with a Phenomenex Kinetix C18 EVO column (3 × 100 mm, 2.6 µm) to confirm the isotope enrichment. The final yield is typically ~0.25 µmol labeled peptide/g cell paste.

The docking parameters included an energy range of 3 kcal/mol and an exhaustiveness level of 20. Each of the 20 NMR structures (conformers) of the peptide was docked individually. Because the peptide NMR data did not provide information about the conformations of the C-terminal carboxylate and the side chain amides of Asn5 and Asn7, the Gly11 Cα-C and Asn5/7 Cβ-Cγ bonds were set flexible. On the receptor, the side chains of Asp19, Thr73, Val199, Ser271, and Leu279 were set flexible because they are in the putative peptide binding site and may change their conformations upon peptide binding. The results of the docking analyses were displayed and exported as pdbqt files using AutoDock Tools (v. 1.5.7) for analysis in PyMOL 2.5.8 (Schrödinger, LLC).

HYSCORE Spectroscopy.MoaA samples were prepared under strictly anaerobic conditions (<0.1 ppm O2). MoaA Arg mutants or wt-MoaA (375 μM) were first prereduced by 1.5 mM SDT in 50 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol with 5 mM DTT at 25 °C for 60 min. The prereduced enzymes were then mixed with [15N5]GTP in the same buffer to the final concentrations of 250 μM MoaA, 1 mM SDT, and 250 µM [15N5]GTP and final volume of 250 µL. The samples were transferred to HYSCORE EPR tubes (4 mm OD, 3 mm ID clear-fused quartz tubes, Quartz Scientific Inc.) and freeze-quenched manually by submerging into liquid nitrogen. HYSCORE spectroscopy was performed on a Bruker Elexsys E580 instrument equipped with a SuperX-FT microwave bridge. For all measurements, we used a Bruker ER 4118X-MS5 resonator in combination with an Oxford CF935 helium flow cryostat. Microwave pulses generated by the microwave bridge were amplified by a 1 kW traveling wave tube amplifier (Applied Systems Engineering, model 117×). HYSCORE measurements were performed using a four-pulse sequence (π/2–τ–π/2–t1–π–t2–π/2–echo) with 8 ns π/2 pulses and a 16 ns π pulse. A four-step phase cycle was applied to suppress unwanted echoes (45). Blind-spot defining delay between first two pulses (τ value) was set to 132 ns. The microwave frequency was set to 9.34 to 9.37 GHz. Data processing included baseline correction using a second-order polynomial, zero filling to 1,024 points, and subsequent Fourier transformation, disregarding the imaginary channel, and was done using home-written programs in the MATLAB environment using the Kazan Viewer package (46).

DFT Calculation.DFT calculation was performed using the previously reported simplified model of the MoaA active site (20). This model includes GTP with methyl-guanidinium ions as mimics of three active site Arg residues (Arg17, Arg266, and Arg268). All calculations were performed using the Gaussian 16 program (47) by applying hybrid functional Becke (48), 3-parameter, Lee-Yang-Parr (49) with the D3 version of Grimme dispersion with Becke–Johnson damping (50) (B3LYP-GD3BJ) and the 6-311G(d) basis set (51). The vibrational frequencies for all structures were calculated at 298.15 K and 1 atm pressure.
