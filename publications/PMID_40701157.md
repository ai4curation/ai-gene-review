Gasdermins are canonically associated with plasma membrane pore formation and
lytic cell death. Gasdermin C (GsdmC), predominantly expressed in intestinal
epithelial cells (IECs), seems to operate independently of these canonical
roles. Here, we show that activated GsdmC is increased in response to type 2
immunity in the gut, driven by Cathepsin S (CTSS)-mediated cleavage. Although
IEC cell death is not the main consequence of GsdmC cleavage, inserting a single
amino acid (aa) within the lipid-binding motif to match that of the other
gasdermins enhanced GsdmC oligomerization and increased GsdmC-mediated cell
death. Mechanistically, instead of localizing to the plasma membrane, we showed
that cleaved GsdmC targeted Rab7+ vesicles, such as late endosomes. This
modulated lipid droplet accumulation, which promoted goblet cell hyperplasia and
type 2 immune responses. These findings demonstrate how GsdmC in IEC protects
against helminth infection and expands the role of gasdermins beyond cell death
and cytokine release.

INTRODUCTION

Gasdermin (Gsdm) proteins play a crucial role in inflammation, particularly in initiating pyroptosis, a form of programmed necrotic cell death1. Unlike many other immune modulators, transcriptional regulation of Gsdm family proteins is not sufficient to execute their biological functions. Gsdms A-E share highly conserved N-terminal (N-ter) and C-terminal (C-ter) domains separated by a variable linker1. The C-ter exhibits self-inhibition by completely masking the hydrophobic pocket of the N-ter that binds lipids. Thus, even if Gsdm expression is transcriptionally induced, in most cases, Gsdm is typically non-functional until cleaved by a protease to release the N-ter from the self-inhibited C-ter. In addition to protease-mediated cleavage, studies have identified other post-translational modifications that may activate Gsdms. Examples of proteases that directly activate Gsdm proteins include, SpeB for GSDMA, Granzyme A for GSDMB, Caspase-1, 4, 5, 8, 11 for GSDMD, and Granzyme B or Caspase-3, 6 for GSDME2. For GSDMC, studies in cancer cells and in the context of radiation have shown that GSDMC activation can drive pyroptosis and that this is mediated by caspase-8 or caspase-6 mediated cleavage of GSDMC3–6. Thus, the proteases that activate Gsdm play a key role in Gsdm biology.

Gsdm proteins are increasingly recognized as important players in gastrointestinal health and disease7. Several Gsdms are highly expressed in the gut, with diverse functions shaped by cellular context and external stimuli7. In contrast to other Gsdms, Gasdermin C (GSDMC) is specifically expressed at low levels in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) under steady-state conditions but is increased during infection with the type-2 promoting helminth Heligmosomoides polygyrus (H. polygyrus). In this context, GSDMC supports IL-33 release from IECs and promote anti-helminth immunity, independent of pyroptosis8,9. Moreover, the mast cell-derived protease Mcpt1 contributes to GSDMC cleavage and IL-33 release during H. polygyrus infection9. While these studies establish a functional link between GSDMC, mast cells, and IL-33 in this model, the direct cleavage of GSDMC by Mcpt1 using purified recombinant proteins has not yet been tested9. GSDMC cleavage can also occur in intestinal organoid cultures treated with recombinant IL-48, an IEC-intrinsic system lacking mast cells, suggesting additional, yet unidentified protease(s) within IECs can directly process intestinal GSDMC.

Here, we found that the cysteine protease Cathepsin S (CTSS) can cleave human and murine GSDMC. Infection with the helminth Nippostrongylus brasiliensis (N.b.) or colonization with the commensal protist Tritrichomonas arnold (T. arnold), was sufficient to induce CTSS-mediated GSDMC cleavage. Deficiency in either GSDMC or CTSS resulted in impaired type 2 responses to helminth infections. CTSS-generated GSDMCN-ter translocated to and penetrated Rab7+ vesicles. Inhibition of Rab7 restored impaired type-2 immunity to helminths in Gsdmc1-4−/− mice. These findings reveal how CTSS-generated GSDMCN-ter generates protective mucosal type 2 immunity and highlight a role for Gsdm proteins in modulating Rab7+ vesicles, such as late endosome/lipid droplet functions.

RESULTSGSDMC is cleaved upon helminth infection or commensal protist colonization and promotes type-2 immunityIntestinal N.b. infection triggers protective type-2 immunity characterized by IL-13-producing innate lymphoid cells (ILC2), eosinophilia, and goblet and tuft cell hyperplasia10. N.b. infection of WT mice led to robust induction of GSDMC2, GSDMC3, and GSDMC4 expression and cleavage in the jejunum compared to uninfected controls (Figure 1A–C and S1A), suggesting GSDMC may regulate type-2 immunity. While GSDMC3 was detected using an antibody capable of recognizing both the full-length protein and the cleaved N-ter fragment, the antibodies used for GSDMC2 and GSDMC4 detection only recognized the cleaved C-ter fragment and not the full-length protein (Figure 1A–C). Nonetheless, as we consistently use Gsdmc1-4−/− cells as a negative control, the signals observed in these blots (Figure 1A–C) should be attributed to GSDMC proteins, rather than non-specific or unrelated proteins that are also induced with N.b. infection. Further, although mice have four GSDMC genes, we only detected gene expression for GSDMC2–4 (Figure S1A), consistent with previous reports8.To study the role of GSDMC in N.b. infection, we generated Gsdmc1-4−/− mice by deleting the entire 160kb Gsdmc locus on the C57BL/6 background (Figure S1B), given the high sequence similarity and potential functional redundancy among GSDMC proteins11 (Figure S1C).As expected, N.b. infection of WT mice induced an increase in IL-13 producing ILC2 and Th2 cells and eosinophils in jejunum-draining mesenteric lymph nodes (mLN) compared to uninfected mice (Figure 1D–E and S1D). In contrast, N.b. infection of Gsdmc1-4−/− mice failed to significant increase in IL-13 producing ILC2 and eosinophils compared to uninfected controls and had fewer IL-13-producing ILC2 and Th2 cells and eosinophils compared to infected littermate controls (Figure 1D–E and S1D). Tuft and goblet cell hyperplasia was also reduced in infected Gsdmc1-4−/− mice compared to infected littermate controls (Figure 1F–G), and the impaired immune response was associated with higher worm burden (Figure S1E), indicating GSDMC is required for effective anti-helminth immunity.Given the role of microbiota in intestinal homeostasis, we next tested whether commensal protists such as Tritrichomonas (T.) arnold could induce GSDMC cleavage. As previously reported by our group12, T. arnold promotes mucosal innate type-2 immunity, initiated by the intestinal tuft cell-derived alarmin interleukin-25 (IL-25) in response to sensing protists or helminths. IL-25 drives a feed-forward tuft cell-ILC2 signaling circuit, in which tuft cell-derived IL-25 acts on local intestinal ILC2s to produce IL-13. This cytokine, in turn, acts on IECs expressing the IL-13/IL-4 receptor complex, promoting their differentiation into tuft and goblet cells (Tuft-ILC2 circuit)13–16. Experimental colonization of Tritrichomonas-free WT mice with T. arnold was sufficient to potently induce GSDMC expression and cleavage in jejunal IECs, as well as GSDMC cleavage in the colon (Figure 1H–I and S1F–G). Further examination showed that T. arnold colonization results in GSDMC cleavage across the intestine (duodenum to colon) (Figure S1H) and that GSDMC is specifically expressed in epithelial but no other cells in the gut (Figure S2A), consistent with previous reports8.In contrast to N.b. infection, which is cleared within 10 days, T. arnold colonization and the associated moderate but heightened type-2 immunity persist throughout the mouse’s lifetime without causing noticeable gut pathology under homeostatic conditions12. Concurrently, GSDMC activation remained detectable up to 30-weeks post-T. arnold colonization (Figure 1J–K). As expected, using IL4ra−/− mice, we demonstrated that T. arnold-mediated GSDMC expression and cleavage depend on IL-4/IL-13 signaling (Figure S2B–D), suggesting that the T. arnold-mediated tuft-ILC2 circuit is critical for GSDMC expression and cleavage. However, T. arnold colonization was not impacted by the presence or absence of GSDMC (Fig S2E), which is in line with our findings and other studies showing that in contrast to N.b. infection, type-2 immunity is not controlling commensal Tritrichomonas colonization (Fig S2F and16). Colonization of mice with T. arnold resulted in increased tuft and goblet cell numbers compared to non-protist colonized mice. However, the tuft cell increase was less robust in T. arnold colonized Gsdmc1-4−/− mice compared to T. arnold colonized littermate controls. No differences were observed in goblet cell numbers between genotypes (Figure S2G–H). These findings suggest GSDMC’s role in anti-helminth immunity differs from its role during commensal protist colonization.In support of our findings, treatment of intestinal organoid cultures with IL-13 was sufficient to promote GSDMC expression and cleavage in IECs (Figure 1L–M and S2I). Given that intestinal organoid cultures consist only of IECs and therefore lack immune cells and other cell types17, we reasoned that IL-13-induced GSDMC cleavage was IEC-intrinsic, occurring independently of support from other cell types.In summary, we identified ~30 kDa GSDMC2–4 cleavage products in two in vivo models, commensal protist colonization and helminth infection, and in intestinal organoids, processed by an unknown protease.Cathepsin S cleaves human and murine GSDMC proteins.As stated previously, GSDMC is likely not functional until it is processed by a protease to release the N-terminus from the self-inhibited C-terminus. Thus, the proteases that activate GSDMC play a central role in GSDMC biology. Caspase-8 cleaves human GSDMC (in certain cancer cells), GSDMD (in macrophages), and GSDME (in certain cancer cells) via direct and indirect mechanisms that lead to cell lysis and pyroptosis3,4,18,19. However, in contrast to GSDMC, IL-13 was insufficient to mediate cleavage of GSDMD or GSDME in IECs (Figure 1L–M and S3A–B). IL-13 also failed to activate caspase-8 in intestinal organoids or under type-2 stimulating conditions in vivo (Figure S3C–E). Using pan-caspase inhibitors and caspase1/11−/− mice, we found that caspases are not required for IL-13-mediated GSDMC cleavage in intestinal organoid cultures (Figure S3F–G). Additionally, caspase-8 inhibition did not affect GSDMC cleavage under N.b. infection conditions in vivo (Figure S3H). Hence, a protease(s) other than caspases must process GSDMC in IECs.GSDMC expression in IEC is regulated in a STAT6-dependent manner in response to type-2 immunity8. Thus, we hypothesized that the protease that cleaves GSDMC is likely also downstream of STAT6. In macrophages, STAT6-activating cytokines IL-4 and IL-13 alone or in combination with other related cytokines (IL-6 and -10) induce six cathepsin (CTS) proteases20. We excluded cathepsins from further analysis that have strict exopeptidase functions (e.g., CTSC and CTSZ)21 or only function in an acidic environment (e.g., CTSH and CTSL) such as lysosomes since GSDMC cleavage is most likely occurring in the cytosol22,23. The two remaining cathepsins, CTSB and CTSS were further evaluated24,25. We found that CTSS, but not CTSB, cleaved both human and mouse GSDMC proteins when co-expressed in HEK293T cells, generating a 25~30 kDa C-terminal fragment (Figure 2A–D). Co-expression of CTSS with GSDMC also induced moderate cell lysis (~20%) in HEK293T cells, suggesting that CTSS can functionally activate GSDMC, rather than just digest GSDMC into non-specific or non-functional fragments (Figure 2E–G). To demonstrate that CTSS is the direct protease for GSDMC, we conducted in vitro protease assays using purified recombinant proteins and found that CTSS produced robust GSDMC cleavage products (Figure 2H and S3I). GSDMC had a 6-His- and SUMO tag at its N-terminus, thus the full length was ~70kDa. CTSS cleaved GSDMC effectively in both pH=5.5 and pH=7.5 buffers in vitro (Figure 2H and S3I), consistent with previous findings that CTSS can function in both acid and neutral pH26.We next investigated potential CTSS cleavage sites on GSDMC and whether they align with the ~30kDa C-terminal fragments identified in Figure 2A. Unlike caspase-8, which exclusively cuts after an aspartic acid (D), CTSS cleavage is not restricted to a specific amino acid or amino acid sequence27. In line with this, we observed multiple cleaved fragments of CTSS-processed GSDMC C-ter in proximity around 25~30 kDa (Figure 2A), suggesting multiple cleavage sites for CTSS. To overcome the challenge of N-ter sequencing (Edman sequencing), which requires a uniform clearcut C-ter protein fragment, we performed mass spectrometry to identify cleavage sites of CTSS on GSDMC. Taking advantage of the fact that trypsin cuts at lysine (K) and arginine (R), and that the P1 residue preference of CTSS is not K or R27 (P1 residue is the amino acid right before the cleavage site), we enriched the CTSS-cleaved C-ter GSDMC fragment by HA-IP (HA is C-tagged to the human GSDMC) from HEK293T cells. These fragments were then subjected to mass spectrometry to identify non-trypsin-generated peptides. Thus, we considered the peptides not generated with a K- or R- cleavage to be generated via CTSS-cleavage. We identified 5 non-trypsin-generated peptides, matching the molecular weight of the cleaved C-ter of GSDMC (~30 kDa) (Figure 2A and Figure S3J). Consequently, we posited that the possible cleavage sites include alanine (A)252, histidine (H)263, threonine (T)264, phenylalanine (F)284, leucine (L)285 and (F)289. The F284 site and F289 site share a similar P1’ L residue, indicating cleavage between F-L (indicated in red, with P1’ residue being the amino acid immediately after the cleavage site) (Figure S3J). To verify whether these sites are indeed cleaved by CTSS, we performed 3 small deletions on GSDMC (Δ250-294aa; Δ284–295aa; and Δ7) (Figure S3K) followed by co-expression with CTSS in HEK293T cells. Our data shows two of the small deletions (Δ7 and Δ250–294aa) successfully diminished CTSS-mediated GSDMC cleavage, while Δ284–295aa deletion largely, but not completely, prevented CTSS-mediated cleavage (Figure S3L). Collectively, these data indicate that CTSS utilizes multiple cleavage sites and acts as the direct protease for GSDMC.Cathepsin S is required for GSDMC cleavage.We utilized Ctss−/− mice to confirm the requirement of CTSS for GSDMC cleavage. IL-13-stimulated Ctss−/− intestinal organoid cultures showed reduced GSDMC cleavage compared to controls (Figure 3A–B), indicating CTSS cleaves GSDMC in an IEC-intrinsic manner, bypassing the requirement for immune cell co-cultures. We then assessed whether GSDMC cleavage is mediated by CTSS in IECs using conditions that led to GSDMC cleavage in the presence of T. arnold colonization or N.b. infection (Figure 1A–C, H). We found that GSDMC2 and GSDMC4 cleavage was not detectable in Ctss−/− mice colonized with T. arnold or infected with N.b. compared to littermate controls (Figure 3C–D, G–J, M–N). In addition, GSDMC3 cleavage was significantly reduced in Ctss−/− mice colonized with T. arnold or infected with N.b. compared to littermate controls (Figure 3E–F, K–L).Having identified CTSS as an essential protease for IEC-specific GSDMC, we next assessed CTSS localization and expression in IECs in the small intestine and colon under conditions that led to CTSS-mediated GSDMC cleavage during N.b. infection or T. arnold colonization (Figure 3C–N). In IECs, CTSS is constantly active after self-processing28 and can retain catalytic activity in both acidic and neutral pH (e.g., pH = 7.5 and Figure 2H and S3I)26, implying that CTSS can function inside and outside of lysosomes. We detected that around 80% of CTSS was localized inside lysosomes (colocalized with Lamp1+, blue arrows) and 20% of CTSS localized outside of lysosomes (a diffusion pattern, white arrows) in IECs of the small intestine and colon (Figure S4A–B), indicating that CTSS and GSDMC could potentially interact in the cytosol. Specificity for CTSS staining was validated in Ctss−/− mice (Figure S4C). Moreover, intestinal organoid cultures increased CTSS gene expression upon IL-13 stimulation and CTSS protein expression was specifically increased in IECs but not in the lamina propria of N.b. infected mice (Figure S4D–E).Unlike GSDMC, which is exclusively expressed in IECs (Figure S2A), CTSS is expressed in both IECs and immune cells (26 and Figure S4E). To determine the importance of CTSS expression in IECs in protecting from N.b. helminth infection, we irradiated CD45.2+
Ctss−/− mice and littermate controls and reconstituted mice with bone marrow (BM) derived from CD45.1+ WT mice (Figure S4F). N.b. infection 6-weeks after successful reconstitution revealed a significant decrease in IL-13 producing ILC2 and Th2 cells and a substantial reduction in eosinophils in BM chimeric Ctss−/− mice compared to BM chimeric littermate controls (Figure 3O–Q). Furthermore, N.b. infection of BM chimeric Ctss−/− mice resulted in significantly decreased tuft and goblet cell expansion compared to infected littermate controls (Figure 3R–S). Based on the phenotypic differences between Ctss−/− and WT mice, we conclude that CTSS is the primary protease in IECs responsible for murine GSDMCs cleavage under type-2 stimulating conditions.Because Ctss−/− mice did not completely abolish GSDMC3 cleavage (Figure 3A–B, E–F, K–L), it is possible that another, as-yet unidentified protease may be able to compensate for loss of CTSS in cleaving GSDMC3. For example, the mast cell-derived protease, Mcpt1, has been implicated in GSDMC cleavage during H. polygyrus infection9. Although, we have demonstrated that CTSS directly cleaves GSDMC in an IECs-intrinsic manner, bypassing the requirement of mast cells and mast cell-derived Mcpt1 in the intestinal organoid system, we also investigated whether Mcpt1 can cooperate with CTSS in mediating GSDMC activation. Indeed, in cell lines co-expressing Mcpt1, CTSS, and GSDMC, Mcpt1 enhanced CTSS-driven GSDMC activation. However, without CTSS co-transfection, Mcpt1 alone was unable to produce detectable GSDMC cleavage by western blotting (Figure S4G).In summary, CTSS-processed GSDMCN-ter in IECs promotes positive type-2 immune feedback for anti-helminth immunity (Figure 3T).IEC death is not a key function of Cathepsin S-generated murine GSDMCN-ter under type-2 stimulating conditions.Next, we aimed to mechanistically understand how CTSS-processed GSDMCN-ter modulates type-2 immunity. While caspase- or granzyme-processed Gsdms typically use their N-ter fragments to form pores on the plasma membrane (PM), leading to pyroptosis2, including caspase-8-processed human GSDMC in certain cancer cell lines4, cell death is not an obligatory outcome of murine intestinal GSDMC activation9. We confirmed that murine GSDMC activation in IECs does not result in pyroptosis under type-2 stimulating conditions: First, IL-13 treatment of intestinal organoids failed to induce cell death, indicated by propidium iodide (PI) staining, despite CTSS-mediated GSDMC cleavage (Figure 3A and S5A). Moreover, no differences in PI+ cells or morphology were seen among IL-13-treated Gsdmc1-4−/−, Ctss−/−, and WT intestinal organoid for up to 72 hours (Figure S5B–C). Second, TUNEL staining showed no differences in IEC apoptosis between Gsdmc1-4−/− mice and littermate controls under N.b. infection or T. arnold colonization conditions (Figure S5D–E). Third, we did not observe intestinal inflammation or any noticeable histopathology in T. arnold-colonized mice compared to non-colonized controls, despite detectable GSDMC activation in T. arnold-colonized mice up to 30 weeks post-colonization (Figure 1J–K and S5F–G). This is a sharp contrast to GSDME, which can induce massive pyroptosis and inflammation in intestines when activated by chemotherapy drugs such as cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil, marked by severely disrupted crypts and villi, which is attenuated in Gsdme−/− mice29.Gsdm pores can also release IL-1 cytokine family members, including IL-1, IL-18, and IL-33, mediating inflammation30,31. GSDMC has been implicated in IL-33 release from goblet cells in H. polygyrus infection8,9. However, we found no differences in IL-1β or IL-18 release from intestinal explants of N.b.-infected Gsdmc1-4−/− mice versus littermate controls (Figure S5H–I), and IL-33 was below detection limits. GSDMC is cleaved upon helminth infection or commensal protist colonization and promotes type-2 immunity

Intestinal N.b. infection triggers protective type-2 immunity characterized by IL-13-producing innate lymphoid cells (ILC2), eosinophilia, and goblet and tuft cell hyperplasia10. N.b. infection of WT mice led to robust induction of GSDMC2, GSDMC3, and GSDMC4 expression and cleavage in the jejunum compared to uninfected controls (Figure 1A–C and S1A), suggesting GSDMC may regulate type-2 immunity. Further, although mice have four GSDMC genes, we only detected gene expression for GSDMC2–4 (Figure S1A), consistent with previous reports8.

To study the role of GSDMC in N.b. infection, we generated Gsdmc1-4−/− mice by deleting the entire 160kb Gsdmc locus on the C57BL/6 background (Figure S1B), given the high sequence similarity and potential functional redundancy among GSDMC proteins11 (Figure S1C).

This cytokine, in turn, acts on IECs expressing the IL-13/IL-4 receptor complex, promoting their differentiation into tuft and goblet cells (Tuft-ILC2 circuit)13–16. Experimental colonization of Tritrichomonas-free WT mice with T. arnold was sufficient to potently induce GSDMC expression and cleavage in jejunal IECs, as well as GSDMC cleavage in the colon (Figure 1H–I and S1F–G). Further examination showed that T. arnold colonization results in GSDMC cleavage across the intestine (duodenum to colon) (Figure S1H) and that GSDMC is specifically expressed in epithelial but no other cells in the gut (Figure S2A), consistent with previous reports8.

In contrast to N.b. infection, which is cleared within 10 days, T. arnold colonization and the associated moderate but heightened type-2 immunity persist throughout the mouse’s lifetime without causing noticeable gut pathology under homeostatic conditions12. However, T. arnold colonization was not impacted by the presence or absence of GSDMC (Fig S2E), which is in line with our findings and other studies showing that in contrast to N.b. infection, type-2 immunity is not controlling commensal Tritrichomonas colonization (Fig S2F and16). Thus, we reveal a mechanism for GSDMC biology under type-2 stimulating conditions that is independent of pyroptosis but reliant on Rab7.

Different from other Gsdms, the CTSS-processed GSDMCN-ter localizes with Rab7+ vesicles. Although many Gsdms primarily target the plasma membrane, both GSDMD and GSDME can also target mitochondria before causing damage to the plasma membrane41,42. For example, activated GSDMD can cause cardiolipin-dependent mitochondrial destruction, in both inner and outer membrane41. It is worth investigating whether GSDMCN-ter has a unique lipid-binding pattern distinct from other Gsdms or, alternatively, whether other protein components on Rab7+ vesicles facilitate the preference of GSDMCN-ter for lipid membranes of Rab7+ vesicles.

On a functional level, Rab7 associates with LDs and regulates lysosome recruitment to LDs35. LDs harbor lipids and enzymes that can readily be made available to synthesize immunomodulatory mediators like PGD2, which modulates anti-helminth immunity40. While our data indicate a role of CTSS-generated GSDMCN-ter in PGD2 release and the restoration of goblet cell hyperplasia in response to helminth infection, the partial restoration of type-2 immunity upon PGD2 signaling blockade, compared to the full restoration achieved with Rab7 inhibition, suggests that GSDMC may modulate other lipids or exert lipid-independent but Rab7-dependent functions critical for type-2 immunity to helminths. For example, GSDMCN-ter may affect Rab7+ vesicles to indirectly regulate secretion of cytokines, chemokines, or lipids.

Our findings on GSDMC using the N.b. infection model and intestinal organoids reveal both consistencies and distinctions compared to previous studies in the H. polygyrus model. GSDMC protects against H. polygyrus infection via IL-33 release from goblet cells8, as shown by rescue of Gsdmc-deficient mice with recombinant IL-338. Finally, in agreement with prior reports we found no evidence for GSDMC-driven pyroptosis during helminth infections8,9. The fact that GSDMC cleavage can be promoted and sustained in mice colonized with the commensal protist T. arnold also argues against a role of GSDMC in pyroptosis.

GSDMC expression in small intestinal IECs is barely detectable without type-2 inducing microbes but increases up to 100-fold, and is cleaved, during T. arnold colonization or helminth infection. In contrast, GSDMC is constitutively expressed in the colon but requires T. arnold for cleavage. This highlights tissue-specific regulation and supports a key role of GSDMC In type-2 responses. While IL-13 is linked to allergic diseases43, the role for GSDMC in extraintestinal tissues remain unknown and warrants further study.
