# Role of astrocytic leptin receptor subtypes on leptin permeation across hCMEC/D3 human brain endothelial cells.

Role of astrocytic leptin receptor subtypes on leptin permeation across hCMEC/D3
human brain endothelial cells.

Hsuchou H(1), Kastin AJ, Tu H, Joan Abbott N, Couraud PO, Pan W.

Author information:
(1)Blood-Brain Barrier Group, Pennington Biomedical Research Center, Baton
Rouge, Louisiana, USA.

Astrocytic leptin receptors (ObR) can be up-regulated in conditions such as
adult-onset obesity. To determine whether the levels and subtypes of astrocytic
ObR modulate leptin transport, we co-cultured hCMEC/D3 human brain endothelial
cells and C6 astrocytoma cells in the Transwell system, and tested leptin
permeation from apical to basolateral chambers. In comparison with hCMEC alone,
co-culture of C6 cells reduced the permeability of paracellular markers and
leptin. Unexpectedly, ObRb over-expression in C6 cells increased leptin
permeation whereas ObRa over-expression showed no effect when compared with the
control group of pcDNA-transfected C6 cells. By contrast, the paracellular
permeability to the sodium fluorescein control was unchanged by over-expression
of ObR subtypes. Leptin remained intact after crossing the monolayer as shown by
HPLC and acid precipitation, and this was not affected by C6 cell co-culture or
the over-expression of different ObR subtypes. Thus, increased expression of
ObRb (and to a lesser extent ObRe) in C6 cells specifically increased the
permeation of leptin across the hCMEC monolayer. Consistent with the evidence
that the most apparent regulatory changes of ObR during obesity and inflammation
occur in astrocytes, the results indicate that astrocytes actively regulate
leptin transport across the blood-brain barrier, a mechanism independent of
reduction of paracellular permeability.

Introduction

Leptin is an adipocyte-derived hormone affecting energy metabolism. It crosses the blood-brain barrier (BBB) by a specific transport system (Banks et al. 1996). All membrane-bound leptin receptor (ObR) subtypes can effectively mediate leptin binding, endocytosis, and subsequent exocytosis (Tu et al. 2007). Among them, the short isoform ObRa is the most abundantly expressed subtype in cerebral microvessels and therefore a major transporting receptor (Bjørbæk et al. 1998; Hileman et al. 2002). The longest isoform – ObRb - has a Box3 domain that can be tyrosine phosphorylated and activates Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (STAT)-3. All membrane-bound isoforms increase Janus kinase (JAK)-2 and extracellular receptor kinases (ERK) (Bjørbæk et al. 1997). These cellular signaling events can also be modulated by several other molecules, including suppressor of cytokine signaling proteins (SOCS)-3, protein-tyrosine phosphatase (PTP1B) (Bouret and Simerly 2004; Ahima et al. 2006; Bence et al. 2006), and most recently described, cyclin-dependent kinase (Cdk)-5 and its activators p35 and p25 kinases (He et al. 2009). The soluble receptor ObRe, however, serves as an antagonist to leptin transport as well as signaling (Tu et al. 2008). The dynamic balance of leptin and ObR subtypes is seen during development (Bouret and Simerly 2006; Bouret 2008; Pan et al. 2008b; Caron et al. 2010), nutrition and exercise (Patterson et al. 2009), and obesity (Pan et al. 2008a; Hsuchou et al. 2009a). A high fat diet upregulates ObRa mRNA in cerebral microvessels, although the immunostaining could not be quantified, possibly because of artifacts and non-specificity (Boado et al. 1998). Inflammation, including that induced by tumor necrosis factor α and lipopolysaccharide, also increases the expression of ObRa and ObRb proteins in C6 cells (Hsuchou et al. 2009b).

The BBB is a three-dimensional structure located in cerebral microvessels and postcapillary venules. It is composed of a monolayer of endothelial cells joined by tight junctions, underlaid by a continuous basement membrane, and reinforced by pericytes and astrocytic endfeet. With a surface area between 150 and 200 cm2 g- 1 tissue, it is the largest interface for blood–brain exchange (Abbott et al. 2006, 2010). Astrocytes are the main component of the BBB in elasmobranch fish and sturgeons (Bundgaard and Abbott 2010), and they facilitate the maintenance of the mammalian BBB (Holash et al. 1993). Neuron-glia interactions are important in BBB formation (Banerjee and Bhat 2007; Iadecola and Nedergaard 2007). Astrocytes also play crucial roles in regulating synaptic transmission and neurovascular coupling by calcium oscillation and release of glial transmitters (Haydon and Carmignoto 2006). However, the effects of astrocytes on leptin transport at the BBB have not been studied.

Leptin-deficient ob/ob mice and ObRb-defective db/db mice of early-onset obesity show reduced brain weight and an immature pattern of expression of synaptic and glial proteins. There is region-specific decrease of myelin basic protein, proteolipid protein, and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (Ahima et al. 1999). By contrast, in mouse models of adult-onset obesity, including agouti viable yellow (Avy) and diet-induced obesity, there is an increase of GFAP and an increase of ObR immunoreactivity in these reactive astrocytes (Pan et al. 2008a; Hsuchou et al. 2009a). Saturation of the transport system for leptin at the level of the BBB is a major component of leptin resistance (Kastin et al. 1999; Banks et al. 2000; Banks 2003; Banks and Farrell 2003; Kastin and Pan 2006; Adam and Findlay 2010).

But how does increased astrocytic ObR further affect leptin transport across the BBB? In the presence of the differential regulation of ObR isoforms in various physiological conditions, how do these subtypes in astrocytes affect leptin transport? Since primary astrocytes are better differentiated and less susceptible to genetic manipulation, we used C6 astrocytoma cells to regulate the level and subtypes of ObR expression. C6 cell co-culture has been used widely in the BBB community (Raub et al. 1992; Abbruscato and Davis 1999; Dohgu et al. 2000; Easton and Abbott 2002; Brown et al. 2003). Therefore we examined the effects of ObR levels and subtypes in C6 cells on leptin permeation across the human cerebral microvascular endothelial cell (hCMEC) monolayer. The permeability kinetic results were strengthened by degradation assays and by cellular imaging studies to determine the distribution of fluorescently tagged leptin. By comparison with paracellular permeability markers, we showed that leptin crosses the endothelial cell monolayer in intact form by two main routes: a small degree of non-specific permeation because of residual “leakiness” intrinsic to the current hCMEC Transwell system, and specific transport that is subject to regulation by the level and subtype of ObR expression in C6 cells. This is the first study showing a definitive role of astrocytic ObR in leptin transport across co-cultured cerebral endothelial cells, and it has strong physiological implications since adult-onset obesity is associated with upregulation of astrocytic ObR in selective brain regions (Pan et al. 2008a; Hsuchou et al. 2009a).

Transwell culture and leptin permeability assaysFor this in-vitro model of the BBB, we used hCMEC/D3 cells (abbreviated as hCMEC), which are immortalized cerebral microvascular endothelial cells of human origin (Weksler et al. 2005; Poller et al. 2008). Carrier-free recombinant mouse leptin was purchased from R & D Systems and radioactively labeled with 125I (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA) by the chloramine-T method or fluorescently labeled with Alexa-568 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and purified by size-exclusion chromatography as previously described (Kastin et al. 2000; Tu et al. 2008). 131I-albumin, TRITC-albumin (MW = 66 KD), FITC-albumin, or FITC-dextran (MW = 20 kD), all from Sigma (St. Louis, MO), were also included as internal controls for different experiments.A. For both group b and t, C6 cells were seeded 1 h before initiation of the co-culture procedure. Both hCMEC and C6 cells were used at about 60 % confluency at the time of co-culture, and were allowed to grow for 36 h before the transport assay (or 48 h for the repeat study with similar results). At this time, both types of cells were fully confluent.A mixture of 125I-leptin and 131I-albumin was added to the apical (donor) chamber of the Transwell insert (about 0.5 μCi in 0.5 ml of hCMEC culture medium). After 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min, 10 μl of medium from the basolateral (receiver) chamber (1.5 ml) was sampled. The assay was terminated at 60 min, when 10 μl of apical medium was also sampled. Radioactivity was measured in a 1470 Wallac Wizard γ-counter (Perkin Elmer) by use of a dual channel program (for 125I and 131I without crossover). The rest of the media in both chambers was collected for subsequent degradation assays.B. Test of the effect of ObR subtypes in C6 cells on leptin permeation across hCMEC cellsTo overexpress ObR subtypes by electroporation, 3 μg of ObRa, ObRb, ObRe plasmid, or the empty vector pcDNA3.1 was mixed with 2×106 of C6 cells in single cell suspension in 100 μl Nucleofector solution. The default U-30 (C6) protocol in the Nucleofector device (Amaxa Inc, Gaithersburg, MD) was used. The overexpression of ObR in each group was verified by flow cytometry after immunostaining of ObR (AF497, R & D Systems, Minneapolis, MN).For determination of the effect of ObR isoforms on leptin permeation across hCMEC cells, the experimental procedure differed slightly from that in the first study because the Nucleofector manual recommends that the expression of the transfected gene in C6 should be detectable from 4 to 8 h after transfection up to 24 h. Accordingly, 105 C6 cells at about 90 % confluency were seeded on the underside of the 12-well Transwell inserts immediately after transfection with pcDNA, ObRa, ObRb, or ObRe plasmid. One additional control group with only hCMEC on top of the Transwell insert was included (n = 3/group). One h later, 1.75×105 hCMEC cells at 100 % confluency were seeded on the upper side of the Transwell insert. 125I-leptin and 131I-albumin transport assays were performed 24 h after seeding of C6 cells (23 h after initiation of co-culture) and the medium was sampled and analyzed as described in section A.C. Test of the endocytosis of leptin in hCMEC cellsTo determine the difference in leptin endocytosis in hCMEC with or without the presence of C6, Alexa568-conjugated leptin (5 or 10 μg/ml) and FITC-dextran (1 mg/ml) were added to the apical chamber as substitutes for 125I-leptin and 131I-albumin mentioned in section A. After incubation for 10, 30, and 60 min, the Transwells were quickly rinsed several times with pre-warmed PBS and fixed with pre-warmed 4 % paraformaldehyde (Sigma) for 10 min at room temperature. After several washes with PBS, the Transwell membrane was removed from the insert with a razor blade and mounted between two coverslips with Vectashield mounting media containing diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA). Test of paracellular and transcellular permeability with fluorescent tracersTranswell preparations of hCMEC with or without C6 cultured on the underside of the insert were studied at 100 % confluency 48 h after co-culture, as described in section 1.A. FITC-dextran (1 mg/ml) and TRITC-albumin (1 mg/ml) were added at time 0, and medium from the apical and basolateral chambers was collected at 60 min. Fluorescent intensity was determined after the samples (10 μl from apical and 200 μl from basolateral chamber) and standards were placed on a 96-well black-walled plate, and FITC (ex/em = 485/538 nm) and TRITC (ex/em = 544/590 nm) fluorescent emission measured by use of a dual channel fluorescent plate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA). A standard curve with dilution of stock solutions was also generated to ensure the linearity of the measurement.An additional test for transcellular permeability was performed in the 24-well Transwell system with 0.5 % sodium fluorescein (MW = 376.28, Sigma) in the apical chamber as a reference marker. As described in section 1.B., immediately after transfection with pcDNA, ObRa, ObRb, or ObRe plasmid, about 90 % of the C6 cells (3×104 cells/well) were seeded on the underside of the 24-well Transwell insert 1 h before seeding of hCMEC cells at 100 % confluency (5.12×104 cells/well) in the Transwell. Ten μl of apical medium and 100 μl basolateral medium were subjected to analysis in the fluorescent plate reader to determine the intensity of FITC, as described above.3. ImmunocytochemistryAfter 48 h (for naïve C6 co-culture, as described in section 1.A.) or 24 h (for ObR transfected C6 cell, as described in section 1.B.) of co-culture, hCMEC and C6 on the Transwells were processed by quick washes in pre-warmed phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by fixation in pre-warmed 4 % paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. After being rinsed in PBS, the cells were then permeabilized with 0.3 % Triton-X 100, blocked with 10 % normal donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA), and immunostained for the tight junction protein ZO-1 with polyclonal rabbit anti-ZO-1 antibody (2.5 μg/ml, Invitrogen) at 4°C for 48 h. After thorough washes, the cells were incubated with an Alexa488- or Alexa594- conjugated secondary antibodies (4 μg/ml, Invitrogen) at room temperature for 2 h. The Transwell membrane was then removed from the insert and mounted between two coverslips with Vectashield mounting media containing DAPI. Confocal images were acquired with an Olympus FV1000 microscope.4. Acid precipitationCollected 60 min after addition of 125I-leptin and 131I-albumin to the apical chamber, 10 μl of medium from the apical chamber (about 20,000 cpm) or 400 μl of medium from the basolateral chamber (about 4,000 cpm) was used (n = 3/group). The solution was brought to 1 ml by addition of PBS containing 1 % bovine serum albumin, and mixed vigorously with 1 ml of 30 % brine trichloroacetic acid. After 15 min of incubation on ice, the mixture was centrifuged at 3500 g at 4 °C. The supernatant and precipitate were separated and their radioactivity measured in a γ-counter. Acid precipitability was calculated as the percent of radioactivity in the precipitate divided by the total amount.5. Reversed phase high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)The second sample of each of the triplicate groups was taken for HPLC analysis, since its value was closest to the mean acid precipitation of 125I-leptin in the basolateral compartment among the three samples. Five μl of apical medium (about 10,000 cpm) or 900 μl of basolateral medium (about 9,000 to 10,000 cpm) was used. Separation was achieved with a C4 protein column. The mobile phase was a mixture of HPLC-grade water (Sigma) and acetonitrile (EMD Chemicals Inc, Gibbstown, NJ) containing 0.1 % trifluoroacetic acid. For each analysis, the proportion of acetonitrile in the mixture increased from 0% to 100 % over 30 min in a linear gradient and was maintained at 100 % for another 10 min. Forty fractions were collected at a flow rate of 1 ml/min, and the radioactivity was measured in a γ-counter.6. The ratio (/cm2) was calculated as follows:(1)Basolateral/apical ratio=Mb×VbMa×Va×Awhere M is the measured radioactivity or fluorescence intensity per unit medium (cpm/μl or RFU/μl, respectively) in the apical (a) or basolateral (b) chamber, V is the total volume of the medium in the apical (500 μl for 12-well or 100 μl for 24-well Transwell) or basolateral side (1500 μl for 12-well or 600 μl for 24-well Transwell), and A is the surface area of the Transwell membrane (1.12 cm2 for 12-well or 0.33 cm2 for 24-well Transwell).The apparent permeability coefficient (Papp, cm/s) of each group was calculated using the following equation:(2)Papp=dM/dtA×Cwhere dM/dt is cumulative radioactivity per unit time (cpm/s) in the basolateral side, A is the surface area of the Transwell membrane (1.12 cm2 for 12-well Transwell), and C is the initial concentration (cpm/ml) in the apical side.Means are presented with their standard errors. Linear regression and comparison of flux rate among groups were performed for permeability data (basolateral/apical ratio over time) by use of GraphPad Prism statistical analysis (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). A difference was considered significant if p < 0.05. All results and statistical differences were verified in replicated experiments.

To do so, we co-cultured hCMEC/D3 endothelial cells and C6 astrocytoma cells in the Transwell system, and tested leptin permeation from apical to basolateral chambers. Before testing the differential effects of ObR subtypes and overexpression, we first performed pertinent validation of the co-culture system by use of several paracellular permeability markers and immunostaining of the tight junction protein ZO-1. The presence of C6 cells induced ZO-1 expression and paracellular distribution in hCMEC cells. This coincided with the reduction of paracellular permeability. The permeation of leptin was also decreased. This indicates that while hCEMC-C6 culture is suitable for permeability assays, the results are probably a combination of paracellular diffusion and specific transport for substances like leptin. Although this may be a shortcoming of the in-vitro system that no researcher has yet been able to overcome, the cellular model remains useful in studying the mechanisms of leptin trafficking. Confocal images of specific uptake of Alexa568-leptin by hCMEC cells and their vesicular distribution further support this. The differential regulation of leptin permeation by different subtypes of ObR and their summation is probably physiological significant.

In this study, almost all of the leptin remained intact after crossing the hCMEC endothelial cell barrier as shown by acid precipitation and HPLC. This is consistent with our previous findings in mice (Banks et al. 1996; Maness et al. 1998; Kastin et al. 2001) and in cellular models (Tu et al. 2007), and represents a unique feature of the polarized transporting structure of the BBB. The role of C6 cells in the dissociation of free iodine and emergence of degraded peptide fragment was negligible. Although acid precipitation is easier to perform than HPLC and provides sufficient data for statistical analyses, it seems to have overestimated the extent of degradation while failing to detect the slight difference between the hCMEC only and co-cultured groups.

It is possible that altered astroglial ObR expression imposes a novel level of leptin resistance.
