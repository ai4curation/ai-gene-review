Metabolism is typically contextualized in conjunction with proliferation and
growth. The roles of metabolic enzymes beyond metabolism-such as in innate
immune responses-are underexplored. Using a focused short hairpin RNA
(shRNA)-mediated screen, we identified CTP synthetase 1 (CTPS1), a rate-limiting
enzyme of pyrimidine synthesis, as a negative regulator of interferon induction.
Mechanistically, CTPS1 interacts with and deamidates interferon regulatory
factor 3 (IRF3). Deamidation at N85 impairs IRF3 binding to promoters containing
IRF3-responsive elements, thus muting interferon (IFN) induction. Employing
CTPS1 conditional deletion and IRF3 deamidated or deamidation-resistant knockin
mice, we demonstrated that CTPS1-driven IRF3 deamidation restricts IFN induction
in response to viral infection in vivo. However, during immune activation, IRF3
deamidation by CTPS1 is inhibited by glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK3β) to
promote IFN induction. This work demonstrates how CTPS1 tames innate immunity
independent of its role in pyrimidine synthesis, thus expanding the functional
repertoire of metabolic enzymes into immune regulation.

INTRODUCTION

Innate immunity is the first line of host defense against invading pathogens.1 Detection of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) by innate immune system depends on different pattern recognition receptors (PRRs).2 Cells are equipped with these mechanisms to rapidly sense and respond to viral infection. Among these PRRs, Toll-like receptors (TLRs) detect viral nucleic acids3, retinoic acid-inducible gene (RIG)-1-like receptors recognize cytosolic double-stranded RNA and 5’ppp RNA4, and cGAS along with AIM2-like receptors (ALRs) recognize cytosolic dsDNA.5,6 Upon binding of viral nucleic acids, these PRRs recruit downstream adaptor proteins including, TRIF, MyD88, MAVS, and STING, which further oligomerize with IκB kinase and TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) kinases to activate NF-κB and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3).7-9 IRF3 is a key transcription factor responsible for induction of type I IFNs and plays a critical role in host antiviral innate immunity.10 Upon viral infection, cytosolic IRF3 is phosphorylated by TBK1 and IKKε, and undergoes conformational change, homo-dimerization, and subsequent nuclear translocation.11 In the nucleus, IRF3 binds to its cognate responsive element, known as IFN-stimulated response element (ISRE) that is present in the promoter of IFN-α/β and of many IFN-stimulated genes.12 With the recruitment of other transcription factors and co-activators, IFNs and other immune response genes are actively transcribed, which culminates in the establishment of an antiviral state.13

Cytidine triphosphate synthases (CTPSs) are key regulatory enzymes in pyrimidine biosynthesis. CTPSs catalyze the rate-limiting step of de novo CTP synthesis by converting UTP to CTP that is a precursor required for the metabolism of DNA, RNA and phospholipids.14 Activated CTPSs are homo-tetramers, with each monomer composed of a glutamine amidotransferase (GAT) domain and a kinase-like ammonia-ligase (AL) domain, joined by an α-helical linker.15,16 Hydrolyzing glutamine to produce ammonia, the GAT domain then transfers ammonia to AL domain, where it reacts with ATP-phosphorylated UTP to form CTP.16 GTP activates this reaction by accelerating the formation of a covalent glutamyl-enzyme catalytic intermediate.17 A well-established model is that CTPSs are regulated through feedback inhibition by CTP binding.18,19 The essential role in de novo CTP synthesis makes CTPSs key to living organisms, including viruses, bacteria, and eukaryotes.15 Emerging evidence recognizes CTPSs as targets for anticancer, antiviral and antiparasitic treatments.14 Humans have two CTPS isoforms encoded by two separate genes, CTPS1 and CTPS2, which share 75% identity.19 CTPS1, but not CTPS2, is reported to play a central role in lymphocyte proliferation, suggesting a specific role of CTPS1 in cellular events.20

In metazoans, cellular GATs catalyze the synthesis of nucleotides, amino acids, glycoproteins and an enzyme cofactor (NAD).21,22 Deamidation catalyzed by cellular GATs and viral enzymes is implicated in regulating immune response in microbial infections.23-26 Specifically, these enzymes can deamidate key signaling molecules to blunt innate immune activation, although the general roles of deamidation in fundamental biological processes remain poorly understood. In this study, we identified CTPS1 as a negative regulator of IFN induction via targeting IRF3 for deamidation. Loss of CTPS1 elevated IFN induction in vitro and in vivo, and protected mice from lethal viral infection. Deamidated IRF3 failed to bind the promoter of IFN-β, thus muting the production of IFNs and the expression of ISGs in response to both DNA and RNA viral infection. Conversely, deamidation-resistant mutant IRF3-N85A elevated immune activation and better protected mice from lethal viral infections. During immune activation, CTPS1 is inhibited by GSK3β to enable IRF3-mediated IFN induction. This study uncovers an immune regulatory role of the metabolic CTPS1, ascribing a cryptic function of metabolic enzymes in host immune defense and forging a molecular link between cellular metabolism and immune response.

RESULTSCTPS1 negatively regulates IFN inductionGATs are important for the synthesis of cellular building blocks, but can also deamidate proteins to regulate fundamental biological processes.26,27 The human genome encodes 11 GATs that can potentially function as protein deamidases.23,27 To probe plausible roles of cellular GATs in innate immune response, we performed a focused screen to assess IFN-β induction when cellular GATs are depleted. The reporter assay showed that shRNA-mediated knockdown of CTPS1 elevated Sendai virus (SeV)-induced IFN expression by ~1.5-fold (Figure 1A) Depletion of the closely related CTPS2 had no significant effect on IFN induction. The knockdown efficiency of these GATs was validated in our previous publication.23 Depletion of several GATs, including PPAT and ASNS, reduced IFN induction, possibly due to the essential roles of these metabolic enzymes in cell proliferation and survival. To examine the role of CTPS1 in innate immune defense, we constructed two 293T cell lines that each expressed a unique shRNA and validated CTPS1 depletion by quantitative real-time PCR and immunoblotting (Figures 1B and S1A). Compared with control cells, SeV infection induced higher transcript levels of IFNB1, CCL5, ISG15 and ISG56, in CTPS1-depleted 293T cells, as analyzed by real-time PCR (Figure 1C). The elevated production of IFN-β and CCL5 was further confirmed by ELISA (Figure 1D). Increased expression of IFNB1 and CCL5 was also observed in VSV-infected CTPS1-depleted 293T cells (Figure S1B). Similarly, depletion of CTPS1 in human THP-1 monocytes (Figure 1E) elevated IFN-β and CCL5 expression and production in response to SeV or vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) infection (Figures 1F, S1C and S1D). Consistent with the elevated antiviral immune response, depletion of CTPS1 reduced the replication of VSV by ~10-fold in 293T cells (Figure S1E). CRISPR-mediated deletion of CTPS1 in 293T and THP-1 cells robustly increased the expression of IFNB1 and interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), but not NF-κB-regulated IL8, in response to SeV and VSV infection as well as HT-DNA transfection (Figures 1G-1I, and S1F-S1H). These results indicate that CTPS1 specifically inhibits IFN induction.We next investigated the effect of CTPS1 on viral replication. Deletion of CTPS1 decreased the number of VSV-GFP positive cells, and reduced VSV titer in the medium by more than two orders of magnitude (Figures 1J and S1I). To determine whether CTPS1 promotes viral replication via dampening IFN induction, we depleted IFN-α/β receptor 1 (IFNAR1) or 2 (IFNAR2) in CTPS1+/+ and CTPS1−/− THP-1 cells and examined VSV replication (Figures S1J and S1K). Depletion of the type I IFN receptors in CTPS1-deficient THP-1 cells restored VSV replication (Figures S1L). A minor difference between wildtype and CTPS1-deficient cells with IFNAR-depletion was observed, potentially due to residual IFNAR expression. Alternatively, other cellular and viral targets of CTPS1 may contribute to the difference in VSV replication. These results collectively demonstrate that CTPS1 negatively regulates IFN induction in response to viral infection.CTP-independent antiviral activity of CTPS1CTP is a precursor required for the synthesis of viral nucleic acids. To determine whether the inhibition of viral yield in CTPS1−/− cells is due to impaired production of CTP, we first analyzed the relative abundance of CTP in CTPS1−/− 293T cells (Figure S2A). As expected, CTPS1 deficiency decreased the intracellular levels of CTP and its downstream metabolites, including CDP, CMP, cytidine and cytosine, while increased intracellular levels of UTP, the immediate precursor of CTP (Figure S2A). Similarly, CTPS1 deficiency reduced CTP and CDP by ~ 85 % in THP-1 cells but had no effect on UTP (Figure 2A), indicating that CTPS1 is essential for CTP synthesis. Next, we assessed the enzymatic activity of CTPS1 and CTPS2 using control (shCTL), CTPS1-depleted (shCTPS1) and CTPS2-depleted (shCTPS2) THP-1 cells for an in vitro CTP synthetase assay (Figures 2B and S2B). The Vmax of lysate of control THP-1 cells was >42-fold higher than that of CTPS1-depleted THP-1 cells, while no significant difference of the Vmax was observed between lysates of control and CTPS2-depleted THP-1 cells under the same conditions (Figure 2C). This result indicates that CTPS1, but not CTPS2, is chiefly responsible for CTP synthesis. Consistent with the role of CTPS1 in lymphocyte proliferation,20 CTPS1 deficiency inhibited the proliferation of both 293T and THP-1 cells. CTP supplementation partially rescued the proliferation of THP-1 cells, but fully restored that of 293T cells (Figures 2D and S2C). CTP supplementation had no effect on innate immune gene expression induced by SeV infection (Figures 2E and S2D). Finally, we analyzed the effect of CTP on the replication of VSV in CTPS1−/− THP-1 cells. Although CTP supplementation at 10 μM or 20 μM fully restored cellular CTP to the level of wild-type cells (Figure S2E), they only slightly increased VSV replication in CTPS1−/− cells and had no effect on that in wild-type cells (Figure 2F). These results collectively indicate that CTPS1 restricts IFN induction independent of its activity in CTP synthesis.CTPS1 deficiency potentiates antiviral innate immunity in miceTo confirm the effect of CTPS1 on IFN induction, mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) were isolated from wildtype C57BL6 and Ctps1fl/fl mice. Cre was expressed in MEFs via retrovirus infection to induce Ctps1 deletion. Upon VSV infection, Ifnb1, Isg15, Isg56, Cxcl10 and Mx1 transcripts were higher in CTPS1-knockout MEFs than those in wild-type MEFs (Figures 3A and 3B), which were consistent with the elevated production of IFN-β and CXCL10 in the medium (Figures 3C and 3D). To determine the effect of CTPS1 on antiviral immune response in vivo, Ctps1fl/fl and Ctps1fl/fl:Cre mice were treated with tamoxifen to induce Ctps1 deletion, when CTP supplementation was delivered via intraperitoneal injection (Figure S3A). CTPS1 deletion in the respective organs, including the brain, lung, spleen, liver and heart, was confirmed by immunoblotting analysis (Figure S3B). Real-time PCR results showed that depletion of Ctps1 in the lung, spleen, and liver was not affected by CTP supplementation (Figures 3E, 3F, and S3C). Whole body deletion of CTPS1 had no significant effect on the relative numbers of T cells (CD4+ and CD8+), B cells, and monocytes/macrophages, but did reduce NK cells in the blood (Figures S3D-S3H). Upon stimulation, CTPS1 deletion impaired the proliferation of peritoneal macrophages and splenic T cells, and CTP supplementation appeared to fully restore the proliferation of T cells and macrophages ex vivo (Figure S3I). When T cell activation was examined by IFN-γ and granzyme B production, CTPS1 deletion had no effect on these parameters of activated T cells (Figure S3J). CTP supplementation increased the mRNA transcripts of immune genes in the spleen, lung, and liver of Ctps1fl/fl:Cre mice, but not that of Ctps1fl/fl mice. Consistently, IFN-β and CXCL10 in sera were correspondingly increased in CTPS1-deficient mice (Figures 3I and 3J). Consequently, CTPS1 deficiency inhibited VSV replication by 83% and 93% in the spleen and lung, respectively (Figures 3K and 3L), and CTP supplementation further diminished VSV replication in the spleen, lung, and liver (Figures 3K, 3L, and S3R). H&E staining showed that loss of CTPS1 reduced VSV-induced immune infiltration in the lung and that CTP supplementation further diminished immune pathology in CTPS1-knockout mice (Figures 3M and S3S). CTP supplementation in wildtype mice had no apparent effect. Considering the glutamine-hydrolyzing activity of CTPS1, we hypothesized that CTPS1 deamidates IRF3 to inhibit IFN induction. To test this hypothesis, we first determined whether the glutaminase activity of CTPS1 is required for the inhibition of IFN induction. We generated a glutaminase-deficient mutant (C399A, H526A, and E528A) of CTPS1 (CTPS1-GD) for the IFN-β reporter assay.17 Wild-type CTPS1, but not the CTPS1-GD mutant, inhibited IFN-β induction in response to SeV infection in a dose-dependent manner (Figure S4B). CTPS1 was readily detected in protein complexes precipitated with antibody against IRF3, indicating the physical interaction between IRF3 and CTPS1 (Figure 4B). The closely related CTPS2 failed to interact with IRF3 in transiently transfected 293T cells (Figure S4C). To determine whether CTPS1 is a plausible deamidase, we depleted CTPS1 with shRNA and examined IRF3 charge by two-dimensional gel electrophoresis. Indeed, knockdown of CTPS1 shifted IRF3 toward the negative end of the gel strip, consistent with the reduced deamidation of IRF3 (Figure 4C). Ectopic expression of the CTPS1-GD mutant did so as well (Figure 4D). We then sought to identify the site of deamidation using tandem mass spectrometry for purified IRF3, without or with ectopic expression of CTPS1-GD. This analysis consistently identified N85, N389, and N397 as putative deamidation sites (Figures 4E, S4D, and S4E). We then generated IRF3 mutants containing individual deamidated residues, i.e., N>D mutations and analyzed their activity in IFN induction. The N85D mutation completely deprived IRF3 of the ability to activate the IFNB1 promoter and ISRE, while N389D and N397D elevated the IRF3-mediated IFN induction (Figures 4F, S4F, and S4G). When IRF3 was analyzed by two-dimensional gel electrophoresis, CTPS1, but not the CTPS1-GD mutant, shifted IRF3 toward the positive end of the gel strip, consistent with the increased negative charge resulting from deamidation (Figure 4H). To assess the specificity of CTPS1-mediated deamidation, we used the deamidated IRF3-N85D mutant and CTPS1 depletion for IRF3 charge analysis. Consistent with this result, IRF3-N85A demonstrated higher IFN induction in a reporter assay compared to wild-type IRF3 (Figure S4G). Immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting analyses showed that wildtype CTPS1, but not CTPS1-S575A, was phosphorylated in VSV-infected THP-1 cells, consistent with the conclusion that serine 575 is the primary phosphorylation site of CTPS1 (Figure 7L). When IRF3 charge status was analyzed, we found that VSV and HSV-1 infection shifted IRF3 toward the negative end of the gel strip in CTPS1−/− THP-1 cells reconstituted with wildtype CTPS1 but failed to do so in CTPS1−/− THP-1 cells reconstituted with CTPS1-S575A (Figure 7M). Furthermore, VSV infection reduced the Vmax of wildtype CTPS1 in THP-1 cells by >70%, while had minimal effect on that of CTPS1-S575A (Figure 7N). When immune gene expression was examined, CTPS1-S575A more potently inhibited the expression of IFNB1 and ISG56 than wildtype CTPS1 in reconstituted THP-1 cells (Figures 7O and S7O), which correlated with the levels of IFN-β secreted in the medium (Figure 7P). Conversely, wildtype CTPS1 and CTPS1-S575A expression increased VSV replication by >20- and 100-fold, respectively (Figure S7P). Together, these results support the conclusion that GSK3β inhibits CTPS1 to boost IFN induction during innate immune activation.

Thus, this work establishes a molecular link between the de novo pyrimidine synthesis and innate immune response, forging a molecular link between two otherwise disconnected biological processes.

De novo pyrimidine synthesis is crucial for cell proliferation and CTPS1 catalyzes the rate-limiting synthesis of CTP using glutamine and UTP as substrates.17,29 Interestingly, the closely related CTPS2 neither interacts with nor deamidates IRF3, suggesting the exquisite specificity of the CTPS1-mediated deamidation of IRF3. The activity of CTPSs in CTP synthesis is tightly regulated by diverse physiological cues, including nucleotide concentrations such as UTP and CTP and various protein kinases, to support distinct metabolic need of a cell under various environmental conditions.31 How these physiological conditions and cues converge to impinge on CTPS1-mediated deamidation and alter immune response requires for further investigation.

Cellular metabolism communicates with fundamental biological processes via distinct means. The roles of metabolites in shaping and instructing the development of immune cells have been intensively investigated.32,33 In addition to providing energy and intermediates for the differentiation and proliferation of immune cells, metabolism of glucose and glutamine yields a plethora of molecules that impact the immune response.34-36 Metabolites of the tricarboxylic acid cycle demonstrate diverse activities to alter immune gene expression.37,38 Acetyl-CoA, a major glycolytic metabolite, enters TCA cycle via fusing with oxoacetate to produce citrate.39 Alternatively, acetyl-CoA can be conjugated to proteins such as histones to open nucleosomes and an array of transcription factors such as IRF3 and RelA to activate gene expression upon immune stimulation.40,41 Via glutaminolysis, glutamine is metabolized into α-ketoglutarate that serves as a cofactor for the dioxygenases, such as histone demethylases Jumonji 2 (JMJD2) and Jmj C domain-containing histone demethylase-1 (JHDM1), and DNA demethylases ten-eleven translocation 2 (TET2) proteins.42-44 Cellular dioxygenases demethylate histones and CpG-rich genomic sequences to shape the epigenome landscape.42 Together with transcription factors and coactivators, these epigenome modifiers choreograph host gene expression to mount a coordinated immune response upon microbial infection.45,46 The observations that metabolic enzymes can influence key transcription factors such as IRF3 and RelA to impinge on immune gene expression demonstrate another dimension of regulation by which cellular metabolism communicates with immune response.

Transcription factors and cofactors function in an ensemble to regulate gene expression. The IFN-β enhanceosome represents one of the best models that integrates several signaling pathways to initiate immune response via regulated gene expression. Within 55bp of DNA, binding sites for the heterodimer ATF-2/c-Jun, IRF3, IRF7, and NF-κB (p50:RelA) cluster to allow the assembly of a super-complex, thus funneling signaling from the cytokine stress response via the MAPK cascade and inflammatory response downstream of innate immune activation to control the expression of IFNs and ISGs.13,47-49 CAD, the rate-limiting enzyme of de novo pyrimidine synthesis, can deamidate RelA, which shunts RelA from mediating an inflammatory response to aerobic glycolysis.27 Along with the CTPS1-mediated IRF3 deamidation, these findings demonstrate that two rate-limiting enzymes of the de novo pyrimidine synthesis pathway dampen innate immune response via deamidating IRF3 and RelA, thus preventing their binding to cognate responsive elements within the IFN-β promoter. Furthermore, highly proliferative cancer cells and microbial pathogens may activate these nucleotide synthetic enzymes to not only support proliferation, but also evade innate immune defense.27,50,51 Further investigation will likely uncover interesting mechanisms used by viruses to manipulate metabolic enzymes and evade immune response.

Upon microbial infection, the innate immune response– chiefly mediated by IFNs and other cytokines– is activated to defeat invading microbes. To enable IFN induction, CTPS1 is inhibited to facilitate IRF3-mediated gene expression. Specifically, GSK3β phosphorylates CTPS1 at serine 575 that suppresses its activities in IRF3 deamidation and CTP synthesis. Interestingly, depletion of CTPS1 in THP-1 monocyte abolished CTP synthesis, indicating the essential role of CTPS1 in CTP synthesis and suggesting non-redundant functions of the closely related CTPS2. Consequently, CTPS1 inhibition elevates IFN induction and host immune defense, and depletes the pyrimidine nucleotide pool, culminating in restricting viral replication. These findings highlight the potential to develop agents that negate CTPS1 for antiviral therapy, promising an antiviral strategy against a broad spectrum of viruses.

Limitations of the studyOur findings are derived from cultured cells and a laboratory mouse strain. The roles of CTPS1-mediated IRF3 deamidation remain to be determined in humans and nonhuman primates. The phenotypes of CTPS1-deficient mice likely embody the biological consequence of other substrates deamidated by CTPS1, whereas those of the IRF3 knock-in mice carrying IRF3 deamidated (N85D) or deamidation-resistant (N85A) mutations may reflect the action of other deamidases. The tissue-specific function of CTPS1 in protein deamidation and CTP synthesis requires further exploration as well.
