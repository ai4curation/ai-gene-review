BACKGROUND: This study aimed to investigate the relationship between
disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC).
METHODS: Using the TCGA-LUSC dataset, WGCNA identified LUSC-related genes, and
hub genes were obtained after intersecting these with DRGs. Consensus clustering
analysis divided LUSC patients into different subtypes and differentially
expressed genes were then identified. LASSO-Cox regression was applied to
establish a prognostic model, followed by analyses of immune cell infiltration
and drug sensitivity. Finally, in vitro experiments confirmed the results of
bioinformatics analysis. RESULTS: After intersecting 9,157 LUSC-related genes
with 24 DRGs, 12 hub genes were identified. Two subtypes (Cluster 1 and Cluster
2) for LUSC were recognized, and 219 differentially expressed genes were
screened. A prognostic model was constructed based on 7 genes (CD300LG,
PCDHGA12, HPD, KANK4, PPBP, ZCCHC5, and CSMD3), with good predictive ability.
Neutrophils, plasma cells, T cells CD4 memory resting, and T cells follicular
helper displayed significant differences between different risk groups and
subtypes. ZCCHC5 and PCDHGA12 were negatively correlated with all 20 screened
drugs. In vitro experiments confirmed the occurrence of disulfidptosis and the
levels of prognostic genes in LUSC. CONCLUSION: The study identified two LUSC
subtypes and established a 7-gene prognostic model based on DRGs, which can be
used as a new prognostic tool for LUSC.

Introduction

Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), a subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), accounts for a large proportion of lung cancer patients [1]. Smoking is the main contributor to LUSC, and long-term exposure to tobacco smoke contributes to cancer-causing mutations in the lung epithelium [2]. Classical therapies for LUSC include surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy [3]. However, surgery is not suitable for patients with advanced LUSC, and there are concerns about toxicity and drug resistance in chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Targeted therapy and immunotherapy are emerging therapies for LUSC, but they are accompanied by a low response rate and adverse reactions [4–8]. Considering that LUSC patients may have no symptoms in the early stages, it is urgent to screen for effective biomarkers for LUSC.

Disulfidptosis is a programmed cell death form closely related to disulfide bond accumulation [9]. Disulfide bonds are produced under oxidative stress and maintain the physical and chemical stability of proteins [10]. High levels of SLC7A11 cause excessive accumulation of disulfide bonds in cells under glucose starvation conditions, resulting in disulfide stress and inducing disulfidptosis [11]. Disulfidptosis is closely associated with cancer biology, as cancer cells release excess reactive oxygen species in response to various abnormal signals, and this high oxidative stress environment induces disulfidptosis [12]. Specifically, cancer cells take up extracellular cystine through SLC7A11, which is converted to cysteine under the role of NADPH [13]. The deficiency or limited utilization of NADPH results in the accumulation of cystine, eventually contributing to disulfidptosis. The effects of disulfidptosis on lung cancer have been widely explored. A risk model based on four radiotherapy disulfidptosis genes (PRELP, FGFBP1, CIITA, and COL5A1) can effectively estimate the outcome of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) cases, with low PRELP expression linked to poor prognosis [14]. Moreover, disulfidptosis-related gene (DRG) ACTN4 was identified as a risk factor for LUAD; ACTN4 levels in LUAD tissues were markedly higher, and its knockdown inhibited LUAD cell proliferation [15].

Although certain studies have explored the role of DRGs in other types of lung cancer, their role in the pathology and prognosis of LUSC remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to identify DRGs associated with LUSC, explore their relationship with patient prognosis, providing new insights for the treatment and management of LUSC patients. In this study, we screened 12 hub genes associated with disulfidptosis and LUSC, divided LUSC patients into two subtypes, and constructed a 7-gene prognostic model. The potential mechanisms were further explored through the analysis of immune cells and drug sensitivity. Finally, in vitro experiments confirmed the bioinformatics results.

RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis showed that CD300LG was successfully overexpressed in SK-MES-1 + oe-CD300LG group compared to SK-MES-1 + oe-NC group (Fig. 8D and E). In cancer cells with elevated SLC7A11 expression under glucose deprivation, excessive cystine intake and reduction deplete NADPH, leading to disulfide bond formation and ultimately inducing disulfidptosis [33]. We found that NADP+/NADPH and SLC7A11 levels were markedly elevated in LUSC cells, suggesting that disulfidptosis occurs in LUSC. Zhu et al. constructed a prognostic model based on 9 key disulfidptosis-related lncRNAs, which can effectively predict prognosis and assist clinical decision-making [34]. We identified 12 DRGs (CAPZB, FLNA, GYS1, LRPPRC, MYH10, MYH9, NCKAP1, NDUFA11, OXSM, RPN1, SLC3A2, and TLN1) as hub genes closely related to the LUSC. The previous study focused on lncRNAs, while we explored protein-coding DRGs, suggesting that the involvement of disulfidptosis in LUSC involves complex mechanisms and multiple types of genetic regulation. Although the role of these genes in LUSC remains unclear, many of them have been implicated in the development of lung cancer. Studies have shown that CAPZB at 1P6.13 is a significant expression quantitative trait locus, which affects the mRNA expression of nearby genes in lung tissue [35]. It may be a potential prognostic gene in never-smoking lung cancer patients. TRIM44 induces cisplatin resistance in LUAD patients by deubiquitinating FLNA [36]. GYS1 activity is a key component of human A549 lung cell metabolism after multiple radiotherapy treatments [37]. LRPPRC can increase lung cancer resistance to CDK4/6 inhibition [38]. LAMC2, located in the endoplasmic reticulum of lung cancer cells, binds to MYH9 and MYH10 to reduce endoplasmic reticulum stress, which is critical for the adaptation and proliferation of cancer cells under stressful conditions [39]. The CCL27-CCR10 axis promotes LUSC progression by activating PI3K/AKT pathway [43]. In addition, apolipoproteins are involved in lipid metabolism, promoting the transport and clearance of lipids, and the apoE and apoA-I pathways play a protective role in lung disease [44]. Bioinformatics experiments showed that isobavachalcone’s intervention in NSCLC was mainly enriched in steroid binding and oxidoreductase activity [45]. Furthermore, the levels of antioxidant marker NAD+/NADH were lower in A549 cells but increased after treatment with salvianolic acid B [46]. The tyrosine metabolic pathway is involved in the inhibition of NSCLC by drugs [47]. Similarly, immune infiltration analysis showed that neutrophils, plasma cells, T cells CD4 memory resting, and T cells follicular helper had notable differences across subtypes and risk groups. Furthermore, T cells CD4 memory resting were significantly correlated with most prognostic genes, suggesting that they may be strongly associated with LUSC progression. We found notable differences in CTLA4, PDCD1, and SIGLEC15 levels between two LUSC subtypes, with lower expression of these genes in Cluster 2. Similarly, CTLA4 is considered to be the most common and promising target in ICIs, and dual-blocking therapy targeting CTLA4, PDCD1, and its ligand L1 can effectively treat patients with advanced NSCLC [61]. AZD8055 and DT2216 treatment synergistically inhibit the development of small-cell lung cancer without causing targeted toxicity [64]. Similarly, CTLA4 is considered to be the most common and promising target in ICIs, and dual-blocking therapy targeting CTLA4, PDCD1, and its ligand L1 can effectively treat patients with advanced NSCLC [61]. In obese NSCLC patients, TFEB binding to the SIGLEC15 promoter increases SIGLEC15 expression and increases glycolytic flux to promote immune escape [62]. Therefore, CTLA4, PDCD1, and SIGLEC15 may be promising targets for ICIs to treat LUSC.

Drug sensitivity analysis identified top 20 drugs with low sensitivity and notable differences in high-risk group. However, some drugs have been found to exhibit good anti-lung cancer effects when used in combination with other treatments. For instance, a previous study found that axitinib with double chemotherapy can treat patients with advanced squamous NSCLC with a low rate of fatal haemoptysis [63]. AZD8055 and DT2216 treatment synergistically inhibit the development of small-cell lung cancer without causing targeted toxicity [64]. KU-55,933 blocks glucose metabolism and elevates the response of drug-resistant lung cancer cells to oncogene-driven inhibitors [65]. Moreover, ZCCHC5 and PCDHGA12 were markedly correlated with all 20 drugs, suggesting that the two genes may serve as promising targets for LUSC treatment.

However, the research still has some limitations. The dataset size is small, which may affect our conclusions. Therefore, subsequent validation with larger datasets is necessary to strengthen the robustness of our results. Additionally, in vivo and clinical experiments are required to validate the obtained findings, as results based merely on in vitro studies are limited.

ConclusionHub genes associated with disulfidptosis and LUSC were screened, and Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 subtypes for LUSC were identified. A 7-gene prognostic model was constructed. The study compared the characteristics of different risk groups and subtypes, focusing on immune cell infiltration and drug sensitivity. The study provides novel approaches for LUSC prognosis and treatment. Future studies could focus on validating the identified hub genes and prognostic model in a more diverse patient population. Moreover, exploring the therapeutic potential of targeting immune cell infiltration and evaluating the efficacy of identified drugs in clinical trials could help improve the therapeutic outcomes of LUSC.
