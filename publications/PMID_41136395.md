# A nuclear TRiC/CCT chaperonin assembles meiotic HORMAD proteins into chromosome axes competent for crossing over.

The meiotic chromosome axis organizes chromatin and sets the stage for homolog
pairing and recombination. Meiotic HORMA domain proteins (mHORMADs) are
conserved axis components that conformationally transform during target binding.
In C. elegans, four functionally distinct mHORMADs directly interact, but how
binding between them is restricted to axis assembly is unknown. Using a mutation
in the mHORMADs that delays axis assembly, we isolated a suppressor mutation in
a TRiC (Tailless complex peptide 1 Ring Complex) chaperonin subunit that
restored mHORMAD localization. CCT-4 associates with meiotic chromatin and forms
in vivo complexes with mHORMADs, while germline disruption of TRiC results in
axis defects, indicating a nuclear function for TRiC alongside meiotic
chromosomes. We propose that chromosome-associated TRiC locally folds mHORMADs
into the binding-competent conformation required for axis morphogenesis. More
broadly, our results support the model that spatially-restricted folding by
TRiC/CCT is a mechanism of controlling the assembly of multimeric complexes that
function in tightly co-ordinated events.

Introduction

A central feature of meiosis is the formation of crossovers that direct the accurate segregation of chromosomes at the first division. Crossover (CO) formation depends on earlier events that pair homologous chromosomes and initiate recombination through the formation of meiotic double-strand breaks (DSBs) that are repaired by homologous recombination (HR)1. Following homology assessment, chromosomes juxtapose along their lengths, and their paired configuration is stabilized by the formation of the synaptonemal complex (SC). The SC is an ordered assembly of central region (CR) proteins that polymerize between the axes of two chromosomes where they promote interhomolog recombination and CO formation. While the resulting tripartite structure is widely conserved at the ultrastructural level2, the individual CR components in different organisms share little sequence similarity3.

Previous studies in a variety of organisms have shown that synapsis per se does not distinguish homology since SC forms between nonhomologous DNA sequences3. CR components have an innate proclivity for self-assembly and can readily polymerize in situations where the meiotic axes cannot support synapsis, instead forming ordered nuclear polycomplexes that ultrastructurally resemble the SC without the involvement of chromosomes4. SC assembly is highly processive and rapid once initiated5,6; consequently, nucleation of SC components at chromosome axes must be tightly coordinated with homolog pairing to reduce the risk of ectopic synapsis between nonhomologous sequences. In most experimental meiotic model systems, this problem is solved by SC nucleation at sites of homologous recombination between paired homologs; alternatively, specific chromosomal sites are used for both pairing and initiation of SC assembly, independent of DSB formation and the HR pathway7. The DSB-independent pathway is most clearly illustrated by C. elegans, where each chromosome contains a pairing center (PC) that recruits one member of a family of DNA-binding proteins required for pairing (zim-1, 2, 3 and him-8)8,9, and is also the preferred site of synapsis initiation5. PC regions are tethered to the nuclear envelope where they connect each chromosome to cytoplasmic forces that drive pairing, promote SC assembly, and eliminate interactions between nonhomologs that could lead to synapsis between them7,10. The assembly of the PC, pairing, synapsis, and recombination are all dependent on the earlier formation of the meiotic chromosome axis, indicating that axis proteins are required for configuring the chromosome for meiotic processes11–16.

The composition of the meiotic axes of yeasts, animals, and plants is dominated by cohesin complexes and member(s) of a family of conserved meiosis-specific proteins containing a HORMA domain (mHORMAD proteins)16–18. The HORMAD is a structural feature first identified in Hop1p, Rev7p, and Mad2p during analysis of budding yeast DNA repair proteins. Early structural and biochemical studies of the mitotic checkpoint protein Mad2 revealed that the HORMA domain could reversibly interconvert between inactive open and active closed conformations, a dramatic transition in which the HORMAD core encircles a “safety belt” in the C-terminal region around a peptide sequence on a binding partner19–21. Unlike Mad2, however, no stable open conformation has been observed for any mHORMAD protein22,23, and they instead adopt a partially unfolded form in which the safety belt is “unbuckled” from the intact HORMA domain core22,23. The mHORMAD proteins have essential functions at chromosome axes in DSB formation, homolog pairing, SC formation, the bias towards the homolog as a DNA repair template, and crossing over24–31. They are represented by Hop1 in S. cerevisiae24, HORMAD1 and HORMAD2 in mice26–29, and ASY1 in plants30. C. elegans has four mHORMAD proteins (HIM-3, HTP-1, HTP-2, HTP-3) that interact to assemble into chromosome axes. HTP-3 binds first, and then recruits HIM-3, HTP-1, and HTP-214,31–35, which use their HORMA domains to bind to multiple short sequences located in the extended HTP-3 C-terminal tail and switch to closed forms31. Although the recruitment of HTP-3 to meiotic chromosomes is the key event in assembling the meiotic chromosome axis, the mechanism is unknown. HTP-3 (and its paralogs) lack the chromatin binding region that is broadly conserved among the mHORMADs of other phyla36, and HTP-3 may instead be recruited to chromatin by binding a sequence within the meiotic cohesin complex34. Despite their direct physical interactions, the mHORMADs have distinct functions in meiotic processes. HTP-3 is required for meiotic DSB formation and sister chromatid cohesion (SCC)14,35, while HIM-3 is required for homolog pairing, SC formation, and the bias to using the homolog as a DSB repair template11,37,38. HTP-1 and HTP-2 (referred to as HTP-1/2) are highly homologous (> 80% a.a. identity), but show little functional redundancy; while htp-2 mutants have no obvious phenotype, htp-1 mutants show severe defects in synapsis and CO formation that are exacerbated by loss of htp-212,13. The abundant nonhomologous synapsis observed in htp-1 mutants is accompanied by severe pairing defects, indicating that HTP-1 plays a regulatory role that restricts SC assembly to paired PCs12,13. Recent work has identified a conserved extended loop in the mHORMAD that could expand the range of possible conformations and create structural signatures for protein interactions that could explain their functional specificity39.

Free mHORMAD proteins that are not bound to their binding partner are thought to exist primarily in an inactive self-closed state by interacting with their own C-terminal domain; consequently, structural remodeling is required to convert self-closed mHORMADs into a binding-competent state, also referred to as an unbuckled conformation22,23. In yeasts, mammals, and plants, mHORMAD localization dynamics depend on Pch2/TRIP13, a conserved checkpoint AAA-ATPase that can unbuckle closed conformations of the mHORMADs to produce binding-competent pools for loading onto chromosomes, and to remove or redistribute axis-bound mHORMADs in response to progression of meiotic cell-cycle events40. Consistent with functions in regulating mHORMAD chromosome association, Pch2 orthologs in budding yeast41, plants42,43, and mice44 localize to chromosomes upon meiotic entry and then colocalize with the SC into pachytene stages. C. elegans PCH-2 similarly appears first as foci on meiotic chromosomes, followed by localization to the SC; however, the localization of the mHORMADs is PCH-2-independent45, indicating that their remodeling into a binding-competent form during axis assembly is mediated by an unknown pathway.

The folding of cellular proteins is mediated by molecular chaperones that recognize and bind newly synthesized polypeptides to prevent premature folding and aggregation. The eukaryotic Tailless complex peptide 1 Ring Complex (TRiC) or chaperonin-containing T-complex protein 1 (CCT) complex is a large type II chaperonin that uses an ATP hydrolysis-driven cycle to fold proteins in a central cavity formed by two stacked rings, each containing 8 highly related CCT subunits46. TRiC substrates are a structurally diverse population with complex topologies that cannot be folded by the simpler chaperone system, and as a general rule, contain extended hydrophobic stretches (particularly β-sheets), are aggregation-prone, and participate in multiprotein complexes47,48. In addition to its obligate cytosolic substrates, actin and tubulin, TRiC is also required for the folding of nuclear proteins that function in cell-cycle regulation, transcription, chromatin modification, and genome stability47. Although TRiC has historically been considered to function exclusively as a cytoplasmic factor, nuclear TRiC subunits have been detected in a broad range of systems using proteomic or cytological approaches, suggesting the existence of TRiC-mediated functions within the nucleus49–53. Moreover, CCT subunits localize to germline nuclei and with the chromatin of rat spermatocytes53, consistent with functions in the germline compartment that extend to chromosome biology. The existence of protein folding machinery working in close proximity to the genome is not without precedent. The major molecular chaperones (Hsp90, Hsp70, and Hsp60) are known to participate in the stabilization of nuclear proteins, and in the assembly and disassembly of complexes functioning in replication, transcription, and other DNA-related pathways54,55. A nuclear function for TRiC in modulating RNAPII activity has also been reported in budding yeast, indicating that chaperonins may similarly be required to locally regulate assembly and disassembly of nuclear multisubunit complexes56.

In this study, we report an essential nuclear function for TRiC in meiotic axis morphogenesis. cct-4, the δ subunit of the nematode TRiC/CCT complex, interacts with mHORMAD proteins in vivo and is required for their axis localization. These results demonstrate an essential role for the chaperonin in regulating meiotic processes leading to CO formation. Based on these data and other evidence, we propose that TRiC associated with meiotic chromosomes converts self-closed mHORMAD proteins into an open or unbuckled conformation that can bind to interacting proteins during axis assembly. Our results suggest that meiotic axis assembly is regulated at the level of local protein folding and release of the mHORMADs in a binding-competent form, a mechanism that is consistent with a model of just-in-time protein folding by TRiC57 as a means of controlling tightly co-ordinated events in meiotic prophase.

ResultsTo identify the pathways regulating the assembly of chromosome axes, we made use of him-3(vv6), a unique missense allele that carries a S35F substitution at a widely conserved serine within the HORMA domain (Fig. 1a, b). him-3(S35F) is expressed at normal levels and the protein localizes to axes, but mutants exhibited severe pairing defects that are accompanied by extensive synapsis between nonhomologous chromosomes11 (this study). Upon closer examination, we discovered that him-3(S35F) mutants exhibited delayed loading of HIM-3 (described in detail in the next section) and used the allele in a suppressor screen to identify genetic pathways that function in axis assembly. The high embryonic lethality that accompanies the loss of crossing over in him-3(S35F) mutants was exploited to perform an EMS-based screen to isolate mutants that increased the brood size. We recovered 4 dominant extragenic suppressors (vv39, vv41, vv50, vv52) that increased the number of progeny produced by him-3(S35F) mutants, and focused on vv39, the strongest suppressor (3.2-fold increase; Supplementary Table 1). SNP mapping and sequencing revealed a single C to T mutation that mapped to the third exon of the cct-4 gene, and results in a serine substitution at P382 in the predicted protein sequence. To confirm that cct-4(P382S) corresponded to the suppressor mutation, a single copy germline expression construct of cct-4(vv39) was integrated into chromosome II using MosSCI58 and tested for its ability to dominantly suppress him-3(S35F) phenotypes (Supplementary Table 1). While him-3(S35F) mutants have brood sizes that are 9.5% of those produced by wild type, vvIs19[pie-1p::cct-4(P382S)::unc-54utr]; him-3(S35F) mutants produced brood sizes that were 32.8% of wild type, a value not different from that observed for the original him-3(S35F); cct-4(P382S) suppressed line (31.3%). This possibility is supported by a previous analysis of the four HTP-3 binding sequences (“closure motifs”) that recruit HIM-3; while the binding of HIM-3 to a single closure motif could support well-defined axes with uninterrupted stretches of HTP-3 and HTP-1/2 localization (but not SC), loss of all HIM-3 closure motifs resulted in punctate and disordered HTP-3 and HTP-1/2 chromosome localization32. cct-4(P382S) defines an interface of CCT-4 required for HIM-3 axis localizationWe next sought to understand the mechanism of CCT-4P382S-mediated suppression of the defect in HIM-3S35F. The HORMA domain has a complex tertiary structure that would be predicted to require active assistance in folding; consequently, a plausible scenario is that HIM-3 is a client of the TRiC chaperonin and that the S35F substitution disrupts its interaction with a CCT-4 interface required for its folding into an unbuckled conformation that can bind HTP-3. The P382 residue of CCT-4 maps to the intermediate domain, a region that contributes to substrate recognition and acts as a hinge to open and close the complex during substrate binding (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1b)46, suggesting that P382 is either part of the substrate recognition interface for the mHORMADs and/or positions them for interaction with the interface(s). If CCT-4P382S restores HIM-3S35F function through a physical interaction, the suppression of him-3(S35F) loading defects by cct-4(P382S) could reflect suppression by a compensatory structural change in CCT-4; the P382S substitution in CCT-4 specifically modifies the interaction interface of the subunit with HIM-3 and physically accommodates the structural change caused by the S35F substitution in HIM-3 during its folding (Model, Fig. 2c). If true, the suppressor activity of cct-4(P382S) would be predicted to be 1) dependent on the presence of the him-3 protein, and 2) restricted to the S35F mutation. We tested the basis of the cct-4(P382S) suppression and found that cct-4(P382S) did not affect the embryonic lethality or high incidence of male progeny (him, an indicator of X-chromosome nondisjunction) of him-3 null mutants11, indicating that the suppression required the him-3 protein. We also tested e1256 and me80, two hypomorphs that also result in missense mutations in the HORMA domain (T24M and R54H, respectively); in both cases, HIM-3 localizes to axes and SC forms (R54H results in HIM-3 instability and short SC), but CO formation is severely reduced37,38. cct-4(P382S) did not suppress the embryonic lethality or the Him phenotype of him-3(T24M) and him-3(R54H) mutants, indicating that cct-4(P382S) suppression is specific to the him-3(S35F) allele (p > 0.001 for all genotypes; Supplementary Table 1).cct-4 is an essential gene, and the surprising viability and fertility of cct-4(P382S) mutants (87% of wild-type brood size; Supplementary Table 1) indicate that the P382S substitution does not detectably affect the folding of essential proteins by the chaperonin. However, we observed that cct-4(P382S) mutant germlines showed a spectrum of defects that could be categorized into two major categories: a common class that showed a variably extended TZ and polarized nuclei interspersed among pachytene stage nuclei (36/40 germlines), and an infrequent class (4/40 germlines) that showed similar phenotypes, but was accompanied by cell-cycle defects in the pre-meiotic region and the appearance of macro and micronuclei throughout the germline (Fig. 4a). To investigate the effect of cct-4(P382S) on axis assembly, we first measured the CoC of HIM-3 and HTP-3 in early TZ nuclei of germlines lacking mitotic defects; HIM-3 colocalization with HTP-3 was lower than wild-type levels in early TZ nuclei, but was not different by midpachytene (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 2). The reduced association of HIM-3 with HTP-3 in cct-4(P382S) mutants was also evidenced by a decrease in the number of ZIM-3-positive nuclei in early TZ nuclei (Fig. 4c, d), by the presence of nuclei within the TZ that showed visibly reduced HIM-3 localization to chromosomes, and localization of SYP-1 to nuclear aggregates or short stretches (Fig. 4e). To gauge the effect of cct-4(P382S) on SC formation, we measured the colocalization coefficient of SYP-1/HTP-3 in early TZ nuclei (Fig. 4f) and found that it was reduced in comparison to wild type, but reached wild-type levels by midpachytene, similar to the HIM-3/HTP-3 dynamics (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 3). We conclude that cct-4(P382S) mutants show an early delay in HIM-3 localization, PC assembly, and synapsis that is ultimately corrected without affecting CO formation (Fig. 1b). The fact that the reproducible meiotic (and occasionally mitotic) defects that appear in cct-4(P382S) mutant germlines do not significantly perturb the fertility of the mutants is likely due to activation of the pachytene checkpoint by nuclei, leading to apoptosis and their elimination from the oocyte population. Importantly, the collective data indicate that the presence of CCT-4P382S in combination with wild-type HIM-3 resulted in phenotypes associated with axis assembly defects, as predicted by our interaction model (Fig. 2c). Although these defects were less penetrant, they nevertheless qualitatively replicated those observed in the reverse combination (CCT-4+/+ and HIM-3S35F). cct-4(P382S) defines an interface of CCT-4 required for HIM-3 axis localization

We next sought to understand the mechanism of CCT-4P382S-mediated suppression of the defect in HIM-3S35F. The HORMA domain has a complex tertiary structure that would be predicted to require active assistance in folding; consequently, a plausible scenario is that HIM-3 is a client of the TRiC chaperonin and that the S35F substitution disrupts its interaction with a CCT-4 interface required for its folding into an unbuckled conformation that can bind HTP-3. The P382 residue of CCT-4 maps to the intermediate domain, a region that contributes to substrate recognition and acts as a hinge to open and close the complex during substrate binding (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1b)46, suggesting that P382 is either part of the substrate recognition interface for the mHORMADs and/or positions them for interaction with the interface(s). If CCT-4P382S restores HIM-3S35F function through a physical interaction, the suppression of him-3(S35F) loading defects by cct-4(P382S) could reflect suppression by a compensatory structural change in CCT-4; the P382S substitution in CCT-4 specifically modifies the interaction interface of the subunit with HIM-3 and physically accommodates the structural change caused by the S35F substitution in HIM-3 during its folding (Model, Fig. 2c). If true, the suppressor activity of cct-4(P382S) would be predicted to be 1) dependent on the presence of the him-3 protein, and 2) restricted to the S35F mutation. We tested the basis of the cct-4(P382S) suppression and found that cct-4(P382S) did not affect the embryonic lethality or high incidence of male progeny (him, an indicator of X-chromosome nondisjunction) of him-3 null mutants11, indicating that the suppression required the him-3 protein. We also tested e1256 and me80, two hypomorphs that also result in missense mutations in the HORMA domain (T24M and R54H, respectively); in both cases, HIM-3 localizes to axes and SC forms (R54H results in HIM-3 instability and short SC), but CO formation is severely reduced37,38. cct-4(P382S) did not suppress the embryonic lethality or the Him phenotype of him-3(T24M) and him-3(R54H) mutants, indicating that cct-4(P382S) suppression is specific to the him-3(S35F) allele (p > 0.001 for all genotypes; Supplementary Table 1).

cct-4 is an essential gene, and the surprising viability and fertility of cct-4(P382S) mutants (87% of wild-type brood size; Supplementary Table 1) indicate that the P382S substitution does not detectably affect the folding of essential proteins by the chaperonin. However, we observed that cct-4(P382S) mutant germlines showed a spectrum of defects that could be categorized into two major categories: a common class that showed a variably extended TZ and polarized nuclei interspersed among pachytene stage nuclei (36/40 germlines), and an infrequent class (4/40 germlines) that showed similar phenotypes, but was accompanied by cell-cycle defects in the pre-meiotic region and the appearance of macro and micronuclei throughout the germline (Fig. 4a). To investigate the effect of cct-4(P382S) on axis assembly, we first measured the CoC of HIM-3 and HTP-3 in early TZ nuclei of germlines lacking mitotic defects; HIM-3 colocalization with HTP-3 was lower than wild-type levels in early TZ nuclei, but was not different by midpachytene (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 2). The reduced association of HIM-3 with HTP-3 in cct-4(P382S) mutants was also evidenced by a decrease in the number of ZIM-3-positive nuclei in early TZ nuclei (Fig. 4c, d), by the presence of nuclei within the TZ that showed visibly reduced HIM-3 localization to chromosomes, and localization of SYP-1 to nuclear aggregates or short stretches (Fig. 4e). To gauge the effect of cct-4(P382S) on SC formation, we measured the colocalization coefficient of SYP-1/HTP-3 in early TZ nuclei (Fig. 4f) and found that it was reduced in comparison to wild type, but reached wild-type levels by midpachytene, similar to the HIM-3/HTP-3 dynamics (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 3). We conclude that cct-4(P382S) mutants show an early delay in HIM-3 localization, PC assembly, and synapsis that is ultimately corrected without affecting CO formation (Fig. 1b). The fact that the reproducible meiotic (and occasionally mitotic) defects that appear in cct-4(P382S) mutant germlines do not significantly perturb the fertility of the mutants is likely due to activation of the pachytene checkpoint by nuclei, leading to apoptosis and their elimination from the oocyte population. Importantly, the collective data indicate that the presence of CCT-4P382S in combination with wild-type HIM-3 resulted in phenotypes associated with axis assembly defects, as predicted by our interaction model (Fig. 2c). Although these defects were less penetrant, they nevertheless qualitatively replicated those observed in the reverse combination (CCT-4+/+ and HIM-3S35F). We next reasoned that if the interaction between mutant CCT-4P382S and wild-type HIM-3 slowed the events surrounding chromosome pairing, cct-4(P382S) would be predicted to suppress the defects of mutants in which these events were inappropriately accelerated. In htp-1(gk174) null mutants (referred to as htp-1(Δ), nuclei prematurely exit from leptotene-zygotene, however, PC-binding proteins localize, and SUN-1 aggregates form in the vicinity of chromosome ends12,13,65, indicating that HIM-3 is recruited to the axes with appropriate timing. In the absence of HTP-1, SC assembly is licensed before pairing is accomplished, leading to nonhomologous synapsis between chromosomes and defects in CO formation12,13 (Fig. 5a). The premature SC assembly observed in htp-1(Δ) mutants is accompanied by highly reduced numbers of the polarized nuclei typical of early prophase, and the TZ is shortened to 5% of the wild-type length (Fig. 4b). In the presence of cct-4(P382S), however, the TZ of htp-1(Δ) mutants increased to 73% of the wild-type zone, indicating an extension of the chromosome pairing stages of the cell cycle. We next assessed the level of pairing and found it to be in agreement with previously published results12,13, only ~ 5% of nuclei at any stage showed pairing of chromosome V in control htp-1(Δ) mutant germlines (Fig. 5c). In comparison to htp-1(Δ) mutants, however, pairing levels in TZ nuclei of htp-1(Δ); cct-4(P382S)-suppressed germlines were already 22% and by late pachytene reached a high of 29%. The increase in pairing levels in the suppressed germlines was accompanied by a visible increase in SYP-1 localization to HIM-3-marked axes (Fig. 5d). This suggests that the SC-free segments of axes observed in htp-1(Δ) mutants represent axes that appear when nonhomologous chromosomes synapse out of register. This interpretation was corroborated by increased SYP-1 colocalization with HTP-3-marked axes in both early TZ and midpachytene nuclei in htp-1(Δ); cct-4(P382S) mutants in comparison to htp-1(Δ) mutants (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 6). The increase in homologous synapsis in the presence of cct-4(P382S) was also evidenced by increased CO formation since htp-1(Δ); cct-4(P382S) mutants exhibited fewer nuclei with 12 DAPI bodies than htp-1(Δ) mutants alone (p < 0.0001; Fig. 5a). These results indicate that cct-4(P382S) can partially suppress the pairing and nonhomologous synapsis defects of htp-1(Δ) mutants. This phenomenon is most simply explained by the delay in HIM-3 loading in the presence CCT-4P382S, which has the practical effect of simultaneously extending the homology search window and delaying SC formation in htp-1(Δ) mutants, giving chromosomes more time to pair and appropriately nucleate the SC at the pairing centers. In the closed conformation of an mHORMAD protein, the αA helix interacts with the β-strands; partial unfolding of this helix by Pch2/TRiP13 leads to disengagement of the safety belt from the HORMA domain core and formation of the binding-competent form refs. 22, 40. Although the structure of the binding-competent form of HIM-3 is not known, the crystal structures of the self-closed (HIM-3 bound to its C-terminal closure motif; Fig. 1d) and HTP-3-bound closed conformations have been determined31, and are consistent with a Mad2-like mechanism for ligand binding; the conversion of an inactive form that is unbound to a partner to a closed form in which the partner is encircled by the safety belt22,23. To understand the defect imposed by the S35F substitution, we used Alphafold366 to model the effect on the HIM-3 structure (Fig. 6). S35 is located near the beginning of αA, and forms hydrogen bonds with residues H232 and H233, part of β8 and β8’. The S35F substitution is predicted to disrupt the HORMA domain core (Fig. 6c), possibly interfering with the formation of a HIM-3 conformation that is competent to bind to HTP-332. This prediction is consistent with the defect in the early loading of HIM-3S35F, and its later stability at the axis at midpachytene, once bound to HTP-3 (Fig. 1h). To corroborate this model, we made use of vv38, an intragenic suppressor isolated in the same unbiased screen that produced him-3(S35F). vv38 results in an E36R substitution next to S35F and suppresses the meiotic defects of him-3(S35F) to the same levels as cct-4(P382S) (Supplementary Fig. 5); these include the reappearance of ZIM-3 at PCs (indicative of timely HIM-3 loading), shortening of the extended TZ of him-3(S35F) by 39%, and increasing the progeny number from 15% of wild type in him-3(S35F) to 84% in him-3(S35F,E36R). In him-3(S35F,E36R) mutants, the lost interaction between S35 and H232 and H233 is predicted to be locally compensated for via a hydrogen bond between H233 and the arginine substitution at position 36 (Fig. 6d). In the case of cct-4(P382S), the affected P382 lies in the region of the subunit that hinges when TRiC changes from an open to a closed conformation (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1b); a serine substitution at this position is predicted to make the hinge even more flexible in the mutant and/or affect its direct interaction with specific substrates during their folding46. These observations raise the possibility that the altered CCT-4P382S in TRiC structurally accommodates the disrupted HIM-3S35F protein and is more effective than its wild-type counterpart at folding it into a binding-competent conformation; this, in turn, increases the pool of HIM-3S35F that can stably bind to HTP-3 by adopting the closed state. We reasoned that if HIM-3 is a client of TRiC that requires a specific CCT-4 interface for its folding, the other mHORMADs would be similarly dependent on this interface to fold into proteins competent for axis localization. To investigate this possibility, CRISPR genome editing was used to introduce the S35F substitution at the corresponding serine in HTP-3 and HTP-1 (S37F and S38F, respectively; Fig. 1a), and the resulting mutants were assayed for defects in axis localization and the ability of CCT-4P382S to suppress them.

In the case of htp-3(S37F) mutants, HTP-3S37F colocalized with meiotic chromosomes in early TZ nuclei, but appeared largely as abundant bright puncta that intermittently formed segments instead of the contiguous linear elements that were present in wild-type nuclei at the same stage (Fig. 7a, c and Supplementary Fig. 7), indicating that HTP-3S37F binding to chromosomes is compromised. cct-4(RNAi) results in axis morphogenesis, PC protein recruitment, and synapsis defectsIn contrast to the lethality associated with null mutations in cct genes, cct-4(P382S) is a hypomorphic allele that resulted in early disruptions to axis formation that were ultimately corrected as prophase progressed. Previous studies found that RNAi depletion of individual cct genes resulted in variable and pleiotropic effects, including embryonic lethality, sterility, and defects in gonad morphogenesis and oogenesis; phenotypes largely attributable to defects in TRiC function in microtubule dynamics, cell-cycle progression, and the regulation of ribonucleoprotein complexes during oogenesis52,67. While it is well-established that each CCT subunit is essential for the formation and function of TRiC, examples of complex-independent functions of some subunits have been reported68. The C. elegans genome encodes eight CCT subunits (CCT-1 through 8) that are orthologs of the conserved eight subunits of TRiC69. To determine if the axis morphogenesis function of CCT-4 occurs in the context of the chaperonin, we reasoned that germline disruption of other subunits of the complex should mimic the effect of loss of function of cct-4. To investigate this possibility, we used RNAi as a tool to deplete individual subunits (cct-1/3/4/5) in the germlines, and in all cases observed that treated hermaphrodites were sterile, similar to published reports67. We first individually depleted cct-1, 3, and 4 by injection of dsRNA into the gonad, which produced severe defects in mitotic cell division and germline organization (Supplementary Fig. 9). In the region of the cct-4(RNAi) germline spatially corresponding to the TZ of control germlines, nuclei lacked the polarization of chromatin characteristic of this stage, but HIM-3 could be detected on chromosomes, indicating that the nuclei had entered meiotic prophase. In these nuclei, HIM-3 localization at the axis was highly disrupted, and ZIM-3 was not localized to the nuclear periphery. In more proximal nuclei, the discontinuous appearance of HIM-3-marked chromosomes persisted, and SYP-1 remained diffuse in the nucleoplasm, phenotypes that were also observed for cct-1(RNAi) and cct-3(RNAi) (Supplementary Fig. 9). Because the severity of the germline organization defects in the injected animals precluded any quantitative analyses, we introduced RNAi by bacterial feeding to animals carrying a functionally-tagged endogenous GFP::him-3 and tested different durations of exposure to identify conditions in which some premeiotic defects could be observed (as a positive control for knockdown), but the meiotic prophase region contained appropriately sized nuclei, and the diakinesis nuclei contained 6 DAPI-stained bodies (Fig. 9a, b). In cct-4(RNAi); GFP::him-3 germlines, the premeiotic region contained some micro and macro nuclei, but the region corresponding to the TZ in wild type contained appropriately sized HIM-3-positive nuclei that abutted HIM-3-negative pre-meiotic nuclei. We also performed RNAi of cct-5 under these conditions and found that while cct-5(RNAi), GFP::him-3 did not show visibly disrupted nuclei in the premeiotic region, the germlines were smaller and the worms were sterile, indicating that the RNAi was at least partially penetrant. To determine the effect of cct-4 and cct-5 depletion on early HIM-3 association with chromosomes, we measured the colocalization coefficient of GFP::HIM-3/DAPI in the first 5 rows of HIM-3-positive nuclei that were adjacent to HIM-3-negative nuclei in the premeiotic region (Fig. 9a, c). In both cct-4 and cct-5-depleted germlines, the CoC of GFP::HIM-3/DAPI dropped to 40% of wild-type, indicating that depletion of either subunit reduced the association of GFP::HIM-3 with chromosomes in early prophase nuclei. Taken together, our results demonstrate that depletion of individual CCT subunits in the germline disrupts mitotic germ cell proliferation, meiotic chromosome structure, and prophase progression, consistent with an essential role for TRiC in these processes. DiscussionA nuclear function for TRiC/CCT in meiotic chromosome organizationGiven that HIM-3, HTP-1/2, and HTP-3 are present in meiotic prophase nuclei from the earliest stages, an outstanding question is how unlicensed binding between them is prevented while interactions at the chromosomes are simultaneously licensed. In this study, we identified an unexpected role for TRiC in meiotic chromosome axis morphogenesis. Using a him-3(S35F) mutant that is impaired for HIM-3 recruitment to chromosome axes, we identified cct-4(P382S) as a suppressor that could partially restore axis loading of the HIM-3S35F mutant protein, leading to improved homolog pairing, synapsis, and CO formation in him-3(S35F) mutants. The suppressor activity of cct-4(P382S) is specific to the S35F allele, a phenomenon typical of suppression that is based on structural accommodation of a mutant protein by a corresponding structural change in its interacting partner. him-3(S35F) affects a highly conserved serine in the HORMA domain, and introducing this change into the HORMA domains of HTP-3 and HTP-1 also resulted in a delay in their axis localization, demonstrating that the substitution disrupts a common structural feature of the mHORMAD proteins that is required for their localization. This scenario also explains the delay in HIM-3 localization to chromosome axes that is observed in cct-4(P382S) mutants; in this case, the wild-type HIM-3 and mutant CCT-4P382S combination would be predicted to have similarly structurally compromised interactions that would interfere with HIM-3 localization. The observation that CCT-4P382S was also able to suppress the axis localization defects of both HTP-3S37F and HTP-1S38F provides strong genetic evidence that the mHORMADs physically interact with CCT-4. Although CCT-4 (and all other subunits) are essential components of TRiC52,67, some complex-independent functions for CCT-4 have been demonstrated in other systems, notably CCT2 as an aggrephagy receptor68. Consistent with a function for TRiC, rather than CCT-4 in HIM-3 localization and axis assembly, germline depletion of individual CCT subunits resulted in meiotic chromosome morphology defects, and in the case of cct-4 and cct-5, reduced loading of HIM-3 to early prophase chromosomes. Importantly, our biochemical analyses corroborated our genetic and cytological results by demonstrating that mHORMADs are in complexes with CCT-4 and with CCT-5 in vivo, consistent with an interaction with the assembled chaperonin. Given that CCT-5 was also found in CCT-4 complexes immunoprecipitated from nuclear extracts, we conclude that the interaction between the mHORMADs and CCT-4 occurs in the context of an assembled TRiC complex in vivo. Furthermore, our data support the interpretation that the TRiC-mHORMAD interactions required for axis assembly occur in meiotic nuclei: (1) CCT-4 localized to meiotic germline nuclei throughout prophase, where it generally overlapped with chromatin, and (2) CCT-4 and CCT-5 were abundant in the chromatin and soluble nuclear fractions of germline extracts. Our results provide evidence that TRiC acts as a nuclear chaperonin to regulate mHORMAD assembly at meiotic chromosomes and prevent ectopic interactions that could lead to aggregation.CCT-4P382S identifies an interaction interface with the mHORMADs required for formation of a binding-competent formThe conserved serine affected by S35F is positioned near the beginning of the αA helix of the structural core of the him-3 protein (Figs. 1a, and 6). Computational modeling of the structure of mutant HIM-3S35F predicts that substitution with phenylalanine at this position affects the structural integrity of the HORMAD core31, and compromises its ability to adopt the form that is competent to bind HTP-3. We considered the alternate scenario that mutant HIM-3S35F binding to HTP-3 is unstable, thereby creating the illusion that its loading is delayed. However, two lines of evidence suggest that the defect in HIM-3S35F lies in forming the binding-competent form that can bind to HTP-3. First, the mutant HIM-3S35F protein is delayed in its localization into contiguous chromosomal stretches in early prophase, but uninterrupted HIM-3S35F-labeled axes eventually appear, indicating that the protein has a reduced ability to localize to the axis early on, but once localized is stably associated. This is particularly relevant in the context of synapsis since SC formation is highly sensitive to HIM-3 levels at the axis32 and unstable HIM-3S35F binding would have a direct effect on SC formation; in him-3(R54H) mutants, the HIM-3R54H protein is unstable and only highly reduced levels appear at pachytene, resulting in thick, short SC segments instead of the contiguous SC that colocalizes with axes in him-3(S35F) mutants38. Secondly, mutant HIM-3S35F at the synapsed axes can perform its wild-type functions in establishing the barrier to using the sister as a repair template during HR11, indicating that HIM-3S35F is functional once localized.The P382 residue affected by cct-4(vv39) resides in the intermediate domain, a region that hinges when the complex changes from an open to a closed conformation, and that can also interact with substrates during protein folding46,72. This suggests that P382 identifies a region in CCT-4 that contacts the mHORMADs during protein folding or positions them for interactions with substrate recognition interfaces in other regions of the central cavity of TRiC. The idea that specific contacts between the mHORMADs and CCT-4 are required for their folding has gained traction from recent studies that resolved the progressively folded states of β-tubulin within the closed TRiC chamber, leading to the model that TRiC provides a topological environment that steers folding of a protein through stepwise formation of partially folded intermediates72. Based on the interactions of tubulin with the inner wall of the TRiC chamber, this mechanism requires contacts with specific TRiC subunits as each intermediate transforms into the next, by extension implying co-evolution between TRiC and some of its substrates72. In this context, the observation that CCT-4P382S can disrupt HIM-3 axis localization while leaving development and the fertility largely unaffected can be reconciled; CCT-4 contains an interface tailored for contact with mHORMADs during their folding and this is disrupted in CCT-4P382S, leading to altered folding of the axis components while leaving folding of other essential substrates largely intact (Fig. 2c). Despite their high levels of fertility, cct-4(P382S) mutants show mitotic defects in the premeiotic germline; these are highly variable in frequency and severity within and between germlines. This dynamic suggests that they originate in the reduced or inefficient ability of TRiC containing CCT-4P382S to produce threshold levels of correctly folded actin, tubulin, and the cell-cycle regulators required for mitotic progression; aneuploid nuclei resulting from mitotic defects trigger the pachytene checkpoint and are eliminated from the oocyte pool, thereby maintaining the observed fertility of cct-4(P382S) mutants. In the converse situation, the altered interface in CCT-4P382S partially restores its interaction with the mutant mHORMAD variants and supports their folding into the unbuckled conformation that can bind to their partners during axis assembly. These data provide functional evidence for specific contacts within the TRiC chamber during protein folding.Chromosome associated TRiC locally remodels mHORMADs for axis assemblyThe TRiC/CCT complex exists in the cytoplasm, raising the question of why the meiotic HORMADs are not folded there into the unbuckled conformation and imported into the nucleus. HIM-3 and HTP-1/2 bind to HTP-3 in vitro32, indicating that they can form stable complexes without the participation of nuclear factors. Consequently, the cytosolic folding of the HORMADs into their binding-competent form could result in aggregation of the proteins and loss of their function. This problem extends into the nucleus as evidenced by mHORMAD dynamics in cohesin-defective mutants (rec-8; coh-3 coh-4 triple mutants); in these cases, HTP-3 fails to localize to the chromosomes and is instead found in one or two nuclear aggregates that also contain HIM-3, HTP-1/2, SC components, and cohesin subunits35,73. Moreover, the localization of HIM-3 into the aggregates is dependent on HTP-3, indicating that mHORMAD proteins can ectopically interact in vivo and form stable complexes without the involvement of chromosomes14,35,73.Meiotic HORMADs are thought to initially adopt an inactive “self-closed” form in which their HORMA domains engage their own C-terminus; this interaction is released as part of the conformational changes leading to binding of interacting partners22,23. Our results indicate that the htp-3(S37F) mutation disrupts HTP-3 localization, providing evidence that the HTP-3 HORMA domain is required for its association with chromosomes, likely using the same conformational switch used by other mHORMADs. Although the structure of the free inactive forms of the nematode mHORMADs has not been resolved, we favor the model that nucleoplasmic HTP-3 also exists in the self-closed form by interacting with its C terminus, a conformation that would sequester the CMs from interactions with the other mHORMADs. Consequently, binding of the HTP-3 HORMA domain to its interacting partner at the nascent axis would necessitate release of the C-terminal tail, and the CMs would become spatially available for binding by HIM-3 and HTP-1/2. This simple binary mechanism could ensure that the mHORMAD protein interactions only occur in the context of axis assembly and identifies the formation of the binding competent species of HTP-3 as the key event.In budding yeast and plants, the remodeling activity of Pch2/TRIP13 is needed to convert the self-closed mHORMADs into an unbuckled conformation to promote their association with chromosomes40. In C. elegans, however, PCH-2 is dispensable for mHORMAD localization45, and our results suggest that the TRiC complex performs the remodeling of the mHORMADs into the binding-competent form that can interact to assemble chromosome axes. Key among these is the observation that mHORMADs containing the serine substitution corresponding to S35F in HIM-3 showed defects in localizing to chromosomes, consistent with the structural prediction that this substitution in the N-terminal helix of the HORMA domain could interfere with the transition to a binding-competent intermediate. The fact that CCT-4P382S could partially rescue these loading defects provides evidence that TRiC is required for the formation of the binding-competent conformation. Our results are compatible with the scenario that nucleoplasmic pools of the mHORMADs exist in a self-closed form, but are locally folded by chromosome-associated TRiC into the unbuckled form that can bind its target during axis assembly. A situation with parallels has been described for TRiC-mediated control of the assembly of functional HDAC complexes; inactive HDAC1 is delivered to a nuclear TRiC complex to complete folding of a β sheet before its release in its active form and incorporation into HDAC complexes with correct timing74. This has led to the proposal that TRiC can cage proteins in an assembly-competent, but inactive state prior to their inclusion into complexes, in this case to prevent uncontrolled deacetylase activity by nascent HDAC1 outside of the regulatory confines of the HDAC complex. We speculate that TRiC-mediated folding similarly addresses the issue of uncontrolled binding activity outside of the confines of axis assembly, in this case by creating a spatially confined pool of binding-competent mHORMADs in the vicinity of axis assembly. An appealing aspect of such compartmentalized just-in-time protein folding is the ability to choreograph complex processes by regulating the folding of essential actors into their active form at the time and place that they are needed75. A spatially restricted activity during meiosis has also been observed for the 26S proteasome, which shows conserved localization to meiotic chromosomes and is essential for events leading to pairing, synapsis, and CO formation57,76; in this case, an example of local protein degradation as a mechanism of regulating meiotic prophase processes. In summary, our study provides evidence that folding of the C. elegans mHORMAD proteins into a binding-competent conformation by the TRiC chaperonin is essential for axis assembly and downstream meiotic processes. Our findings contribute to the growing evidence of nuclear functions for the chaperonin and suggest that localized protein folding may be an unappreciated and more widely conserved mechanism of regulating meiotic chromosome dynamics and other nuclear processes.

The observation that CCT-4P382S was also able to suppress the axis localization defects of both HTP-3S37F and HTP-1S38F provides strong genetic evidence that the mHORMADs physically interact with CCT-4. Although CCT-4 (and all other subunits) are essential components of TRiC52,67, some complex-independent functions for CCT-4 have been demonstrated in other systems, notably CCT2 as an aggrephagy receptor68. Given that CCT-5 was also found in CCT-4 complexes immunoprecipitated from nuclear extracts, we conclude that the interaction between the mHORMADs and CCT-4 occurs in the context of an assembled TRiC complex in vivo. Furthermore, our data support the interpretation that the TRiC-mHORMAD interactions required for axis assembly occur in meiotic nuclei: (1) CCT-4 localized to meiotic germline nuclei throughout prophase, where it generally overlapped with chromatin, and (2) CCT-4 and CCT-5 were abundant in the chromatin and soluble nuclear fractions of germline extracts. Our results provide evidence that TRiC acts as a nuclear chaperonin to regulate mHORMAD assembly at meiotic chromosomes and prevent ectopic interactions that could lead to aggregation.

Preparation of protein extractsProtein fractionation was performed as described in ref. 71 without modifications. Briefly, 10 confluent 100 mm Petri dishes containing synchronized young adults (24 h post-L4) of the indicated genotypes were washed with M9 buffer, and worms were collected by sedimentation. Excess M9 buffer was removed, and worms were resuspended in 4 mL of NP Buffer (10 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.6, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.25 mM sucrose, PMSF 1 mM, and DTT 1 mM)/1 mL of worm pellet.
