Mitochondrial cristae are essential for respiration, yet the molecular basis of
how the high curvature of these membrane folds is maintained remains unclear.
Using structure prediction tools and multiscale simulations, we examined the
role of the MIC10 subcomplex of the mitochondrial contact site and cristae
organizing system (MICOS). We found that the MIC10 proteins Mic10, Mic26, and
Mic27 strongly recruit cardiolipin at conserved positive loop motifs, driving
oligomerization of these subunits and resulting in the stabilization of
curvature in model membranes. Reconstruction of the full MIC10 complex in a
realistic crista junction setup shows its capability to maintain membrane
bending, while intrinsically disordered regions may form a permeability barrier
between cristae and the intermembrane space. These findings provide a
mechanistic model for cristae curvature formation and suggest how MICOS
components cooperate with cardiolipins to maintain mitochondrial architecture.

INTRODUCTION

Mitochondria have complex topologies, having two membranes with the inner membrane composed of inward folds called cristae. The cristae increase the surface area for accommodating adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP)–generating respiratory complexes along the mitochondrial inner membrane that are critical for aerobic metabolism. A lack of cristae or aberrant morphology has been linked to mitochondrial diseases (1, 2), underscoring the importance of proper formation and maintenance of these structures. In addition, the structure of cristae can change to adapt to different metabolic demands (2). However, the mechanisms involved in the modeling of cristae remain elusive to date.

There have been multiple membrane components implicated in the formation and maintenance of cristae. The lipid cardiolipin (CDL2) is known to stabilize the curvature at crista junctions (3, 4), while F-type ATP synthases [adenosine triphosphatases (ATPases)] are required to maintain curvature at the cristae ridges (5, 6). Mutations in the ATP synthase dimerization domain [i.e., Up-regulated during skeletal muscle growth protein 5 (USMG5)] prevent the formation of dimers and change the number and morphology of cristae formed (1). Optic atrophy protein 1 (OPA1), specifically the long isoform (l-OPA1), a protein critical for mitochondrial membrane fusion, is also known to influence the dynamics and formation of cristae (7). In combination with the ATPase and l-OPA1, the mitochondrial contact site and cristae organizing system (MICOS) complex plays a vital role in crista formation and maintenance (7–14).

The MICOS complex can be further divided into subcomplexes, often referred to as the MIC60 subcomplex and the MIC10 subcomplex (here referred to as MIC60 and MIC10, respectively), as seen in Fig. 1 (14–17). MIC60 comprises Mic60 (formerly Mitofilin), Mic19 [formerly Coiled-Coil-Helix-Coiled-Coil-Helix Domain Containing 3 (CHCHD3)], and Mic25 (formerly CHCHD6) and has been implicated in maintaining the distance between the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) and the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) by making contacts with proteins such as Sorting and assembly machinery component 50 (Sam50) (8). Specifically, Mic60 localizes to the crista junctions (18) and coordinates with the subunits found in MIC10 of the MICOS complex (19, 20). MIC10 is formed through associations between Mic10 [formerly Mitochondrial inner membrane organizing system 1 (MINOS1)], Mic26 [formerly Apolipoprotein O (APOO)], and Mic27 [formerly Apolipoprotein O-like (APOOL)]. Mic13 (formerly C19orf70 or QIL1) is also associated with MIC10 but is reported to be responsible for the interaction between MIC10 and MIC60 (19, 21). However, while Mic10 can act alone to form oligomers, which, in turn, causes membrane bending (22, 23), it is not solely responsible for the formation and maintenance of cristae (22). Knockouts of Mic13 and other MIC10 substituents, Mic26, and Mic27, result in reduced numbers of cristae (15, 24).

Despite its importance, there is limited structural information about the MICOS complex. Partial structures of the Mic19-Mic60 subcomplex exist (9), but there are no structures of MIC10 or its homologs. However, structure prediction tools such as AlphaFold 3 (25) can be leveraged to examine possible structures of such elusive proteins. Because of the lack of information about these complexes within the data that such tools are based upon, care needs to be taken in ensuring that these structures represent the three-dimensional (3D) arrangement found in vivo.

To investigate the feasibility of predicted structures, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations can be used, which provide important dynamical insights into systems of interest. For example, when used in combination with biochemical data, MD simulations can be used to assess whether a given protein structure behaves as reported in the literature. Atomistically detailed simulations [all-atom (AA)] can aid in examining the stability of complexes and resolving specific interactions, such as those between proteins and between proteins and lipids. To provide access to large spatiotemporal scales needed to observe crista formation in simulations, we can use coarse-grained (CG) models, allowing simulations of membranes in situ (26).

Here, we used structure prediction and multiscale simulations to investigate the possible mechanisms by which MIC10 induces and maintains crista formation in mitochondria. Simulations were performed using the Martini 3 force field (27) for the larger-scale CG systems and the CHARMM36m force field (28) for smaller-scale AA validation runs. We observed strong curvature generated by Mic10 oligomerization, driven by recruitment of cardiolipin (CDL2). This effect was enhanced by additional subunits of the MIC10 complex, causing a faster rate of curvature with increased CDL2 interaction. Higher oligomerization states aided in the localization of MIC10 to the crista junction, enabling stabilization of curvature and interaction of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) between the subunits across the cristae. Moreover, MIC10 at crista junctions may provide a permeability barrier between the cristae and intermembrane space (IMS).

This body of work provides a putative mechanism for the formation of mitochondrial cristae. This has not previously been studied computationally and has been aided by the improvements in structure prediction tools such as AlphaFold 3 (25). The techniques used here allow specific investigations of lipid binding and protein-protein interaction, which is difficult to directly detect experimentally.

Stabilization of curvature at the crista junction was observed, although similar results were obtained with preferential CDL2 placement at areas of negative curvature. Our results demonstrate how CDL2 could be recruited to these areas to maintain the curvature, and the MIC10 subcomplex could aid with this process as well. Once the crista junctions are formed, the role of the MIC10 subcomplex could transition to aid in establishing the permeability barrier, as observed in this work. In addition, MIC10 is known to be necessary for the correct localization of the MIC60 subcomplex to crista junctions (12).

In addition to aiding with the understanding of the steps, proposing structural models enables the inclusion of these complexes in large-scale models, such as the mitochondrial crista (40). While the structures have not been experimentally verified, their inclusion will more closely make the crista behavior match that observed experimentally and therefore will improve the current model.

There are still many unknowns about the MIC10 subcomplex, not least being experimental structures for the subunits and any possible complexes that form. Experimentally determined structures will remain elusive in the case of transient populations of MIC10 complexes, which act to form and maintain crista junctions and interactions with MIC60. Predicted structures, such as the ones presented here, could provide possible conformations that experimental density could be fitted to. This will provide information about the accuracy of the predicted models, as well as aiding in experimental elucidation.

Although the role of CDL2 is found to be crucial, the spontaneous curvature of cardiolipin remains somewhat controversial. The curvature observed in our simulations is quite large, and although CDL2 has been shown to sort into regions of high curvature both computationally and experimentally (3, 45–47), in the inverted hexagonal phase (HII), CDL2 has mild curvature (48). While it is difficult to state whether the Martini force field overestimates the curvature generated, the overall conclusions of this work would remain the same even if the membrane distortion measured in vivo is less than that measured here.

Future work can test the hypothesis that the IDRs of the MIC10 subcomplex form a permeability barrier in vivo by deletion of these regions in Mic26/Mic27 to determine whether there is still formation of cristae, which we do not expect to be affected. There could, however, be a difference in the effectiveness of respiration in these mitochondria. In addition, mutations could be made to the residues highlighted as important for maintaining contact between Mic10 and Mic26/Mic27 or those that interact with CDL2 within these complexes. These mutations should impact the ability of this complex to effectively form cristae. Overall, this work proposes a mechanism by which the MIC10 subcomplexes recruit cardiolipins to induce and maintain membrane curvature and impose a permeability barrier, shining light on the formation of cristae on a level not previously reported.

The sequences for N-terminal region (26:32) and C-terminal region (185:198) from Mic26 and N-terminal region (28:60) and C-terminal region (189:268) from Mic27 were used as an input for Polyply (70) to generate parameters, with the Martini3-IDR force field (66) and construct a simulation box with 25 copies of each. AnalysisPyLipID (71) was used to measure the protein CDL2 head group and POPC lipid tail interactions, with a dual-cutoff system of 0.5 and 0.8. The lateral box size was measured using “gmx energy” and selecting “Box-X,” averaging plotted points over the surrounding 10 data points. Membrane curvature was measured using an in-house script using the MDAnalysis MembraneCurvature tool (72, 73). Protein-protein contact was analyzed using an in-house script with a distance cutoff of 0.8 nm. The root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) was measured using the “gmx rmsf” tool and selecting the c-alpha atoms. Density of the components in the system was measured using “gmx density” over all time points. Plots were generated using Matplotlib (74). Images of structures or simulation systems were generated using PyMOL or VMD.
