Caenorhabditis elegans is a useful model organism for combining multiple
imaging, genetic, and biochemical methodologies to gain more insight into the
biological function of specific proteins. Combining both biochemical and genetic
analyses can lead to a better understanding of how a given protein may function
within the context of a network of other proteins or specific pathway. Here, we
describe a protocol for the biochemical isolation of serpin-interacting proteins
using affinity purification and proteomic analysis. As the knowledge of in vivo
serpin interacting partners in C. elegans has largely been obtained using
genetic and in vitro recombinant protein studies, this protocol serves as a
complementary approach to provide insight into the biological function and
regulation of serpins.

1. Introduction

Proteases are core components of many biological pathways where they cleave peptide bonds to either activate or degrade other proteins. Thus, regulation of proteolytic activity is essential [1]. Serine and cysteine protease inhibitors (serpins) are the largest family of active site inhibitors, where the majority of members function to prevent the negative effects of excessive peptide bond hydrolysis via a unique suicide substrate-like mechanism where a serpin inhibits a target protease with a 1:1 stoichiometry [2–6].

Members of the serpin family are also conserved within the Caenorhabditis elegans genome, including 5 members that have bona fide inhibitory function [6, 7]. Using both genetic and RNA interference (RNAi)-based studies, we have previously demonstrated that one of these serpins, SRP-6, exhibits a pro-survival function by blocking intestinal cell necrotic cell death in response to several stress-inducing stimuli [8]. The C. elegans model system has proven ideal for providing information regarding the identification of SRP-6 regulatory targets from a genetic standpoint. Although informative, these genetic approaches do not demonstrate that SRP-6 can be found in complex with a particular protease or non-protease regulatory target in vivo. Overall, there is little direct biochemical evidence regarding the identity of the specific proteins that interact with SRP-6 in C. elegans. Recently, proteomics-based approaches have been used more frequently to complement the genetic evidence obtained through chemical mutagenesis and RNAi studies. To date, multiple strategies have been developed and employed to isolate protein complexes from C. elegans [9–13]. In this report, we describe a modified version of our biochemical approach to isolate SRP-6 interacting proteins using affinity purification steps in combination with nano-scale liquid chromatography online coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (nanoLC-MS/MS) [10]. This methodology is advantageous because it is an unbiased approach and provides the specificity needed to isolate and identify in vivo SRP-6 containing protein complexes from lysates prepared from animals that express the affinity tagged version of SRP-6. This approach serves as an important way to combine both biochemical and genetic information in combination to further define the biological function of serpins. We describe methods for large-scale worm growth, whole worm lysate preparation, affinity purification procedures, proteomic analysis, and bioinformatic analysis of identified proteins.

4. DiscussionThe overall goal of these studies was to determine whether the TrAP::SRP-6 transgene was detectable using our protocol. Taken together, our data demonstrate that this affinity purification procedure yields a SRP-6 species that is detectable by nanoLC-MS/MS after a single round of purification. We are working to verify which of the associated proteins are legitimate SRP-6 interacting partners by addressing several issues. It is a possibility that the overexpression of SRP-6 in our system may lead to the identification of false positive interacting proteins. However, serpin complexes are degraded via the proteasome, resulting in a short predicted half-life [19]. Thus, an overexpression system is used to increase overall SRP-6 abundance to maximize the probability of detecting in vivo SRP-6 complexes. To alleviate the concern of increased false positive detection, we are carrying out our procedure in several different ways. First, we are comparing mass spectrometry data obtained from multiple different IP reactions from a single round of purification and identifying those proteins that arise in all data sets. Second, we are performing a second round of purification. Ideally, if the interacting protein is in a true complex with SRP-6, it should remain throughout the purification. However, we expect that with increased purification steps the sensitivity for detecting SRP-6 complexes will decrease, but the specificity of proteins identified will increase. Third, we can also eliminate potential contaminating proteins by increasing the stringency of the IP wash conditions. Our current protocol uses 150 mM NaCl, but other reports have used up to 300 mM NaCl [13]. Fourth, Quantitative mass spectrometry can also be applied to compare the relative abundance of pull-down proteins in the presence of SRP-6 vs. in the absence of SRP-6 to overcome the problem of false positive identification [20]. The addition of protease inhibitors does not occur while SRP-6-expressing animals are intact, eliminating the concern that their addition could interfere with in vivo SRP-6:protease complex formation. 4. Discussion

The overall goal of these studies was to determine whether the TrAP::SRP-6 transgene was detectable using our protocol. Taken together, our data demonstrate that this affinity purification procedure yields a SRP-6 species that is detectable by nanoLC-MS/MS after a single round of purification. It is a possibility that the overexpression of SRP-6 in our system may lead to the identification of false positive interacting proteins. Finally, once these steps are applied, to confirm that the identified proteins are “true” interactors of SRP-6 and not false positives, it is important to perform secondary screening using genetics and cell biological techniques to confirm the interaction.

In the purification procedure described here, the addition of protease inhibitors is added to animal lysates post-lysis to to (1) prevent spurious interactions between SRP-6 and proteases released from non-cytosolic compartments during the lysis process, and (2) to prevent the degradation of SRP-6:protease complexes that are present in the prepared lysate. The addition of protease inhibitors does not occur while SRP-6-expressing animals are intact, eliminating the concern that their addition could interfere with in vivo SRP-6:protease complex formation. We have demonstrated that treatment of C. elegans with bortezomib efficiently inhibits proteasomal function [21], suggesting that this treatment may increase the concentration of SRP-6 containing complexes in our experimental system. In addition, while serpin:protease complexes are held together via a covalent bond, non-protease SRP-6 interacting partners are most likely held via ionic interactions, creating the potential risk for loss of these components throughout the purification process. To alleviate this issue we are developing the use of chemical cross-linking reagents to stabilize these non-covalent interactions in either animal lysates or live animals.

To confirm that any potential SRP-6:protease interaction is specific, we can perform pulldown experiments using transgenic animals expressing a srp-6 gene with either a wild-type or mutated reactive site loop (RSL). The classical P14 mutation prevents covalent serpin:protease complex formation with any target protease [22]. We would predict that a putative protease target identified from pulldown performed from wild-type SRP-6 lysates would not be present in lysates prepared from animals harboring the P14 mutation. This mutant strain will provide specificity to any proteases isolated as SRP-6 interacting partners however, will not prevent non-protease regulatory proteins that bind to SRP-6 in other locations other than the RSL. This 29 KDa protein will be expressed under the control of the nhx-2 promoter and contain an N-terminal TrAP tag, providing us with a cytosolic, non-serpin control to identify the identity of non-specific protein interactions that are present within our purification setup. Once the identities of non-specific interacting proteins have been identified, these specific proteins can be eliminated as potential SRP-6 interactors.

Using the controls described above will provide increased specificity to the process of identifying specific SRP-6 interacting proteins. Once a specific candidate protein has been identified through MS analysis, the interaction will be verified in multiple ways. First, we will verify the interaction by performing reciprocal immunoprecipitations between SRP-6 and the candidate protein. Second, we can verify the biological relevance of this interaction by using RNAi to rescue known phenotypes associated with srp-6 deletion in C. elegans [8]. Thirdly we can use visualize the interaction in vivo by microscopy using FRET based reporter constructs.

We have described a method to biochemically isolate in vivo serpin-interacting proteins using an affinity tagged version of SRP-6. As the knowledge of in vivo SRP-6 interacting partners using C. elegans has largely been obtained using genetic and in vitro recombinant protein studies, we sought to complement these findings using an affinity protein purification setup to isolate interacting proteins from animals that express TrAP::SRP-6.
