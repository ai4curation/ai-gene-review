PURPOSE: Keratin contamination is a common problem in mass spectrometry
proteomic analyses, particularly in bottom-up mass spectrometry. The purpose of
this study was to determine the protein contaminants introduced during the
proteomic analysis of tear fluid.
METHODS: Human tear fluid samples were collected using Schirmer strips.
Proteomic analyses were performed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass
spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) on blank Schirmer strips and tear fluid samples, with
empty vials serving as controls for assessing environmental contaminant
proteins.
RESULTS: We detected 26 contaminant proteins (18 keratins and 8 non-keratins).
98.2% of the total protein contamination can be attributed to the 9 keratins,
including KRT10 (23.6%), KRT1 (23.5%), KRT2 (15.7%), KRT14 (7.6%), KRT16 (7.0%),
KRT5 (6.1%), KRT9 (5.9%), KRT6B (4.6%), and KRT6A (4.3%). A comparison to the
proteomic profile of blank Schirmer strips and controls (empty vials) found a
strong correlation (R2 = 0.9753), indicating that these proteins were not from
the blank Schirmer strips but are environmental contaminants. On the other hand,
several keratins including KRT19, KRT13, KRT4, KRT7, KRT15, KRT8 and KRT18 were
present in tear fluid, but either not detected or were negligible in blank
strips. Another set of keratins, including KRT5, KRT6A, KRT14, KRT16, and KRT17,
were identified as components of tear fluid as well as environmental
contaminants.
CONCLUSIONS: This study revealed nine major contaminant keratins in the mass
spectrometry analysis. Several other keratins were identified as constituents of
tear fluid. Background subtraction is necessary for the accurate analysis of
tear fluid using mass spectrometry.

Introduction

Tear fluid is a heterogeneous mixture that covers the ocular surface, providing lubrication, protection against pathogens, and essential nutrients to the cornea (Chang and Purt, 2021; Pflugfelder and Stern, 2020). The diverse array of proteins in tears offers a non-invasive insight into physiological and pathological states, making tear fluid a valuable source of biomarkers for various ocular and systemic diseases (Beckman et al., 2016; Gijs et al., 2021; Jackson et al., 2016; von Thun Und Hohenstein-Blaul et al., 2013; Yoon et al., 2023). As research progresses, tear fluid analysis holds the potential to revolutionize healthcare with its convenient and accessible means for early disease detection and management.

Schirmer strips, small strips of filter paper, are commonly used in ophthalmology to measure tear production, particularly for conditions like dry eye disease (Ngo et al., 2018; Pieczyński et al., 2021; Posa et al., 2013). During the test, the Schirmer strip is placed in the lower eyelid to absorb tears over five minutes, with the wetting length indicating tear production volume. This simple, quick, and non-invasive procedure is valuable for collecting tear fluid for molecular analysis.

Tear fluid proteins play crucial roles in maintaining the integrity and function of the ocular surface. Among these proteins, keratins are fibrous structural proteins known for providing strength and protection to various tissues. Certain keratins are known to contribute to tear film stability, prevent evaporation, and act as antimicrobial peptides to protect against corneal infection (Schermer et al., 1986; Zasloff, 2012). Further, alterations in tear film keratins have been linked to ocular surface disorders, including Sjögren's and Meibomian gland dysfunction (Hirai et al., 2006; Ong et al., 1991). However, identifying keratins using mass spectrometry analysis is challenging due to potential contamination from skin cells and hair during sample handling.

This study aims to differentiate between keratins that are intrinsic components of tear fluid and those introduced as contaminants. Additionally, we investigate the potential introduction of proteins from blank Schirmer strips used in tear fluid collection. By categorizing the keratins detected in tear fluid and assessing protein profiles from blank Schirmer strips, we aim to ensure accurate proteomic analysis, enhancing our understanding of tear fluid protein composition.

3.Results3.1.Keratins Detected in LC-MS/MS Analysis of Tear FluidThe keratin family consists of approximately 54 different members (Moll et al., 2008b). Certain keratins are crucial components of the tear film, maintaining its stability, preventing evaporation, and acting as antimicrobial peptides (Schermer et al., 1986; Zasloff, 2012). In this study, a total of 40 keratin proteins (mean PSM>2) were identified in tear fluid using LC-MS/MS analysis (Table 2, Supplemental Table S1). These keratins constituted approximately 10% of the total protein content found in the tear fluid samples (Figure 2). The most abundant keratins detected in tear fluid include KRT19 (826.0 ± 166.6 PSM), KRT13 (753.8 ± 166.2), KRT4 (726.2 ± 145.5), KRT5 (536.8 ± 72.7), KRT10 (391.5 ± 26.6), KRT1 (320.3 ± 22.2), KRT6A (319.0 ± 25.2), KRT7 (306.5 ± 66.7), KRT14 (282.9 ± 36.8), KRT15 (276.0 ± 74.6), KRT8 (250.3 ± 59.0), KRT2 (244.9 ± 15.3), KRT16 (143.2 ± 50.3), and KRT17 (86.6 ± 40.3) (Table 2). Some of these keratins are partially or fully contributed by the environmental contamination.3.2.Keratins Detected in Blank Schirmer Strips and Empty VialsKeratin contamination is a common issue in mass spectrometry analysis because keratins are structural components of hair, nails, and the outer layer of skin. Thus, it is essential to distinguish between keratins that are true components of the tear fluid and those that are contaminants. Therefore, we analyzed the proteins in blank Schirmer strips and empty vials to reveal the identity and levels of contaminant proteins. There was no significant difference between the total keratin protein detected in blank strips and empty vials (Figure 2). Comparisons of the proteomic content of blank Schirmer strips and empty vials showed a high coefficient of correlation (R2 = 0.9753) between the relative abundances of proteins in both groups (Figure 3). This indicates that the majority of detected proteins were contaminants introduced via the environment or sample processing rather than by the Schirmer strips themselves.Notably, 98.2% of the proteomic content in these blanks belonged to the keratin family. Specifically, nine keratins: KRT1 (320.3 ± 10.2 PSM), KRT10 (314.5 ± 7.0), KRT2 (223.8 ± 6.8), KRT14 (106.6 ± 6.7), KRT16 (98.2 ± 7.0), KRT6A (86.9 ± 8.0), KRT5 (86.7 ± 5.3), KRT9 (79.9 ± 2.9), and KRT6B (75.6 ± 24.0) were highly abundant in both blank groups (Figure 3). Certain keratins, including KRT5, KRT6A, KRT14, KRT16, and KRT17, are components of tear fluid and are also contributed by the environment (Figure 4). Bar plots displaying the relative levels of these nine proteins across all three groups are displayed in Figure 5. The remaining proteins detected in trace amounts were Albumin (ALB), Protein S100-A8 (S100A8), Polyubiquitin-C (USB), Collagen triple helix repeat-containing protein 1 (CTHRC1), and major skin components such as Dermcidin (DCD), Desmoplakin (DSP), and Desmoglein-1 (DSG1) (Table 3). 4.DiscussionKeratins are highly detectable in mass spectrometry due to their rapid proteolytic digestion and efficient ionization, which amplify their signals even at low concentrations. This makes keratins prone to overrepresentation in proteomic analyses, as even minor contamination, such as from skin or hair, can produce disproportionately strong signals. To mitigate this issue and distinguish contaminants from biologically relevant keratins, strict contamination controls, blank sample analyses, and careful handling during sample preparation are essential.This study aimed to identify potential contaminants in the tear proteome introduced during sample collection and analysis. We found that most contaminants were primarily keratins originating from sample processing and the environment rather than from the Schirmer strip itself. This finding is consistent with existing literature that recognizes keratins as common contaminants in mass spectrometry (Hodge et al., 2013; Keller et al., 2008). Specifically, three major keartins detected in blank controls (KRT1, KRT2, and KRT10) have been extensively reported in airborne environments (Fox et al., 2008; Keller et al., 2008; Voloshina et al., 2017). The identified contaminant proteins are prevalent in epithelial cells and hair follicles, which rapidly proliferate and shed, making keratins ubiquitous in the collection environment (Fischer et al., 2016; Fu et al., 2014; Guo et al., 2020; Roth et al., 2012; Schweizer et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2016). Additionally, exhaled breath condensate, containing proteins like Dermcidin and S100A9, presents another contamination source (Núñez-Naveira et al., 2019). Trace amounts of albumin, dermcidin, desmoplakin, and desmoglein-1, known structural components of the epidermis or present in sweat (Bharathan and Dickinson, 2019; Csősz et al., 2015; Hammers and Stanley, 2013; Rieg et al., 2006), were also detected. Minimizing such contaminants is challenging due to the difficulty of restricting sample exposure to airborne keratins and shedding proteins, especially without filtered environments (Fox et al., 2008; Voloshina et al., 2017). Therefore, background subtraction is necessary for accurate analysis of tear fluid proteins especially keratins. Environmental contaminants identified in our study may vary across different laboratory settings, so we recommend analyzing blank samples to accurately gauge external contaminants.After background subtraction, the most abundant keratins detected in tear fluid included KRT19, KRT13, KRT4, KRT5, KRT6A, KRT7, KRT14, KRT15, and KRT8. Among these, KRT5, KRT13, and KRT14 have been identified as potential biomarkers for tear-based diagnostics of keratoconus and pterygium (de Almeida Borges et al., 2020). KRT13 is recognized as a specific marker for conjunctival epithelial cells (Ramirez-Miranda et al., 2011), although it is also expressed in other epithelial cells and various proliferative cell types (Malecha and Miettinen, 1991; Moll et al., 2008a). KRT7, KRT15, and KRT19 are common conjunctival epithelial cells (Català et al., 2021; Poli et al., 2015; Poli et al., 2011; Ramirez-Miranda et al., 2011; van Velthoven et al., 2021), while KRT4 has been reported in the human conjunctival epithelium proteome and is associated with mucosal lineage (Soria et al., 2018). KRT8, a well-established epithelial marker for retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), is also present in human corneal endothelial cells (Hunt and Davis, 1990; Merjava et al., 2009). The use of Schirmer strips for tear collection is known to capture conjunctival and corneal epithelial cells, which likely contributes to the high levels of cellular keratins observed in the tear fluid samples.KRT5 and KRT17 play important roles in regulating epithelial cell proliferation and differentiation (Alam et al., 2011; DePianto et al., 2010). KRT5 is primarily recognized as a structural protein found in epithelial tissues, particularly in the skin and various mucosal surfaces throughout the body. It has also been detected in tear fluid (de Almeida Borges et al., 2020; Ramos et al., 2015). In contrast, the presence of KRT17 in corneal or conjunctival cells remains poorly documented. However, KRT17 has been identified in the acinar and ductal epithelial cells of human Meibomian glands (Reneker et al., 2020), and is predominantly associated with hair and skin cells (Kim et al., 2006; McGowan and Coulombe, 2000; Zhang et al., 2019).Several additional keratins, including KRT18, KRT72, and KRT75, were detected exclusively in tear fluid, albeit in lower amounts. KRT18 is a crucial structural protein in epithelial cells, playing a role in maintaining cellular integrity and supporting various cellular processes. Its presence in tear fluid is likely derived from multiple ocular surface tissues, such as conjunctival and corneal epithelial cells.The exclusive detection of KRT72 and KRT75 in tear fluid is intriguing, given that these keratins are typically classified as hair follicle-specific epithelial keratins (Moll et al., 2008a). Their presence may be explained by contact with eyelashes during the tear collection process using Schirmer strips.One limitation of this study is the inability to determine the exact source of keratins detected in tear fluid. While existing literature suggests that many of these keratins are expressed in epithelial, corneal, and conjunctival cells, determining their precise sources is essential for understanding their biological significance within the tear proteome. Future studies could employ advanced molecular techniques to confirm whether these keratins are actively produced at tear production sites or are derived from other biological fluids, such as blood filtrate. Such investigations would provide deeper insights into the roles of tear fluid keratins in ocular health and disease.Another limitation of this study is the reliance solely on bottom-up mass spectrometry. While effective for identifying and quantifying peptides, this method does not provide comprehensive insights into intact proteins. Integrating bottom-up with top-down mass spectrometry could have offered a more holistic perspective by analyzing intact proteins alongside peptides. Such an approach would enable more robust confirmation of the presence and consistency of specific proteins, including keratins, across both methods. Furthermore, additional targeted approaches are required to validate the findings of this study.

One limitation of this study is the inability to determine the exact source of keratins detected in tear fluid. While existing literature suggests that many of these keratins are expressed in epithelial, corneal, and conjunctival cells, determining their precise sources is essential for understanding their biological significance within the tear proteome. Future studies could employ advanced molecular techniques to confirm whether these keratins are actively produced at tear production sites or are derived from other biological fluids, such as blood filtrate. Such investigations would provide deeper insights into the roles of tear fluid keratins in ocular health and disease.

Integrating bottom-up with top-down mass spectrometry could have offered a more holistic perspective by analyzing intact proteins alongside peptides. Such an approach would enable more robust confirmation of the presence and consistency of specific proteins, including keratins, across both methods. Furthermore, additional targeted approaches are required to validate the findings of this study.

5.ConclusionIn this study, we successfully distinguished between contaminant keratins and tear fluid keratins using bottom-up LC-MS/MS. Our findings identified nine of the most abundant contaminant proteins as environmental keratins, emphasizing the importance of background subtraction for accurate analysis. Further investigation into the potential sources of environmental keratins in tear fluid could provide valuable insights into strategies for minimizing contamination during sample collection and processing. Additionally, we identified a set of keratins exclusively present in tear fluid. Investigating their biological origins and specific functions could provide deeper insights into their role in maintaining eye health.
