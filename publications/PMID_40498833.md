membrane proteins present a substantial challenge for achieving precise
localization within the secretory pathway. In this study, we demonstrate that
atypical signal sequences, characterized by high hydrophobicity and/or the
absence of characteristic charges, are recognized by the signal recognition

fidelity in protein subcellular localization.

INTRODUCTION

Accurate protein localization to their respective destinations is essential for maintaining cellular structure and homeostasis. Approximately one-third of the eukaryotic proteome consists of secreted and membrane proteins destined for extracellular compartments or intracellular organelles (1). During biogenesis, most of these proteins undergo co- or posttranslational translocation into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to traverse its impermeable membrane (2–6).

The ER translocation process is a complicated yet tightly regulated process, involving two major machineries. Initially, the N-terminal signal sequences or transmembrane helices of translating polypeptides are recognized by the signal recognition particle (SRP) in the cytosol and then delivered to the SRP receptor (SR) at the ER membrane (7). The nascent polypeptides are then transferred onto the SEC61 translocon and conducted into the ER lumen until stop signals, such as transmembrane helices, emerge from ribosomes (8–12). Signal sequences are cotranslationally removed from the translocated polypeptides by the signal peptidase complex (SPC) to release the mature proteins (13). Alternatively, posttranslationally translocated proteins use cytosolic chaperones to prevent their folding before translocation and require Sec62-Sec63 for translocation (4, 14–19).

Dysfunctions in the protein translocation machinery have been linked to various developmental disorders and diseases. For example, mutations in the SRP complexresult in syndromic neutropenia (20) and familial aplasia and myelodysplasia (21). Similarly, dysregulation of components within the SEC61 complex is associated with kidney and liver diseases, diabetes, and human cancers (22). Beyond the SEC61 translocon, the ER targeting of specific signal sequences and tail-anchored (TA) proteins is mediated by the ER membrane protein complex (EMC) and the guided entry of TA complex (23–25). Notably, targeting sequences exhibit high variability and degeneracy, having evolved to direct proteins to different organelles via distinct targeting or translocation pathways (26). However, the precise mechanisms by which the secretory machinery accurately recognizes, interprets, and sorts diverse targeting sequences remain poorly understood.

P-type adenosine triphosphatases (ATPases) constitute a phylogenetically conserved superfamily that transports diverse substrates—including ions, phospholipids, and polyamines—across membranes (27, 28). Among these, P5A-ATPases (e.g., human ATP13A1) have recently been identified as dislocases that extract mislocalized TA proteins and misoriented transmembrane proteins from the ER, thereby preventing their degradation via ER-associated degradation pathways (29–31). In addition, ATP13A1 facilitates the signal sequence–mediated ER translocation of secreted and membrane proteins (32, 33). Quantitative proteomic studies underscore its essential role in stabilizing N-terminal ER-targeting signal sequence–containing proteins and TA proteins, with the former being the most destabilized class upon ATP13A1 knockout (KO) (29).

Physiological studies reveal that P5A-ATPases exert pleiotropic effects in metazoans through diverse substrates. In Caenorhabditis elegans, null mutations in catp-8 (the ATP13A1 ortholog) impair the ER translocation of the neuronal receptor dendrite-morphogenesis-abnormal (DMA-1) and morphogen Wnt proteins, disrupting neuronal development and life span (30, 32–34). In mice, ATP13A1 is critical for maintaining the stability of the antiviral protein MAVS; Atp13a1 KO mice exhibit growth retardation and embryonic lethality, highlighting the broad physiological importance of P5A-ATPases (35). Moreover, a hypomorphic mutation in human ATP13A1 is linked to developmental defects, including intellectual disability, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and facial and nail abnormalities (34, 36). However, existing research has primarily focused on the fate and pathological consequences of N-terminal signal sequence–containing proteins in ATP13A1 KO cells, leaving unresolved how ATP13A1 mediates ER translocation of these proteins under physiological conditions.

In this study, we integrated bulk fluorescence assays, biochemical analyses, structural biology, and genetic approaches to dissect the ATP13A1’s role in signal sequence–mediated ER translocation. We found that atypical signal sequences—characterized by unusually high hydrophobic cores and/or lacking N-terminal positively charged residues—are prone to topogenic errors. These sequences are dislocated by ATP13A1 from the ER membrane and subsequently translocated into the ER via the SEC61 complex. Cryo–electron microscopy (Cryo-EM) structures of human ATP13A1 (resolved at 3.40 to 3.87 Å) in distinct conformations revealed key residues governing substrate binding, specificity, and conformational transitions. Thus, our work delineates a substrate-specific translocation pathway that ensures high efficiency and fidelity. The evolutionary emergence of P5A-ATPases in eukaryotes reflects the increasing complexity of signal sequences, which diversify to target distinct organelles.

In addition, we used amber codon suppression to introduce the photolabile ncAA pAzF (fig. S8H) for cross-linking residues between interacting proteins within a distance range of 3 to 4 Å (54). By mapping the insertion position of pAzF in LRP8SS through an amber stop codon (TAG), we determined that LRP8(25TAG)SS, while affecting neither the expression level nor the P5A dependence of LRP8SS (fig. S8I), could be used to cross-link its interacting proteins. Upon UV illumination, LRP8(25TAG)SS cross-linked with ATP13A1(D533N) (Fig. 4C). On the basis of the cryo-EM structures of ATP13A1, we selected residues (Y265, M272, F276, E492, I495, L499, and A1025) within the substrate binding pocket of ATP13A1 (Fig. 4D) to incorporate pAzF. Among these residues, ATP13A1 cross-linked with LRP8SS at L499 (Fig. 4E). Thus, these findings suggest that ATP13A1 establishes a direct interaction with LRP8SS through its substrate binding pocket.

To elucidate the roles of negatively charged invariant residues E492 and E496 within the PPELPM/IE motif positioned centrally in the lipid bilayer, as well as proximal hydrophobic residues on the cytoplasmic side, namely, F276 (TM2, moderately conserved), L499 (TM4, moderately conserved), and L1029 (TM6, conserved), in substrate binding/translocation, we engineered mutants for E492/E496 and F276/L499/L1029. Our findings demonstrated that alanine or lysine substitution at E492 and E496, or substituting the highly hydrophobic F276, L499, and L1029 with low hydrophobic polar (or charged) residues, disrupted ATP13A1’s normal functionality (Fig. 4, F and G). Moreover, alanine substitution for polar/charged residues at S279/N502/T503/S1032/R1033 near the cytoplasmic opening [referred to as ATP13A1(cyto-H)] or at Y265/S487/S1001/S1003/T1018/Q1020/G1021 near the luminal opening [referred to as ATP13A1(lumen-H)] substantially impaired ATP13A1’s activity (Fig. 4, F and G), indicating their critical roles in substrate recognition and translocation.

Negatively charged residues E492 and E496 in ATP13A1 are highly conserved across eukaryotes (32). Consequently, we conducted extensive functional analyses by mutating these two glutamic acid residues to aspartic acid, glutamine, or alanine residues (Fig. 4, G and H) and analyzed the effects of these mutations on the translocation of WT substrates or their mutants. Several key observations emerged: First, substituting glutamic acid residue at positions 492 or 496 of ATP13A1 with aspartic acid or glutamine did not affect its functionality (Fig. 4, G and H). Second, Q25 is the polar residue immediately following the hydrophobic leucine segment of LRP8SS. The Q25E mutant, in which the less effective proton-donating amide group (─CONH2) of Q25 (highlighted in blue, fig. S1B) is replaced by an effective proton-donating carboxylic acid group (─COOH), was more efficiently processed by ATP13A1(E492Q, E496Q) (Fig. 4H). Third, the Q25S mutant of LRP8SS exhibited similar ATP13A1(E492Q, E496Q) dependence as WT LRP8SS. Last, mutations such as ATP13A1(E492A, E496A), LRP8SS(Q25A) (Fig. 4H), and DMA-1SS(S17L) (fig. S8J) severely impaired substrate recognition and translocation. These findings suggest that E492 and E496 of ATP13A1 likely interact with the polar residues following the highly hydrophobic segment of substrates via polar interactions or even hydrogen bonding.

Signal sequences play crucial roles in protein biogenesis and trafficking by interacting with the SRP complex for ER targeting and ensuring accurate topogenesis during protein translocation (5, 6, 41, 42, 55, 56). The translocon-associated protein complex and translocating chain-associated membrane protein are known to initiate cotranslational translocation of weakly hydrophobic signal sequences through SEC61 (66, 67). Similarly, the SEC62-SEC63 complex facilitates posttranslational translocation of weakly hydrophobic signal sequences (68). Our findings reveal that P5A-dependent signal sequences deviate from the typical pattern, displaying unusually high hydrophobicity in the central core and a lack of positive charges at the N terminus (Fig. 1, H and I, and fig. S1), resulting in inverted insertion into the ER membrane (Figs. 2, A to C, and 4A). The misoriented P5A-dependent signal sequences are dislocated by ATP13A1 from the ER (Fig. 2, D to G). Similar to SPF1 (29), our structural results suggest that the substrate-binding pocket of ATP13A1 alternating its orientation toward the ER lumen or cytosol while maintaining lateral openings accessibility (Fig. 3). Our study aligns with McKenna et al.’s (29) findings that ATP13A1 is crucial for dislocating mislocalized mitochondrial TA proteins from the ER, offering misoriented atypical signal sequences with a secondary opportunity for ER translocation.

Although atypical signal sequences are error prone in topogenesis and become arrested on the ER membrane (Fig. 2), ATP13A1 engages SEC61 to promote their translocation (Fig. 6, C to E). Moreover, the ATP hydrolysis activity of ATP13A1 is essential for dislocating signal sequences to SEC61, as the D533N mutation abolishes the ATP13A1-SEC61 interaction (Fig. 6C) while enhancing interactions between signal sequences and ATP13A1 (Fig. 6E). It remains to be characterized how ATP13A1 engages SEC61 and dislocates signal sequences to SEC61 for a secondary translocation.

In summary, our investigation delineates a comprehensive translocation pathway crucial for error-prone signal sequences, with ATP13A1 playing a pivotal role in dislocating misoriented signal sequences and facilitating subsequent translocation by engaging the SEC61 translocon. The proteins were expressed in HEK293S GnTI cells (Invitrogen) that were infected with the respective recombinant baculoviruses. After 60 hours postinfection, the cells were harvested and collected by centrifugation at 1500g for 20 min at 4°C. The resulting cell pellets were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent use.

All purification steps were performed at 4°C. The cell pellets were resuspended in a lysis buffer containing 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, and 10 mM MgCl2, supplemented with 1 mM DTT (ABCONE), DNase (50 μg/ml; Sinopharm), and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Selleck). Following homogenization using a Wheaton Dounce homogenizer, the cell lysate was mixed with a final concentration of 1.0% (w/v) n-dodecyl-β-d-maltoside (Anatrace) along with 0.1% (w/v) cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS; Anatrace). The mixture was solubilized for 3 hours under rotation, followed by centrifugation at 25,000g for 30 min to remove insoluble debris. Cryo-EM structure determination

The motion correction was applied to all dose-fractionated images using patch motion correction, and their contrast transfer functions (CTFs) were estimated through patch CTF estimation in cryoSPARC (78).

For the ATP13A1-D533N sample, 2,025,969 particles were autopicked from 4633 images using the Blob Picker and extracted with a box size of 240 pixels. These particle images underwent two-dimensional (2D) classification in cryoSPARC (78), generating templates for further particle picking. After three rounds of 2D classification, 741,213 particles were retained for initial model building, followed by one or two rounds of heterogeneous refinement. Separate 2D classification was performed for each class obtained from heterogeneous refinement, and additional rounds of heterogeneous refinement were conducted to improve particle quality. The best class, containing 180,661 particles, underwent further refinement via homogeneous refinement and nonuniform refinement, resulting in a final map at 3.40-Å resolution.

For the ATP13A1-BeF3 sample, 9,075,818 particles were autopicked from 7365 images using the template picker in cryoSPARC, based on the templates from the ATP13A1(D533N) sample. These particles were extracted with a box size of 240 pixels. After two rounds of 2D classification and heterogeneous refinement, we identified two classes of particles with favorable features for homogeneous refinement. The first class, consisting of 201,585 particles, yielded substrate-bound maps with an overall resolution of 3.87 Å. The structure of the second class (157,126 particles) at 3.68-Å resolution closely resembled the apo ATP13A1(D533N) structure.

Last, for ATP13A1-BeF3 in nanodisc, 5,008,597 particles were autopicked from 4895 images. After 2D classification, only 718,101 particles were selected. Combining two classes of particles from heterogeneous refinement, we proceeded with 2D classification and five additional rounds of heterogeneous refinement. Subsequently, 82,168 particles were chosen for homogeneous refinement, resulting in a map with a global resolution of 3.83 Å. The overall resolution assessment was based on the gold standard Fourier shell correlation of independently refined half maps using the 0.143 cutoff criterion.
