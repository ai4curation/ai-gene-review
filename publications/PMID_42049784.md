Gene silencing at the mating-type loci in budding yeast (HMRa and HMLα) depends
on the Sir proteins. Sir2, Sir3 and Sir4 are indispensable, whereas Sir1 has a
limited role. Sir proteins are also involved in repression at telomeres and rDNA
repeats. Sir proteins may mediate silencing by limiting access to DNA. Using an
inducible DNA methyltransferase expression system, we showed previously that the
silenced mating-type loci are methylated at a much slower rate than the rest of
the genome in vivo. Here, we show that Sir2, Sir3 and Sir4 are all required to
impede access to the mating-type loci and telomeric X-elements. rDNA access is
impeded by Sir2 and Sir3, but not Sir4. Methylation rates at adjacent rDNA
repeats are not strongly correlated, suggesting that Sir proteins silence rDNA
repeats randomly. Sir1 is required to impede access to HMRa, but not to HMLα,
telomeres or rDNA. Since silenced DNA is accessible in vivo, albeit at a slower
rate than elsewhere in the genome, steric occlusion is unlikely to be the
primary mechanism of silencing.

Introduction

DNA in eukaryotes associates with histones and non-histone proteins to form chromatin. Chromatin can adopt active states permissive to transcriptional activity or inactive silent states, the regulation of which is vital for growth and development in eukaryotes. The process of establishment, maintenance and inheritance of silent chromatin states is known as silencing1. In the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, silent chromatin domains are found at the cryptic homothallic mating (HM) loci HMLα and HMRa, certain telomeric regions, and ribosomal DNA elements (rDNA) (reviewed by2,3).

The HM loci are flanked by silencer elements that are bound by Abf1, Rap1 or Orc1, which recruit the silent information regulator (Sir) proteins, all of which are necessary for the stability of the silent state. Sir1 helps to recruit Sir2, Sir3 and Sir4. Sir2 preferentially deacetylates lysine 16 of histone H4 (H4K16), Sir3 binds to nucleosomes with unacetylated H4K16 residues, whereas Sir4 binds the other Sir proteins as well as DNA, and serves as scaffolding. These observations led to the classical model in which deacetylation of histones in adjacent nucleosomes by Sir2 facilitates the spreading of Sir2, Sir3 and Sir4 along the locus, resulting in a silent domain (reviewed by2,3).

At telomeres, Orc1 and Abf1 recruit Sir2, Sir3 and Sir4 to the telomeric X-elements4, while Rap1 recruits Sir2, Sir3 and Sir4 to the telomere ends, where they contribute to nuclear organization and facilitate protective folding of the telomere back to the X-element5–7. Sir1 is not required for telomeric silencing8. At the rDNA locus, Sir1, Sir3 and Sir4 are all dispensable for silencing9. The nucleolus contains 150 to 200 tandem 9.1 kb rDNA repeats, around half of which are transcriptionally active10–12. Each repeat includes a 35S rRNA gene transcribed by RNA polymerase I (Pol I), a 5S rRNA gene transcribed by RNA polymerase III (Pol III), and two non-transcribed spacers, NTS1 and NTS2. Sir2 is a subunit of the RENT complex13,14, which represses RNA polymerase II (Pol II) non-coding transcription from the bidirectional E-pro promoter in NTS115.

Sir proteins are thought to act through steric hindrance of some or all of the various stages of transcription, such as activator binding, recruitment of the transcription machinery and transcript elongation, resulting in limited accessibility16. Endonucleases and DNA methyltransferases have been used to probe silent chromatin accessibility in isolated yeast nuclei17 and in living yeast cells18–22. Our own study used E. coli dam methyltransferase (Dam) and M.SssI methyltransferase to assay DNA accessibility quantitatively in living yeast cells. We demonstrated that the yeast genome is globally accessible, even in α-factor arrested cells, except for the centromeres and, to a lesser extent, the HM domains21,22. Here, we determine the contributions of the Sir proteins to limiting the accessibility of silent chromatin in vivo, shedding light on how silent domains achieve transcriptional repression in the context of a dynamic chromatin environment.

Sir2 and Sir3 impede methylation of the rDNA repeats

The rDNA repeats in S. cerevisiae occur in a single cluster of 150 to 200 copies of a 9.1 kb sequence located on chromosome XII. Each repeat contains an RDN37 gene that is transcribed by Pol I to produce a 35S transcript that is processed to yield the 5.8S, 25S and 18S rRNAs. The intergenic region contains a 5S rRNA gene and the bidirectional E-pro promoter. Active repeats are heavily nucleosome-depleted, whereas inactive repeats are nucleosomal; about half of the repeats are active in log phase cells10–12.

Since sir2∆ cells have many fewer rDNA repeats than wild type cells34, we estimated the rDNA copy number in our strains by measuring the relative rDNA coverage (Supplementary Fig. S5). Our wild type cells have ~ 150 rDNA repeats as expected, as do sir1∆ cells, whereas sir2∆ and sir3∆ cells have only about half as many repeats. One sir4∆ clone has wild-type copy number, but the other has only about half the number of copies, similar to sir2∆ and sir3∆. These data confirm that sir2∆ cells tend to lose rDNA repeats and show that this is also true for sir3∆ cells and, perhaps to a lesser extent, for sir4∆ cells. These copy number differences do not affect the analysis below, since we are comparing individual CG site methylation rates or individual reads with two rDNA copies.

In wild-type cells, the rDNA repeats are methylated 1.6 (replicate: 1.7) times faster than the genomic median (Fig. 3a, e). A potential explanation is that the active rDNA repeats are methylated faster than the inactive repeats because they are nucleosome-depleted, resulting in a faster methylation rate than the genomic median. The rDNA methylation rate in sir1∆ cells is very similar to wild type, indicating that Sir1 does not affect rDNA accessibility. In contrast, in sir2∆ and sir3∆ cells, rDNA is methylated faster than in wild-type cells, at 2.1 to 2.4 times the genomic median (Fig. 3a, e). Loss of Sir4 has little or no effect on rDNA methylation rate (Fig. 3a, e). This observation suggests that both Sir2 and Sir3 impede access to rDNA, but Sir4 does not.

Moreover, when artificially recruited in a dose-dependent manner, Sir1 can attenuate the expression of genes that are strong enough to overwhelm transcriptional repression at a silent locus39. We observe that the methylation rate at HMLα is unaffected in sir1∆ cells, except for the silencers, whereas HMRa is methylated somewhat faster than in wild type cells. This is consistent with the fact that HMRa is de-repressed in a higher proportion of sir1∆ cells than HMLα30,32. However, we note that we are measuring relative accessibility, which may not relate directly to expression data30.

In summary, it seems unlikely that steric occlusion is the primary mechanism through which transcriptional silencing is achieved, since silenced DNA is still accessible to methyltransferases in living cells, albeit at a slower rate than elsewhere in the genome. A potential caveat is that protein complexes larger than Dam or M.SssI might be occluded from silent chromatin, such as Pol II and TFIID. Sir protein mobility in vivo could explain how Sir proteins impede, but not prevent, access to silenced DNA3,49. Slower nucleosome mobility may hinder transcription, although it is unlikely to result in efficient silencing by itself. Other potential contributing factors to transcriptional silencing3 include: (1) Sir2-mediated histone deacetylation increasing the affinity of histone tail domains for DNA; (2) inhibition of an ATP-dependent remodeling activity by histone deacetylation; and (3) putative formation of Sir-mediated condensed chromatin loops. Such loops might involve local interactions between elements defined by Sir2 peaks and/or long-range Sir-dependent interactions between HMLα and HMRa and with telomeres41,47,50,51. We propose that the Sir proteins define chromatin domains in which nucleosome dynamics are reduced, but not eliminated, resulting in slower methylation than elsewhere in the genome.

SDS PAGE and Western blotting

Cell pellets were resuspended in NuPAGE lithium dodecyl sulfate buffer (Invitrogen NP0007) with 20 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (5 OD600 units of cells per 100 µL) and heated at 99 °C for 5 min. Samples were centrifuged at 9,400 g; the supernatant was stored at −20 °C. For SDS PAGE, samples were thawed and heated at 70 °C for 10 min before loading into two 4–12% Bis-Tris NuPAGE Midi Gels (Invitrogen WG1403BOX) in an XCell4 Surelock Midi-Cell (Invitrogen WR0100) and run with NuPAGE MOPS SDS Running Buffer (Invitrogen NP0001) as per manufacturer instructions. One gel was used for Coomassie staining; the other gel was transferred to a PVDF membrane with the iBlot Dry Blotting System (Invitrogen IB1001) with regular sized transfer stacks (Invitrogen IB401001) per manufacturer instructions. To detect HA, the membrane was placed in a hybridization tube and blocked with 20 mL 5% skim milk in TBST (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 0.5 M NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20) with rotation at room temperature for 1 h, followed by hybridization in 10 mL 1:5,000 dilution of an HRP-conjugated α-HA antibody (Roche F310) in 5% skim milk TBST blocking buffer with rotation at 4 °C overnight. The membrane was washed three times with 20 mL TBST with rotation at room temperature for 10 min, then incubated with SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Scientific 34577) using 2 mL of a 1:1 mixture of the substrate and stable peroxide components from the kit at room temperature for 5 min to develop. The membrane was imaged for HA using the Azure 600 Chemiluminescent Western Blot Imager (Azure Biosystems AZI600). To detect tubulin, the membrane was returned to a hybridization tube and stripped with 20 mL PBST (phosphate buffered saline, 0.1% Tween 20) with rotation at room temperature for 1 h and blocked with 20 mL 5% skim milk in PBST with rotation at room temperature for 1 h. The membrane was hybridized in 5 mL 1:20,000 dilution of an HRP-conjugated α-tubulin antibody (Abcam ab185067) in 5% skim milk PBST blocking buffer with rotation at room temperature for 1 h, followed by three 15 mL PBST washes with rotation at room temperature for 10 min each. The membrane was developed and imaged for tubulin as above.
