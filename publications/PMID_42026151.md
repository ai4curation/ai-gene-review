The orderly architecture and energy supply of mitochondria, orchestrated by
proteins, are essential for oocyte maturation. Acylglycerol kinase (AGK) is
known to predominantly localize to the mitochondrial membrane and regulate
multiple cellular processes related to Sengers syndrome and tumorigenesis.
However, the functions of AGK in the ovaries remain largely unknown. Here, we
generate mice with oocyte-specific disruption of Agk. Ablation of Agk in oocytes
causes ovarian atrophy characterized by the arrest of early secondary follicles
and a significantly reduced number of mature follicles, ultimately leading to
female sterility. Furthermore, mitochondria in Agk-deficient oocytes exhibit
significant structural and organizational defects. The developmental competence
of Agk-deficient oocytes is compromised with decreased mitochondrial membrane
potential, reduced mtDNA copy number, impaired ATP synthesis, and elevated
reactive oxygen species. Mechanistically, single-cell RNA sequencing shows that
genes associated with the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)
pathway are among the most significantly downregulated in Agk-disrupted oocytes.
Spatial metabolomics further identifies a substantial reduction in
phosphate-associated metabolites linked to the OXPHOS pathway in Agk-deficient
oocytes. These findings demonstrate that AGK plays an essential role in oocyte
development and folliculogenesis by maintaining mitochondrial function. The
present study identifies AGK as a potential diagnostic marker and therapeutic
target for female infertility.

Introduction

The quality of follicle development in the ovary is a primary determinant of successful pregnancy outcomes in both natural and assisted reproduction. Ovarian follicles progress through primordial, primary, secondary, and antral follicle stages1. Secondary follicles are further subdivided into early and late stages based on the number of granulosa cell layers2. The developmental continuum from primordial follicles to antral follicles undergoes substantial cellular and molecular reprogramming, including organelle restructuring, protein turnover, and modification1. Critically, morphological and functional transformations of mitochondria are key determinants of oocyte developmental competence throughout folliculogenesis3. Emerging evidence has demonstrated that mitochondria play a crucial role in oocyte development by supplying ATP through the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) system4,5.

Mitochondria possess their own unique DNA, known as mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). The mtDNA is a double-stranded circular DNA with a length of 16.6 kb and contains 37 genes which encode 13 OXPHOS complex subunits, along with 22 transfer RNAs and 2 ribosomal RNAs required for intramitochondrial protein synthesis6. Oogenesis begins with approximately 200 copies of mtDNA in each primordial germ cell, which undergoes dynamic expansion to reach 50,000 to 550,000 copies in mature mouse and human oocytes, albeit with considerable variability across developmental stages7. Compared to somatic cells, oocytes contain a higher abundance of mitochondria, which are characteristically smaller and rounder8. These mitochondria transition from a circular shape in primary oocytes to a more elongated, oval form upon maturation3. Consequently, defects in mitochondrial structure, shape, number, mtDNA copy number, and energy synthesis in oocytes can lead to follicle atresia, oocyte maturation disturbance, and female infertility9. Indeed, mitochondrial dysfunction in oocytes, often arising from specific gene mutants, has been directly linked to such pathologies. For instance, oocyte-specific deletion of genes crucial for mitochondrial structural dynamics, such as mitofusin 1 (Mfn1) and dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1, fission), disrupts mitochondrial architecture and leads to female sterility or subfertility, respectively10,11. Similarly, mutations in genes essential for OXPHOS complex assembly (COX15) or the mitochondrial stress response (Clpp) result in severe oocyte developmental defects and infertility12,13. Notably, human mutations in CLPP are also associated with premature ovarian insufficiency14. These examples underscore the critical dependence of female fertility on a diverse set of mitochondria-associated proteins.

Acylglycerol kinase (AGK) is an evolutionarily conserved enzyme, exhibiting 96% sequence homology between rodents and humans15. It is widely expressed in tissues including the heart, muscles, kidneys, and brain15,16. AGK is also frequently overexpressed in various malignancies, such as uterine, cervical, esophageal, gastric, prostate, and breast cancer16–18, implicating its important role in tumorigenesis. In somatic cells, AGK localizes to mitochondria and phosphorylates monoacylglycerol (MAG) and diacylglycerol (DAG) to generate phosphatidic acid (PA) and lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), respectively19. Furthermore, independent of its kinase activity, AGK has been shown to be essential for the assembly and function of the mitochondrial inner membrane TIM22 complex, a process critical for mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and cell proliferation20,21. In addition, extramitochondrial AGK participates in glycolysis of CD8 T cells and platelet formation by interacting with PTEN or JAK2, respectively15,22. The mechanism of mitochondrial or non-mitochondrial AGK action, as well as its enzymatic and non-enzymatic activities, is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1. However, the expression profile and physiological functions of AGK in the reproductive system remain uncharacterized.

In this study, we generated an oocyte-specific Agk knockout (Agkflox/flox; Dppa3-Cre) mouse model to elucidate the role of AGK in female fertility. Our results demonstrate that oocyte-specific ablation of Agk results in female infertility due to defective folliculogenesis characterized by arrest at the early secondary follicle stage and severe mitochondrial dysfunction. These findings identify AGK as a critical regulator of oocyte development and establish it as a potential diagnostic marker and therapeutic target for female infertility.

A critical consequence of electron transport chain (ETC) dysfunction is the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are known to induce follicular arrest and impair fertilization when present in excess10,39,40. Most ROS is produced by the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) I and III complexes41. Spatial metabolomicsThe spatial metabolome was performed according to the steps provided by Applied Protein Technology Company (Shanghai, China). In brief, the post-collection ovarian tissue of 4-week-old mice was rapidly embedded in carboxymethylcellulose sodium salt (CMC) and sectioned into frozen slides with 10 μm thickness. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization-mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI-MSI) was performed by TimsTOF fleX MADLI 2 (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) to generate spatially resolved metabolic profiles with a focus on compounds under 1000 Da and lipids. The mass spectrometer was calibrated and set to scan a predefined mass range, capturing a broad spectrum of metabolites with a specified spatial resolution of 20 µm. Raw data underwent processing for spatial variation correction, baseline subtraction, and normalization using specialized software, with metabolite identifications confirmed against reference libraries or standards. For oocyte metabolic analysis, we circle each oocyte of the early secondary follicle and extract data from this region. Statistical analyses, including t-tests and ANOVA, were conducted to identify significant differences in metabolite levels. The web server for metabolomic data analysis, MetaboAnalyst (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca), was used to analyze metabolomics data. Spatial metabolomics data are available in the METASPACE under this link: https://metaspace2020.org/project/Agk-mouse.
