Mammalian opsin 3 (OPN3) is a member of the opsin family of G-protein-coupled
receptors with ambiguous light sensitivity. OPN3 was first identified in the
brain (and named encephalopsin) and subsequently found to be expressed in other
tissues. In adipocytes, OPN3 is necessary for light responses that modulate
lipolysis and glucose uptake, while OPN3 in human skin melanocytes regulates
pigmentation in a light-independent manner. Despite its initial discovery in the
brain, OPN3 functional mechanisms in the brain remain elusive. Here, we
investigated the molecular mechanism of OPN3 function in the paraventricular
nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus. We show that Opn3 is coexpressed with the
melanocortin 4 receptor (Mc4r) in a population of PVN neurons, where it
negatively regulates MC4R-mediated cAMP signaling in a specific and
Gαi/o-dependent manner. Under baseline conditions, OPN3 via Gαi/o potentiates
the activity of the inward rectifying Kir7.1 channel, previously shown to be
closed in response to agonist-mediated activation of MC4R in a Gαs-independent
manner. In mice, we found that Opn3 in Mc4r-expressing neurons regulates food
consumption. Our results reveal the first mechanistic insight into OPN3 function
in the hypothalamus, uncovering a unique mechanism by which OPN3 functions to
potentiate Kir7.1 activity and negatively regulate MC4R-mediated cAMP signaling,
thereby promoting food intake.

## Full Text

The G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are a large group of seven-transmembrane domain proteins that transduce extracellular stimuli into intracellular responses (1). The opsins are a subfamily of GPCRs that are activated by photons, thus providing a means for detection of light information. The opsin family includes opsin 3 (OPN3) (encephalopsin), OPN4 (melanopsin), and OPN5 (neuropsin), that have been implicated in nonvisual signaling both inside and outside the eye (2). OPN4, originally found in frog melanophores (3), was the first nonvisual photosensor identified in mammals (4, 5). OPN4 functions in circadian clock photoentrainment (5), as an irradiance detector for visual circuits (6), in regulation of mood and learning (7), and in synaptic development in the brain (8). Developmentally, OPN4 and OPN5 (also expressed in retinal ganglion cells (RGCs)) can function as deep tissue photosensors to regulate ocular blood vessel development prior to eye opening (9, 10). Further, light stimulation of hypothalamic OPN5 regulates the body temperature cycle (11). Finally, OPN5 in melanocyte precursors is a light sensor for circadian clock photoentrainment in the skin (12).

The Opn3 gene is expressed throughout the mouse brain including in the preoptic area and the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) 13–16. OPN3 modifies the acoustic startle reflex and increases its expression in hypothalamic magnocellular neurons in response to osmotic stimuli (17, 18). OPN3 has a mixed record as a light sensor. For example, OPN3 expressed in white adipocytes is necessary for light-dependent regulation of adipocyte lipolysis and glucose uptake (19, 20). Mouse and human adipocytes also show a light-dependent electrophysiological response (21). By contrast, OPN3 has been shown to function in a light-independent manner in melanogenesis (22). Purified human OPN3, although able to bind the chromophore 11-cis-retinal, does not absorb light in the visible spectrum (22). It remains possible that OPN3 functions as a light sensor only in some settings, perhaps dependent on complex formation. It also remains possible that OPN3 may have nonphoton ligands. For example, in Drosophila, opsins can be directly activated by bitter tastants (23) and can function in temperature discrimination (24). This establishes that opsins can be multifunctional GPCRs (25, 26).

OPN3 signals via Gαi/o to decrease intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels (22, 27, 28). Many GPCRs have constitutive activity and show G-protein-dependent signaling in the absence of an agonist (29, 30). This constitutive activity can partly depend on the intracellular and molecular environment (30). The light-independent functions of OPN3 are likely mediated by its constitutive activity (22). For example, in melanocytes, OPN3 acts as a negative regulator of melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R), a Gαs-coupled receptor responsible for melanin production. This light-independent function is abolished when OPN3 is downregulated or when the Gαi/o subunit that mediates OPN3 signaling is blocked by pertussis toxin (PTx) (22), suggesting that the constitutive activity is responsible for this OPN3 function. Interestingly, MC1R also has high constitutive activity (31–33) that is further increased by the α-melanocyte stimulating hormone (αMSH) agonist.

The current study was motivated by the observation that Opn3 null mice eat less (19) and that OPN3 is expressed in neurons of the PVN (13–15), a nucleus that regulates energy balance and food consumption through the activity of melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R) (34–39). MC4R has high homology to MC1R and, although it has constitutive activity (29, 40, 41), αMSH-mediated activation of MC4R via Gαs further stimulates adenylyl cyclase, thus increasing intracellular cAMP levels (42, 43). More recently, agonist-mediated activation of MC4R was found to close the inward rectifying potassium channel Kir7.1 in a G-protein-independent manner, thereby increasing neuronal firing activity (44, 45).

We investigated the functional interaction between OPN3 and MC4R, and the signaling mechanisms mediating the function of OPN3 in the regulation of food consumption. We show that Opn3 and Mc4r are coexpressed in several neuronal populations related to food consumption, including those of the PVN in which we also detect Kcnj13 (encoding Kir7.1). We show that constitutive OPN3 activity regulates MC4R-mediated cAMP signaling and that Opn3 in Mc4r-expressing neurons of the PVN regulates neuronal firing activity. We also demonstrate that OPN3 can form a tripartite complex with MC4R and Kir7.1 and potentiates Kir7.1 activity. Finally, using behavioral analyses, we reveal that Opn3 in Mc4r-expressing neurons regulates food intake in mice. These data provide a novel function for OPN3 in the regulation of MC4R activity and downstream feeding behavior.

OPN3 Potentiates Kir7.1 Channel Opening.

Activation of MC4R not only initiates an increase in cAMP production but also increases the firing rate of PVN neurons in the hypothalamus (43, 44), in part by closing Kir7.1 channels (encoded by Kcnj13) in a G-protein-independent manner (44). Because OPN3 regulates G-protein-mediated MC4R signaling, we wondered whether OPN3 could also regulate the G-protein-independent, MC4R-dependent closing of Kir7.1 channels. We first investigated whether Opn3 and Kcnj13 transcripts have overlapping expression in mouse PVN neurons. We used FISH to detect transcripts for Opn3, Mc4r, and Kcnj13 after validation of the Kcnj13 probe (Fig. 4 A–F and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). We found that ~53% of the Opn3+ cells were Kcnj13+ and ~15% of Opn3+ cells also expressed Kcnj13+ and Mc4r+ (Fig. 4 G and H). These data suggest that a significant number of PVN neurons express both Opn3 and Kcnj13 and a fraction of these also express Mc4r.

To determine whether OPN3 could modulate MC4R-mediated changes in firing activity of neurons, we conditionally deleted Opn3 from Mc4r-expressing neurons (33) (Mc4r2a-Cre; Opn3fl/fl mice) and recorded from neurons in PVN brain slices (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). Mc4r-expressing neurons were identified through activation of the Ai14 allele that provides Cre-dependent expression of tdTomato. Interestingly, the spontaneous firing rate of Mc4r2a-Cre; Ai14 PVN neurons was higher in Mc4r2a-Cre; Opn3fl/fl slices compared with Opn3+/+ slices (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). We could not detect a significant difference in the membrane potential of these neurons (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). This result prompted us to investigate whether at the cellular level OPN3 might regulate MC4R modulation of Kir7.1 channels. To assess plasma membrane potassium flux in GT1-7 cells, we optimized the live-cell fluorescence-based thallium (Tl+) flux assay (56–58) to estimate the open K+ channels in individual cells. The initial slope of the Tl+-mediated fluorescence increase is a close measure of the open K+ channels when Tl+ is added (Fig. 5 A and B). We first confirmed that GT1-7 expressed Kcnj13 mRNA and that MTII activation of MC4R led to a decrease in the initial slope of Tl+ flux responses (as expected for MC4R-mediated closing of Kir7.1 channels) (Fig. 5C and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). Kir7.1 channels exhibit unique properties for a potassium channel, in particular, Kir7.1 is insensitive to tetraethylammonium, a blocker of potassium channels (59). To determine the contribution of Kir7.1 to the total Tl+ flux, we used a specific Kir7.1 channel blocker, VU590 (60). We found that the addition of VU590 decreased the flux response to a larger extent than MTII stimulation (Fig. 5C). Additionally, MTII did not further decrease the flux response when added together with VU590 compared to VU590 alone (Fig. 5C). This suggests that MTII results in the closure of only a fraction of the Kir7.1 channels.

Opn3 in Mc4r-Expressing Neurons Promotes Food Consumption.MC4R signaling is crucial for the regulation of food intake and metabolic homeostasis (34–39), and is mediated in part through Kir7.1 (44, 45, 62). Since OPN3 could form complexes and regulate the activity of both MC4R and Kir7.1, we wondered whether its activity in PVN neurons might be required for normal regulation of energy balance. To assess this, we used indirect calorimetry and metabolic cage analysis to define the phenotype of mice in which Opn3 was deleted conditionally in neurons that express Mc4r. Mc4r2a-Cre has been established as a Cre recombinase driver for the MC4R agonist, αMSH, and inverse agonist, agouti-related peptide, responsive neurons of the PVN (37). This allele expresses Cre from the 3′ untranslated region and it is designed to retain the function of MC4R (37) but has been noted to increase food consumption and body weight. In part to validate the Mc4r2a-Cre allele, it was combined with the Opn3fl (19) to generate single and double heterozygotes. Analysis of food consumption in these mice showed that the Mc4r2a-cre cohort have elevated food consumption compared with Opn3fl/+ control. Consistent with this, the Mc4r2a-Cre cohort showed increased body weight (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). The increased food consumption of Mc4r2a-cre mice was attributable to increased food intake during the dark phase (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 C and D). This analysis confirms that the Mc4r2a-cre allele produces a phenotype, probably by compromising MC4R expression. Despite this, the Mc4r2a-Cre; Opn3fl/+ conditional heterozygotes showed reduced food consumption compared with both the Opn3fl/+ and Mc4r2a-Cre controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Mc4r2a-Cre has been established as a Cre recombinase driver for the MC4R agonist, αMSH, and inverse agonist, agouti-related peptide, responsive neurons of the PVN (37). This allele expresses Cre from the 3′ untranslated region and it is designed to retain the function of MC4R (37) but has been noted to increase food consumption and body weight. In part to validate the Mc4r2a-Cre allele, it was combined with the Opn3fl (19) to generate single and double heterozygotes. Analysis of food consumption in these mice showed that the Mc4r2a-cre cohort have elevated food consumption compared with Opn3fl/+ control. Consistent with this, the Mc4r2a-Cre cohort showed increased body weight (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). The increased food consumption of Mc4r2a-cre mice was attributable to increased food intake during the dark phase (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 C and D). This analysis confirms that the Mc4r2a-cre allele produces a phenotype, probably by compromising MC4R expression. Despite this, the Mc4r2a-Cre; Opn3fl/+ conditional heterozygotes showed reduced food consumption compared with both the Opn3fl/+ and Mc4r2a-Cre controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). This is consistent with OPN3 activity promoting food consumption.

In a separate analysis, we generated cohorts of Opn3fl/+ control mice as well as the experimental genotypes Mc4r2a-Cre; Opn3fl/+ and Mc4r2a-Cre; Opn3fl/fl. We observed no significant differences between these genotypes for O2 consumption, CO2 expiration, energy expenditure, water consumption, or respiratory quotient (a measure of substrate utilization) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). However, we did detect significant differences in food intake and locomotor activity. Notably, both heterozygous and homozygous conditional mutants showed a cumulative 72 h food intake that was lower than in Opn3fl/+ control mice (Fig. 7A). Although the average 12 h food intake during light and dark phases was not significantly different (Fig. 7 B and C), the average 24 h cumulative food intake was lower for the Mc4r2a-Cre; Opn3fl/fl and Mc4r2a-Cre; Opn3fl/+ mice compared to Opn3fl/+ control mice (Fig. 7D).

In addition, the locomotor activity of Mc4r2a-Cre; Opn3fl/fl and Mc4r2a-Cre; Opn3fl/+ mice was significantly reduced compared with Opn3fl/+ control mice (Fig. 7E) (63). The reduced locomotor activity may help explain why the lower food intake of Opn3 mutant mice did not result in lower body weight (Fig. 7F) or reduced energy expenditure (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). We also noticed that heterozygous Opn3 conditional deletion mice show a phenotype that is either more robust than (72 h food consumption) or equal to (24 h food intake and locomotor activity) the phenotype of the Opn3 homozygous conditional mutant (Fig. 7 A, D, and E). Haploinsufficiency phenotypes have been noted for other genes involved in the regulation of food consumption, including for Mc4r (34), but we do not currently have an explanation for the more robust effect of heterozygous Opn3 deletion. Taken together, these data indicate that in MC4R neurons, the activity of OPN3 promotes food consumption.

OPN3 Forms a Complex with and Negatively Regulates MC4R.

Opn3 is expressed in the PVN (13, 14, 16), where it overlaps significantly with Mc4r expression. Opn3 is expressed in anterior and posterior PVN, but with significantly more Opn3-Mc4r coexpressing cells in posterior PVN. Recently, MC4R was found to be localized to the primary cilium of PVN neurons. This localization was also required for the function of MC4R in regulation of food intake and body weight (67, 68). Whether OPN3 is also within primary cilia and regulates the activity of MC4R in this compartment remains to be determined.

We dissected OPN3 signaling mechanisms in model hypothalamic neurons using live-cell imaging. We showed that OPN3 negatively regulates MC4R-mediated cAMP signaling in a Gαi/o-dependent manner. Reducing or increasing OPN3 expression led to respective augmentation or reduction of endogenous MC4R-mediated cAMP responses, an effect that was not due to changes in Mc4r expression. Furthermore, OPN3 regulation of cAMP signaling was specific and did not occur for another endogenous Gαs-coupled receptor, the β1-AR. This specific effect can be explained by the formation of OPN3-MC4R molecular complexes in conjunction with spatially restricted cAMP signaling (69). MC4R-evoked cAMP signaling has been shown to be the main driver of excitability in these neurons (39). Our results suggest that MC4R-evoked cAMP signaling is not only controlled by αMSH from proopiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons, but also intracellularly by OPN3-mediated tuning of baseline cAMP concentration.

OPN3 modulation of MC4R-evoked cAMP signaling was eliminated when cells were treated with PTx, a Gαi/o inhibitor (70–72). Treatment with PTx had a similar effect as OPN3 KD on MC4R-evoked cAMP signaling, suggesting that in these neurons OPN3 is the main Gαi/o-coupled receptor with high constitutive activity. Constitutive activity is common for the class A GPCRs. Notably, MC4R also has high constitutive activity, as shown by the use of inverse agonists (40, 41). This supports our conclusion that under baseline conditions and in the absence of MC4R agonists or inverse agonists the constitutive activity of OPN3-Gαi/o partially suppresses constitutive activity of MC4R-Gαs to create an intermediate level of cAMP at rest, which indirectly controls the amplitude of MC4R-evoked cAMP signaling and thus the neuronal responses to αMSH.

These mice show reduced food intake, consistent with a physiological role for OPN3 in promoting food consumption. The reduced locomotor activity of the conditional Opn3 null mice may explain why energy expenditure and body weight are unchanged. Notably, both Opn3 conditional heterozygotes and homozygotes show reduced food intake and locomotor activity. The haploinsufficiency of OPN3 in PVN neurons may be explained by a food consumption regulation mechanism that functions more like a rheostat than an on–off switch. Notably, Mc4r mutant mice also show haploinsufficiency (34). The observation that Mc4r2a-cre driven conditional Opn3 heterozygotes show a more robust phenotype that the homozygotes is less easily explained but may be a result of developmental adaptation in the homozygotes.

POMC neurons in the arcuate nucleus project to the PVN and release αMSH, which activates glutamatergic MC4R neurons of the PVN to increase their firing activity and regulated food consumption (34–39, 44, 64–66). The MC4R neurons of the PVN project to multiple brain nuclei to regulate anorectic behavior (36, 37, 66). The deletion of Opn3 in PVN neurons results in enhanced spontaneous firing rate, potentially due to reduced Kir7.1 activity, and also in augmented MC4R-evoked cAMP signaling, shown to promote satiety (39) (Fig. 8). Our findings suggest that OPN3 regulates food consumption via a basal, constitutive activity.

The current analysis does not resolve the question of whether OPN3 can function as a light sensor in the mouse brain. Previously we did not detect light responses in OPN3 expressing epidermal melanocytes, another setting in which OPN3 functions together with a melanocortin family receptor (22), consistent with the current analysis using model hypothalamic neurons. Despite this, it remains possible that primary neurons that express OPN3 would respond differently. This question is especially interesting given the OPN3-dependent light sensing that has been shown for white adipocytes (19, 20) and the function of the related OPN5 as a deep brain photosensor (11, 80). It is challenging to address the question of OPN3 light sensing in the hypothalamus of mice because OPN3, OPN4, and OPN5 have overlapping absorption spectra, Opn3 and Opn5 hypothalamic neurons are intermingled, and all three regulate some aspect of energy homeostasis (11, 19, 20, 81). The question of whether OPN3 functions as a photon sensor in mouse brain neurons remains be addressed in future studies.

ConclusionAlthough OPN3 was first discovered in the mouse brain (16), its signaling mechanism and function have only been characterized in other tissues, including melanin synthesis in skin melanocytes and regulation of body temperature and glucose homeostasis via adipocytes (19, 20, 22). Our results identify the first function for OPN3 in the neurons of the PVN and define the underlying molecular mechanism. We show that Gαi/o-dependent constitutive activity of OPN3 has a dual action: It negatively regulates MC4R-evoked cAMP signaling and potentiates Kir7.1. Consistent with a function in PVN neurons, we show that Opn3 conditional mutant mice exhibit decreased food consumption. Our data provide new insights into the role of OPN3 in the hypothalamus and reveal a new mechanism of action for an opsin receptor and more broadly for GPCRs.
