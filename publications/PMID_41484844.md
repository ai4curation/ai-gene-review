BACKGROUND: Tropical maize germplasm offers a valuable resource of genetic
diversity for temperate maize breeding programs but its utilization is often
constrained by poor flowering adaptation under long-day photoperiods, which act
as a major adaptive barrier in temperate environments. RESULTS: To dissect the
genetic basis of this adaptive barrier, a multi-parent population (MPP) was
developed using an elite temperate inbred (Ye107) and three tropical inbred
lines exhibiting strong flowering delays under long-day photoperiods. Using
genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS), a total of 579,346 high-quality SNPs were
identified, and flowering-time related traits were evaluated across multiple
environments. By integrating a joint-mapping strategy of genome-wide association
study (GWAS) and linkage analysis, two high-confidence candidate genes were
identified on chromosome 3: Zm00001d042884, encoding an acyl-CoA oxidase that
likely regulates days to tasseling by affecting jasmonic acid (JA) biosynthesis
and Zm00001d042961, encoding a respiratory burst oxidase homolog (RBOHH) that
potentially modulates reactive oxygen species (ROS) signalling during
reproductive development. CONCLUSIONS: We suggest that these genes operate
within a bipartite regulatory framework whereby Zm00001d042884 may act as an
upstream module for floral initiation and Zm00001d042961 as a downstream module
for reproductive assurance, controlling pollen viability through ROS (reactive
oxygen species) signaling after floral transition. The information presented
here reveals previously unrecognized functional potential for JA and ROS
signaling pathways regulating maize flowering adaptation, extending the present
understanding of flowering regulation, traditionally based on signal perception;
signal transduction, to inclusively involve a second layer of physiological
regulation for reproductive assurance. Furthermore, the positive early-flowering
haplotypes identified provide significant genetic targets to overcome the poor
flowering adaptation of tropical germplasm under long day conditions through
molecular design breeding approaches, thus accelerating the utilization and
exploitation of elite trait resources from tropical germplasm in temperate maize
improvement program.

Over the past decades, the power of genetic and genomic tools has revealed a great deal about the complexity of flowering control in maize [10]. A canonical “decision-making” pathway governed by a small number of major-effect genes e.g. ZmCCT, which is a powerful floral repressor under non-inductive long days [11, 12] and the florigen ZCN8 encoding for a mobile product which transmits the signal from the leaves to the shoot apical meristem to commence flowering [13, 14]. This well-characterised transcriptional cascade gives rise to a central regulatory module by which the precise timing of floral commitment is determined in maize.

Nevertheless, our understanding of the regulation of flowering time is not yet complete, and there are still gaps in our knowledge. The present models are largely focused on the upstream “decision-making” mechanisms [15–18]. However, a robust “physiological execution” of the floral signal is also important if the reproductive cycle is to be successfully completed. This downstream phase involves complex metabolic and cellular responses that ensure the development of the reproductive organs and the viability of gametes (e.g. pollen), and it is a prerequisite for establishing the flowering decision in yield [19, 20]. However, the genetic basis of this execution phase, especially the signalling pathways that ensure reproductive success, is still largely unexplored. This is an important gap in our knowledge, especially for the adaptation of tropical germplasm, where maladaptation may arise not only from delayed flowering initiation but also frrom impaired downstream reproductive processes. Moreover, classical biparental QTL mapping and GWAS approaches suffer from limited resolution and are confounded by the complex population structure present in diverse germplasm, which further restricts the power to detect novel and more subtle regulatory loci associated with this execution stage [21, 22].

To address this major deficiency in the current literature, we postulated that a distinct downstream regulatory layer controlled by metabolic signalling pathways is critical for reproductive success and is therefore likely to be a component of the adaptive significance of flowering time itself. In particular, we suggest that pathways involving signalling by jasmonic acid (JA) or by reactive oxygen species (ROS) which are already known to be involved in stress responses and developmental regulation may constitute the overlooked “execution module” we were seeking to elucidate. Thus, the principal aim of the present study was to dissect the genetic architecture of flowering time within a framework capable of identifying these novel regulatory layers. To achieve this, we constructed a strategic multi-parent population (MPP) that encompasses elite temperate and tropical genetic backgrounds and used a high-resolution combined linkage-association mapping approach. The specific aims of this study were to identify novel genetic loci controlling flowering adaptation and to characterize genes which, by their function, appear to be involved in the downstream metabolic and signalling pathways that constitute the “execution” phase of flowering.

This research advances the scientific frontiers in two major ways. First, it moves beyond the established paradigm of transcriptional decision-making by providing direct genetic evidence for a new, functionally separable layer of flowering-time regulation centred on metabolic signalling and physiological assurance. Second, by identifying certain genes and favourable haplotypes that form such modular execution pathways, it offers concrete molecular tools for more sophisticated precision breeding. These results set the stage for greater adaptability of tropical germplasm and broaden the genetic basis of temperate maize breeding programs.

Materials and methodsPlant materials and field designThe elite inbred line Ye107, belonging to Reid heterotic group and widely used in Chinese maize breeding, was selected as the male parent. Three elite tropical inbred lines, CML373, CML395, and YML46, with broad genetic base and diverse genetic backgrounds (Table 1), were used as female parents. Among these, Ye107 was developed by Shandong Denghai Seed Industry Co., Ltd. in China. CML373 and CML395 were introduced from CIMMYT. YML46 was developed by the Institute of Food Crops, Yunnan Academy of Agricultural Sciences, China. These female parental lines were crossed with Ye107 to produce F1 hybrids, and three F9 recombinant inbred line (RIL) sub-populations were subsequently developed through eight generations of selfing using the single-seed descent method (Fig. 1). In total, three sub-populations comprised 541 RILs, including 176 in Pop1 (CML373 × Ye107), 204 in Pop2 (CML395 × Ye107), and 161 in Pop3 (YML46 × Ye107). All RILs were evaluated in the spring of 2023 at three experimental sites in Yunnan Province, China: Dehong City (23DH) (24º43’N, 98º58’E, altitude: 900 m), Baoshan City (23BS) (25º12’N, 99º17’E, altitude: 1700 m ), and Yanshan County (23YS) (23º59’N, 104º33’E, altitude: 1500 m). The experiments followed a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications at each location. Each plot was 2.5 m long with a plant spacing of 25 cm, row spacing of 0.7 m, and 14 plants per row.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and field designThe elite inbred line Ye107, belonging to Reid heterotic group and widely used in Chinese maize breeding, was selected as the male parent. Three elite tropical inbred lines, CML373, CML395, and YML46, with broad genetic base and diverse genetic backgrounds (Table 1), were used as female parents. The parameter r², ranging from 0 to 1, measures the intensity of the non-random association of alleles at two loci. The closer r² is to 1, the stronger the linkage disequilibrium between them. The rate of LD decay, which reflects the rate at which LD declines with increasing physical distance, is a critically important parameter for determining the marker density required for GWAS and is directly related to the power of detection and the mapping resolution of GWAS [33].

Zm00001d042884 encodes an Acyl-CoA oxidase (ACX), a key rate limiting enzyme in the JA biosynthesis pathway [44]. JA is well known as a defense hormone, but is also crucial in plant development, especially in the formation of male reproductive organs [45–47]. Jasmonic acid-defective mutants are characterized by male sterility [48]. He et al. indicated that JA is indispensable for pollen biogenesis and fertility control [49]. Interestingly, phenotypes of JA-defective mutant can be counteracted by re-conversion of JA [49]. Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that Zm00001d042884 participates in fatty acid metabolism, consistent with its enzymatic role in JA biosynthesis. Previous studies showed that impairment of the ACX gene family is necessary for maintaining male reproductive capability, and its disruption causes severe abnormalities in maize [50]. Based on these findings, we propose that allelic variations in Zm00001d042884 lead to varying efficiencies in JA biosynthesis, thus affecting hormone status in the tassel primordium, varying tassel morphogenesis, and eventually influencing days-to-tasselling in maize. The balance of ROS signaling in plant sexual reproduction processes has been shown to play an essential role in the proper degradation of the tapetum, as well as in pollen development and pollen tube germination [56, 57]. Lassig et al. demonstrated in Arabidopsis thaliana that homozygous double mutants of NAD(P)H oxidases RBOHH and RBOHJ exhibit impaired pollen tube growth and sterility due to low ROS signaling [58]. Interestingly, in our study, Zm00001d042961 exhibited dynamic expression correlated with the flowering period. Numerous studies of diverse populations have mapped QTLs for DTT, DTS, and ASI to this chromosome which gives increasing weight to this chromosome, emphasizing its importance in flowering adaption in maize [60–62]. In the current study, the strongest signals for the JA and ROS pathway genes Zm00001d042884 and Zm00001d042961 were located approximately at 182.8 Mb and 184.9 Mb, respectively. These loci define previously uncharacterized regions within this well-studied flowering-time hotspot. In contrast, earlier QTL studies identified large genomic intervals containing hundreds of genes, making it difficult to identify causal genes, a situation sometimes referred to in the literature as the “QTL black box” problem [21, 63].Modern genetics methods, including fine-mapping and multi-parent population (MPP) designs are increasingly used to overcome this limitation. For example, the classical Vgt1 locus was ultimately resolved to a non-coding regulatory element by fine mapping [14]. The MPP design allowed greater mapping resolution and allelic diversity [64], while integrated GWAS and QTL mapping methods provided the opportunity a robust framework for identifying high-confidence candidate genes [65, 66]. By utilising these advanced mapping methodologies in conjunction with functional annotation and pathway analysis, we resolved a previously ambiguous hotspot into two functionally distinct candidate genes that are mechanistically supported. This transition, from statistical correlation to biological causation, represents a significant step forward in our deconstruction of the molecular basis of flowering time adaption in maize.The role of the jasmonic acid (JA) pathway in male reproduction: from “defense hormone” to “developmental regulator"The crucial role of JA in male reproductive development is now firmly established [50]. The classical tasselseed1 (ts1) mutant of maize, which possesses a defect in the lipoxygenase gene required for JA biosynthesis, shows a feminization phenotype of the tassel that can be chemically complemented by the application of exogenous JA [67]. This mechanism is evolutionary conserved. In Arabidopsis, mutations in the ACX1 and ACX5 genes, which encodes acyl-CoA oxidases homologous to the maize candidate gene Zm00001d042884 lead to severely reduced JA levels, causing significant reduction in pollen viability and fertility [50]. This confirms ACX activity represents an important enzymatic node in the JA-mediated reproductive regulation. However, the key finding of our work is that the JA pathway is not a qualitative switch determining fertility (i.e., fertile versus sterile), but rather a “quantitative regulator,” controlling the timing of reproductive development. We found that natural allelic variation in Zm00001d042884, an important JA biosynthetic gene, moderates the rate of JA synthesis, which in turn moderates the days to tasselling (DTT). This differs from previously described qualitative mutations, such as ts1, in which silks were produced on tassels in the absence of JA. In our study, differences between haplotypes of Zm00001d042884 are statistically significant but reflect quantitative variation in mean DTT. This finding marks a considerable advance in understanding the role of JA, shifting the model from one in which the presence or absence of this hormone determines organ identity, to one in which the rate of synthesis, modified by allelic variation, modulates the developmental timing of the organ. From an adaptation perspective, this quantitative modulation is of greater evolutionary and agronomic importance, as it allows continuous phenotypic variation that can be utilized in plant breeding to optimize flowering time and enhance environmental adaptability.The role of ROS signalling in reproductive assurance: beyond a general stress responseStrong evidence suggests that tightly controlled ROS production is necessary for plant reproduction [55]. In particular, ROS produced by RBOH enzymes are important signalling molecules triggering programmed cell death (PCD) of the tapetum, which is essential for supplying nutrients and building blocks for pollen wall construction [56]. In Arabidopsis, rbohe mutants show delayed tapetal PCD and produce sterile, collapsed pollen, thus demonstrating the critical role of RBOH in pollen viability [68]. The completion of pollen maturation and release via timely tapetal cell death leads to anther dehiscence, the variation of which is expressed as DTA in this study.The major significance of this study is in establishing the first genetic link between an elementary cellular mechanism (ROS-mediated pollen maturation) and phenotypic variation in an important agronomic trait (DTA). Our results indicate that the RBOH gene Zm00001d042961 is involved in the intrinsic developmental programme under normal physiological conditions that controls flowering time. The haplotypic variation in Zm00001d042961 is significantly associated with differences in anthesis timings (DTA), providing a genetic entry point for manipulating ROS homeostasis during anther development. This finding explains the mechanistic basis of DTA variation observed in the field and highlights ROS signalling as an important part of the reproductive assurance module responsible for the flowering time adaptation in maize.Supplementing the classic pathwayResearch into the flowering time in maize has traditionally been dominated by a comprehensive “decision” framework centered on a few major genes determining the flowering time [69]. Of these, ZmCCT (a floral repressor) on chromosome 10 processes the photoperiodic signal, causing delayed flowering under long-day condition [12] while ZCN8 (florigen) on chromosome 8 encodes a mobile flowering signal that travels from the leaf to the shoot apical meristem [70]. The JA and ROS pathway genes identified in this study are not primary sensors of environmental signals such as photoperiod; instead, they act downstream of the floral induction network to ensure that the flowering decision translates to a successful reproductive development. The maladaptation of tropical germplasm in temperate environments may arise not only from a delayed “flowering decision” (e.g., unfavourable ZmCCT alleles), but also from a failure in the “execution” phase under novel environmental conditions such as altered temperatures and humidity. Crucially, both the JA and ROS pathways are highly environmentally sensitive [45, 56]. Therefore, even if a tropical line is photoperiod-adapted (e.g. by introgressing or fixing favorable ZmCCT alleles), it may still show low pollen volition and poor yield stability if the JA/ROS-mediated pathways responsible for executing its gametophytic developmental program remain inefficient under temperate conditions. Our dual-layer model thus provides not a conceptual framework but also specific targets for research and improvement in the second-tier adaptive mechanisms that control the reproductive potential of tropical germplasm for temperate maize breeding.Theoretical and applied innovations: paradigm expansion and precision breeding toolsThis study offers a novel contribution in both theory and practice by presenting a previously unrecognized regulatory layer involved in the control of flowering time in maize. Theoretical innovation: Prior knowledge about maize flowering time has focused largely on the so-called ‘decision pathway’ containing the core genes ZmCCT and ZCN8 [69]. This classical model emphasises the perception of environmental signals, particularly photoperiod, and the transcriptional regulation that determines when the ‘plant commences flowering’. Results from our investigations support this hypothesis as significant loci were identified through GWAS on chromosomes numbers 8 and 10, thus validating the reliability of our experimental platform. However, the roles of the JA and ROS signalling pathways, represented by Zm00001d042884 and Zm00001d042961, respectively, do not directly involve photoperiod. Instead, they regulate physiological and cellular events downstream of flowering initiation that are necessary for floral organ maturation and successful reproduction. Their roles are not in determining flowering per se, but in ensuring that reproductive development proceeds properly after flowering has begun. Therefore, our findings widen the conceptual frameworks of flowering regulation from a linear ‘environmental perception → signal transduction → flowering initiation’ model to a more comprehensive two-layer ‘decision → execution’ model.Application Innovation: The emergence of this novel regulatory layer represents a modular genetic mechanism with potential for precision breeding of maize. One of the ongoing challenge in temperate maize improvement using tropical germplasm is linkage drag, in which desirable alleles for disease resistance are linked to major loci controlling flowering delay, such as ZmCCT under long-day photoperiods, making them difficult to separate from selection for flowering-time selection [72]. The two candidate genes identified in this study, both located on chromosome 3, are genetically independent of these major-effect loci and can serve as independent regulatory modules influencing the downstream flowering execution process without interfering with the upstream photoperiod-dependent decision pathway. Furthermore, molecular markers derived from Zm00001d042884 and Zm00001d042961 can be used for targeted selection of haplotypes associated with earlier flowering, while still allowing introgression of tropical resistance alleles. This approach provides an efficient route to facilitate utilization of the adaptive potential of tropical germplasm, thus accelerating its deployment in temperate maize breeding programs.Academic reflection, limitations, and future perspectivesWhile this study represents significant progress in elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of maize flowering, we also acknowledge its limitations and propose future research directions to strengthen and extend our findings.Although the combined GWAS and QTL mapping approaches significantly improved resolution, the identified SNPs remain statistical association rather than confirmed casual variants. To address this, we are currently developing near-isogenic lines (NILs) with high recombination frequency in the target QTL regions on chromosome 3 for fine mapping. Moreover, although the genetic and bioinformatic evidence strongly implicates Zm00001d042884 and Zm00001d042961 in flowering regulation, definitive functional validation is required. We are employing CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing to generate knockout mutants and allele-swapped lines for these candidate genes. Observation of phenotypes such as delayed tasselling or pollen abortion in engineered lines will directly confirm their causal role in flowering regulation. Furthermore, exogenous hormone and chemical treatment will be applied to verify pathway-level effects: late-flowering haplotypes in RILs and parental lines will be treated with methyl jasmone during tassel differentiation, while ROS-restricting agents will be applied to developing anthers to assess their impact on pollen viability.The MPP was constructed from a single temperate core parent and three tropical/subtropical parents. While this targeted design effectively dissected key genetic components, the generalizability of the findings across broader genetic backgrounds remains to be validated. Similarly, the field trials, conducted at three ecological locations in Yunnan Province, China, do not fully encompass the diverse environmental conditions of major temperate maize-growing regions in China or globally, limiting a thorough analysis of gene by environment (G×E) interactions. Future studies, thus, need to include multiple environment experiments to assess the reliability and adaptability of the alleles identified here.The downstream regulatory mechanisms governed by Zm00001d042884 and Zm00001d042961 still need clarification. To decipher these pathways, we will pursue multi-omics analyses (including RNA-seq and metabolomics) using lines bearing different haplotypes at important stages of tassel and anther development. These analyses will allow a systematic characterization of gene networks and metabolites regulated by JA and ROS signalling that will elucidate the complete molecular mechanism linking these candidate genes to the broader flowering regulatory network.To translate these findings into breeding applications, we will use marker-assisted selection (MAS) to introgress the elite early flowering haplotypes identified in this study into a wider range of elite temperate and tropical inbred lines. Evaluating their performance across diverse genetic and environmental backgrounds will provide insights into their adaptive potential and breeding value. These efforts will form a solid theoretical and material basis for the development of “plug-and-play” genetic modules that can be directly incorporated into molecular design breeding, thereby accelerating the adaptive improvement of tropical germplasm for temperate maize production.

The role of the jasmonic acid (JA) pathway in male reproduction: from “defense hormone” to “developmental regulator"The crucial role of JA in male reproductive development is now firmly established [50]. The classical tasselseed1 (ts1) mutant of maize, which possesses a defect in the lipoxygenase gene required for JA biosynthesis, shows a feminization phenotype of the tassel that can be chemically complemented by the application of exogenous JA [67]. This mechanism is evolutionary conserved. In Arabidopsis, mutations in the ACX1 and ACX5 genes, which encodes acyl-CoA oxidases homologous to the maize candidate gene Zm00001d042884 lead to severely reduced JA levels, causing significant reduction in pollen viability and fertility [50]. This confirms ACX activity represents an important enzymatic node in the JA-mediated reproductive regulation. However, the key finding of our work is that the JA pathway is not a qualitative switch determining fertility (i.e., fertile versus sterile), but rather a “quantitative regulator,” controlling the timing of reproductive development. We found that natural allelic variation in Zm00001d042884, an important JA biosynthetic gene, moderates the rate of JA synthesis, which in turn moderates the days to tasselling (DTT). This differs from previously described qualitative mutations, such as ts1, in which silks were produced on tassels in the absence of JA. In our study, differences between haplotypes of Zm00001d042884 are statistically significant but reflect quantitative variation in mean DTT. This finding marks a considerable advance in understanding the role of JA, shifting the model from one in which the presence or absence of this hormone determines organ identity, to one in which the rate of synthesis, modified by allelic variation, modulates the developmental timing of the organ. From an adaptation perspective, this quantitative modulation is of greater evolutionary and agronomic importance, as it allows continuous phenotypic variation that can be utilized in plant breeding to optimize flowering time and enhance environmental adaptability.

Theoretical and applied innovations: paradigm expansion and precision breeding toolsThis study offers a novel contribution in both theory and practice by presenting a previously unrecognized regulatory layer involved in the control of flowering time in maize. Theoretical innovation: Prior knowledge about maize flowering time has focused largely on the so-called ‘decision pathway’ containing the core genes ZmCCT and ZCN8 [69]. This classical model emphasises the perception of environmental signals, particularly photoperiod, and the transcriptional regulation that determines when the ‘plant commences flowering’. Results from our investigations support this hypothesis as significant loci were identified through GWAS on chromosomes numbers 8 and 10, thus validating the reliability of our experimental platform. However, the roles of the JA and ROS signalling pathways, represented by Zm00001d042884 and Zm00001d042961, respectively, do not directly involve photoperiod. Instead, they regulate physiological and cellular events downstream of flowering initiation that are necessary for floral organ maturation and successful reproduction. Their roles are not in determining flowering per se, but in ensuring that reproductive development proceeds properly after flowering has begun. One of the ongoing challenge in temperate maize improvement using tropical germplasm is linkage drag, in which desirable alleles for disease resistance are linked to major loci controlling flowering delay, such as ZmCCT under long-day photoperiods, making them difficult to separate from selection for flowering-time selection [72]. The two candidate genes identified in this study, both located on chromosome 3, are genetically independent of these major-effect loci and can serve as independent regulatory modules influencing the downstream flowering execution process without interfering with the upstream photoperiod-dependent decision pathway. Furthermore, molecular markers derived from Zm00001d042884 and Zm00001d042961 can be used for targeted selection of haplotypes associated with earlier flowering, while still allowing introgression of tropical resistance alleles. This approach provides an efficient route to facilitate utilization of the adaptive potential of tropical germplasm, thus accelerating its deployment in temperate maize breeding programs.

Academic reflection, limitations, and future perspectives

While this study represents significant progress in elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of maize flowering, we also acknowledge its limitations and propose future research directions to strengthen and extend our findings.

Although the combined GWAS and QTL mapping approaches significantly improved resolution, the identified SNPs remain statistical association rather than confirmed casual variants. To address this, we are currently developing near-isogenic lines (NILs) with high recombination frequency in the target QTL regions on chromosome 3 for fine mapping. Moreover, although the genetic and bioinformatic evidence strongly implicates Zm00001d042884 and Zm00001d042961 in flowering regulation, definitive functional validation is required. We are employing CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing to generate knockout mutants and allele-swapped lines for these candidate genes. Observation of phenotypes such as delayed tasselling or pollen abortion in engineered lines will directly confirm their causal role in flowering regulation. Furthermore, exogenous hormone and chemical treatment will be applied to verify pathway-level effects: late-flowering haplotypes in RILs and parental lines will be treated with methyl jasmone during tassel differentiation, while ROS-restricting agents will be applied to developing anthers to assess their impact on pollen viability.

The MPP was constructed from a single temperate core parent and three tropical/subtropical parents. While this targeted design effectively dissected key genetic components, the generalizability of the findings across broader genetic backgrounds remains to be validated. Similarly, the field trials, conducted at three ecological locations in Yunnan Province, China, do not fully encompass the diverse environmental conditions of major temperate maize-growing regions in China or globally, limiting a thorough analysis of gene by environment (G×E) interactions. Future studies, thus, need to include multiple environment experiments to assess the reliability and adaptability of the alleles identified here.

The downstream regulatory mechanisms governed by Zm00001d042884 and Zm00001d042961 still need clarification. To decipher these pathways, we will pursue multi-omics analyses (including RNA-seq and metabolomics) using lines bearing different haplotypes at important stages of tassel and anther development. These analyses will allow a systematic characterization of gene networks and metabolites regulated by JA and ROS signalling that will elucidate the complete molecular mechanism linking these candidate genes to the broader flowering regulatory network.

To translate these findings into breeding applications, we will use marker-assisted selection (MAS) to introgress the elite early flowering haplotypes identified in this study into a wider range of elite temperate and tropical inbred lines. Evaluating their performance across diverse genetic and environmental backgrounds will provide insights into their adaptive potential and breeding value. These efforts will form a solid theoretical and material basis for the development of “plug-and-play” genetic modules that can be directly incorporated into molecular design breeding, thereby accelerating the adaptive improvement of tropical germplasm for temperate maize production.

These modular genetic mechanisms hold substantial potential for broadening the genetic base of temperate maize and supporting effective marker-assisted precision breeding desirable for improving environmental adaptation and yield stability.
