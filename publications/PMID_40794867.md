Erratum in
    Nucleic Acids Res. 2025 Oct 28;53(20):gkaf1068. doi: 10.1093/nar/gkaf1068.

The secondary metabolite pyocyanin (PYO) significantly enhances the antibiotic
resistance of Pseudomonas aeruginosa by inducing expression of the
resistance-nodulation-cell division efflux systems. However, the mechanisms
underlying the initial derepression and subsequent upregulation of MexAB-OprM,
regulated by multiple transcriptional repressors, remain unclear. Here, we
demonstrate that MexR and NalD, the transcriptional repressors of MexAB-OprM,
function as PYO receptors, with dissociation constants of 5.17 and 7.03 μM,
respectively. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays show that PYO binding to
MexR and NalD induces their dissociation from the mexA promoter, leading to the
derepression of mexAB-oprM expression. Structural simulations and mutagenesis
assays identified residues F17, R21, and M61 in MexR, as well as R66, N129, and
F132 in NalD, as critical for PYO binding. Additionally, PYO and the MexAB-OprM
substrate novobiocin competitively bind to MexR and NalD, suggesting that PYO
can attenuate the effectiveness of clinical antibiotics by triggering mexAB-oprM
expression. Furthermore, PYO establishes a negative feedback regulatory
mechanism through NalD, which also functions as a transcriptional activator of
phenazine biosynthesis genes, thereby restricting PYO overproduction to maintain
cellular homeostasis. This regulatory pathway provides an energy-efficient
survival strategy for P. aeruginosa in hostile environments.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a Gram-negative opportunistic human pathogen, is responsible for ∼10% of hospital-acquired infections [1, 2], particularly in intensive care units (ICUs) [3]. Its intrinsic and acquired drug resistance [4] complicates the clinical management of infections [5], prompting the World Health Organization (WHO) to classify it as a priority pathogen requiring the urgent development of new antibiotics [6, 7]. This multidrug resistance is primarily driven by the resistance-nodulation-cell division (RND) multidrug efflux system and biofilm formation. Intriguingly, increasing evidence indicates that the virulence factor pyocyanin (PYO) not only plays a crucial role in promoting biofilm formation [8], but also modulates antibiotic sensitivity by inducing the expression of the RND efflux system [9]. Therefore, understanding the relationship between the virulence and antibiotic resistance of P. aeruginosa is crucial for developing new treatment strategies to combat its persistent infections.

The phz operon of P. aeruginosa produces four redox-active heterocyclic compounds known as phenazines. Their biosynthetic pathway is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, with PYO being the most toxic among these phenazines [10]. Following its synthesis, PYO is secreted via the Type II Secretion System (T2SS), enhancing P. aeruginosa survival by inhibiting competing species [11, 12]. Additionally, PYO induces oxidative stress by elevating intracellular levels of reactive oxygen species and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) [13]. This oxidative stress disrupts cell cycle components, causing cell lysis and the release of extracellular DNA [14], which promotes biofilm formation. Beyond its role in virulence, PYO functions as a signaling molecule that induces the expression of RND multidrug efflux pumps, such as MexGHI-OpmD [15], MexEF-OprN [16], and MexJK-OprM [17], thereby enhancing P. aeruginosa’s resistance to various antibiotics. Among the RND efflux systems, MexAB-OprM has the broadest substrate spectrum [18], capable of expelling various clinical and nonclinical antimicrobial agents, making it one of the most critical factors in P. aeruginosa multidrug resistance [19]. Interestingly, phenazine-deficient strains exhibit reduced MexAB-OprM expression [20], while inhibition of MexAB-OprM by phenylalanine-arginine β-naphthylamide (PaβN) upregulates phenazine biosynthesis gene expression [21], suggesting a reciprocal relationship between phenazine production and MexAB-OprM.

Extensive research over the past two decades has elucidated a complex regulatory network of transcriptional repressors and activators controlling MexAB-OprM efflux pump expression. Direct repression is mediated by MexR [22], NalD [23], MdrR [24], MexT [25], and the two-component system RocS2-A2 [26], while NalC functions as an indirect repressor [27]. MexR and NalD, the primary repressors, form a dimer and bind to the distal and proximal promoters of the MexAB-OprM operon, respectively. Environmental factors such as oxidative stress can cause MexR to form intermolecular disulfide bonds [28], releasing it from the promoter DNA. Similarly, small molecules like pentachlorophenol can bind to NalC [29], ultimately leading to the dissociation of MexR from the promoter. Notably, certain efflux pump substrates, such as novobiocin (NOV) [30], can directly bind to both MexR and NalD, causing their detachment from the promoter DNA. On the activation side, BrlR, AmpR [31], and CpxR [32] directly promote MexAB-OprM expression, with BrlR activity co-regulated by direct binding of PYO and c-di-GMP [33]. Despite these advances, the precise molecular mechanism governing the transition of MexAB-OprM from repression to enhanced expression remains to be fully elucidated, representing a critical gap in our understanding of antibiotic resistance mechanisms in P. aeruginosa.

MexR, a member of the MarR family, is characterized by a conserved ligand-binding pocket that, when occupied, can affect the dimer interface or alter the structure and dynamics of the DNA-binding domain [34]. NalD, from the TetR family, features a conserved N-terminal DNA-binding domain coupled with a C-terminal ligand-binding pocket [35], enabling it to sense and bind various small-molecule ligands. MexR and NalD are the two most important repressors of MexAB-OprM, and mutations in mexR and nalD frequently occur in clinically isolated strains of P. aeruginosa [36, 37]. The transposon sequencing (Tn-seq) analysis identified MexR and NalD as critical factors for the PAO1 strain’s tolerance to high concentrations of PYO [20]. Interestingly, PYO and NOV (the signal and substrate of MexAB-OprM), both contain multiple aromatic conjugated ring systems and exhibit structural planarity. However, whether MexR and NalD can function as receptors for PYO to activate the expression of the multidrug efflux pump remains to be determined.

In this study, we demonstrate that PYO positively regulates the MexAB-OprM efflux pump by directly binding to and inhibiting its transcriptional repressors, MexR and NalD. The binding sites for PYO in MexR and NalD were identified through both in vivo and in vitro experiments, which also showed that PYO and clinical antibiotics compete for these sites. Meanwhile, PYO negatively regulates its own synthesis through a feedback mechanism by interfering with NalD binding to the promoters of the phenazine biosynthesis gene. Together with previous studies showing that PYO can bind to the activator BrlR, our findings reveal a dual regulatory mechanism in which PYO regulates MexAB-OprM through both activation and derepression pathways. This sophisticated control likely achieves precise spatiotemporal coordination through different PYO concentration thresholds and binding affinities, ensuring that the expression level of the MexAB-OprM efflux pump maintains a dynamic equilibrium with intracellular PYO levels.

ResultsPYO is essential for MexAB-OprM-mediated antibiotic resistanceTo investigate the impact of the secondary metabolite PYO on the antibiotic resistance of P. aeruginosa, gene knockout mutations ΔphzA-G (not producing phenazines) and ΔphzMSH (not producing PYO) were generated for the strain PAO1 [8]. Antibiotic susceptibilities of wild-type PAO1 and the mutant strains were determined using the two-fold broth microdilution method, with the ΔmexAB-oprM strain as a positive control. As the concentrations of PYO and NOV increased, phzA2 and phzM promoter DNA gradually dissociated from NalD (Fig. 6F and G). In summary, although NalD is a repressor of the MexAB-OprM efflux pump, it can act as a transcriptional activator to increase the expression of phenazine biosynthesis genes, thereby increasing phenazine production. When PYO is overproduced, it binds to NalD, thereby reducing its own production.

High level PYO negatively feedback to regulate its own synthesis

Since PYO is a virulence factor produced by P. aeruginosa PAO1, the exogenous addition of PYO, as expected, does not affect the growth of P. aeruginosa (Fig. 2E). To investigate whether PYO is a substrate for the MexAB-OprM efflux pump, we examined the PYO production in mexAB-oprM knockout strains. The results demonstrated an increase in PYO production following the deletion of mexAB-oprM (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, PYO production was assessed in strains with mexR and nalD knocked out and complemented (Fig. 6C). We found that knockout of mexR and nalD led to a reduction in PYO production, indicating that overexpression of mexAB-oprM reduces PYO levels. To further explore the mechanisms behind this phenomenon, the transcription levels of PYO synthesis genes were analyzed in the knockout strains (Fig. 6A). The results revealed that in mexAB-oprM knockout strains, the transcription of genes phzA1, phzA2, and phzM were upregulated by ∼2-fold. In contrast, the knockout of mexR and nalD resulted in a downregulation of phzA1, phzA2, phzB1, and phzF2 transcription by 2- to 15-fold. Complementation with mexR resulted in minimal changes, whereas complementation with nalD significantly upregulated the transcription of phzA1, phzA2, phzB1, and phzF2 by ∼5- to 20-fold (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, the biofilm formation in strains with mexR or nalD knocked out was also decreased. Complementation with mexR led to minimal changes, whereas complementation with nalD significantly increased biofilm formation (Supplementary Fig. S9B).

These findings not only help to understand the beneficial role of PYO production throughout the lifecycle of P. aeruginosa [52], but also suggest that MexR/NalD homologous proteins may act as PYO receptors.

Our study provides biochemical evidence supporting the specific recognition of PYO by MexR and NalD. However, several questions remain unanswered. The instability of PYO has posed challenges in resolving the structures of the protein–ligand or the protein–ligand-DNA complexes. Despite our efforts, attempts to optimize the crystallization of the protein–ligand complex were unsuccessful. As a result, the precise mechanism by which PYO affects MexR/NalD binding to the mexAB-oprM promoter DNA and regulates its transcription remains unclear. Alternatively, molecular dynamics simulations revealed significant conformational differences between MexR bound to PYO and its oxidized structure. The results revealed that while PYO was positioned closer to the MexR dimer interface, the overall conformational changes in the MexR structure were minimal. This suggests that PYO may affect the MexR dimer interface (Fig. 5C), thereby inducing changes in the spacing between the DNA-binding helix-turn-helix (HTH) domains [53, 54]. Interestingly, structural simulations showed that both NOV and PYO bind to the same pocket in NalD, suggesting that this binding site could accommodate various structurally similar antibiotics, potentially weakening their efficacy through the same mechanism (Supplementary Fig. S6C and G).

Our in vivo qRT-PCR analysis showed that the level of PYO synthesis gene did not change significantly after complementation with mexR, but the reason for this phenomenon remains unclear. Additionally, the production of PYO is higher in clinical strains [8], indicating the presence of additional, yet unidentified, receptors. These receptors may interact with MexR and NalD, potentially exhibiting synergistic action in regulating the MexAB-OprM system. However, due to the complex regulation of MexAB-OprM, it remains challenging to quantify a specific regulatory pathway, and it is unknown how much each pathway contributes to the expression of MexAB-OprM.

Efflux is a primary mechanism by which P. aeruginosa tolerates its own phenazine compounds, with resistance mainly mediated by two efflux systems, MexGHI-OpmD [15] and MexEF-OprN [16], both of which also transport fluoroquinolone antibiotics. Notably, the MexAB-OprM efflux pump can transport not only fluoroquinolones, but also aminoglycosides and β-lactam antibiotics [47]. Therefore, the growth of P. aeruginosa promoted by PYO in the presence of aminoglycosides (e.g. gentamicin and tobramycin) and beta-lactam antibiotics (e.g. carbenicillin) may be attributed to the induction of mexAB-oprM overexpression, rather than being solely linked to the PYO-induced oxidative stress response [55,56].

Recent reports have suggested that MexL, a homolog of NalD in P. aeruginosa, inhibits the expression of the MexJK-OprM efflux pump and its own gene, and binds to the promoter regions of phz1, phz2, and phzM to activate phenazine synthesis [17, 57]. This implies that the association of PYO with the efflux system is ubiquitous. In addition, NalC [58] and MexZ [59] in P. aeruginosa are also homologs of NalD. They are transcriptional repressors of the MexAB-OprM and MexXY-OprM efflux pumps, respectively, and may also be involved in the regulation of PYO. The promoter region between phzM and phzA1 is 696 bases [60], indicating that the phz gene cluster may have a complex regulatory pattern like mexAB-oprM, which is worthy of future study.

Given the pivotal roles of PYO and the mexAB-oprM efflux system in pathogenesis and bacterial survival, these components present promising targets for the development of novel antimicrobial drugs [21, 61–63]. Our findings in this study suggest a new pathway for regulating the MexAB-OprM pump and highlight the diversity of regulatory mechanisms in P. aeruginosa. This significantly enhances our understanding of the self-regulatory mechanisms of mexAB-oprM in
P. aeruginosa and supports the development of potential antimicrobial strategies targeting PYO and mexAB-oprM.
