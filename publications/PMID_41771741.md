## Full Text

Abstract

The geniculate ganglion (GG) consists of two populations of neurons, sensory neurons that innervate the oral cavity and express the transcription factor Phox2b , and somatosensory neurons that innervate the pinna and express Brn3a . To identify signaling pathways necessary for oral sensory neuron development and physiology, the translatome of Phox2b+ GG neurons was selectively profiled using the RiboTag method. From this analysis, Anaplastic Lymphoma Kinase (ALK) was identified as one of the most highly enriched tyrosine kinase receptors. In situ hybridization revealed that Alk was expressed in nearly all Phox2b + neurons and was absent from Phox2b - neurons. Alk was robustly expressed in the GG at all ages examined, from E14.5 into adulthood. To determine whether Alk is necessary for development of the peripheral gustatory system, GG and taste buds (TBs) from postnatal day 3 (P3), P14 and P30 Alk −/− and Alk +/+ male and female mice were examined. Neither oral sensory neurons (PHOX2B+) nor total GG neurons (TUJ1+) were lost in Alk −/− mice. However, TB number, volume and innervation were all significantly decreased in Alk −/− mice, as compared to Alk +/+ mice. ALK mutations cause a portion of non-small cell lung cancers, and treatment with selective ALK inhibitors, such as Ceritinib, frequently causes dysgeusia. Male and female mice receiving Ceritinib for 30 days showed a dramatic reduction in TB volume and TB innervation, as compared to vehicle-treated controls. Somal diameters of oral sensory neurons atrophied and a significant portion of PHOX2B+ neurons died in Ceritinib-treated mice. ALK is thus critical for development and life-long maintenance of oral sensory neurons.

Keywords: Geniculate Ganglion, taste, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), taste, chemosensory neuron

Summary Statement:

The geniculate ganglion consists of two neuronal populations, oral sensory neurons that express the transcription factor Phox2b , and somatosensory neurons that innervate the pinna and express Brn3a . Translatomics of Phox2b + neurons identified they receptor tyrosine kinase ALK as enriched in these neurons. While there was no loss of Phox2b+ neurons in Alk knockout mice, there were target abnormalities including a reduction in taste bud number and volume, along with diminished taste bud innervation. Treatment of cancer patients with selective ALK inhibitors, such as Ceritinib, causes dysgeusia. Mice receiving Ceritinib had a dramatic reduction in taste bud volume and innervation, and a significant portion of PHOX2B+ neurons died, demonstrating that ALK is critical for development and maintenance of oral sensory neurons.

Introduction:

The geniculate ganglion (GG) is composed of two unrelated populations of sensory neurons: oral sensory neurons that innervate the mouth, and somatosensory neurons that innervate the pinna of the ear. GG neurons arise from an initially single population that diverge early in development, and at E12.5 oral sensory neurons begin to express the transcription factor PHOX2B, and the pinna-projecting neurons express BRN3A. These two transcription factors are necessary for the differentiation of these neurons because deletion of Phox2b , for example, results in the transition of what would normally be oral sensory neurons into BRN3A+ somatosensory neurons ( Dauger et al., 2003a ; D’Autreaux et al., 2011 ).

Neurotrophic factor pathways are critical for axon growth, guidance and survival of PHOX2B+ sensory neurons that project to the oral cavity. Initial axon projection and neuronal survival depends on NT-4, and later innervation of tongue and target-dependent survival requires BDNF ( Nosrat et al., 1997b ; Krimm et al., 2001 ; Nosrat et al., 2004 ; Ma et al., 2009 ; Patel and Krimm, 2012 ). BDNF and NT-4 are members of the neurotrophin family of neurotrophic factors, and both of these developmental functions are conveyed by the receptor tyrosine kinase TrkB ( Fei and Krimm, 2013 ; Rios-Pilier and Krimm, 2019 ). A second family of neurotrophic factors that are involved in development of oral sensory neurons is the glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) family of ligands ( Nosrat et al., 1997a ). The receptor tyrosine kinase for the GDNF ligands is RET, and RET is necessary for the early expression of PHOX2B ( Donnelly et al., 2017 ). After birth a subset of PHOX2B neurons upregulate RET, which is required for their maintenance in adulthood ( Donnelly et al., 2017 ). BDNF is also required for the phenotypic maintenance of TrkB-expressing geniculate neurons in adulthood, which is a separate population from RET-expressing neurons ( Meng et al., 2015 ; Tang et al., 2017 ).

While these two families of neurotrophic factors explain much of the survival, differentiation and maintenance of geniculate neurons that innervate the oral cavity, there are likely to be additional growth factor pathways that are necessary for development events such as synaptogenesis and cell fate acquisition. Because the GG is a mixed population of neurons containing both PHOX2B+ and BRN3A+ neurons, identifying new growth factor pathways using standard gene sequencing methods is challenging. We utilized cell type-specific ribosomal profiling as a means of performing RNAseq on cell types of interest in vivo . We took advantage of the RiboTag system in which HA-tagged RPL22, a core member of the ribosome, is expressed in a CRE-dependent manner ( Sanz et al., 2009 ). By crossing RiboTag mice with Phox2b -Cre mice, we were able to HA-tag ribosomes selectively in oral sensory neurons. By immunoprecipitating HA-tagged ribosomes, followed by mRNA sequencing of the associated transcripts, we obtained deep sequencing of oral sensory neurons. This method allowed us to identify anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) as a receptor tyrosine kinase that is highly expressed in Phox2b + neurons and not Brn3a + neurons. Activating mutations in ALK cause neuroblastoma, a childhood sympathetic nervous system cancer. Knockdown of Alk in chick sympathetic ganglia impairs neurogenesis ( Reiff et al., 2011 ), but the peripheral nervous system has not been investigated in detail in Alk knockout mice ( Janoueix-Lerosey et al., 2018 ). Our examination of Alk −/− mice revealed that it is critical for phenotypic maintenance of taste bud innervation and may explain the dysgeusia experienced by cancer patients treated with ALK inhibitors.

Materials and Methods:

Animals:

The RiboTag mice (B6N.129- Rpl122tm1.1Psam/J , 011029) were crossed with Phox2b-Cre mice (B6(Cg)-Tg(Phox2b-cre)3Jke/J, 016223) to generate Phox2b-Cre; Ribo HO experimental mice and Phox2b-WT; Ribo HO control mice. Geniculate ganglia in adult mice (2–3 months) from experiment and control groups were used to perform immunostaining, western blotting and RNA-seq experiments. To investigate the role of ALK gene in taste system, Alk −/− mice ( Lasek et al., 2011 ) were produced by breeding Alk +/− with Alk +/− mice. Adult wild-type C57BL6/J and Alk −/− mice at postnatal day 90 (P90) were used for in situ hybridization experiments to determine the expression level of Alk genes and its ligands in the geniculate ganglion and taste bud. For developmental time course experiments, Alk +/+ , Alk +/− and Alk −/− mice at postnatal day 3 (P3), postnatal day 14 (P14) and postnatal day 30 (P30) were used. All experiments were performed in compliance with the guidelines of the Association for Assessment an Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC) and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Indiana University School of Medicine. Mice were group-housed whenever possible, were provided environmental enrichment, and were on a 12/12 hour light/dark cycle. An equal number of male and female mice were examined in all experiments.

ALK inhibitor (LDK378) administration

In the experiment group, adult wild-type C57BL6/J mice at postnatal day 90 (P90) were administered LDK378 (Ceritinib, 50 mg/g body weight), an ALK inhibitor drug, every other day for a period of 30 days through intraperitoneal injection. LDK378 was initially dissolved into DMSO. This solution was diluted into PEG300 and then subsequently further diluted into phosphate buffered saline (PBS). This mixture (5% DMSO/15% PEG300/PBS) was then immediately injected into mice. Mice in the control group were given the same volume of vehicle (DMSO/PEG300/PBS). The purpose of this administration was to investigate the effects of blocking the ALK receptor on the peripheral taste system. The mice were euthanized on the day following the final dose of LDK378 or vehicle administration to examine the impact of ALK receptor inhibition.

HA immunoprecipitation and RNA isolation

Tissue homogenization and HA immunoprecipitation were performed as previously described ( Sanz et al., 2009 ). Briefly, GG from Ribotag mice were placed into IP buffer containing 50 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 100 mM KCl, 12 mM MgCl2, 1% Nonidet P-40 [NP-40], along with 200 U/mL Promega RNasin, 1 mg/mL heparin, 100μg/mL cycloheximide and protease inhibitor mixture (cOmplete, Sigma-Aldrich) and subjected to homogenization in a microfuge tube (Kimble Biomasher II Tube/Pest; 749625–0030; Japan). After homogenization, the samples were rotated at 4°C for 20 minutes and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C to obtain a clarified supernatant. A 50 μl aliquot of the input was saved and processed separately for protein and/ or RNA analysis. The remaining sample was precleared with Protein G magnetic beads (25 ml of beads per 200 ml of supernatant, New England Biolabs) for 1 hour at 4°C. Following preclearing, the samples were incubated with an HA antibody (1:150 dilution, #MMS-101P, BioLegend) by rotating at 4°C for 4 hours. The Protein G magnetic beads were added to samples to incubate under rotation at 4°C overnight. On the following day, the samples were placed on a magnetic rack and supernatants were collected. The IP product associated with the magnetic beads was then washed 3 times for 5 mins in high salt buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 300 mM KCl, 12 mM MgCl2, 1% NP-40 supplemented with 100 mg/mL cycloheximide). RNA associated with the HA IP was then isolated using the RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA, catalog #74034) according to the manufacturer instructions.

Bioanalyzer analysis, cDNA preparation, Real-time RT-qPCR and sequencing

The concentration of isolated RNA and RNA quality were measured using the 2100 Bioanalyzer Instrument (Agilent Genomics). Only RNA samples with RNA Integrity Numbers (RIN) ≥ 8.0 were used for further analysis. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using Superscript III First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix (Invitrogen). Real-time RT-qPCR was performed using a 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche) and oligonucleotide primer sets ( Table 1 ), which were designed from sequences available in the GenBank Database. For each sample, each assay was conducted in three technical replications and the resulting CTs were averaged. For normalization of cDNA loading, all samples were run in parallel with the housekeeping gene Actin.

Table 1:

One nanogram of total RNA was used for library preparation with the SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit (Clontech, Mountain View, CA) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Each uniquely dual-indexed library was quantified and quality accessed by Qubit and Agilent TapeStation, and multiple libraries were pooled in equal molarity. The pooled libraries were sequenced with 2×100 bp paired-end configuration on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer.

The sequencing reads were quality-checked and were then mapped to the mouse reference genome mm10 using the RNA-seq aligner STAR (v.2.5). Uniquely mapped reads were used to quantify the gene expression level employing featureCounts (subread v.1.5.1) with the following parameters: “-s 0 -Q 10”. Differential expression analysis was performed using edgeR (v.3.36.0), and false discovery rate (FDR) was computed from p-values using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Differential gene expression data is displayed in Supplemental Table 1 , and raw sequencing data has been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database.

Western blotting

The samples were resolved on 12 or 14% (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked in 4% Milk in Tris buffered saline, pH 7.4 containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T) for 1 hour at room temperature. Primary antibodies [Actin (1:1,000, #SC-1616-G, Santa Cruz), HA (1:1,000, #3724, Cell Signaling) and Phospho-ALK (Tyr1604)(1:500, #3341, Cell Signaling)] were diluted in 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated overnight at 4°C. On the following day, the membranes were washed and then incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked secondary antibodies (1:10,000, Jackson ImmunoResearch) in 3% BSA followed by visualization with a chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Scientific).

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were euthanized and transcardially perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The geniculate ganglia and the anterior tongue containing fungiform papillae were isolated and then postfixed in 4% PFA for either 2 hours or overnight. After fixation, the tissues were transferred to 30% sucrose at 4°C overnight, frozen in OCT, and stored at −80°C until sectioned on a cryostat. Geniculate ganglia and the anterior tongue were either sectioned at 20 or 50 μm, respectively, on a cryostat (CM1950; Leica Biosystems) and mounted onto precleaned slides (Superfrost plus, ThermoFisher). The cryostat sections were washed with PBS for 15 minutes at room temperature and then blocked with 10% normal donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch) in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100, 1% BSA (Sigma) and mouse-on-mouse blocking reagent (Vector Laboratories). The slides were then incubated with the following primary antibodies ( Table 1 ), which were diluted in 0.3% Triton X-100 with 1% BSA in a humidified chamber overnight at 4°C: rabbit α-HA-TAG (1:100, catalog no. 3724, Cell Signaling, RRID: AB_1549585), goat α-PHOX2B (1:200; catalog no. AF4940, R&D Systems, RRID: AB_10889846), mouse α-TUJ1 (βIII-tubulin; 1:200; T8578, Sigma, RRID: AB_1841228), rat α–CYTOKERATIN 8 [TROMA-I; 1:100 (supernatant), DSHB, RRID: AB_531826] and rabbit α-P2X3 (1:200; catalog no. AB5895, Millipore, RRID: AB_177544). After incubation in primary antibodies, the slides washed four times in PBS with 0.3% Triton X-100 and then were incubated with secondary antibodies for 4 hours at room temperature (1:200; donkey CF488, CF543, or CF633; Biotium). The tissues were next washed three times of PBS with 0.3% Triton X-100 and one time of PBS alone, then cover-slipped with DAPI Fluoromount-G (Southern Biotech).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

Tissues were collected as described earlier for immunostaining. GG and tongue sections (20 μm thickness) were washed in PBS for 5 min and then baked at 60°C for 45 min in an incubator (RNAScope). After this, the sections were fixed with 4% PFA for 1 hour at room temperature. Subsequently the sections underwent alcohol dehydration with sequential incubations in 50%, 70%, and 2 times in 100% ethanol for 5 min each. Slides were dried for 5min at room temperature and then incubated for 10 min with RNAScope ® hydrogen peroxide. Tissue sections were briefly soaked in 95°C distilled water for 10 seconds and then subjected to antigen retrieval in RNAScope ® Target Retrieval solution (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, ACD) at 95°C for 5 min. Slides were baked again at 60°C for 45 min. The following in situ hybridization experiments were conducted using the RNAScope ® Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2, following the manufacturer guidelines. The following probes ( Table 1 ) were used for all experiments: Alk (501131-C1), Phox2b (407868-C2), Krt8 (424521-C1 and C3), Fam150a ( Alkal1 , 316021-C2), Fam150b ( Alkal2 , 531808-C3), Ltk (530801-C1), Mdk (537841-C1), Ptn (486381-C3), InsR (401011-C1) and Igf1r (417561-C3) (Advanced Cell Diagnostics).

Fluorescence Imaging

Immunoreacted geniculate ganglia and taste buds were imaged using LAS-X software on an SP8 Lightning confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems). Optical images were captured at 1 μm intervals, with either 20X or 63X magnification with high resolution (2048 × 2048). For all images, a single PMT channel and two HiD channels were imaged separately using single-wavelength excitation to generate a composite image.

Quantification of geniculate ganglion neurons

Whole geniculate ganglia were collected, cryo-sectioned (20 μm), imaged and processed as just described. The quantification methods were conducted as previously described (Tang et al., 2020). Briefly, for FISH experiments the number of single labeled ( Phox2b + or Alk +) and double labeled ( Phox2b+ and Alk +) neurons were counted in each Z-stack section, ensuring that neurons were not counted more than once. In the immunohistochemistry experiment, the number of single labeled (TUJ1+, PHOX2B+, or HA+) and double labeled (PHOX2B+ and HA+) neurons were counted in each Z-stack section. All sections containing geniculate ganglion were counted and summed to determine the total number of neurons in the entire ganglion.

Quantification of taste bud numbers

Taste buds were counted from serial cryosections of the tongue labeled with K8, not from confocal optical sections. Sections were cut at 50 μm thickness. To avoid duplicate counts caused by taste buds appearing partially in consecutive sections, adjacent sections were carefully examined such that each taste bud was counted only once.

Quantification of taste bud innervation and volume

The quantification methods were conducted as previously described (Tang et al ., 2020). Briefly, Z-stacks were acquired during confocal imaging to ensure complete coverage of the fungiform papillae. After image acquisition, the images were imported into ImageJ (RRID:SCR_003070) using the Fiji image-processing package (RRID:SCR_002285). For the quantification of taste bud innervation, taste buds were outlined based on K8 labeling to define their boundaries. When a single taste bud extended into two adjacent sections, the innervation and volume were measured separately in each section, and the values were then combined to represent the total innervation for that taste bud. If an entire taste bud was contained within a single section, its innervation and volume were measured from that section alone. Typically, 6–8 taste buds from each tongue were measured, and care was taken such that they were evenly distributed throughout the tongue (anterior-posterior and dorsal-caudal). Once the border of taste bud regions was defined, the channels were split and converted to grayscale, and thresholds were set for each channel to minimize inclusion of nonspecific background. The software quantified all TUJ1+ and P2X3+ pixels within the K8+ regions. All measurements were averaged to obtain a mean value, and the mean value of each tongue was considered a single independent sample; 3–4 samples of each genotype were evaluated.

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 8 software (GraphPad). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to all analyses, except for electrophysiological recordings, described in detail next. Normality of the data was evaluated using a Shapiro-Wilk test, which is reported in the Supplemental Table #1 in the Supplemental Materials . Significant differences among pairwise comparisons were determined by Bonferroni’s post hoc tests. Statistical significance was defined as follows: *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001. The sample sizes for each analysis are provided in the results section and figure legends.

CT Nerve Recording

Nerve recordings were performed in 3–8 month old Alk KO mice (3 females, 7 males) and WT littermates (3 females, 5 males). Littermate Alk +/+ and Alk −/− mice were analyzed at each age tested, and there were no age differences in their taste and tactile responses. Animals were anesthetized with urethane (2.0 mg/kg, i.p.) given in three doses separated by 15 min. After reaching a surgical plane of anesthesia, a tube (PE 60) was inserted into the trachea to facilitate breathing and the hypoglossal nerves were sectioned bilaterally to prevent tongue movement. Mice were placed in a non-traumatic headholder, and body temperature was maintained with a water-circulating heating pad. After exposing the CT by pulling the posterior mandible laterally with a suture, the nerve was sectioned and placed on a platinum wire electrode (diameter 76 μm). No attempt was made to desheathe the CT. The cavity was filled with Kwik-Sil (World Precision Instruments) to insulate the nerve and prevent desiccation. A copper reference electrode was clamped onto nearby muscle. The tongue was gently pulled out of the mouth using a suture inserted into the ventral, anterior tongue. Electrical activity was amplified 50,000x and bandpass filtered (30–1000 Hz; Grass P511K) before being routed to an oscilloscope, loudspeaker, and computer. Neural activity was recorded with Spike 2 (Cambridge Electronic Design) and electrical signals were digitally integrated with a time constant of 0.5 s.

The following reagent grade taste stimuli were used: sucrose (sweet; 100–1000 mM), citric acid (sour; 1–10 mM), quinine (bitter; 2–20 mM), 100 mM monopotassium glutamate (MPG) + 10 mM inosine monophosphate (IMP; umami mixture), and NaCl (salty; 30–1000 mM) with and without the epithelial sodium channel blocker amiloride (50 μM). Deionized water was used to dissolve chemical stimuli and to rinse the tongue between stimulus presentations. Taste stimuli were applied to the tongue via hand-operated syringe at a rate of ~1/3 ml/s over ~3 s. Stimuli were on the tongue for 20 s before rinsing with deionized water for 40 s and applying the next stimulus 15 s later. After recording response to all other taste stimuli, the tongue was rinsed with 50 μM amiloride for 1 min and responses were recorded to NaCl mixed with 50 μM amiloride, with 50 μM amiloride used as the rinse.

NH 4 Cl (500 mM) was applied between different stimuli to normalize responses and ensure the stability of neural activity. After recording responses to all taste stimuli, the tongue was brushed with a glass rod to evoke mechanosensory responses. The glass rod was brushed across the anterior tongue starting from the intermolar eminence (posterior tongue) toward the tongue tip in a rapid motion. Brushing occurred every 2 s for 1 min.

Responses to taste stimuli were quantified by subtracting the average voltage 3–13 s prior to stimulus onset from the average voltage 5–15 s after stimulus onset. For mechanical stimuli, the average baseline activity for the 10 s period prior to and after stimulation was subtracted from the averaged peak integrated response to brushing the tongue. Amiloride-sensitive responses to NaCl were determined by subtracting the relative response of NaCl mixed with amiloride from the response to NaCl alone. The experimenter was blind to animal genotype during the procedure.

Data Analysis for Nerve Recordings

Normalizing gustatory nerve responses to a reference stimulus (NH 4 Cl) is common practice because many factors can influence the absolute magnitude of the neural response, including: the extent to which the nerve is handled/stretched, the distance of the electrode from nearby tissue, and the amount of nerve that rests on the electrode. These factors make relative responses much less variable between animals compared to absolute neural responses. However, the use of relative responses does not permit one to identify whether global changes in neural responsiveness have occurred because group differences in response to one stimulus would be proportional to changes in response to all stimuli. To overcome this limitation, we compared the absolute magnitude of voltage changes for each stimulus. This method was possible because the same experimental settings (amplification, filters, etc) were utilized for all nerve recordings.

Welch’s t -tests were used to compare responses to the umami mixture, NH 4 Cl, and mechanical stimulation. For the taste qualities in which a range of concentrations of each chemical were examined within each individual mouse (sweet, sour, bitter, NaCl, NaCl+Amiloride) a 3-level mixed linear regression model was used. This model recognizes the nested nature of the data in which observations are nested within a mouse, and mice are nested within groups (genotypes Alk +/+ and Alk −/− ). Therefore, each observation of a mouse’s responses to taste qualities is more similar to other observations of that same mouse across concentrations than to observations of a difference mouse, and that all observations of mice with a specific genotype are more similar to observations of other mice with the same genotype than to observations of mice with the other genotype.

Results:

Ribosomal profiling of PHOX2B-expressing geniculate neurons identified RTKs that are enriched in oral sensory neurons

In order to identify growth factor receptors that are expressed in oral sensory neurons in an unbiased manner we utilized ribosomal profiling with the RiboTag mouse ( Sanz et al., 2009 ), a method we used previously for the analysis of spinal motor neurons ( Shadrach et al., 2021 ; Stansberry et al., 2024 ). The RiboTag mouse drives the expression of an epitope tag (hemagglutinin, HA) on RPL22, a core ribosomal protein, in cells that express Cre recombinase. We crossed RiboTag mice with Phox2b -Cre mice, resulting in the expression of HA-tagged ribosomes specifically in oral sensory neurons ( Figure 1A ). HA immunoprecipitation (IP) followed by Western blotting for the HA-tag confirmed the expression of HA-RPL22 in Ribo HO ; Phox2b -Cre mice (homozygous for the RiboTag allele) and not in Ribo HO ; Phox2b -WT mice ( Figure 1B ). This was further confirmed by quantification of the immunoblots ( Figure 1C ). To confirm that HA-RPL22 was only expressed in PHOX2B-expressing neurons, GG were sectioned and immunolabeled with antibodies to HA, PHOX2B and the pan-neuronal marker β-tubulin (TUJ1). Nearly all PHOX2B+ neurons expressed HA-RPL22, and less than 1% of PHOX2B- neurons expressed HA-RPL22 ( Figure 1D , E ). Similar to the IP experiments, GG from Ribo HO ; Phox2b -Cre displayed robust HA-RPL22 expression, in contrast to GG from Ribo HO ; Phox2b -WT mice that had almost no detectible HA-RPL22 expression ( Figure 1D , E ). To confirm the ability to isolate intact mRNA from HA-tag immunoprecipitated ribosomes, we performed Phox2b , P2rx3 and Brn3a qRT-PCR on mRNA isolated from HA IPs of GGs dissected from Ribo HO ; Phox2b -Cre mice. When we compared the amount of these mRNAs in the HA IPs to the amount of mRNA from the total GG lysates (whole cell lysates, WCLs) we found that Phox2b and P2rx3 were highly enriched in the IPs as compared to the WCLs, as expected ( Figure 1F ). Analysis of Brn3a revealed the opposite, with Brn3a expression being higher in the WCLs as compared to the IPs ( Figure 1F ). These data indicate that Ribo HO ; Phox2b -Cre mice express HA-tagged ribosomes selectively in PHOX2B+ neurons, allowing for the enriched purification of ribosome-associated mRNAs from oral sensory neurons, such as Phox2b and P2rx3 , and the concomitant depletion of mRNAs from Brn3a -expressing pinna-projecting neurons.

Figure 1: The Ribotag method enables isolation of ribosome-associated transcripts selectively from Phox2b -expressing neurons in the geniculate ganglia.

GGs were isolated from several Ribo HO ; Phox2b -Cre mice and pooled together for the purification of mRNA that was then subjected to RNAseq analysis. Four GGs were combined for each sample, and n=3 samples were analyzed, along with the corresponding total GG mRNA (whole cell lysates, WCLs). The RNA integrity numbers (RIN) were between 8.5–9.5 for all samples that were sequenced, with mRNA yields of between 1.5–7 ng/μl ( Supplemental Figure 1A , B ). Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) analysis demonstrated that the sequence data from the IP samples were tightly clustered and did not overlap with the sequence data from the WCLs ( Supplemental Figure 1C ). To determine how many genes were significantly enriched in PHOX2B+ neurons, a volcano plot analysis was performed. More than 2,000 genes were enriched, as compared to WCLs, and greater than 1,500 genes were depleted as well ( Figure 2A , Data S1 ). Evaluation of these differentially expressed genes confirmed that genes known to be expressed predominantly in oral sensory neurons were significantly enriched, including P2rx3 , P2rx2 , Phox2a , Phox2b and tyrosine hydroxylase ( Th )( Figure 2B ) ( Dauger et al., 2003b ; Finger et al., 2005 ; Ohman-Gault et al., 2017 ; Tang and Pierchala, 2022 ). Likewise, glial genes and genes known to be enriched in BRN3A-expressing GG neurons were significantly depleted in the IP samples, such as Pou4f1 and Pou4f2 ( Figure 2B ). Likewise, glial genes such as Mag , Mobp , Sox10 and Ednrb were depleted in the IPs as compared to the total GG mRNA ( Figure 2B ). To determine whether specific signaling or metabolic pathways, or biological processes, are prevalent in oral sensory neurons, gene ontology was performed. Interestingly, there were several gene categories that were enriched related to g-protein function and GTPase signaling, along with ubiquitination pathways and membrane transporter systems ( Figure 2C ). When a heat map was produced showing the top 25 most highly enriched and depleted genes ( Figure 2D ), several transcription factors were highly upregulated, including Egr4 that was recently discovered to be critical for the postnatal cell fate specification of subsets of PHOX2B+ neurons ( Dutta Banik et al., 2023 ). It is interesting to note that there was an enrichment of several GABA receptors in Phox2b+ neurons, including Gabrr1 , Gabrb2 , Gabra1 , Gabrg1 , Gabra4 , Gabra3 and Gabrb3 ( Data S1 ). There was, however, no enrichment of any of the Gad synthesis enzymes for GABA, nor were the GABA transporters enriched ( Slc6a family). Oral sensory neurons are reported to express GABA receptors and their role in modulation of taste transduction is not fully understood ( Chaudhari, 2021 ).

Figure 2: Tranlatome profiling of Phox2b + oral sensory neurons in Geniculate ganglia.

To identify receptor tyrosine kinase pathways that are critical for oral sensory neuron development and/or maintenance, well evaluated which RTKs were differentially expressed in PHOX2B+ neurons. Surprisingly, only five RTKs were enriched in oral sensory neurons: Ntrk1 (TrkA), Epha3 , Epha5 , Ret and anaplastic lymphoma kinase ( Alk ), which was the most highly enriched ( Figure 3A ). Ephrins and Ephs have been reported to be involved in axon pathfinding in oral sensory neurons ( Treffy et al., 2016 ), and Ret is known to be critical for both prenatal and postnatal differentiation of PHOX2B+ neurons ( Donnelly et al., 2017 ). To validate the RNAseq analysis for Alk and Ntrk1 , qRT-PCR was performed on mRNA isolated from HA IPs and WCLs of GGs dissected from Ribo HO ; Phox2b -Cre mice. As expected, Alk and Ntrk1 were significantly enriched in PHOX2B+ neurons (HA IPs) as compared to WCLs ( Figure 3B ). To compare the level of expression of these receptors to that of other peripheral ganglia innervating craniofacial structures, we isolated GG, trigeminal ganglia (TG) and superior cervical ganglia (SCG). Total mRNA was purified from these ganglia and qRT-PCR was performed for Alk and Ntrk1 , with Actin being used to normalize across samples. Alk was expressed at considerably higher levels in GG as compared to TG, and SCGs also expressed high levels of Alk , as reported previously ( Reiff et al., 2011 ; Janoueix-Lerosey et al., 2018 ) ( Figure 3C ). The level of Ntrk1 expression in GG was significantly lower than either the TG or SCG ( Figure 3C ), suggesting that either a very small population of oral sensory neurons express high levels of TrkA, or many PHOX2B+ neurons express TrkA but at a low level. To differentiate these two possibilities, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed on cryosections of GGs with probes to Ntrk1 and Phox2b . The latter possibility appeared to be true in that many Phox2b + GG neurons expressed Ntrk1 , but at low levels ( Figure 3D ). Most of the FISH signal for Ntrk1 was in the nucleus ( Figure 3D ), suggesting Ntrk1 mRNA is likely pre-mRNA, which will be detected with the FISH probe (RNAscope, ACD). Alk expression was also assessed by FISH, and it was robustly expressed Phox2b + neurons and was not expressed in Phox2b- neurons ( Figure 3D ). To further evaluate when Alk begins to be expressed during development, GGs were isolated from E14.5, E17.5, P0, P7 and P60 (adult) mice and FISH was performed on cryosections. For the analysis of E14.5-P0 GG, mice at these ages are too small to accurately isolate just the GG, and instead the anatomic location of the GG was grossly isolated, cryosectioned and every other section immunolabeled for TUJ1 to identify neural structures, and PHOX2B to identify oral sensory neurons and, therefore, the GG ( Supplemental Figure 2 ). Once the sections were identified that contained the GG, the second serial set of sections containing the GG were subjected to FISH ( Figure 3E ). We found that Alk was prominently expressed in the GG in Phox2b+ neurons at all of these ages, even as early as E14.5 ( Figure 3D ). In summary, ribosomal profiling of oral sensory neurons identified a large set of genes preferentially expressed in Phox2b+ neurons. A specific analysis of RTKs identified Alk as highly expressed only in Phox2b+ neurons in the GG, with expression at least as early as E14.5 to adulthood, raising the possibility that it could be involved in neuroblast proliferation, axon guidance, target-dependent survival and cell fate specification pathways.

Alk is necessary for taste bud development and innervation

To evaluate the extent to which Alk is necessary for geniculate ganglion development, Alk −/− mice were obtained and their GG dissected and analyzed. The total number of neurons in the GG, along with the number of PHOX2B+ neurons, were analyzed in Alk +/+ , Alk +/− and Alk −/− mice at P3, P14 and P30. There were no significant differences in the total number of neurons (TUJ1+ neurons) or in the number of oral sensory neurons (PHOX2B+ neurons) at any of these postnatal ages ( Figure 4A ), indicating that Alk is not necessary for the survival GG neurons, either during or after development. Whether any aspect of oral sensory neuron innervation of fungiform taste buds, and taste bud development, were impaired upon deletion of Alk , anterior tongues were isolated from Alk +/+ , Alk +/− and Alk −/− mice. These tissues were serially sectioned and tongues from P3, P14 and P30 mice were immunolabelled for keratin-8 (K8, green) to identify taste buds, with P2X3 to label chemosensory fibers innervating taste buds (blue), and with TUJ1 to label all axons innervating fungiform taste papillae (magenta), including intragemmal and extragemmal fibers ( Figure 4B ). The number of fungiform taste buds were counted across ages and genotypes, and there was a significant loss of taste buds at P3, P14 and P30 in Alk −/− mice, between a 10–20% reduction; the p values were p= 0.0033, p= 0.0013 and p=0.0033, respectively. ( Figure 4B , C ). There was no significant loss in the number of taste buds in Alk +/− mice as compared to Alk +/+ mice. The volumes of taste buds increase significantly during postnatal development, and we evaluated whether this was affected by Alk deletion. Indeed, measured volumes of fungiform taste buds were smaller in Alk −/− mice versus Alk +/+ mice at all ages (p values were 0.008, 0.0005 and 0.0013, respectively) and there was also a significant reduction in Alk +/− mice at P30 (p= 0.0255, Figure 4D ). Both the loss of taste buds and reduction in taste bud volume was present at P30, and the amount of these losses did not increase with postnatal age, suggesting that this developmental function of Alk is manifest at least by P3. Innervation of taste buds was determined by measuring the intensity of TUJ1 and P2X3 labeling within the K8+ taste bud region ( Figure 4E ). We observed a significant reduction in taste bud innervation, both in TUJ1+ and P2X3+ fibers, in Alk −/− mice compared to Alk +/+ mice (TUJ1+, p values 0.0027, 0.002 and 0.0012, respectively; P2X3, p values 0.0062, 0.0229 and 0.043, respectively). Taste bud innervation in Alk +/− mice was only significantly different at P30, with a p value of 0.0025 ( Figure 4E ). As with taste bud volume, the amount of innervation of taste buds normally increases with postnatal age. This increase in innervation was still observed in Alk −/− mice as compared to Alk +/+ mice, but the innervation deficit in Alk −/− mice remained apparent throughout postnatal age, suggesting that this innervation impairment occurred prior to P3 ( Figure 4E ).

Chorda Tympani responses to taste qualities are abnormal in Alk −/− mice

To determine whether the loss of taste bud number, volume and innervation affected oral sensory neuron responses to chemical and tactile stimulation of fungiform taste buds, chorda tympani (CT) nerve recordings were performed on Alk +/+ and Alk −/− mice. In between the application of different taste qualities to the anterior tongue during CT recording, ammonium chloride (NH 4 , 500 mM) was applied as a positive control stimulus ( Figure 5 ) and compared across the entire period of stimulation to monitor that there was no loss of viability of the tongue preparation during the recording time. Traditionally with CT recordings, responses to taste qualities are made relative to NH 4 to standardize responses across subjects, as there is variability in the magnitude of chemical responses that is inherent to the experimental whole nerve recording method. In this case, however, responses to NH 4 were significantly reduced in Alk −/− mice as compared to Alk +/+ mice ( Figure 5A , I ), so statistical comparisons between genotypes were made without standardization of the data to any particular taste quality. CT responses to concentration curves of citric acid (sour), quinine (bitter), NaCl (salty) and amiloride-sensitive NaCl were all significantly reduced in Alk −/− mice compared to Alk +/+ mice at the highest concentrations, but not at the lowest concentrations ( Figure 5C , D , F , G ). In contrast, responses to sucrose were not statistically different at any concentration ( Figure 5B ). CT responses to an umami stimulus were also not significantly different between genotypes ( Figure 5E ). Some GG neurons that project to the oral cavity remain extragemmal and are responsive to mechanosensory stimuli, and we evaluated these tactile responses by gently brushing the anterior surface of the tongue. Interestingly, mechanical stimulation of the tongue was significantly different between Alk −/− and Alk +/+ mice (stroking, Figure 5H ). In summary, Alk −/− mice have impaired physiologic responses to sour, bitter and salty taste qualities, along with reductions in tactile sensitivity, but Alk −/− mice were not significantly impaired in their responses to sucrose and umami stimuli.

The ALK inhibitor Ceritinib causes degeneration of GG oral sensory neurons in adult mice

Mutations in Alk underlie a subset of non-small cell lung cancers, and small molecule tyrosine kinase (TK) inhibitors have been designed to selectively target the Alk TK. The first generation of these compounds, such as Crizotinib, inhibited other structurally related tyrosine kinases, and second and third generation compounds have been made that are more selective for the Alk TK ( Shaw et al., 2013 ; Golding et al., 2018 ; Sgambato et al., 2018 ). Crizotinib, as well as the second-generation Alk inhibitor, Ceritinib (Zykadia), are FDA-approved for the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer, and these drugs frequently cause dysgeusia as a side-effect ( Khozin et al., 2015 ; Kassem et al., 2019 ; Tian et al., 2020 ). To determine whether chemotherapy-mediated inhibition of ALK negatively impacts oral sensory neurons, thereby causing abnormal taste, we evaluated the more selective ALK inhibitor Ceritinib (LDK-378). Adult, 2-month-old, mice were given either vehicle alone or Ceritinib (50 μg/g body weight) every other day for 30 days. To confirm that intraperitoneal injection of Ceritinib inhibited ALK kinase activity in the peripheral nervous system of mice, the SCGs of mice treated with vehicle alone, or Ceritinib, were isolated 48 hours after injection. The ganglia were detergent extracted and ALK was immunoprecipitated. These immunoprecipitates were subjected to phospho-ALK immunoblotting to determine whether kinase activity, and thus autophosphorylation, was inhibited. We observed a significant reduction in ALK autophosphorylation in Ceritinib-treated mice as compared to control mice ( Figure 6A , B ), suggesting this chemotherapy treatment paradigm was effective. The SCG was used for the phospho-ALK measurements as opposed to the GG because the GG was too small to detect P-ALK levels, even when several GG were pooled together.

Figure 6: An ALK inhibitor used for chemotherapy, Ceritinib (LDK378), causes degeneration of oral sensory neurons of the geniculate ganglion of adult mice.

To determine whether ALK inhibition with Ceritinib affected neuron survival, the GG were isolated from mice after 30 days of LDK-378 treatment and serially sectioned, followed by immunolabelling for TUJ1 and PHOX2B. Interestingly, there was a significant loss of oral sensory neurons (PHOX2B+) in mice treated with LDK-378 (p= 0.001, Figure 6B , C ). The number of TUJ1 neurons also concomitantly declined, reflecting the loss of PHOX2B-expressing neurons (p= 0.0006, Figure 6B , C ). Growth factors often support the metabolism and trophic status of postnatal neurons, and growth factor receptor inhibition frequently leads to atrophy. To determine whether there was a loss of soma size, a measure of neuronal atrophy, the somal diameters of PHOX2B+ neurons in these same sections were measured. There was a significant decrease in the overall somal diameter of oral sensory GG neurons in mice that were administered Ceritinib (p= 0.0008, Figure 6D , E ). When this data was binned across the range of somal size, there was a general shift in the entire population by 2–3 μm, indicating that no single sensory population was affected selectively ( Figure 6E ).

To examine whether Ceritinib administration caused denervation of taste buds, anterior tongues were isolated from mice injected with LDK-378, or vehicle alone, every other day for 30 days. The tongues were serially sectioned and immunolabeled for K8 (green), TUJ1 (magenta) and P2X3 (blue). As before, the innervation of the K8+ area was quantified and compared between experimental groups. Unlike germline deletion of Alk , we did not observe a reduction in the number of fungiform taste buds in the anterior tongues of Ceritinib-exposed mice ( Figure 6G ). We did, however, observe a decrease in the volume of fungiform taste buds (p= 0.002, Figure 6H ) and in the innervation of these taste buds (TUJ1 p= 0.0075 and P2X3 p=0.0305, Figure 6I ) in mice that were administered LDK-378. Mice lost weight during the 30 day LDK-378 treatment regimen ( Figure 6J ) which is common with this chemotherapy drug. Taken together, these results indicate that administration of the chemotherapeutic ALK inhibitor Ceritinib for only one month caused a reduction in fungiform taste bud volume and innervation and resulted in the death of PHOX2B+ oral sensory neurons in the GG. These data also imply that ALK signaling is not only necessary for development of GG oral sensory neurons ( Figure 4 ) but is also necessary for the maintenance of adult GG neurons ( Figure 6 ).

ALK ligands are expressed both in GG and fungiform taste buds

ALK is highly related to a second tyrosine kinase, leukocyte tyrosine kinase (LTK), and these receptors share a high level of homology in their extracellular ligand binding domains. Recently the high affinity ligands for ALK and LTK were discovered to be ALKAL1/Augmentin-β/FAM150A and ALKAL2/Augmentin-α/FAM150B, which we will simplify to ALKAL1 and ALKAL2 ( Zhang et al., 2014 ; Guan et al., 2015 ; Reshetnyak et al., 2015 ). Both ALKAL1 and ALKAL2 bind to ALK, although ALKAL1 has a lower affinity than ALKAL2, and both ALKALs bind with high affinity to LTK ( Guan et al., 2015 ; Reshetnyak et al., 2015 ). To determine which ligands are expressed in the peripheral taste system, we performed FISH with probes to ALKAL1 and ALKAL2 on both GG and fungiform taste buds. ALKAL1 was not expressed in the GG itself, but had an enriched expression in fungiform taste buds, whereas ALKAL2 showed the opposite pattern: low expression in taste buds but higher expression in GG ( Figure 7B , C ). Interestingly, in Alk −/− mice, the expression of ALKAL1 declined in fungiform taste buds, suggesting that ALK expression in geniculate oral sensory neurons influences ALKAL1 expression in its target of innervation ( Figure 7C ). These data suggest that activation of ALK could be occurring on cell bodies via ALKAL2 expressed in GG neurons, or at the axon terminal by ALKAL1 expressed in taste buds.

Figure 7: ALK ligands are expressed in the lingual epithelium and Geniculate ganglion.

While kinase inhibition of ALK with Ceritinib caused a loss of oral sensory neurons in the GG, germline deletion of Alk did not ( Figures 6A , 7C ). With this in mind, we sought to confirm that Alk was completely lost in the Alk −/− mice we were evaluating. Using FISH to evaluate Alk expression, we observed a reduction of Alk in the GG of Alk −/− mice, but not a complete elimination ( Figure 7B ). In these knockout mice, exons 20 and 21 of Alk were targeted for the deletion ( Bilsland et al., 2008 ), and the probe that we used for FISH covers the entire mRNA, raising the possibility that a 5-prime fragment of the Alk mRNA may remain. To test this possibility, we utilized qRT-PCR with primers that amplified a region of Alk prior to exons 20 and 21, primers within the targeted region, and primers downstream of exons 20 and 21. The amplicon prior to exons 20 and 21 showed an approximately 50% reduction in expression in Alk − /− GG as compared to Alk +/+ ( Figure 7A ). In contrast, there was a nearly complete loss of the amplicon within exons 20 and 21, and an 80% loss of the amplicon downstream of these exons ( Figure 7A ). Taken together, these data indicate that Alk −/− mice represent a complete null allele, as reported previously ( Bilsland et al., 2008 ).

To determine whether Ltk is also expressed in GG oral sensory neurons, we examined its expression using FISH, and found widespread expression of Ltk in GG neurons, both in Phox2b + and Phox2b - neurons ( Figure 7B ). Importantly, Ltk expression increased in GG from Alk −/− mice as compared to Alk +/+ mice, suggesting that it may partially compensate for the loss of ALK ( Figure 7B ).

Discussion:

Ribosomal profiling of Phox2b + oral sensory neurons in the GG allowed us to identify genes whose expression is enriched in these neurons, as opposed to pinna-projecting somatosensory neurons or glia that also reside in the ganglion. Our evaluation of RTKs revealed that Alk was the most enriched in oral sensory neurons. In situ hybridization confirmed that Alk is selectively expressed in Phox2b + neurons and is expressed as early as E14.5. Deletion of Alk results in a reduction of taste bud number, volume and innervation during development, culminating in deficits in the electrophysiologic responses to sour, bitter, salty and mechanical stimuli applied to the anterior tongue. Kinase inhibitors of ALK, such as Ceritinib, are used for chemotherapy against some non-small cell lung cancers, and their use often causes dysgeusia. Ceritinib administration to mice resulted in the loss of GG oral sensory neurons, atrophy of cell bodies, and a loss of taste bud volume and innervation, demonstrating that ALK signaling is necessary for maintenance of adult oral sensory geniculate neurons.

These data reveal a new growth factor pathway that is critical for the development and maintenance of oral sensory neurons. ALKAL1 and ALKAL2 are high affinity ligands that activate ALK and LTK, with ALKAL1 having a lower affinity for ALK than LTK. Because ALKAL1 is expressed in fungiform taste buds, and ALKAL2 is expressed in the GG, it is possible that this pathway is involved in retrograde signaling from the axon terminals in taste buds, or local signaling on the cell bodies directly. In the future, compartmentalized cultures of GG neurons may be able to distinguish between these two possibilities. Likewise, the production of ALKAL1 and ALKAL2 knockout mice will help to determine which of these ligands is most important for development of oral sensory neurons. LTK is also expressed in GG neurons and is not restricted to Phox2b + neurons. Its expression in GG is increased upon Alk deletion, suggesting there may be compensation between these two receptors, and that LTK may have a physiologic role in the development and/or maintenance of Phox2b + neurons.

Thinking deeper about the specific function that ALK/ALKAL signaling complexes have in oral sensory neuron development, the knockout studies reported here are informative. The taste bud and nerve terminal deficits in Alk −/− mice were present as early as P3 and did not dramatically change with further postnatal age. This suggests that ALK signaling functions prior to the elaboration and remodeling of the innervation of taste buds. The location and size of the GG were normal in Alk −/− mice, and PHOX2B+ cells were present, indicating that ALK is not required for neurogenesis, migration or coalescence of the GG, nor is it important for early cell fate specification of oral sensory neurons. This raises the possibility that ALK functions in axon growth, guidance or perinatal differentiation, although these conclusions are clouded somewhat by the potential cooperation of LTK in these developmental processes. ALK is also highly expressed in sympathetic neurons, such as in the superior cervical ganglion (SCG), and may play a more general role in the establishment of visceral neural circuits ( Reiff et al., 2011 ; Janoueix-Lerosey et al., 2018 ). Activating mutations in ALK have been identified in neuroblastoma, a cancer of the sympathetic nervous system ( Janoueix-Lerosey et al., 2018 ). When ALK with an activating mutation, ALK-F1174L, is expressed in the neural crest in mice, normal development of the SCG is disrupted, resulting in an expansion of PHOX2B+ neural precursors, reduced neural differentiation and a larger SCG overall ( Ono et al., 2019 ; Vivancos Stalin et al., 2019 ), indicating that activation of ALK during development promotes proliferation of neuroblasts. Likewise, knockdown of Alk in chick embryos causes a reduction in the number of sympathetic neurons produced during development ( Reiff et al., 2011 ). The sympathetic nervous system has not been examined in Alk −/− mice, however, limiting our ability to compare ALK functions in the GG versus the SCG. Alk −/− mice do, however, show a reduction of hippocampal neurons in the brain, further supporting a role in neurogenesis ( Weiss et al., 2012 ). The deficits we observed in the GG of Alk −/− mice suggest its involvement in later developmental events after neuroblast proliferation.

Considering the role of ALK in comparison to the functions of TrkB and RET in oral sensory neuron development, there is still much that is unknown. RET is required during PHOX2B-dependent specification of oral sensory neurons, and then later for the postnatal differentiation of a subset of PHOX2B+ neurons ( Donnelly et al., 2017 ). TrkB, on the other hand, is necessary for survival of most PHOX2B+ neurons during both early axon projections and later target innervation ( Patel and Krimm, 2010 , 2012 ; Fei and Krimm, 2013 ). ALK, based on our examination of postnatal ages, is more likely to be involved in the differentiation of oral sensory neurons and not their target-dependent survival. This conclusion is speculative, however, without yet knowing the phenotype of Alk/Ltk double knockout mice and Alkal1/2 mutants.

Based on the CT recordings described here, it is tempting to speculate that aversive taste responses are selectively ALK-dependent since sour and bitter responses are significantly reduced, but not sweet and umami responses. However, both amiloride-sensitive and amiloride-insensitive NaCl responses were impaired, as well as tactile responses, implying that this is idea is overly simplified. In addition, there were trends towards significance in both the umami and sweet CT response data, suggesting that ALK most likely is important for development and maintenance of oral sensory neurons across all response profiles, and is not specific to any subtype or valence.

Chemotherapy and radiation treatment for head and neck cancer often result in taste disturbances ( Khozin et al., 2015 ; Kassem et al., 2019 ; Tian et al., 2020 ; Gaillard and Barlow, 2021 ). The explanation for these side effects has been that taste bud cells are constantly turned over and replaced, suggesting they would be more sensitive than postmitotic cells. Indeed, numerous studies have demonstrated dramatic losses of taste buds with chemotherapeutic agents such as hedgehog inhibitors and radiation ( Nguyen et al., 2012 ; Kumari et al., 2015 ; Ermilov et al., 2016 ; Jewkes et al., 2017 ; Kumari et al., 2017 ; Kumari et al., 2018 ; Gaillard and Barlow, 2021 ). The effects of ALK kinase inhibitors, such as Ceritinib, most likely are due to their degenerative effects on the oral sensory neurons contacting taste bud cells, with the atrophy of taste buds being secondary to the loss of neural support. It is therefore possible that any number of non-selective tyrosine kinase inhibitors could cause dysgeusia due to inhibition of ALK, and perhaps TRKB and RET, with the effects akin to peripheral neuropathy. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors may also cause dysgeusia by directly affecting taste bud cells, as in the case of the KIT inhibitor Cabozantinib ( Piarowski et al., 2025 ). Recently the chemotherapeutic cyclophosphamide, an alkylating agent, was found to affect taste bud innervation, suggesting that several classes of chemotherapy drugs may disturb taste in a neuropathy-based mechanism ( Wood et al., 2024 ).

Inhibition of ALK with Ceritinib had a greater impact on oral sensory neurons than Alk deletion, which cannot be explained by LTK since this is not significantly inhibited with Ceritinib. Ceritinib also potently inhibits the kinase activity of the insulin receptor (INSR) and IFG-1 receptor (IGF1R). We evaluated the expression of InsR and Igf1r in GG neurons using in situ hybridization, and both are expressed ( Supplemental Figure 3 ). Igf1r is expressed at a higher level in Phox2b- neurons ( Brn3a+ neurons) and both transcripts were present at a low level overall. Nevertheless, GG oral sensory neurons do express InsR and Igf1r mRNA and their inhibition could potentially account for some of the degenerative effects of Ceritinib. IGF1R is also expressed in taste bud cells, raising the possibility that Ceritinib could directly affect them, although this is unlikely given that Igf1r −/− mice do not show deficits in fungiform taste buds of adult mice ( Biggs et al., 2016 ).

An important unanswered question is whether the loss of innervation and somal size seen with kinase inhibition of ALK would be reversible after the cessation of treatment. Ceritinib, over the course of a month of treatment, led to loss of 15–20% of PHOX2B+ neurons, suggesting that these effects would be permanent if neuron number is reduced, although terminal remodeling may occur. Answering this question could have important implications for chemotherapeutic regimens that use ALK kinase inhibitors in order to preserve taste sensitivity long-term. Furthermore, understanding the growth factor pathways necessary for life-long maintenance of oral sensory neurons and taste buds holds promise as a means of identifying new therapeutic strategies for dysgeusia.
