The oxidation of manganese from Mn(II) to Mn(III) or Mn(IV) is a common trait in
the Pseudomonas genus. Many pseudomonads oxidize Mn enzymatically using one or
more multicopper oxidases (MnxG and McoA) and in some cases a heme peroxidase
(MopA). The oxidation of Mn leads to the extracellular precipitation of reactive
Mn oxides in association with extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). Here, we
review the current state of knowledge on Mn oxidation and biomineralization in
pseudomonads. While most of the genetic, biochemical, and physiological insights
we present derive from studies on Pseudomonas putida GB-1, we also examine the
mechanisms of Mn oxidation in other Mn-oxidizing pseudomonads and survey the
potential for Mn oxidation across the genus through phylogenetic analysis.
Although the physiological and microbial community function(s) for Mn oxidation
and biomineralization have yet to be elucidated, we discuss possible roles for
Mn oxidation and identify key knowledge gaps regarding the mechanism and
function of this trait in the Pseudomonas genus.

INTRODUCTION

The ability of microorganisms to precipitate minerals has deep implications for Earth’s history, the evolution and metabolism of myriad organisms, and the biogeochemical cycling of carbon, nutrients, and metals (1–4). For the transition metal manganese (Mn), biomineralization is a critical component of its biogeochemical cycle, as biological activity catalyzes the oxidation of Mn(II) to form Mn(III) and biogenic Mn(IV) oxides (Fig. 1). This process can be carried out by a diverse group of organisms that oxidize Mn either indirectly through the production of metabolites like reactive oxygen species (ROS) or enzymatically through the use of multicopper oxidases (MCOs) and/or heme peroxidases (5).

The oxidative manganese cycle in Pseudomonas. Enzymatic oxidation of Mn(II) to Mn(IV) oxides proceeds via two one-electron transfer reactions with Mn(III) as an intermediate. Mn(III) may be trapped extracellularly as Mn(III) complexes in the presence of complexing inorganic or organic ligands (Mn(III)–L), which may remain in solution, disproportionate to Mn(II) + Mn(IV) oxides, or be further oxidized to Mn(IV) oxides. Under iron-limiting conditions, bacteria may produce siderophores that promote the abiotic oxidation of Mn(II) to Mn(III)–L. (After Tebo et al. (6), with permission from Springer).

This minireview focuses on Pseudomonas bacteria that are known or have the genetic potential to oxidize manganese (Mn) enzymatically. Manganese oxidation is widespread among Pseudomonas species and has been studied most in P. putida strains GB-1 and MnB1, with some investigation in P. putida KT2440, Pseudomonas sp. strains MS-1 and DSV-1, and P. resinovorans species. Other Mn-oxidizing pseudomonads have been isolated from a variety of environments, including marine oxic and anoxic interfaces (7), mid-ocean ridges (8), caves (9), groundwater (10), lake sediments (11), compost (12), soil (13–15), wetlands (16), and potable water systems (17), to name a few.

In the sections that follow, we begin with a primer on the significance of Mn in the environment. We then present the current state of knowledge on the genetics, biochemistry, and physiology of known Mn-oxidizing Pseudomonas species. In the final section, we explore potential functions for Mn oxidation and highlight open questions in this field. While bacterial Mn oxidation has been recognized for over a century (18, 19), its biochemical mechanisms, regulatory pathways, and response to environmental conditions are only partially understood, and its physiological and/or community function(s) are largely unknown. Research on Mn oxidation and biomineralization in pseudomonads is poised to answer many of these questions, aided not only by existing foundational literature but by the three key properties of pseudomonads: they are ubiquitous, can be cultured with ease, and are amenable to genetic manipulation.

ENVIRONMENTAL SIGNIFICANCE AND CHEMISTRY OF MANGANESEManganese is the second most abundant transition metal in the Earth’s crust (20). Its biological functions include oxygen production in oxygenic photosynthesis, protection against superoxide, DNA repair and replication, among others (21). In nature, Mn can cycle between three oxidation states, II, III, and IV (22). Mn(II), the form that is most biologically available to organisms, occurs as an aqueous ion (Mn(H2O)62+) in solution, forming weak metal complexes with a variety of anion ligands, or mineral phases such as Mn carbonate, Mn phosphate, and Mn sulfide. Mn oxidation, which can occur through both chemical and biological mechanisms, is thermodynamically favorable but kinetically slow under common environmental conditions (22). It occurs in the form of Mn oxides, typically as layer-type minerals that can be found as discrete particles (e.g., in the water column), as coatings on surfaces (e.g., in soil and sediment), and in association with Fe oxides forming ferromanganese deposits (20). In the absence of oxygen, Mn(IV) oxides can serve as an electron acceptor for anaerobic bacterial respiration (24, 25). They are among the strongest solid-phase oxidants in natural systems (26, 27) and have high sorption reactivity toward other transition metals, oxyanions, rare earth elements, and radionuclides (2, 28–30). Given this high reactivity, Mn oxides impact many biogeochemical cycles, environmental processes, and technological applications (2, 5).The intermediate redox state, Mn(III), forms both soluble and insoluble species but is distinguished by its instability as a free aqueous ion (Mn3+) (22). Mn(III) can form Mn(III) oxide minerals or be incorporated into or adsorbed by Mn(IV) oxides (20). As an aqueous ion, Mn3+ will rapidly disproportionate to Mn(II) and Mn(IV) unless it is stabilized by a complexing ligand such as pyrophosphate, oxalate, or organic biomolecules such as siderophores, which are produced by bacteria, fungi, and roots primarily for iron acquisition (31–34). Ligand-bound Mn(III) is much more abundant and prevalent in the environment than previously thought, with marine/estuarine concentrations of up to 7 µM and 86% of the total dissolved Mn (35–37). In organic-rich soils, fungi produce organic Mn(III) complexes, which participate in the degradation of organic matter (30). Finally, Mn(III) can serve as both an electron acceptor and an electron donor and therefore is a reactive intermediate that affects the redox chemistry of many other chemical species and plays an important role in carbon, sulfur, and metal cycles in the environment (38).

GENETICS, BIOCHEMISTRY AND PHYSIOLOGY OF KNOWN MN-OXIDIZING PSEUDOMONADSPseudomonas putida GB-1While the ability to oxidize Mn(II) is found in diverse bacteria (5), Pseudomonas putida GB-1 (ARS Culture Collection (NRRL) accession number B-65545; Leibniz-Institut DSMZ-Deutsche Sammlung von Mikroorganismen und Zellkulturen GmbH number DSM 120,756) has been used as a model Mn-oxidizing bacterium for almost 30 years (39) due to its sequenced genome (accession #NC_010322.1) and genetic tractability. The strain was isolated from Mn micronodules in sediment from Lake Michigan in Green Bay, Wisconsin (Kenneth Nealson, personal communication) and initially named Pseudomonas fluorescens GB-1 (39). Later, the strain was renamed P. putida GB-1 based on its 16S rRNA gene sequence (40). In laboratory cultures, Mn oxidation results in a visible brown precipitate, with Mn oxides accumulating extracellularly as illustrated in Fig. 2 (41). The brown precipitate phenotype is regularly used as a proxy for Mn(II) oxidation, although Mn(II) may be oxidized only to Mn(III) or environmental and cellular conditions may lead to concurrent or subsequent reduction of the Mn(IV) to Mn(III) or even Mn(II) (5). In P. putida GB-1, Mn oxides appear at the beginning of the stationary phase with a maximum oxidation rate at neutral pH and 26% oxygen saturation (39) and form nanoscale layer-type Mn(IV) oxides (42, 43). Manganese oxidation can occur at concentrations of up to and greater than 1 mM in complex media (44). Transport and localization of Mn oxidases

The accumulation of Mn oxides on the cell surface (Fig. 2) suggests that Mn oxidation occurs outside the cell, necessitating the transport of the oxidases across the cell envelope. Both MnxG and McoA possess amino-terminal Sec system signal peptides, indicating that they are transported across the cell membrane by the Sec system (Fig. 4) (48). MopA lacks a signal peptide but can be detected in culture supernatant, suggesting it is also secreted (47). Further evidence for the export of these proteins is provided by work by the de Vrind group, which identified several transposon insertion mutants where intact cells failed to oxidize Mn, while cell lysates showed wild-type levels of Mn oxidation (64, 65). The transposon insertions mapped to genes with high similarity to type II secretion system (T2SS) genes, including xcmX (PputGB1_2462), a gene conserved in Mn-oxidizing pseudomonads (Table 1, Fig. 5A and B). This pathway may be specialized for the transport of Mn oxidases, as there is a second T2SS operon in the GB-1 genome with greater similarity to the P. aeruginosa T2SS.

In addition to the involvement of a σ54 regulator, Mn oxidation may also be regulated by the integration host factor (IHF). The promoter regions of mcoA and mnxG have predicted binding sites for the integration host factor (IHF) (51), which is a global regulator of the transition into stationary phase (74). The presence of IHF binding sites in these promoters is consistent with the onset of Mn oxidation in stationary-phase growth.

In Pseudomonas spp., FleQ, another σ54-dependent transcription factor, controls the switch between planktonic and sessile lifestyles by regulating flagellar synthesis and biofilm formation (75–77). FleQ is regulated by the second messenger cyclic diguanylate monophosphate (c-di-GMP) and by FleN (75, 78). FleQ and FleN also regulate Mn oxidation in P. putida GB-1 (Fig. 4). Deletion of fleN restores Mn oxidation to the mnxR deletion strain, while deletion of fleQ from the mnxR deletion strain upregulates mopA (47). Because Mn oxides accumulate outside the cell in an EPS matrix (Fig. 2A) (44, 79), Mn oxidation is likely co-regulated with biofilm formation through the activities of FleN and FleQ.

Recent work shows that Mn oxidation is regulated at the population level (see below, (51, 59). Because Mn oxidation takes place outside the cell (Fig. 2) this activity may benefit other members of the community and could be dependent on cell-to-cell interactions, as shown with Mn-oxidizing consortia (80–82). Any community-level regulation of Mn oxidation is likely to involve quorum sensing, which in P. aeruginosa involves the Gac/Rsm system (83, 84). Disruption of rsmA by a transposon insertion resulted in increased Mn oxidation (85). RsmA is a regulatory protein that binds mRNAs, blocking their translation (83). Thus, Mn oxidation may also be regulated post-transcriptionally.

Environmental conditions influence mnxG and mcoA gene expressionThrough the use of reporter gene fusion strains, Gehin et al. (51) found that mnxG and mcoA are successively activated in stationary-phase cells irrespective of planktonic or sessile lifestyle. No gene expression or Mn oxide precipitation was observed in the absence of Mn(II) or in a ΔmnxGΔmcoA double knockout strain, respectively, under any of the conditions tested (51, 59). The colocalization in microcolonies of Mn oxide formation and reporter expression in the mnxG but not mcoA reporter strains demonstrated that MnxG is responsible for the initial precipitation of Mn oxides, whereas McoA contributes to biomineralization under conditions where MnxG activity is saturated or inhibited. The observed patterns in promoter expression were consistent with the measured apparent whole-cell Mn(II) Km for MnxG of 26 µM and McoA of 117 µM (59). However, gene expression likely depends on environmental factors besides the Mn concentration. The comparison of gene expression patterns in microcolonies formed under oxygen-limiting versus oxygen-replete conditions suggested that low oxygen may decrease mnxG expression, while enhancing mcoA expression (51). The multiple Mn oxidases in P. putida GB-1 thus appear to facilitate Mn oxidation under a broad range of environmental conditions.Time-lapse imaging of the reporter strains also revealed that Mn oxidation is differentially regulated among individuals within a population (59). Specifically, a concentration-dependent response in gene activation was observed, where the proportion of individuals activating mnxG increased with increasing Mn(II) concentration up to a threshold concentration of 10 µM Mn(II). Once the full population activated mnxG, then mcoA expression occurred, with mcoA expression also following a concentration-dependent behavior until most of the population activated mcoA (10–500 µM Mn(II)). Subsequent experiments using single-gene deletion strains identified MnxG as the dominant oxidase, with faster rates of Mn oxide precipitation for the MnxG-only strain than the McoA-only strain at low Mn concentrations. Wild-type rates were greater than the sum of the rates measured in the single-gene deletion strains at high Mn concentrations, which suggests that gene coexpression and/or interaction between MnxG and McoA may be required for full conversion of Mn(II) to Mn oxide at high Mn(II) concentrations. The regulation of mnxG and mcoA expression by Mn(II) concentration supports the conclusion that the function of MnxG and McoA is Mn oxidation, but the underlying mechanism by which these proteins work together to precipitate Mn oxides remains an open question.

Siderophores promote Mn(III) formation and inhibit Mn oxide formationEarly investigations into the effect of Fe on Mn oxidation in P. putida MnB1 (discussed in “Mn oxidation in other pseudomonads” and “P. putida MnB1”) revealed that under Fe-limiting conditions, Mn oxide formation was very slow or did not occur (B. M. Tebo, unpublished). Subsequent work showed that under Fe-limiting conditions, fluorescent pseudomonads like P. putida GB-1 and MnB1 produce the fluorescent siderophore pyoverdine (PVD). This siderophore complexes Mn(III) and inhibits the formation of Mn(IV) oxides (32, 86). Random transposon mutagenesis selecting for non-Mn-oxidizing mutants in P. putida GB-1 produced mutants that were constitutive for siderophore production and did not produce MnO2 (40). In another study, PVD was found to complex Mn(III) more strongly than it binds Fe(III) (32). Consistent with the high thermodynamic favorability for Mn(III) complexation by PVD, PVD promotes the oxidation of Mn(II) to form PVD-Mn(III) (Fig. 1), even in Mn oxidation-deficient mutants (86). Only in the presence of bioavailable Fe, leading to the absence of PVD, do P. putida GB-1 and MnB1 produce MnO2. This is true whether PVD is produced by the cells or is exogenous (87). Furthermore, when Mn(III)-PVD forms, if Fe is made available to the cells, both GB-1 and MnB1 will oxidize the Mn(III) bound to the PVD, producing MnO2 and an altered PVD molecule. Interestingly, siderophores can also contribute to the reductive dissolution of Mn oxides (86, 88–90). Collectively, this work suggests that the fluorescent pseudomonads mediate the environmental chemistry of Mn even if they do not oxidize Mn(II) enzymatically.

The presence of up to three Mn oxidases in pseudomonads (Fig. 5 and 6) raises many questions. Variable responses to growth conditions such as media type, Mn concentration, and oxygen availability (48, 51, 59), combined with cell-to-cell variation within a population, suggest that the different Mn oxidases are specialized and regulated for different environmental conditions. By merging mutagenesis and imaging approaches using reporter strains to untangle the regulation of Mn(II) oxidation in Pseudomonas, clues regarding the evolutionary origin and function of Mn(II) oxidation will likely emerge. This knowledge will have large implications for Earth and environmental science as well as in fundamental microbiology.

Mn oxidation leads to the precipitation of Mn oxides on or near the cell’s surface (Fig. 2). How do Mn-oxidizing bacteria protect themselves from the potentially deleterious effects of these potent oxidants? Mn biomineralization coincides with the production of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS, Fig. 2A) (44, 79). High-pressure freeze-substitution preparation of laboratory cultures shows that Mn oxides are near but not directly bound to the cell envelope (Fig. 2B and C). Thus, the biofilm matrix itself may provide bacterial cells with both physical and chemical protection against the high reduction potential of the Mn oxides (114). Studies on the role of EPS should consider that Mn oxidation is regulated by pathways involved in biofilm production (e.g., c-di-GMP in P. resinovorans or FleQ in P. putida). The role played by the biofilm matrix in modulating the reactivity and proximity of biogenic Mn oxides to the bacteria, and subsequently their ability to retrieve an advantage from the proximity of these minerals, thus requires further investigation.

Manganese-oxidizing bacteria, and in particular, Mn-oxidizing pseudomonads, also have great potential for biotechnological applications (14, 115–117). Broad application, however, requires further knowledge about the biochemistry of the enzymes catalyzing Mn oxide biomineralization. For example, the enzymes could be engineered to produce new biomaterials with specific chemistries and stability under conditions relevant for bioremediation of metal pollution, degradation of organic contaminants, recovery of critical minerals or for the production of nanomaterials that possess novel electronic, optical, chemical, and catalytic properties. The morphology and surface chemistry of biogenic Mn oxides can be varied by changing the protein-to-substrate ratio or the Ca2+ concentration (118). The possibilities are vast.

To effectively harness these opportunities, a deep understanding of the mechanism of Mn oxide mineral formation is needed. How do these different proteins take soluble Mn2+ and make a MnO2 particle? Do the properties of the enzymes dictate the properties of the biogenic Mn oxides? How can the different enzyme systems be controlled? We have gained some insights to these questions through studies of the Bacillus sp. Mnx system (53), but similar progress must be made in Pseudomonas. That Pseudomonas spp. are genetically tractable, easily culturable, and offer a plethora of Mn oxidases should greatly motivate biochemical studies of their Mn oxidases.
