Amphotericin B, nystatin, and natamycin are the only polyene antifungals in
clinical use and remain last-resort therapies for severe fungal infections.
These agents disrupt fungal membranes through ergosterol binding, but how
structural differences between the three polyenes influence subcellular
distribution, and whether such distribution is linked to resistance, remains
uncharacterized. To investigate these relationships, we developed fluorescent
probes from each clinically used polyene by conjugating a common fluorophore to
a conserved functional group, preserving the amphoteric nature and
ergosterol-dependent antifungal activity of the parent drug while enabling
live-cell imaging. This unified design allowed direct comparison of localization
and trafficking across Candida species, including sterol biosynthesis mutants
with a high level of polyene resistance. The probes revealed structure-specific
distribution patterns, with resistant strains showing enhanced accumulation
within the vacuole lumen for all three polyenes. These findings suggest that
vacuolar sequestration contributes to polyene resistance and may represent an
unrecognized aspect of the fungal stress response. More broadly, this work
provides the first direct visual evidence that polyene structure dictates
subcellular distribution patterns in fungal cells.

Results and DiscussionDesign and Synthesis of Fluorescent Polyene ProbesThe amphoteric nature of the three polyenes in this study stems from the presence of both a positively charged amine group and a negatively charged carboxylic acid group under physiological conditions. The positive charge on the mycosamine sugar unit facilitates interactions with negatively charged membrane components, such as phospholipids, and with other polyene molecules, enabling the formation of molecular assemblies that disrupt the fungal cell plasma membrane.[

7
,
28

] Consequently, neutralizing this positive charge, for example by conjugating a dye via an amide bond, was shown to lead to a loss of antifungal activity.[

27
,
29

] However, replacing the carboxylic acid group of amphotericin B with various substituted amide derivatives has resulted in active compounds. Burke and colleagues demonstrated this in their development of AM‐2‐19, an experimental antifungal drug based on amphotericin B that displayed no detectable nephrotoxicity in animal models.[

30

]
In designing the fluorescent polyene probes for this study, we aimed to preserve the amphoteric character of the parent polyenes by attaching the fluorescent dye to the primary amine of the mycosamine moiety, thereby generating a secondary amine through a reductive amination reaction. Selection of an appropriate fluorophore required balancing several criteria: sufficient fluorescence intensity at biologically relevant concentrations, high photostability, minimal spectral overlap with cellular autofluorescence, limited pH sensitivity, and minimal effects on molecular size, charge, and hydrophobicity. It was also essential to evaluate how dye attachment influenced the molecular weight and LogD of the resulting conjugates relative to the native compound (see Table S1). Based on these considerations, 7‐diethylaminocoumarin (excitation/emission maxima: 407/472 nm) was selected as the fluorophore. This dye has been shown to be well suited for live‐cell imaging of yeast cells and, owing to its relatively small molecular weight, was expected to minimize steric interference and preserve antifungal activity more effectively than bulkier fluorescent dyes.[

23

]
The three polyenes, amphotericin B, nystatin, and natamycin, were reacted with an aldehyde‐functionalized 7‐diethylaminocoumarin under reductive amination conditions to yield the corresponding fluorescent polyene probes 1, 2, and 3, respectively (Scheme 1). The fluorescent polyene probes 1, 2, and 3 were purified by reverse‐phase HPLC, resulting in isolated yields of 37%, 27%, and 31%, respectively. During the optimization of the reductive amination reaction, we evaluated three different reducing agents: sodium borohydride, sodium cyanoborohydride, and sodium triacetoxyborohydride. Interestingly, each polyene exhibited the highest conversion with a different reducing agent: amphotericin B with sodium borohydride, nystatin with triacetoxyborohydride, and natamycin with sodium cyanoborohydride.Scheme 1Synthesis of fluorescent probes 1, 2, and 3 derived from amphotericin B, nystatin, and natamycin, respectively.The fluorescent properties of the polyene probes were first examined by recording their emission spectra in PBS buffer (pH 7.4) at an excitation wavelength of 400 nm (Figure S1). To account for potential pH effects on fluorescence, the probes were evaluated across a pH range of 5.0–7.4, encompassing vacuolar (approximately pH 5.5) and physiological conditions. Notably, fluorescence intensity decreased only moderately at pH 5.0 relative to pH 7.4 (by about 28%–45%, depending on the fluorescent polyene probe; Figure S2). ,
28

29

30

]
In designing the fluorescent polyene probes for this study, we aimed to preserve the amphoteric character of the parent polyenes by attaching the fluorescent dye to the primary amine of the mycosamine moiety, thereby generating a secondary amine through a reductive amination reaction. This dye has been shown to be well suited for live‐cell imaging of yeast cells and, owing to its relatively small molecular weight, was expected to minimize steric interference and preserve antifungal activity more effectively than bulkier fluorescent dyes.[

Live‐cell fluorescence microscopy was initially used to examine the subcellular distribution of fluorescent polyene probes 1, 2, and 3 in yeast cells of C. albicans SN152 (strain 1, Table S5) following a 3 h incubation at 30 °C with 8 µg mL−1 of each probe in nutrient‐free PBS buffer (pH 7.4). The results also demonstrate that changes in subcellular distribution are not solely dependent on membrane sterol composition but are also polyene dependent. Specifically, the differences in vacuolar sequestration between probes 1 and 2 across the knockout strains suggest that their intracellular trafficking pathways diverge despite the close structural similarity of the two polyenes.

This study presents the first comprehensive comparison of subcellular distribution patterns in both polyene‐susceptible and polyene‐resistant live cells of clinically prevalent Candida species. We designed, synthesized, and studied fluorescent probes derived from all three clinically used polyene antifungals: amphotericin B, nystatin, and natamycin. Each probe was generated by attaching a common fluorophore at a conserved position, yielding derivatives that retained ergosterol‐dependent antifungal activity. This uniform design enabled precise, side‐by‐side comparisons of subcellular localization and its relationship to antifungal potency and resistance‐associated traits across diverse Candida species and defined ergosterol pathway mutants that confer polyene resistance.

In polyene‐susceptible C. albicans, all three probes retained ergosterol‐dependent antifungal activity and, notably, exhibited distinct, structure‐dependent localization patterns. Probe 1 (amphotericin B‐based) localized mainly to the cytosol and cell surface, but not to the vacuolar lumen. Probe 2 (nystatin–based) labeled both the cell surface and vacuoles, either outlining the vacuole or accumulating within its lumen. Probe 3 (natamycin–based) remained primarily associated with the cell surface when incubated in PBS. These results provide the first direct visual evidence that polyene structure influences subcellular distribution, despite their shared targeting of ergosterol in the plasma membrane and the conserved molecular motifs among the three polyenes.

When incubated under nutrient‐rich conditions, amphotericin B and nystatin probes 1 and 2, respectively, showed a marked shift from surface and cytosolic labeling to vacuolar accumulation, consistent with a stress‐induced trafficking response. In contrast, natamycin‐derived probe 3 maintained a stable localization profile, supporting its distinct, non‐permeabilizing mode of action. This divergence reveals that metabolic state influences polyene behavior and suggests vacuolar sequestration as a cellular response to polyenes. Across the tested C. glabrata and C. parapsilosis, the three probes exhibited localization patterns broadly consistent with those observed in C. albicans, confirming that subcellular distribution is primarily structure‐driven and more modestly affected by species‐ and/or strain‐specific factors.

Fluorescence imaging of C. albicans mutants lacking key enzymes in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway revealed that altered sterol composition alters the distribution of the fluorescent polyene probes inside the mutated yeast cells. Deletion of ERG2, ERG6, or both ERG3 and ERG11 led to elevated vacuolar sequestration of all three probes, coinciding with a reduction in antifungal activity. The results suggest that resistance‐associated changes in membrane sterol composition not only weaken polyene–ergosterol interactions but also redirect the probes from the plasma membrane toward elevated accumulation in the vacuolar lumen. This shift in subcellular localization may contribute to reduced drug efficacy, suggesting that vacuolar sequestration is a contributing factor in polyene resistance. In C. auris, an emerging member of the Candida genus that causes infections often resistant to multiple drugs, fluorescent polyene probes were used to assess resistance‐associated changes in subcellular distribution.

Notably, a high‐level polyene‐resistant strain carrying a nonsense mutation in ERG11 exhibited elevated vacuolar accumulation of the natamycin‐derived probe 3, well above background autofluorescence. This redistribution correlated with loss of antifungal activity, underscoring the connection between altered sterol composition, vacuolar sequestration, and resistance. Moreover, these results identify probe 3 as a useful reporter of high‐level polyene resistance in C. auris.

By linking polyene chemical structure, membrane sterol composition, and vacuolar sequestration, this study establishes a molecular framework using fluorescent polyene probes to investigate polyene function and to detect and study resistance to this antifungal drug class in pathogenic fungi.
