Ninjurin-1 (NINJ1), a protein required for plasma membrane rupture (PMR) during
inflammasome-mediated cell death, has no known role in hematopoiesis. Here,
using zebrafish and human hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs), we
identify an evolutionarily conserved, PMR-independent function of Ninjurins in
HSPC development. Ninj1 deficiency reduced HSPC, neutrophil and erythrocyte
numbers, whereas PMR-deficient Ninj1 mutants demonstrated that this function is
independent of PMR. Ninj1 deficiency also impaired immune cell recruitment and
increased susceptibility to bacterial infection. Mechanistically, Ninj1 and
Ninj2 cooperatively promoted early HSPC amplification through canonical WNT
signaling. Pharmacological and genetic activation of WNT signaling rescued
hematopoietic defects in Ninjurin-deficient larvae, whereas inhibition of WNT
abolished the effects of Ninj1 gain of function. Human CD34⁺ HSPCs showed
reduced expansion and impaired WNT signaling following NINJ1/2 deficiency,
demonstrating conservation of this pathway. These findings identify Ninjurins as
regulators of HSPC expansion with potential applications in regenerative
medicine.

Introduction

Ninjurin-1 (short for nerve injury-induced proteins, NINJ1) is a cell surface protein first identified in rats following nerve axotomy, where it was shown to play a role in mediating homophilic adhesion and promoting neurite extension in vitro1,2. Shortly after, a homolog, NINJ2, was identified in human and mouse, and found to be upregulated in Schwann cells surrounding the distal segment of injured nerves, exhibiting a similar time course to NINJ13. Like its counterpart, NINJ2 also supports neurite outgrowth from primary cultured dorsal root ganglion neurons, likely through homophilic cellular interactions3. Additionally, a homolog of Ninjurin, termed Ninjurin A (NijA), was discovered in the fruit fly and found to regulate cell adhesion through its cleavage by matrix metalloproteinase 1 (Mmp1)4. Similarly, NINJ1 was found to be upregulated in brain-blood barrier endothelial cells (BBB-ECs) and on infiltrating antigen-presenting cells during experimental allergic encephalomyelitis and multiple sclerosis, participating in the adhesion and migration of human monocytes across BBB-ECs5. This suggests that Ninjurins have conserved roles in cellular adhesion and response to injury across species.

More recently, a forward-genetic screen of randomly mutagenized mice identified NINJ1 as a key regulator of plasma membrane rupture (PMR)6. In this context, NINJ1-deficient cells were found to exhibit impaired PMR in response to various inducers of pyroptosis, necrosis, apoptosis, cuproptosis, ferroptosis, parthanatos, but not necroptosis6–8. Importantly, the PMR activity of NINJ1 takes place downstream metabolic cell death and independently of the pore forming proteins mixed lineage kinase domain like pseudokinase (MLKL), gasdermin D (GSDMD) and GSDME7. Super-resolution and cryo-electron microscopy studies have demonstrated that, during lytic cell death, the extracellular helices of NINJ1 insert into the plasma membrane, facilitating the polymerization of NINJ1 monomers into amphipathic filaments9. These filaments ultimately disrupt the plasma membrane, leading to its rupture. Strikingly, NINJ1 has been identified as the long-sought target of the cryoprotective molecule glycine10. Glycine inhibits NINJ1 oligomerization without affecting IL-1β secretion, confirming that it functions downstream of GSDMD pore formation and mimics the phenotype observed in NINJ1 deficiency10.

The PMR activity of mammalian NINJ1 appears to be evolutionarily conserved, as the fruit fly homolog NijA is also capable of inducing cell death11. However, while mammalian NINJ2 lacks PMR activity6,12, its deficiency has been shown to result in overactivation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, exacerbated pyroptosis, disrupted lipid and carbohydrate metabolism, systemic inflammation, and spontaneous tumorigenesis13. Despite these insights into mammalian and invertebrate systems, the biological roles of Ninjurins in non-mammalian vertebrates remain largely unexplored.

Here, we show that Ninj1 and Ninj2 regulate the amplification of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) in the hemogenic endothelium through canonical WNT signaling independently of their plasma membrane rupture activity. Using gain- and loss-of-function approaches in zebrafish, we identify a non-canonical function of Ninjurins in hematopoiesis. We further demonstrate that this mechanism is conserved in humans, where NINJ1 and NINJ2 regulate the expansion of CD34+ HSPCs. Together, our findings establish Ninjurins as conserved regulators of HSPC amplification and extend their biological functions beyond their previously characterized roles in plasma membrane rupture, cell death and inflammation.

To anticipate in which process Ninj1 was involved, we used DanioCell (https://daniocell.nichd.nih.gov/), a repository of single cell data at different stages of zebrafish development23. We focused on the hematopoietic/vasculature cluster (Fig. S4A–C), and it was found that ninj1 was expressed in immune cells and HSPCs but, more importantly, it was early expressed in hemangioblasts, then in the aorta and, consecutively, in the hemogenic endothelium (HE) (Fig. S4D, E), whose formation stages go from 12 to 32 hpf (Fig. S4B). This suggests that Ninj1 could be involved in the emergence of HSPCs from the HE located in the dorsal aorta (DA) of zebrafish larvae24,25. We next sought to analyze the expression of ninj2, which also exists in human and mouse, and showed a high degree of conservation in these species with NINJ1, but it lacks PMR-forming activity6,12. We first aligned zebrafish Ninj1 and Ninj2 proteins, and they showed 70.80% of identity, curiously including the D50 and A56 residues (Fig. S4F). Notwithstanding the expression of ninj2 gene was radically lower in all the tissues, its tissue expression profile was rather like that of ninj1 (Fig. S4G–I).

Our following step was, therefore, to analyze the effect of diminishing the levels of ninj2 in zebrafish. Genetic inhibition of ninj2 (KO efficiency of about 80%, Fig. S5A) resulted in decreased erythroid and neutrophil counts, and caspase-1 activity in wild type and Spint1a-deficient larvae (Fig. 4A–E).
