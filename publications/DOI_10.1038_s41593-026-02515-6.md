The development and function of cortical circuits require an appropriate
balance of excitation and inhibition. Pyramidal cells, the principal excitatory
cells in the cerebral cortex, recruit inhibitory synapses in proportion to their
activity. How each pyramidal cell subtype achieves the cell-type-specific
levels of inhibition it requires is unknown. Here we show in mice that a
neuronal identity gene is repurposed postnatally to regulate inhibitory
connectivity. Fezf2, which encodes a transcription factor that establishes the
identity of extratelencephalic pyramidal cells in layer 5 during embryonic
development, is regulated by activity after birth only in those neurons.
Activity-dependent FEZF2 controls the number of inhibitory synapses
formed by parvalbumin-expressing interneurons through regulation of the
cell-surface protein Cadherin-22. Our findings reveal that a gene specifying
neuronal subtype identity is redeployed to control how activity refines wiring,
giving activity-dependent circuit development its cell-type specificity.
The function of the cerebral cortex relies on the precise interplay
between two neuronal populations—excitatory glutamatergic
principal or pyramidal cells and inhibitory γ-aminobutyric acid
(GABAergic)-expressing interneurons. The connectivity motifs established between these two neuronal populations are highly specialized
and crucial for the function of cortical circuits. For example, different
types of inhibitory interneurons have been shown to target specific
subcellular compartments of principal cells, enhancing the computational power of cortical circuitry1,2.
A remarkable diversity of molecular programs supports the exquisite variety of cortical inhibitory connectivity that emerges during
development. The prevailing view conveys that synaptic development
has the following two components: (1) a genetically determined process

sustained by specific molecular codes and (2) an activity-dependent
mechanism that fine-tunes circuitry3–5. Several cell-surface molecule
complexes are required for the development of GABAergic synapses,
including Neuroligin-2, NRG1/NRG3-ErbB4 and neurexin2αβ-IgSF21
(refs. 6–9). Moreover, the specific cellular and subcellular targeting
of pyramidal cells by interneurons is also regulated by cell-surface
molecules7. The development of inhibitory synapses in the cerebral
cortex unfolds over a protracted period of postnatal development10–12
and activity sculpts the inhibitory cortical circuitry until it reaches the
mature state10,13,14.

mechanisms3,16–18. Synaptic and cell-intrinsic changes underlying homeostatic plasticity are mediated through the activity-dependent expression of immediate early genes (IEGs). Cortical inhibitory synapses onto

pyramidal cells undergo a steady increase in number during the first
month of development11,12, probably matching the progressive surge
in excitatory inputs and activity of pyramidal cells19,20. Consistent with
this, in vitro experiments revealed that the formation of inhibitory

synapses onto excitatory neurons is regulated by activity10,21, and in
the hippocampus requires Npas4, an IEG21. In adults, these transcription factors have been shown to promote the subsequent expression

or proteins with synaptic localization22. However, despite the relevance
of inhibitory synapses and activity in the maturation of cortical circuitry, the molecular programs used by pyramidal cells to respond
to temporally specific and cell-type-specific activity patterns during
development remain unknown.
Here we address this question in layer 5 (L5) extratelencephalic (ET) pyramidal cells, the most excitable cortical pyramidal
neurons23–25, which receive remarkably more inhibitory inputs from
parvalbumin-expressing (PV+) basket cells than other pyramidal cells
from early postnatal development26. By combining the manipulation
of neuronal activity with ribosome-associated mRNA profiling, we
find that Fezf2, the selector gene that specifies L5 ET identity in the
embryo27–30, is regulated by neuronal activity postnatally in these same
neurons, but not in other pyramidal cell types. Activity-dependent
changes in Fezf2 expression determine the density of PV+ inhibitory
synapses received by L5 ET pyramidal cells, acting through the cell
adhesion molecule Cadherin-22 (Cdh22), which we identify as a downstream effector. Our findings reveal a gene that first establishes a
neuron’s subtype identity and is repurposed after birth to determine
how activity affects its inhibitory input, providing cell-type specificity
to the activity-dependent assembly of cortical circuits.

Results

Neuronal activity regulates the development of inhibitory
inputs onto pyramidal cells
L5 ET pyramidal cells receive a high density of perisomatic inhibition that increases steadily during the first month of postnatal life in
mice26. To investigate whether activity regulates the formation of PV+
perisomatic inhibitory synapses onto these neurons, we reduced the
excitability of L5 ET pyramidal cells during early postnatal development by expressing the inwardly rectifying potassium channel Kir2.1
(ref. 31). We used an intersectional strategy to label L5 ET pyramidal
cells in the primary somatosensory cortex (S1). In brief, we simultaneously injected a retrograde adeno-associated virus (AAV) expressing
Cre recombinase (rAAVretro-cre) into the pontine nuclei of postnatal day
(P) 1 wild-type mice and a Cre-dependent AAV into S1 ipsilateral to the
pons injection that expressed tdTomato and Kir2.1 or, in control experiments, a mutated version of Kir2.1 encoding a nonconducting channel
(mutKir2.1 (ref. 17); Fig. 1a,b). We used this approach in all subsequent
experiments to label L5 ET neurons unless stated otherwise.
To confirm that Kir2.1 expression lowers the excitability of L5
ET cells before the initial surge of inhibitory synapse formation around
P10 (ref. 11), we prepared acute slices from mice at P8 expressing Kir2.1
or mutKir2.1. We then performed electrophysiological recordings of
infected L5 ET pyramidal cells. Compared with mutKir2.1-expressing
cells or uninfected cells, Kir2.1-expressing cells showed a hyperpolarized membrane potential and decreased input resistance, leading to
a marked increase in rheobase that denoted a decrease in excitability
(Fig. 1c and Extended Data Fig. 1a,b).
To establish whether the observed reduction in excitability led
to a functional change in inhibitory inputs, we measured miniature
inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs). We found a reduction
in the frequency and amplitude of mIPSCs of 78% and 18%, respectively, in Kir2.1-expressing L5 ET pyramidal cells compared with
mutKir2.1-expressing L5 ET pyramidal cells at P10 (Fig. 1d,e). In addition, we observed an increased frequency (+44%) and amplitude (+27%)
of miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) in the same
cells (Fig. 1f,g). Thus, the ratio of the charge per second of excitatory
to inhibitory miniature postsynaptic currents (mPSCs) was increased
(Fig. 1h). Control experiments revealed that the reduction in mIPSCs
in Kir2.1-expressing cells was not solely due to a network effect, as

expression of Kir2.1 reduces the frequency and amplitude of mIPSCs
compared with neighboring uninfected cells (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d).
As a further control to ensure that this phenotype is not only due to the
viral infection, we also compared mutKir2.1-expressing cells with neighboring uninfected cells and did not find a reduction in mIPSC frequency
or amplitude (Extended Data Fig. 1e–h). Therefore, we conclude that
the phenotype we see is due specifically to the action of Kir2.1 and is
at least in part cell autonomous.
We then asked whether the decrease in inhibition was due to a
reduction in the number of PV+ presynaptic inputs, the primary source
of perisomatic inhibition of L5 ET neurons26,32,33. To this end, we labeled
control and Kir2.1-expressing L5 ET cells with tdTomato and quantified
the density of perisomatic PV+ boutons using the presynaptic marker
synaptotagmin-2 (Syt2), which is specifically expressed in PV+ basket
cells34. We observed a reduction of 32% in the density of Syt2+ boutons
contacting the soma of L5 ET neurons expressing Kir2.1 compared with
control neurons at P10 (Fig. 1i,j). Altogether, our results demonstrate
that neuronal activity during development regulates the number of
PV+ basket-cell inputs that L5 ET pyramidal cells receive and changes
the balance of excitatory and inhibitory input.

Fezf2 is regulated by neuronal activity in a cell-type-specific
manner
To identify the activity-dependent molecular mechanisms regulating
the development of PV+ perisomatic inhibition onto L5 ET pyramidal
neurons, we analyzed changes in the translatome of L5 ET neurons
following the modulation of their activity during postnatal development. To this end, we used viral translating ribosome affinity
purification (vTRAP)35 to isolate ribosome-associated mRNA from
pontine-projecting ET neurons expressing Kir2.1 or mutKir2.1. In
brief, we generated Cre-dependent AAVs expressing a hemagglutinin
(HA)-tagged ribosomal protein (RPL10A) and either Kir2.1 (Kir2.1-vTRAP)
or mutKir2.1 (mutKir2.1-vTRAP; Extended Data Fig. 2a). To verify that
Kir2.1-vTRAP decreases the excitability of L5 ET cells, we injected
Kir2.1-vTRAP and mutKir2.1-vTRAP AAVs into Rbp4cre/+ mice36, in which
Cre is expressed in most L5 pyramidal cells (Extended Data Fig. 2b). We
then assessed excitability using electrophysiology, and filled recorded
cells with neurobiotin to verify post hoc that they expressed the corresponding virus using immunohistochemistry against the HA-tag
(Extended Data Fig. 2c). As expected, the excitability of cells expressing
Kir2.1-vTRAP was decreased, as was the density of PV+ basket-cell inputs
they received, compared with neurons expressing mutKir2.1-vTRAP
(Extended Data Fig. 2d–h).

Fig. 3). We identified 673 differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

(Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table 1 and Extended Data Fig. 3). Gene

processes (Extended Data Fig. 3d). We focused on transcription factors and genes directly involved in synaptic assembly and synaptic

To confirm these observations, we transiently increased the excitability of L5 ET neurons using a chemogenetic approach based on
Designer Receptors Exclusively Activated by Designer Drugs (DREADDs). In brief, we injected rAAVretro-cre in the pons and AAVs encoding

specifically in hM3Dq-expressing neurons (Extended Data Fig. 4).
We then injected CNO twice daily between P8 and P10 and examined

the expression of Fezf2 at P10 (Fig. 3a). We found that the expression
of Fezf2 was increased (+24%) in hM3Dq-infected L5 ET cells in mice
treated with CNO compared with vehicle (Fig. 3b,c). These experiments
confirmed a bidirectional link between the activity of L5 ET pyramidal
cells and Fezf2 expression.
To investigate whether the activity-dependent expression of Fezf2
was cell-type specific or could be triggered by changes in activity in
other types of pyramidal cells, we expressed hM3Dq in the other primary type of pyramidal neurons in L5 (IT neurons). To this end, we used
Tlx3-cre mice, in which Cre expression is specific to L5 IT pyramidal
cells37. We found that increasing the activity of L5 IT neurons between P8
and P10 does not change the expression of Fezf2 in these cells (Fig. 3d–f).

We hypothesized that, as a cell-type-specific activity-regulated
gene, Fezf2 could function as an LRG and be activated by IEGs. To
assess whether IEGs, including Fos, can activate the Fezf2 promoter,
we searched for putative binding sites of the AP-1 complex, which
comprises a heterodimer of Fos/Jun family member proteins, within

the Fezf2 promoter region. We identified two putative AP-1 binding sites
within the 2.7 kb region upstream of the transcription start site that
was previously reported to drive Fezf2 expression reliably across the
developing cortex38,39. This region also contains a binding site for the
IEG EGR1 (Extended Data Fig. 6a). We engineered a luciferase reporter
fused to the 2.7 kb Fezf2 promoter and cotransfected this construct
with a combination of plasmids expressing the full-length Fos, Jun or
Egr1 cDNAs into HEK293 cells. Coexpression of Fos and Jun resulted in
more than a twofold increase in luciferase activity compared with the
promoter alone, while the combination of Fos, Jun and Egr1 produced
the highest activation of the promoter with a sixfold increase over control (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). These results suggest that the Fezf2 promoter can be activated by members of the FOS and JUN transcription

Fezf2 regulates the assembly and maintenance of inhibition
onto ET neurons
FEZF2 has a critical role in the specification of L5 ET neurons during
embryonic development27–30. We found that neuronal activity regulates the expression levels of Fezf2 in L5 ET cells during postnatal
development, suggesting that this transcription factor may have
additional roles in defining important features of these cells in an
activity-dependent manner, long after they have been specified. We
hypothesized that FEZF2 might regulate the density of perisomatic
PV+ inhibitory inputs received by L5 ET cells in an activity-dependent
manner. To test this idea, we engineered Cre-dependent AAVs

containing the coding sequence for green fluorescent protein (GFP)
and a short-hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequence against Fezf2. As a control,
we used an shRNA sequence against β-galactosidase (LacZ), which
does not target mammalian genes11. We verified the efficiency of
shFezf2 to downregulate the expression of Fezf2 in vitro and in vivo
(Extended Data Fig. 7a–e). We then injected rAAVretro-cre in the pons
and shRNA AAVs in the S1 at P1 and prepared acute slices from these
mice at P15–P17 to allow for sufficient downregulation (Fig. 4a).
Recordings of mIPSCs and mEPSCs in the same cell showed that, while
downregulation of Fezf2 reduced the frequency of mIPSCs (−45%),
it did not alter the frequency and amplitude of mEPSCs (Fig. 4a–e).
Consistent with this, reduction of Fezf2 expression increased the E/I
charge ratio (Fig. 4f). The mIPSC phenotype was replicated by measuring pharmacologically isolated mIPSCs (−45% mIPSC frequency;
Extended Data Fig. 7d,f,g).
Supporting the idea that a decrease in PV synapses is, at least
in part, contributing to the reduction of mIPSCs, the density of
somatic PV+ basket-cell boutons contacting L5 ET cells was decreased
(−18%; Fig. 4g,h). Rescue experiments using an shRNA-resistant Fezf2
(AAV-hSyn-HA-mutFezf2-T2A-EGFP-shFezf2) revealed that the phenotypes were caused by the specific downregulation of the target gene
rather than off-target effects (Fig. 4a–h). Interestingly, although no
change in mIPSC amplitude was observed between controls and shFezf2,
in the rescue experiments, the mIPSC amplitude was decreased, likely
due to a secondary effect of prolonged Fezf2 overexpression. These
experiments demonstrate that FEZF2 is cell-autonomously required for
the regulation of appropriate PV+ synapses to L5 ET neurons in response
to activity during development. To evaluate whether Fezf2 mediates
activity-dependent changes in inhibitory synapses, we activated
neurons using hM3Dq, while simultaneously modulating the expression of Fezf2 (Extended Data Fig. 8). While increased activity raised
the frequency of mIPSCs in control cells (Extended Data Fig. 8a–e),
this synaptic increase was blocked by downregulation of Fezf2
(Extended Data Fig. 8f–j). We observed no changes in mEPSCs under
any of these conditions (Extended Data Fig. 8). Altogether, our data
show that FEZF2 controls the PV interneuron inputs to ET pyramidal
cells in an activity-dependent manner.

Fezf2 continues to be expressed in L5 ET neurons in adulthood44,
suggesting that it may also regulate the maintenance of perisomatic inhibition in these cells later in life. To explore this possibility, we downregulated Fezf2 expression at the end of the PV+ synapse
formation period26. In brief, we injected a retrograde AAV encoding an inducible Cre (CreERT2) into the pontine nuclei and introduced either shFezf2 or shLacZ Cre-dependent AAVs into the S1 of
wild-type mice at P1. We then induced Cre-dependent recombination
by administering 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) intraperitoneally at
P21 (Extended Data Fig. 7h–i) and confirmed efficient downregulation of Fezf2 expression by P35 (Extended Data Fig. 7j,k). Our results
showed that, as in early knockdown experiments, late downregulation
of Fezf2 in L5 ET neurons reduces the density of Syt2+ boutons contacting the soma of these cells (−14%; Extended Data Fig. 7l,m). Altogether, our findings demonstrate that FEZF2 is an activity-dependent,
cell-type-specific transcription factor that regulates the formation and
maintenance of PV+ perisomatic synapses onto L5 ET pyramidal cells.

FEZF2 regulates Cdh22 expression for the assembly of
perisomatic PV+ inhibition
Because FEZF2 is a transcription factor, we sought to uncover other
LRGs that could be more directly involved in inhibitory synapse formation. Notably, the three top-ranked genes downregulated by a
reduction in the activity of L5 ET neurons, Cdh22, Cntn6 and Igsf21,
encode cell-surface molecules (Fig. 2c–f), a type of protein involved
in the formation and maintenance of synapses in the central nervous
system5,45–47. We hypothesized that one of these proteins could function downstream of FEZF2 to regulate PV+ perisomatic inhibition in
L5 ET cells. To test whether changes in activity could bidirectionally
regulate the expression of Cdh22 and Cntn6, we then increased the
activity of ET pyramidal neurons using chemogenetics and observed
a subsequent increase in the expression of Cdh22 (+34%) but not Cntn6
(Fig. 6a–d). Interestingly, activity promotes FOS binding to intronic
regions of the Cdh22 locus, but not of Cntn6 (Extended Data Fig. 6f).
To evaluate whether CDH22 functioned downstream of FEZF2, we
first assessed whether, after increasing activity, Fos, Fezf2 and Cdh22
expression followed a temporal pattern consistent with a successive
activation of these genes. Fos RNA was immediately upregulated, showing strong expression at 1 h after CNO injection, decreasing slightly at
6 h and being almost absent after 24 h (Extended Data Fig. 4d–f). Fezf2
expression was upregulated 6 h after CNO injection (+16%, Fig. 6e–g).
Cdh22 expression increased only between 6h and 24 h (+85%; Fig. 6e–g),
demonstrating sequential upregulation of Fos followed by increased
expression of Fezf2 and later Cdh22.

flexibility to cope with fluctuations in neuronal activity during
ongoing environmental changes15. Pyramidal cells maintain their

Our experiments demonstrated that neuronal activity regulates Cdh22

density of PV+ perisomatic synapses contacting L5 ET pyramidal cells.
The increase in both PV inputs and Cdh22 expression mediated by activity can be prevented by reducing Fezf2 levels. Likewise, the decrease
in PV+ perisomatic synapses in ET neurons expressing shFezf2 can be
rescued by overexpressing Cdh22. These findings show that Cdh22 is a
downstream effector of FEZF2 in the activity-dependent regulation of
inhibitory synapses. While overexpression of Kir2.1 led to an increase
in mEPSC frequency and amplitude, knockdown of Fezf2 or Cdh22
did not affect mEPSCs, suggesting that activity-dependent plasticity
of excitatory and inhibitory synapses may use different molecular
pathways. CDH22 can form homophilic and heterophilic interactions
with other cadherin-family members50. Of these, Cdh7 expression is
enriched in PV+ basket cells during postnatal development11,51, suggesting a potential direct mechanism for the involvement of CDH22 in PV+
basket-cell synapse formation onto ET neurons. We also observed that
the downregulation of Cntn6 does not affect the number of inhibitory
inputs targeting L5 ET pyramidal cells. Thus, Cntn6 may have a different role in shaping the maturation or connectivity of L5 ET neurons.
The excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance in the cerebral cortex
is essential for the stabilization of neural circuit function52, as well
as cortical functions such as learning and memory53,54. To maintain
an appropriate E/I balance in response to changes associated with
sensory experience, pyramidal cells recruit inhibitory input in an
activity-dependent manner3,16,17. This is mediated by the rapid initial
expression of IEGs, most of which encode transcription factors that
regulate a second wave of LRGs22. While most IEGs encode transcription
factors such as FOS, JUN and NPAS4 (ref. 22), LRGs often encode molecules that are secreted or localized to the synapse55–60. These molecular
programs generate the foundation for structural and functional modifications at the synapses. In the last decade, it has become clear that
activity-regulated gene expression is not homogeneous across neuron
types. Instead, while a common set of IEGs can be expressed across all
neurons, the complement of LRGs that a neuron expresses in response
to IEG induction often varies from one neuron type to another58,61,62.
This cell-type specificity is hypothesized to come about through the
combined action of commonly expressed activity-dependent transcription factors and cell-type-specific transcription factors that are
not activity regulated at the same enhancers63, but such factors have
not been identified.
We have demonstrated that activity regulates the expression
of the transcription factor Fezf2 in ET cells but not in other pyramidal cells. To our knowledge, this is an example of a cell-type-specific
transcription factor that controls the identity of the neuron and is
later repurposed and regulated by activity to shape the wiring of the
same neuron during development. While our data cannot exclude
activity-independent functions of Fezf2, our findings demonstrate that
Fezf2 and Cdh22 regulate inhibitory synapse formation and E/I balance
through an activity-dependent mechanism. We have evidence that
IEGs may regulate Fezf2. Thus, in addition to directly triggering known
effector molecules like BDNF, IGF1 and SCG2 (refs. 3,63), IEGs may also
regulate intermediate transcription factors that confer specificity to
the wiring of specific populations of neurons, such as Fezf2. Interestingly, the transcription factor Etv1/Er81 is required for the development
of specific proprioceptive neuron connectivity in the spinal cord64
and is an activity-regulated gene in PV interneurons65. It remains to be
explored whether ETV11 has a role similar to FEZF2 in other neurons.
Fezf2 is a selector gene for L5 ET pyramidal cells, as its removal
in embryos causes a loss of cell-type identity27–30. Interestingly, visual
experience regulates upper-layer cell identity but not deep-layer cell
identity in the visual cortex66. In addition, previous work has shown that

Fezf2 cannot reprogram upper-layer cell identity after the first postnatal week67. Although we cannot rule out the possibility that complete
deletion of Fezf2 in ET neurons postnatally might lead to loss of cell
identity, we found that downregulating Fezf2 expression by 50% during
the postnatal period—an approach more similar to the Kir2.1 hyperpolarization experiments and likely closer to in vivo Fezf2 expression
dynamics—does not affect major cell identity features. The dual role
of Fezf2 during development is probably due to changes in the target
genes it interacts with at different developmental stages. Consistent
with this idea, Fezf2 downregulation in adult mice affects a markedly different set of genes than during development30,44. Our findings revealed
that Fezf2 expression is regulated by neuronal activity postnatally.
Other transcription factors also appear to be activity regulated during
postnatal stages55,59,68–70. For example, Satb1 is expressed during early
development, but its expression is only influenced by activity after
birth70. Fezf2 may follow a similar two-stage regulatory process, being
activity-independent before birth and activity dependent afterward.
It is tempting to speculate that cell-type-specific transcription
factors regulated by IEGs may function as master regulators coordinating multiple transcription programs. Master regulators would
differ among neurons to better fine-tune the circuitry depending on
cell-type-specific demands. In this scenario, what better choice for a
neuron than recycling a selector gene—an already cell-type-specific
transcription factor—and using it postnatally in an activity-dependent
manner? Future investigations should aim to elucidate whether other
neurons use cell-type-specific activity-regulated transcription factors
or whether this is a unique feature of ET pyramidal cells.

CNO treatment
CNO (Tocris Bioscience, 4936) was dissolved in 0.5% dimethyl sulfoxide
(DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, D0418) in 0.9% saline and stored at −20 °C.
Vehicle control of 0.5% DMSO in 0.9% saline was used. Mice were treated
with CNO for 48 h starting at P8—two injections per day (morning,
between 8 and 9:30 a.m. and evening, between 6 and 8 p.m.) for a total
of five injections. Animals were then perfused starting 2 h after the

In vitro electrophysiology
For all experiments, an experimental animal and an age-matched—
and, where possible, sex-matched—littermate control was used for
electrophysiology on the same day. Mice were deeply anesthetized
with an overdose of sodium pentobarbital and transcardially perfused
with 10 ml ice-cold slicing solution containing 87 mM NaCl, 75 mM
sucrose, 26 mM NaHCO3, 11 mM glucose, 7 mM MgCl2, 2.5 mM KCl,
1.25 mM NaH2PO4 and 0.5 mM CaCl2, oxygenated with 95% O2 and 5%
CO2. The brain was quickly removed, and the injected hemisphere
was glued to a cutting platform before being submerged in ice-cold
slicing solution. Then, 300-µm-thick coronal slices containing S1 were
cut using a vibratome (Leica, VT1200S) and incubated for 45–60 min
at 32 °C, and subsequently at room temperature in the same solution.
All salts were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Slices were transferred
to the recording setup and superfused with recording artificial cerebrospinal fluid containing 124 mM NaCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 3 mM
KCl, 26 mM NaHCO3, 10 mM glucose, 2 mM CaCl2 and 1 mM MgCl2,
which was oxygenated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 and heated to 34 °C.
Pipettes (3–5 MΩ) were made from borosilicate glass capillaries using
a PC-10 pipette puller (P10; Narishige) and filled with intracellular
solution containing 70 mM K-gluconate, 70 mM KCl, 10 mM HEPES,
4 mM Mg-ATP, 4 mM K2-phosphocreatine and 0.4 mM GTP, adjusted
with KOH to pH 7.3 (±290 mOsm), or 125 mM Cs-gluconate, 5 mM CsCl,
4 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES, 0.2 mM EGTA, 2 mM K2-phosphocreatine,
2 mM Mg-ATP, 0.3 mM GTP, adjusted with KOH to pH 7.3 (±290 mOsm).
Where applicable, 1% neurobiotin (Vector Laboratories) was added to
the intracellular solution.
Recordings were made using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier
(Molecular Devices). The signal was passed through a Hum Bug Noise
Eliminator (Digitimer), sampled at 50 kHz (intrinsic properties) or
10 kHz (synaptic currents) and filtered at 3 kHz using a Digidata 1440A
(Molecular Devices). Cells were excluded if the access resistance (Ra)
exceeded 30 MΩ. Resting membrane potential (Vm) was measured in
current-clamp mode for 5–10 s immediately after breaking in. Intrinsic
properties were measured by applying a series of negative or positive current injections while maintaining a baseline Vm of −70 mV,
at a step size of 25 pA. Active and passive properties were analyzed
in MATLAB (MathWorks) using custom scripts. Input resistance was
calculated as the linear slope of the current-voltage (I–V) relationship of the last 200 ms of all negative stimuli. Voltage sag was measured during the current step in which the peak voltage deflection
was nearest to −90 mV, and was calculated as the percentage change
between the peak of the response and the average voltage deflection of the last 200 ms of the same step. Rheobase was determined as
the average first current injection to elicit an action potential across
three series of current steps at a step size of 2 pA. Isolated mIPSCs
recordings were performed at a holding potential of −76 mV in the
presence of 1 µM tetrodotoxin (HB1035) 10 µM 2,3-dioxo-6-nitro-1,2,3,
4-tetrahydrobenzo(f)quinoxaline-7-sulfonamide (NBQX, HB0443) and
50 µM D-(−)-2-amino-5-phosphonopentanoic acid (D-APV, HB0225),
all of which were purchased from Hello Bio. mEPSCs and mIPSCs were
measured from the same cells using cesium-based intracellular recording at holding potentials of −65 mV (mEPSCs) or +10 mV (mIPSCs) in
the presence of 1 µM tetrodotoxin. mPSCs and mIPSCs were analyzed
using Mini Analysis (SynaptoSoft).

were detected using the findpeaks function with MinPeakProminence
3 and MinPeakDistance 20, and the distances between each CDH22-HA
peak and the closest Syt2 peak were calculated. Subsequently, for each
cell, shuffled data were produced by randomly generating ‘peak’ positions while maintaining the total length of the profile and the number of
peaks in each channel. The average distance between each CDH22-HA
position and the closest Syt2 position was calculated for each of 1,000
iterations, creating the expected distribution of distances between
CDH22-HA and Syt2 if localization of these proteins was uncorrelated.
The observed mean distance for each cell was then compared with this
distribution, generating a z score for that value.

RNA-seq

Ribosome-associated RNA isolation. Mice were deeply anesthetized with an overdose of sodium pentobarbital. The infected cortex was rapidly dissected in ice-cold RNase-free PBS. Lower layers
of S1 were dissected out and immediately homogenized in ice-cold
lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 12 mM MgCl2,
1 mg ml−1 heparin (Sigma-Aldrich), complete EDTA-free protease
inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich), 200 U ml−1 RNasin (Promega), 100 µg ml−1

cycloheximide and 1 mM dithiothreitol (Sigma-Aldrich)). Tissue from
two to three brains were pooled per sample. Samples were centrifuged
at 2,000g for 10 min at 4 °C, and IGEPAL CA-630 (Sigma-Aldrich) was
added to the samples to a final concentration of 1%. Samples were then
centrifuged at 13,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was added to
100 µl of anti-HA magnetic beads (Pierce, 88837) for 3–4 h at 4 °C with
gentle rotation. After incubation, beads were washed thrice in ice-cold
washing buffer (300 mM KCl, 1% IGEPAL CA-630, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH
7.5, 12 mM MgCl2, 1 mM dithiothreitol and 100 µg ml−1 cycloheximide)
and eluted in 350 µl of RLT Plus buffer (RNeasy Plus Micro kit, Qiagen)
supplemented with 2-mercaptoethanol (Bio-Rad).
RNA purification, library preparation and sequencing. Purification
of immunoprecipitated RNA was performed using the RNeasy Plus
Micro kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality of immunoprecipitated RNA was verified on a 2100 Bioanalyzer
instrument (Agilent Technologies) using an RNA 6000 Pico Chip. All
RNA samples had RNA integrity number values of 9.8 or higher. Three
biological replicates were analyzed for each genotype. Library preparation and RNA-seq experiments were performed by the Genomics Unit
of the Centre for Genomic Regulation. Libraries were prepared using
the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England
Biolabs, 7490) and the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep
Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, 7760) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were then sequenced in paired-end mode
using an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform to a minimum of 100 million
mapped reads per sample.
RNA-seq analysis. Bioinformatic analyses were carried out by GenoSplice. Briefly, data quality was assessed using FastQC and RSeQC75.
Reads were mapped using STAR_2.4.0 (ref. 76) to the reference genome
mm10. Counts were obtained through FAST DB (v2018_1), gene annotations (GenoSplice) and normalization using DESeq2 (ref. 77). One
sample (mutKir1) did not cluster sufficiently and was excluded from
further differential gene analysis and selection of target genes. GO
analysis was performed using WebGestalt78 with the terms biological
process, cellular component and molecular function, as well as the
KEGG and Reactome pathway databases. GO terms were considered
to be significantly enriched at a false discovery rate <0.05.
To select gene targets, DEGs within the following PANTHER GO
terms were considered: ‘cell adhesion molecule’ (PC00069), ‘scaffold/adaptor protein’ (PC00226) and ‘C2H2 zinc-finger transcription
factor’ (PC00248). Genes were then ranked using three criteria. First,
genes were scored according to the expression FC between Kir2.1 and
mutKir2.1. Second, because low-count genes are often unreliable, genes
were ranked according to their expression levels in mutKir2.1 controls.
Third, to bias toward ET-specific genes, genes were scored according
to the differences in expression levels between ET cells and IT cells
using snRNA-seq data from the Allen Brain Institute database79. These
criteria were converted into a single rank score by calculating z scores
for each criterion and averaging the absolute z score of criterion 1 and
the z scores of criteria 2 and 3. Rankings for all criteria and the final rank
score are shown in Fig. 2c.

ChIP–seq analysis
ChIP–seq was performed on whole hippocampi of mice treated with
KA or saline. For each condition, three biological replicates were generated, each derived from an individual mouse. A Dounce homogenizer
was used to dissociate hippocampal tissue suspended in ice-cold
hypotonic NEB fixing buffer containing 250 mM sucrose, 25 mM KCl,
5 mM MgCl2, 20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.8), 0.5% IGEPAL CA-630, 0.2 mM
spermine, 0.5 mM spermidine, 1× protease inhibitor and 1.1% PFA. The
homogenate was incubated at room temperature for 15 min followed
by incubation with 0.125 M glycine for 5 min to stop the fixation and
was washed twice by centrifugation with ice-cold NEB buffer without

PFA. The pellet of nuclei was suspended in 400 μl of RIPA lysis buffer
(1% IGEPAL CA-630, 0.1 SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, PBS 1×; pH
8) before sonication in a Sonifier digital SFX 250 (Branson) for nine
15-s on/30-s off cycles. After 15 min of centrifugation at 17,000g, the
supernatant containing isolated chromatin was collected. Samples
were diluted in 4,000 μl of ChIP dilution buffer (0.01% SDS, 1.1% Triton
X-100, 1.2 mM EDTA, 16.7 mM Tris–HCl, 167 mM NaCl; pH 8) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the α-Fos monoclonal antibody (rabbit;
Thermo Fisher Scientific, T.142.5). The next day the chromatin was
incubated 3 h with 60 μl of slurry Dynabeads coated to Protein G (Invitrogen) at 4 °C. Beads were thoroughly rinsed at 4 °C as follows: two
washes in RIPA-150 buffer, three washes in RIPA-500 buffer (50 mM
Tris–HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1%
sodium deoxycholate; pH 8), two washes in RIPA LiCl buffer (50 mM
Tris–HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 0.7%, sodium deosycholate, 500 mM
LiCl2) and two final washes in TE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM
EDTA; pH 8.0), for 5 min each. Samples were resuspended in 200 μl Elution buffer (1% SDS, 0.1 M NaHCO3) and treated with 1 μl of 10 mg ml−1
RNase A (Fermentas) overnight at 65 °C with agitation for crosslink
reversion. Samples were treated with 2 μl of 20 mg ml−1 proteinase
K (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 5 h at 55 °C with agitation. Finally,
DNA precipitation was performed by phenol-chloroform-isoamyl
alcohol (Sigma-Aldrich). As a control, a parallel input sample (without
antibody) was processed to assess background signal. Sequencing
(50 bp single end) was performed using a HiSeq 2500 sequencer. Raw
reads were trimmed with trim_galore (v0.6.4_dev)80 and aligned to
the mm10 genome using Bowtie 2 (v2.3.4.3)81. Reads with mapq > 30
were retained using SAMtools (v1.9)82. Peak calling was performed
with MACS2 (v2.1.1.20160309)83 using default parameters. Differential
binding analysis was performed with the DiffBind package (v3.12)84
using the following parameters: minOverlap = 2 and summits = FALSE
for count calculation and minMembers = 2 for contrast generation.
Profile plots were generated using deepTools (v3.5.0)85 and visualized
with Integrative Genome Viewer (v2.17.4)86.

snRNA-seq analysis
For the reanalysis of snRNA-seq data, processed expression matrices
were downloaded from GSE158843 (ref. 3) and analyzed in R (v4.5;
R Core Team, 2021) using Seurat (v5.4.0)87. Sample matrices were
assigned metadata, including the original sample identifier and
whether the hippocampal hemisphere had been virally injected with
Cre. Control samples and Cre+ samples were merged separately and
assessed for viral expression to exclude Cre+ cells in the control and
to exclude Cre− cells in the Cre+ samples. Matrices were merged, and
cells were then preprocessed for RNA quantity, number of RNA species and mitochondrial reads. RNA from merged matrices were then
normalized, queried for variable features and scaled and centered.
Principal component analysis was performed for 100 principal components (PCs) that were then tested for significance of contribution
through JackStraw testing. Of those 100 PCs, 67 were found to be
significant when compared with a randomized shuffle. Thus, when
performing k-nearest neighbors, we included those 67 PCs for dimensionality reduction. Default Leiden clustering was then performed
using the Annoy algorithm, followed by additional uniform manifold
approximation and projection (UMAP) reduction of PC space using
the first 67 PC dimensions. Matrices were finally merged using default
Harmony algorithm88 to correct PCs for sample-dependent variance,
and samples were reprocessed for k-nearest neighbors, Leiden clustering and UMAP reduction. Harmonized clusters were assigned cell
types by first determining DEGs of each harmonized cluster using a
Model-based Analysis of Single-cell Transcriptomics89 to iteratively
compare each cluster to all other clusters. DEGs were thresholded
by requiring a minimum of 25% of cells in the cluster to express a 0.25
log2(FC) increase compared with all other cells. We then assessed the
list of DEGs using markers from the literature for all cell types, for

example, CA1 (Pou3f1, Satb2), CA3 (Grik4)90 and DG (Prox1)91. All cell
identities were corroborated with the Allen Brain Mouse Cell Type
Atlas92. We then performed Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Bonferroni
multiple comparisons correction to perform differential gene expression between FFJ-WT and FFJ-KO nuclei using default ‘FindMarkers’
function. Plots were generated using scCustomize on Zenodo (v3.2.4)93
and SeuratExtend (v1.2.1)94.

Statistical analyses
All statistical tests were performed in Prism 10 (GraphPad). Outliers
were identified using the ROUT method, with Q = 1. Normality of data
was assessed using Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Normally distributed data were analyzed using a Student’s t-test in the case of equal
variances between groups and a Welch’s t-test in the case of unequal
variance (>2-fold difference in s.d. between groups). For nonparametric data, a Mann–Whitney U test was used. For data containing three
experimental groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed
by Tukey’s post hoc tests was used, or a Brown–Forsythe ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons tests if the assumption of
sphericity was not met. The synaptic densities shown in Figs. 4h and
7n were analyzed using a single one-way ANOVA, as they share control
groups. For these figures, rescue experiments were performed after
the initial knockdown experiment was completed. When performing
the rescue experiments, some animals were injected with shLacZ and
shFezf2 to verify that baseline Syt2 density values for these groups
were comparable to the original dataset. The distribution of these
is shown in Extended Data Fig. 9g. The luciferase assay was analyzed
using a two-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s LSD test. Nonparametric
data were analyzed using a Mann–Whitney U test. For in vitro validation of shRNA sequences, each sequence was compared with shLacZ
using a t-test and Bonferroni correction was applied. For analysis of
the correlation of RNA signal values in the Fezf2 overexpression experiments, Pearson correlation was used. To assess the Fraction of CTIP+/
GFP+ cells, a chi-square test was used. A mixed-effects analysis was
used to assess the effect of the experimental group on Sholl analysis
values. P values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All
data are presented as mean ± s.e.m. All statistical analyses are shown
in the Supplementary Table 2. No statistical methods were used to
predetermine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those
reported in previous publications11,26,95.
