The growing crisis of antimicrobial resistance demands urgent need for
antibiotics with alternative targets and modes of action (MOAs). Lipoproteins
play crucial roles in bacterial survival and immunoregulation. The lipoprotein
transporter, known as LolCDE or LolDF, has recently emerged as an effective
target for selectively killing pathogenic bacteria such as Acinetobacter
baumannii while sparing gut microbiota. While the heterodimeric LolCDE in
Escherichia coli has been extensively studied, the druggable pocket and
structural dynamics of the distinct homodimeric LolDF that exists in many
critical pathogens are poorly understood. Such a knowledge gap limits our
ability to exploit the Lol system to develop drugs with desired spectra of
antibacterial activity. Here we determine the cryo-EM structures of homodimeric
LolDF of A. baumannii in nucleotide-free apo-closed, vanadate-trapped fully
closed, and inhibitor-bound open conformations, revealing the distinct
structural features and conformational cycle of LolDF. Further cryo-EM,
biochemical and functional analyses uncover the MOA of abaucin, a recently
identified LolDF-targeting compound, demonstrating how multiple abaucin
molecules open LolDF in a stepwise manner to establish an induced-fit pocket.
Together, our results advance the understanding of LolDF function and
inhibition, and provide the cryptic druggable conformation and specific
inhibitor-bound pocket for structure-based drug discovery to target the dynamic
lipoprotein transporter in A. baumannii.

Introduction

Multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacteria are a leading cause of public health infections. These pathogens adapt to harsh environmental conditions and quickly develop resistance to a wide array of antibiotics, including the last resort colistin, carbapenems, and tigecycline1. Acinetobacter baumannii is a prevalent nosocomial Gram-negative pathogen that causes a serious threat in health care institutions, and was categorized as the most critical multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogen by the WHO and CDC1. The growing crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) demands an urgent need for antibiotics. Developing new antibiotics to combat MDR Gram-negative bacteria is particularly challenging because of their distinct membrane structure.

Gram-negative bacteria produce dual membranes consisting of the inner membrane (IM) and outer membrane (OM). The IM contains symmetric lipid leaflets of phospholipids, while the OM is an asymmetric lipid bilayer consisting of phospholipids, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), outer membrane β-barrel proteins (OMPs), and lipoproteins2. The outer membrane serves as a permeation barrier, helps maintain optimal membrane fluidity, facilitates proper cellular function, and confers antibiotic resistance3–7. Previous studies have demonstrated that colistin resistance in A. baumannii can result from mutational inactivation of genes essential for LPS biosynthesis. In response to total LPS loss, specific lipoproteins are overexpressed and presented on the cell surface8,9. Targeting lipoprotein biosynthesis has emerged as a promising strategy in the development of antimicrobial agents10–13.

Lipoproteins are otherwise soluble proteins that are anchored to the bacterial membrane by lipids covalently linked to their N-terminal cysteine14. Lipoproteins fulfill critical roles in outer membrane integrity15, peptidoglycan biogenesis16, cell division17, and modulation of the immune response of the host18. The lipoprotein processing and sorting pathways have been extensively studied in Escherichia coli. The preprolipoprotein that has an N-terminal secretion signal containing a consensus sequence is processed in the cytosol and translocated across the IM through SecYEG19. The maturation of the preprolipoprotein occurs on the outer leaflet of the IM by sequential functions of three enzymes, prolipoprotein diacylglyceryl transferase (Lgt)20, lipoprotein signal peptidase (Lsp)21,22 and phospholipid/apolipoprotein transacylase (Lnt). Lnt catalyzes the addition of the third acyl chain, converting the diacylated lipoprotein to its final triacylated form23–25(Supplementary Fig. 1a). Subsequently, lipoproteins either remain in the IM or are exported to the OM by the lipoprotein outer membrane localization (Lol) pathway25. The Lol pathway comprises three distinct components, an IM ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter complex (LolCDE in E. coli), a periplasmic chaperone (LolA), and an OM lipoprotein (LolB). LolCDE extracts lipoproteins from the IM at the expense of ATP hydrolysis and transfers them to LolA. LolA then shuttles the lipoprotein across the periplasm for delivery to LolB. Finally, LolB facilitates lipoprotein insertion into the inner leaflet of the OM26,27.

Many Gram-negative pathogens, such as A. baumannii, Francisella tularensis, Neisseria meningitidis, and Helicobacter pylori, express a homodimer of LolF proteins instead of a heterodimer of LolC and LolE4,28,29, forming the homodimeric lipoprotein transporter LolDF30–33 (Supplementary Fig. 1b). In LolCDE-containing bacteria such as E. coli, Lnt is essential for bacterial growth, which is consistent with LolCDE recognizing the triacylated form of lipoproteins24. Interestingly, A. baumannii can tolerate Lnt deletion, suggesting that LolDF recognizes diacylated as well as triacylated lipoproteins and transfers both types of substrate to LolA for sorting to the OM28(Fig. 1a). Due to its importance in OM localization of lipoproteins and antibiotic resistance, and the distinct homodimeric architecture compared to the heterodimeric LolCDE, A. baumannii LolDF is an attractive target for developing an alternative class of antibacterial drugs. Deep-learning-guided discovery of an antibacterial compound, abaucin, was recently reported to inhibit the growth of A. baumannii, and surprisingly, not to affect gut microbiota38. It has been shown that abaucin perturbs lipoprotein trafficking involving the lipoprotein transporter LolDF. The intriguing selectivity of abaucin likely stems from the difference between LolDF in the pathogen and LolCDE in gut bacteria, thus pointing to a promising strategy of developing narrow-spectrum antibiotics. However, direct biochemical evidence or structural information of abaucin-LolDF interaction is lacking, excluding the possibility of structure-based discovery of small molecules that specifically target LolDF in many pathogenic bacteria. Therefore, to exploit LolDF as a therapeutic target, it is imperative to understand its distinct structural landscape, conformational cycle, and how it can be inhibited, knowledge that remains entirely elusive.

In this work, we determine the structures of LolDF of A. baumannii in its major functional states, and reveal unexpected conformational plasticity of LolDF to accommodate multiple abaucin molecules. These results not only advance our mechanistic understanding of the function and small-molecule inhibition of bacterial lipoprotein transporters but also provide the cryptic druggable conformation and specific inhibitor-bound pocket for structure-based drug discovery targeting the dynamic lipoprotein transporter in A. baumannii.

ResultsPurification and biochemical characterization of A. baumannii LolDFThe lol gene organization varies among Gram-negative bacteria. For example, E. coli has a lolCDE operon, while A. baumannii contains a lolDF operon. To generate the two transmembrane domains (TMDs) in the Lol ABC transporter, A. baumannii uses a homodimer of LolF instead of a heterodimer of LolC and LolE. The A. baumannii LolF is 38% identical and 59% similar to E. coli LolC/E. As the more conserved nucleotide binding domains (NBDs), the A. baumannii LolD shares 53% identity and 69% similarity with E. coli LolD (Supplementary Fig. 1c–f). To better understand the function and structure of the homodimeric LolDF in a native-like lipid environment, we set out to purify the A. baumannii LolDF complex (AbLolDF) and reconstitute it into lipid nanodiscs.AbLolDF was overexpressed in E. coli strain BL21(DE3), purified in dodecyl maltoside (DDM) detergent, and reconstituted in nanodiscs with palmitoyl-oleoyl-phosphatidylglycerol (POPG) (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). The variable stoichiometry observed in size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) profiles obtained in DDM likely reflects the co-purification of incomplete complexes enriched in free LolD. In contrast, nanodisc reconstitution enriches for fully assembled, functional LolDF complexes. The ATPase activity of AbLolDF in nanodiscs was ~3 times higher than that in DDM, indicating that the lipid membrane is important to support the transporter function (Fig. 1b). AbLolDF with increasing concentrations of ATP demonstrated a Km value of 0.523 ± 0.136 mM and a Vmax value of 47.9 ± 3.5 mole of phosphate per min per mole transporter complex (Fig. 1c). In comparison, the ATPase activity of E. coli LolCDE (EcLolCDE) is ~two-fold higher (Supplementary Fig. 2c), reflecting the differences in their ATPase activities and likely in conformational dynamics.Nucleotide-free LolDF in apo-closed conformationThe nanodisc-embedded AbLolDF was subjected to single-particle cryo-EM analysis, generating a three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction at an overall resolution of 4.0-Å (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 3). In this structure of nucleotide-free AbLolDF, the two LolD proteins as NBDs are associated together (Fig. 1d). The two LolF proteins as TMDs each contains an N-terminal elbow helix (EH), four TM helices (TM1-4), a large periplasmic domain (PD) between TM1 and TM2, a coupling helix (CH) between TM2 and TM3, and a shoulder loop (SL) between TM3 and TM4 (Fig. 1e). Within the membrane, the two TM2 helices are positioned at the interface between the two TMDs (Fig. 1e). Each LolF interacts with one LolD via the CH and C-terminal sequence of LolF.Distinct from the nucleotide-free EcLolCDE that is in the outward-open conformation with a lipoprotein bound inside the TMDs34, AbLolDF adopts a closed conformation with its TM1 and TM2 tightly associated, leaving no space for lipoprotein binding (Fig. 1d). Thus, the structure of nucleotide-free AbLolDF represents an apo-closed state of Lol transporters, which had not, to the best of our knowledge, been identified in previous studies35,36. Using corresponding helices in EcLolCDE as reference, the TM1 and TM2 of LolF close inward by ~4.5 and ~6.3 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5j). Gly33, Val44, Val47 and Glu54 in TM1 form close contacts with Asn285, Leu275, Val271 and Glu267 in opposing TM2 of LolF (Supplementary Fig. 5k). Leu260, Phe261, Lys268, Leu275, Ile279 and Met292 in both TM2 helices form hydrophobic contacts (Supplementary Fig. 5l).Compared to EcLolCDE, AbLolDF has more closely associated NBDs (Supplementary Fig. 5d). Nevertheless, the ATP binding site between the NBDs of AbLolDF remains open by ~7.6 Å (Supplementary Fig. 8c), with no nucleotide density observed in the pocket, which is in line with the nucleotide-free state. More notable differences between AbLolDF and EcLolCDE are in the PD and SL regions (Supplementary Fig. 5a). The porter domains within the periplasmic domains (PDs) of LolF, which contain the characteristic β-hairpin ‘hooks’, are positioned more closely to each other compared to those in EcLolCDE (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b, e). As compared with the SL regions of EcLolC (residues ~339–361) and EcLolE (residues ~343–378), the SL of AbLolF (residues ~345–375) is about five residues shorter than the SL of EcLolE, and eight residues longer than the SL of EcLolC (Supplementary Fig. 1d). A shorter shoulder helix is formed in the SL sequence of LolF. Since the relatively long SL of LolE plays a crucial role in substrate recognition by making hydrophobic contacts with two lipoprotein acyl chains (R2 and R3)33, the shorter SL of AbLolF may lose specific interaction with the acyl chains of lipoprotein (Supplementary Fig. 5g, h). Due to the lack of a lipoprotein-bound structure, the mechanism by which AbLolF mediates lipoprotein recognition remains unclear.Nucleotide-bound LolDF in closed conformationTo understand the overall conformational transition cycle of AbLolDF, sodium ortho-vanadate was used to trap the transporter in the nucleotide-bound state. Vanadate acts as a potent inhibitor of ATPase by forming an ADP-vanadate complex that mimics a tetrahedral intermediate state39. The ATPase activity of AbLolDF was inhibited by ~77% in the presence of 100 µM vanadate and 2 mM ATP (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 2d). Our cryo-EM analysis generated the structure of vanadate-trapped AbLolDF at 3.6-Å resolution, showing tightly dimerized LolD with ADP-vanadate bound in the ATP sites (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Fig. 4c–g). The LolD dimers trapped with vanadate in AbLolDF and EcLolCDE are highly similar, with an RMSD of 1.223 Å over 443 Cα atoms (Supplementary Fig. 5n), in consistence with the conserved structures of NBDs in ABC transporters. Importantly, the two LolF proteins are tightly associated together, leaving no space in between (Fig. 1f). Thus, the vanadate-trapped LolDF exhibits a fully closed conformation (Supplementary Fig. 5j, k), representing a state after substrate release.Notably, the TMDs of vanadate-trapped and nucleotide-free AbLolDF are essentially identical, with an RMSD of 1.637 Å over 776 Cα atoms (Supplementary Fig. 5m), indicating that, after ATP binding-induced TMD closure and substrate release, the TMDs remain closed until the binding of the next substrate. It is conceivable that AbLolDF in the apo-closed conformation selects lipoproteins that can open the TMDs, a mechanism underlying substrate specificity. Such conformational transition is reminiscent of human ABCG2, which switches from the apo-closed to inward-open conformation during drug binding40.TMD-open conformation LolDF bound to two abaucin moleculesThe druggable conformation and exact drug-binding pocket of LolDF had been elusive. Despite previous efforts of structural alignment using predicted Lol structures38, the exact abaucin-targeted LolDF conformation and pockets are not known. We first confirmed the bacteriostatic activity of abaucin (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b), and then characterized the ATPase activity of AbLolDF, showing a 51% reduction in the presence of 50 µM abaucin (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Nucleotide-bound LolDF in closed conformationTo understand the overall conformational transition cycle of AbLolDF, sodium ortho-vanadate was used to trap the transporter in the nucleotide-bound state. Importantly, the two LolF proteins are tightly associated together, leaving no space in between (Fig. 1f). It is conceivable that AbLolDF in the apo-closed conformation selects lipoproteins that can open the TMDs, a mechanism underlying substrate specificity. Such conformational transition is reminiscent of human ABCG2, which switches from the apo-closed to inward-open conformation during drug binding40.

A combination of cryo-EM, biochemical and functional analyses has revealed the abaucin binding pocket, revealing the distinct conformational plasticity of AbLolDF upon abaucin binding.

Our discovery of an apo-closed conformation for AbLolDF is different from the previously reported apo state for the EcLolCDE complex34–37. A notable distinction between these two apo states lies in the opening or closure of TMD. Our study describes an apo (lipoprotein-free) and TMD-closed state, whereas the reported EcLolCDE apo state was in TMD-open conformation. Due to the absence of endogenously co-purified lipoproteins in the nucleotide-free apo-closed structure of AbLolDF, this strongly suggests species-specific substrate recognition specificity. Consequently, critical information about the lipoprotein-binding pocket remains unclear. Notably, the small-molecule inhibitor abaucin has been reported to target AbLolDF38, however, the direct structural evidence for abaucin binding to AbLolDF is absent. This gap represents a major barrier to rational drug design targeting AbLolDF.

Our biochemical assays demonstrate that abaucin inhibits the ATPase activity, and the structural analyses suggest that abaucin binds and stabilizes LolDF in different conformations. Two abaucin molecules open up the apo-closed conformation, and four abaucin molecules further split two TMDs open to generate a wider open conformation. When LolDF adopts TMD-open conformations, multiple small molecules bind in the pocket formed by the TM1, TM2 and SL from the two opposing LolF proteins (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). Although these molecules bind in the overlapping pocket, the molecules exhibit distinct binding modes (Supplementary Fig. 9). As compared with the previously reported lipoprotein-interacting pockets in EcLolCDE (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b, d, e, g–i), the abaucin-binding pocket exhibits distinctions in the electrostatic potential surface (Supplementary Fig. 10c), hydrophobicity surface (Supplementary Fig. 10f) and interacting residues (Supplementary Fig. 10j–l). Notably, abaucin loses the specific side-chain interactions with the SL regions. The results suggest that the distinct abaucin-binding pocket may contribute to altered substrate recognition and provide the structural basis for the species- selective inhibition by abaucin.

Our results have provided detailed insights into the structure and mechanism of the homodimeric LolDF, allowing us to propose a working model for the function and inhibition of LolDF (Fig. 4). In this model, the resting LolDF is in the apo-closed conformation with the TMDs tightly closed and NBDs loosely associated (state 1). Lipoproteins in the outer leaflet of the inner membrane interact with the TMDs, which leads to the opening of the TMDs and the entry of lipoprotein (state 2). While the exact conformation of lipoprotein-bound LolDF is not yet known, various compounds such as abaucin mimic lipoprotein to induce different TMD-open conformations of LolDF and occupy the central cavity, resulting in the blockage of lipoprotein transport (state 2’). After ATP hydrolysis and dissociation from the NBDs, the TMDs remain closed (state 1) until the next lipoprotein substrate binds and opens the TMDs.
