BACKGROUND: The inner dynein arm (IDA) plays a crucial role in regulating
ciliary and flagellar beating; however, the molecular mechanisms underlying IDA
regulation remain largely unclear. METHODS: Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was
performed to identify candidate pathogenic variants in an infertile man with
asthenoteratozoospermia. A knockout mouse model was generated using CRISPR/Cas9
to investigate the pathophysiological effects of the identified variant.
Phenotypic characterization included semen parameter analysis (e.g., sperm
concentration, motility), morphological assessments (electron microscopy,
immunostaining, histology), and mechanistic studies (e.g., scRNA-seq,
proteomics, co-immunoprecipitation, mass spectrometry, computational
prediction). RESULTS: A homozygous missense variant in DNAH3 was identified in
the patient. DNAH3, an IDA component, is predominantly expressed in post-meiotic
cells, specifically within the sperm flagella. Notably, Dnah3 knockout
(Dnah3−/−) mice exhibited abnormal flagellar morphology and male infertility.
Immunofluorescence and transmission electron microscopy revealed that the
absence of IDAs in Dnah3−/− male mice. Additionally, scRNA-seq analysis
indicated that Dnah3 deficiency in elongating spermatids significantly alters
the expression of genes related to sperm motility. Combined comparative
proteomics and co-immunoprecipitation analyses demonstrated that DNAH3 forms a
complex with DNALI1. Furthermore, DNAH3-associated male infertility in human and
mice was resolved by intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). CONCLUSIONS: Our
findings suggest that the interaction between DNAH3 and DNALI1 is critical for
flagellum assembly. DNAH3 is a candidate gene for the genetic diagnostic of
severe asthenoteratozoospermia and primary male infertility.

Introduction

Infertility is a global public health issue, affecting approximately 17% of reproductive-aged couples [1], with male factors contributing to nearly 50% of cases [2]. Male infertility is recognized as a condition with multifactorial etiology and a highly heterogeneous phenotype [3]. Genetic abnormalities, including chromosomal anomalies, gene mutations and polymorphisms, are identified in about 15% of infertile men [4]. Although several genetic factors have been implicated in male infertility, the underlying causes of idiopathic cases remain largely unknown.

Spermiogenesis is the complex process by which round spermatids transform into mature spermatozoa characterized by highly specialized structural features [5]. A key transformation during this process is the assembly of the sperm flagellum. This evolutionarily conserved organelle is necessary for sperm function and male fertility [6]. The flagellum consists of a central pair (CP) of microtubules along its axis and nine peripheral microtubule doublets (MTDs) [7]. Structural defects in the flagella, such as abnormal assembly of microtubules and microfilaments, disruption of centriole structure, or loss of kinesin, can lead to sperm malformation and impaired motility, ultimately causing male infertility [8]. Recent proteomic studies have identified hundreds of proteins within the human sperm flagellum, highlighting the genetic heterogeneity underlying flagellar structural defects [9]. To date, over 30 genes have been implicated in the regulation of flagellar formation, including members of the cilia and flagella-associated protein (CFAP), the nexin-dynein regulatory complex (DRC) and the dynein axonemal heavy chain (DNAH) family [10–13]. Nevertheless, the genetic etiology for the majority of cases involving flagellar dysplasia remains to be elucidated.

The DNAH family is characterized by a highly conserved catalytic domain across species. Proteins in this family are mainly components of the inner and outer dynein arms (IDAs and ODAs) of axonemal microtubules and are extensively involved in the assembly of cilia and flagellar structures [14]. In recent years, pathogenic mutations in several DNAH family genes (e.g., DNAH1, DNAH2, DNAH6, DNAH7, DNAH8, DNAH10 and DNAH17) have been identified in infertile men presenting with severe asthenoteratozoospermia, highlighting the critical role of this family in sperm flagella biogenesis [15–26].

Among human DNAH family genes, DNAH3 is located on chromosome 16 and is predominantly expressed in testicular tissue and spermatozoa [27]. Interestingly, DNAH3 has been linked to neurodevelopment and tumorigenesis. For instance, the UK "Deciphering Developmental Disorders" (DDD) study identified de novo missense mutations in DNAH3 in six patients with neurodevelopmental and intellectual disabilities [28]. Similarly, whole-exome sequencing (WES) in Finnish individuals with intellectual disabilities revealed neonatal heterozygous mutations in DNAH3, suggesting its potential role as a pathogenic gene for intellectual disabilities [29]. Furthermore, WES analysis of seven Tunisian family members with breast cancer identified potential pathogenic heterozygous DNAH3 mutations, indicating its candidacy as a breast cancer susceptibility gene [30]. Additionally, overexpression of DNAH3 in colon epithelial cells has been shown to impair cellular microtubule function [31]. Recent studies show that DNAH3, abundantly expressed in the testis and germ cells, is a novel asthenoteratozoospermia gene whose mutations disrupt IDA/ODA assembly in sperm flagella and thereby cause male infertility in both humans and mice [32–34]. However, the molecular mechanism through which DNAH3 contributes to sperm flagella assembly remains unclear. In this study, we identified a homozygous DNAH3 missense variant in an infertile man with asthenoteratozoospermia. We demonstrate that Dnah3 deficiency in mice results in male infertility characterized by disrupted sperm flagellar ultrastructure and severely impaired motility. Most importantly, we discovered that DNAH3 forms a complex with DNALI1 and complex formation was required for IDA assembly.

ResultsIdentification of DNAH3 bi-allelic variant in infertile manA 25-year-old man with infertility from a consanguineous Chinese family was recruited in our study (Fig. 1A). Clinical examinations revealed normal testicular size, external genitalia development, and secondary sexual characteristics. Chromosomal analysis showed a normal karyotype (46, XY) with no large-scale deletions detected in the Y chromosome. To identify the genetic cause of infertility, WES was performed for the affected individual. After a series of variant filtration steps, a DNAH3 homozygous missense mutation (c.6515A > G, p.K2172R) was identified (Fig. 1A). Sanger sequencing confirmed that the patient’s parents were both heterozygous carriers of this mutation. The identified mutation was present at an extremely low allelic frequency in public population databases, including 1000 Genomes, GnomAD and ExAC (Table 1), and highly conserved across various species (Fig. 1B). Human DNAH3 is located on chromosome 16p12.3 with a total length of 226 kb and contains 62 exons. The mutation site (c.6515A > G) was identified in exon 44 and maps to located within the AAA3 structural domain (Fig. 1B). After standard quality control, 12,029 WT and 14,383 Dnah3−/− testicular cells were retained for downstream analysis. Cell clustering was conducted using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and marker gene analyses to identify major testicular cell types. Given that the Dnah3 knockout phenotype, we next performed analysis on the three elongated spermatid subclusters. To validate this interaction, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays followed by Western blotting, confirming that DNAH3 interacts with DNALI1 (Fig. 7G). Since the in vivo interaction involving the full-length mouse DNAH3 protein had been established, we subsequently constructed plasmids expressing the DNAH3 Stem domain (amino acids 1–1357) and full-length DNALI1. Further in vitro Co-IP assays validated that the DNAH3 Stem domain indeed mediates the interaction with DNALI1 (Fig. 7H). The missense mutation identified in our study is located within exon 43 of the mouse Dnah3 gene. To determine whether this specific region is critical for the interaction, we tested a fragment corresponding to the DNAH3-E43 region (amino acids 2097- 2157). Unlike the DNAH3 Stem domain, DNAH3-E43 failed to interact with DNALI1, indicating that the E43 region alone does not serve as the direct binding site for DNALI1 (Fig. 7H). To determine DNAH3-interacting partners, we performed immunoprecipitation (IP) using testicular extracts and anti-DNAH3 antibodies. A distinct protein band was observed in the immunoprecipitated samples derived from WT testes, whereas it was entirely absent in Dnah3−/− samples (Fig. 7D). Mass spectrometry analysis of this band identified DNALI1 as a putative interacting partner of DNAH3 (Fig. 7E, Table S5). Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis demonstrated co-localization of DNAH3 and DNALI1 in the flagella of WT sperm (Fig. 7F), suggesting that their interaction may depend on their co-expression. Similar to the expression patterns of DNAH3 in male mice, Dnali1 mRNA was mainly expressed in post-meiotic cells, with localization in sperm flagella, as reported in a previous study [43]. STRING and AlphaFold2 interaction predictions further supported the potential association between DNAH3 and DNALI1 (Figure S8). To validate this interaction, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays followed by Western blotting, confirming that DNAH3 interacts with DNALI1 (Fig. 7G). Since the in vivo interaction involving the full-length mouse DNAH3 protein had been established, we subsequently constructed plasmids expressing the DNAH3 Stem domain (amino acids 1–1357) and full-length DNALI1. Further in vitro Co-IP assays validated that the DNAH3 Stem domain indeed mediates the interaction with DNALI1 (Fig. 7H). The missense mutation identified in our study is located within exon 43 of the mouse Dnah3 gene. To determine whether this specific region is critical for the interaction, we tested a fragment corresponding to the DNAH3-E43 region (amino acids 2097- 2157). Unlike the DNAH3 Stem domain, DNAH3-E43 failed to interact with DNALI1, indicating that the E43 region alone does not serve as the direct binding site for DNALI1 (Fig. 7H). These results show that DNAH3 interacts with DNALI1 to form an axonemal complex and is required for IDA assembly (Fig. 7I).

Here, we identified a homozygous missense variant (c.6515A > G, p.K2172R) in DNAH3 in an infertile man with asthenoteratozoospermia from a consanguineous family. Multiple sequence alignment analysis showed the variant was conserved across multiple species. In silico prediction tools suggested a high probability of pathogenicity for this mutation. Dnah3 knockout male mice exhibited reduced sperm motility and abnormal sperm flagella. Importantly, we found that DNAH3 interacts with DNALI1 and is essential for proper assembly of the flagellum.

The DNAH family proteins are characterized by the presence of AAA (ATPases Associated with diverse cellular Activities) domains, which are essential for their motor functions, enabling ATP hydrolysis to drive conformational changes necessary for movement. Mutations in the AAA domain of dynein proteins can lead to various sperm flagellar abnormalities. Previous studies have shown that mutations in DNAH1 are a major genetic cause of asthenoteratozoospermia, accounting for approximately 25% of cases, with the most common mutation (c.11726_11727del, p.P3909fs*33) located in the AAA domain [10, 16]. Mutations in DNAH2, DNAH6, DNAH7 and DNAH10 have been associated with asthenoteratozoospermia due to reduced ATPase activity, absence of the central pair, and disorganized microtubules and IDAs [40]. In addition to IDA-associated proteins, mutations in DNAH8 and DNAH17 cause asthenoteratozoospermia due to the lack of ODAs in infertile male patients [22, 25]. Similar asthenoteratozoospermia phenotypes have been observed in knockout mice for Dnah2, Dnah8, Dnah10 and Dnah17 [22, 23, 26, 44]. In our study, the homozygous variant c.6515A > G in DNAH3 is located at the AAA3 domain, leading to incorrect protein folding. Morphological assay detected that the patient presented typical asthenoteratozoospermia phenotype. Ultrastructural analysis by TEM further showed abnormalities in IDAs within the sperm flagella. Investigations in Dnah3−/− male mice suggested that disruption of Dnah3 impairs IDA assembly in sperm flagella. Collectively, these findings suggest that mutation in DNAH3 causes asthenoteratozoospermia, likely due to failure in IDA assembly and axonemal disorganization.

Dynein protein arms play a crucial role in the motility of cilia and sperm flagella by generating sliding forces [45]. These dynein complexes are responsible for the coordinated bending of the axoneme. Previous studies have shown that deficiencies in IDA complex proteins result in multiple morphological abnormalities of the flagella (MMAF) and/or primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) [46]. The assembly and structural integrity of IDAs involve a complex interplay of dynein heavy, intermediate, and light chains, along with radial spoke proteins, IFT proteins, and nexin links [47]. For example, DNALI1 is a core component of the IDA in the flagellum axoneme, playing a role in the formation of sperm flagella. Mutations in DNALI1 cause male infertility by impairing IDAs assembly [43, 45]. In this study, we carefully analyzed the gene expression changes of the three proteins in KO testes based on our scRNA-seq data. The results show that the mRNA level of Dnali1 is unchanged, suggesting DNAH3 does not primarily regulate the transcription of Dnali1. We hypothesize that DNAH3 is essential for the stability of DNALI1 within the cytoplasmic pre-assembly complex. Moreover, using DIA proteomics analysis, we found that DNALI1 expression was significantly decreased in Dnah3−/− male mice, indicating that DNAH3 is involved in flagellar assembly by regulating the expression of proteins related to flagellar formation. We therefore immunoprecipitated DNAH3 from testicular extracts using an anti-DNAH3 antibody and combined mass spectrometry and co-IP analyses confirmed that DNALI1 associates with DNAH3. Moreover, immunofluorescence analysis showed that DNALI1 colocalized with DNAH3 in sperm flagella. Collectively, these results indicate that DNAH3 interacts with DNALI1 and is essential for the proper assembly and structure of the sperm flagellum.

Several studies have reported that mutations in DNAH3 cause asthenoteratozoospermia in infertile men [32–34], and two of these studies also identified that Dnah3 knockout mice exhibit similar phenotypes, including significant reductions in sperm motility and partial or complete loss of IDAs. Most importantly, the existing studies have not explored the molecular mechanisms by which DNAH3 regulates sperm flagellar assembly. In this study, we identified the interacting proteins of DNAH3 through IP-MS and confirmed the interaction between DNAH3 and DNALI1 via co-IP experiments; both proteins are involved in the formation of the IDA structure in the sperm tail. Interestingly, all studies, including ours, indicate that abnormalities of the sperm tail in Dnah3 knockout mice are less severe than those observed in infertile men carrying DNAH3 pathogenic mutations, suggesting that the physiological functions of DNAH3 are not entirely consistent across species. Similar species divergence has been reported for DNAH1 and DNAH12: men harbouring biallelic loss-of-function variants display MMAF, while the corresponding knockout mouse lines exhibit reduced motility but largely normal morphology. These findings indicate that orthologs may play slightly different roles in human and mouse spermatogenesis [48, 49]. In addition to defects in IDA structure, Dnah3 mutant mice also exhibit certain defects in mitochondrial structure [32, 33]. Moreover, a recent article has also pointed out that DNAH3 is involved in energy metabolism [50]. Therefore, it is speculated that DNAH3 is not only a structural protein of the dynein arm but also participates in regulating energy metabolism pathways. Notably, the lack of significant enrichment in mitochondrial-related pathways in our scRNA-seq data suggests that the potential role of DNAH3 in energy metabolism may not be regulated at the transcriptional level.

Assisted reproductive technologies (ART), such as in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), provide opportunities for infertile couples to have children. Currently, ICSI is an effective technique for achieving positive clinical outcomes in infertile men with mutations in various DNAH family genes (e.g., DNAH1, DNAH2, DNAH6, DNAH7, DNAH8, DNAH9 and DNAH10) [51]. In this study, an infertile man carrying a DNAH3 variant underwent ICSI treatment and obtained blastocysts. These findings suggest that ICSI could be a recommended treatment for DNAH3-associated male infertility.

In conclusion, our study highlights that DNAH3 is an essential protein for the IDAs assembly, and its deficiency causes male infertility in both humans and mice. Our findings provide valuable insights that may support future genetic counseling and clinical decision-making in cases of infertility related to DNAH mutations.
