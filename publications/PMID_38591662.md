# Myxococcus xanthus translesion DNA synthesis protein ImuA is an ATPase enhanced by DNA.
**Authors:** Lichimo K, Sowa DJ, Tetenych A, Warner MM, Doubleday C, Dev HS, Luck C, Andres SN

## Abstract

Myxococcus xanthus translesion DNA synthesis protein ImuA is an ATPase enhanced
by DNA.

Translesion DNA synthesis pathways are necessary to ensure bacterial replication
in the presence of DNA damage. Translesion DNA synthesis carried out by the PolV
mutasome is well-studied in Escherichia coli, but ~one third of bacteria use a
functionally homologous protein complex, consisting of ImuA, ImuB, and ImuC
(also called DnaE2). Numerous in vivo studies have shown that all three proteins
are required for translesion DNA synthesis and that ImuC is the error-prone
polymerase, but the roles of ImuA and ImuB are unclear. Here we carry out
biochemical characterization of ImuA and a truncation of ImuB from Myxococcus
xanthus. We find that ImuA is an ATPase, with ATPase activity enhanced in the
presence of DNA. The ATPase activity is likely regulated by the C-terminus, as
loss of the ImuA C-terminus results in DNA-independent ATP hydrolysis. We also
find that ImuA binds a variety of DNA substrates, with DNA binding affinity
affected by the addition of ADP or adenylyl-imidodiphosphate. An ImuB truncation
also binds DNA, with lower affinity than ImuA. In the absence of DNA, ImuA
directly binds ImuB with moderate affinity. Finally, we show that ImuA and ImuB
self-interact, but that ImuA is predominantly a monomer, while truncated ImuB is
a trimer in vitro. Together, with our findings and the current literature in the
field, we suggest a model for translesion DNA synthesis, where a trimeric ImuB
would provide sufficient binding sites for DNA, the β-clamp, ImuC, and ImuA, and
where ImuA ATPase activity may regulate assembly and disassembly of the
translesion DNA synthesis complex.

INTRODUCTION

Translesion DNA synthesis (TLS) is a highly mutagenic process that leads to genetic adaptation and species diversity (Bębenek & Ziuzia‐Graczyk, 2018; Knobel & Marti, 2011; McCulloch & Kunkel, 2008). In bacteria, such adaptation leads to increased survival rates and causes antimicrobial resistance. The importance of the TLS protein complex involving ImuA, ImuB, and ImuC in generating antimicrobial resistance is established from studies showing that deletions of imuA (imuA′ in mycobacteria), imuB or imuC in mycobacteria lead to reduced resistance to the antibiotic rifampicin after UV‐induced damage, as well as impaired survival after treatment with mitomycin C, a DNA‐damaging antibiotic (Erill et al., 2006; Gessner et al., 2023; Warner et al., 2010). Similarly, deletion of imuC in Pseudomonas aeruginosa increases sensitivity of the bacteria to alkylation damage (Jatsenko et al., 2017). Given that the TLS damage response and adaptive mutagenesis promote antibiotic resistance, understanding the critical features of TLS in these organisms is an important strategy for developing new antimicrobials and slowing the evolution of antibiotic resistance.

In the one third of bacteria that employ ImuA, ImuB, and ImuC for TLS, ImuC is a C‐family polymerase, with low fidelity during replication, a characteristic of TLS polymerases (Boshoff et al., 2003; McHenry, 2011). The error‐prone nature of ImuC is evidenced by the TLS mutation signatures observed in Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Caulobacter crescentus after UV‐induced DNA damage (Boshoff et al., 2003; Galhardo et al., 2005). However, the function of the accessory proteins, ImuA and ImuB, remains poorly understood. Both ImuA and ImuB, like ImuC, are indispensable for TLS (Gessner et al., 2023; Warner et al., 2010). Based on sequence homology, ImuB is a Y‐family polymerase, but is inactive as it lacks the required acidic residues in the catalytic site (Warner et al., 2010). Interestingly, ImuB binds the β‐clamp through a conserved β‐clamp binding motif that is required for DNA damage tolerance after UV damage, with both proteins colocalizing as foci in Mycobacterium smegmatis after DNA damage (Gessner et al., 2023; Warner et al., 2010). Yeast two‐hybrid analysis also indicates that ImuB from M. tuberculosis can self‐associate, as well as interact with both ImuC and ImuA homologs (Warner et al., 2010). However, neither ImuC nor ImuA homologs interact with one another or the β‐clamp. ImuA and ImuC also are not necessary for ImuB to localize to DNA damage sites in M. smegmatis, suggesting ImuB binding the β‐clamp may be the initiation point for TLS (Gessner et al., 2023; Warner et al., 2010).

The function of ImuA in TLS remains an enigma. The M. tuberculosis ImuA homolog exhibits low sequence homology (~30%) to RecA in other mycobacteria (Erill et al., 2006; Jensen et al., 2009), yet structural homology modeling suggests ImuA resembles the core of RecA, including the ATPase domain (Warner et al., 2010). RecA has many cellular functions, including aiding DNA recombination, but its ATPase activity is linked to DNA binding. RecA bound to ATP forms a nucleoprotein filament, but once RecA hydrolyzes ATP, the nucleoprotein filament disassembles (Chen et al., 2008; Cox, 2003; Kowalczykowski et al., 1994; Menetski & Kowalczykowski, 1985; Roca et al., 1990). However, ImuA is not predicted to be functionally homologous to RecA, as an imuA homolog from Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus could not trans‐complement recA‐defective Escherichia coli (Campoy et al., 2005). This lack of complementation may be because RecA has N‐ and C‐terminal polymerization motifs that allow it to polymerize on single‐stranded DNA (ssDNA), but these motifs are not conserved at the N‐ and C‐termini of ImuA (del Val et al., 2019; Leite et al., 2016; Sheng et al., 2021). Myxococcus xanthus ImuA, though, does maintain a conserved RecA polymerization motif in the core of the protein (ImuA amino acids 111–115) and has been shown to bind the RecA homolog and inhibit RecA‐mediated recombination in M. xanthus, thus promoting TLS (Sheng et al., 2021).

Here we show that M. xanthus ImuA, like RecA, hydrolyzes ATP. The ImuA ATPase activity is enhanced in the presence of DNA and appears to be regulated by the ImuA C‐terminus. ImuA and a 34 amino acid N‐terminal truncation of ImuB (ImuBNΔ34) bind DNA, with a preference for a 3′‐overhang DNA substrate and long double‐stranded (dsDNA), with the addition of nucleotides altering DNA binding activity. We also demonstrate that ImuA and ImuB interact with one another, in a region near the ImuB β‐clamp binding motif, similar to what is observed in M. tuberculosis (Warner et al., 2010). We show that this interaction occurs directly between both proteins, in the absence of DNA. Furthermore, we find that ImuA predominantly forms monomers, while ImuBNΔ34 forms trimers in solution. These results suggest that an ImuB trimer could form a binding platform that would accommodate all TLS binding partners, while ATP hydrolysis may change the ImuA structure to allow binding to RecA and/or DNA, inhibiting recombination and thus favoring TLS.

ImuA ATPase activity is stimulated by DNA
Given the similarity between ImuA and RecA, we tested if ImuA also possessed ATPase activity, by measuring the release of phosphate and initial rate of ATP hydrolysis (Figures 1 and 2 and Tables S1 and S2). On its own, ImuA did not exhibit strong ATPase activity; however, the presence of DNA stimulated ATP hydrolysis, with 0.46 ± 0.08 nmol of phosphate released in the presence of 40 nucleotides (nt) of ssDNA and 1.18 ± 0.12 nmol of phosphate with 40 basepair (bp) of dsDNA. Interestingly, a 40 bp dsDNA substrate resulted in ~11‐fold more phosphate being hydrolyzed compared with ImuA alone. The rate of ATP hydrolysis followed a similar trend, where the addition of DNA also stimulated the rate ~2‐fold but was independent of whether ssDNA or dsDNA was added (Figure 2 and Table S2). Predictions of M. xanthus ImuA by AlphaFold2 run with ColabFold suggest the C‐terminal region is disordered (Figure S2; Jumper et al., 2021; Mirdita et al., 2021). Therefore, we truncated 53 amino acids from the C‐terminus (ImuACΔ53), encompassing the majority of the predicted disordered region and tested for ATPase activity. Surprisingly, the ATPase activity of ImuACΔ53 alone was almost equivalent to that of wild‐type ImuA with dsDNA (ImuACΔ53, 0.91 ± 0.12 nmol released; rate = 2.1 ± 0.2 pmol/s), while addition of dsDNA to the ImuA C‐terminal truncation further elevated ATP hydrolysis 1.8‐fold compared with ImuACΔ53 alone but did not alter the rate. ImuB may remain targeted to the DNA longer and bound to the β‐clamp, but unable to create additional mutagenesis, as observed by Gessner et al. (2023). Earlier reports that ImuA binds to RecA1 and inhibits RecA1 recombination and DNA binding in M. xanthus (Sheng et al., 2021) suggest ImuA would also fit into this proposed model, where some of the diffuse ImuA observed in M. smegmatis cells could bind RecA on DNA and/or displace RecA from the DNA, while additional ImuA forms the transient mutasome with ImuB.

We also observed that ImuA can bind dsDNA. We can only hypothesize that ImuA may act as a block to RecA, binding and removing it from dsDNA, similar to what we propose for ssDNA in TLS, as a means to inhibit RecA recombination and promote TLS. This aligns with previous studies showing that ImuA binds RecA and blocks RecA recombination (Sheng et al., 2021) and that RecA binds dsDNA as part of recombination (Mazin & Kowalczykowski, 1999; Tsang et al., 1985). Future structural and other studies of ImuA and ImuB with DNA will provide added insight and clarification into the precise roles and mechanisms of ImuA and ImuB in TLS.

A complete list of plasmids created can be found in Table S8. pUT18C::zip and pKT25::zip were used as positive controls and supplied in the BACTH System Kit (Euromedex). Combinations of vectors were cotransformed into E. coli BHT101 (Euromedex). Bacteria were grown in 50 μg/mL kanamycin, 100 μg/mL ampicillin, and 0.5 mM IPTG in LB broth overnight at 37°C. Two microliters of culture was spotted onto LB agar plates containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin, 100 μg/mL ampicillin, 0.1 or 0.5 mM IPTG, and 40 μg/mL 5‐bromo‐4‐chloro‐3‐indolyl‐β‐d‐galactopyranoside (X‐Gal). The plates were incubated for 24 h at 30°C, prior to imaging. Colonies with positive interactions between proteins of interest were identified by blue‐white screening. Here, the interaction of T18 and T25 fragments results in full‐length adenylate cyclase converting ATP to cAMP, and in turn, promotes the expression of β‐galactosidase, X‐gal hydrolysis, and the formation of blue‐pigmented colonies.

4.7Mass photometryMP experiments were performed on a Refeyn TwoMP mass photometer (Refeyn Ltd, Oxford, UK). Microscope coverslips (24 × 50 mm2, Thorlabs Inc.) were cleaned by serial rinsing with Milli‐Q water and HPLC‐grade isopropanol (Sigma Aldrich) followed by drying with a filtered air stream. Silicon gaskets (Grace Bio‐Labs) to hold the sample drops were cleaned in the same procedure immediately prior to measurement. All MP measurements were performed at room temperature. The instrument was calibrated using a protein standard mixture: β‐amylase (Sigma‐Aldrich, 56, 112, and 224 kDa), and thyroglobulin (Sigma‐Aldrich, 670 kDa). Before each measurement, 15 μL of buffer was placed in the well to find focus. The focus position was searched and locked using the default droplet‐dilution autofocus function after which 5 μL of protein in 20 mM HEPES, pH 8.0, 400 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 2 mM β‐mercaptoethanol (final concentrations of 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 nM tested) was added and pipetted up and down to briefly mix before movie acquisition was promptly started. For protein‐DNA or protein‐nucleotide mixtures, 5 μL of protein and 30 nt ssDNA (20:1 ratio mix) or 5 μL protein and ADP or AMP‐PNP, (1:10 ratio mix) in 20 mM HEPES, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol and 2 mM β‐mercaptoethanol (final protein concentration of 25 nM) was added to 15 μL of buffer in the well. Mixtures of protein and nucleotide were incubated at 30°C for 20 min, while mixtures of protein and DNA were incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Movies were acquired for 60 s (3000 frames) using AcquireMP (version 2023 R1.1; Refeyn Ltd) using standard settings at room temperature. All movies were processed and analyzed using DiscoverMP (version 2023 R1.2; Refeyn Ltd). All protein concentrations were analyzed with duplicate measurements, except 25 nM of each protein and all protein–DNA and protein–nucleotide reactions were measured in triplicate.
