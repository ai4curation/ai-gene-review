The increasing prevalence of triazole resistance among Aspergillus fumigatus
clinical isolates threatens the efficacy of this front-line treatment,
highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic targets. In this study, we
characterized three putative lanosterol synthase (also known as oxidosqualene
cyclase) paralogs and identified Erg7A as the dominant enzyme. Although erg7B
provided functional redundancy when overexpressed, deletion of erg7A alone
created a significant ergosterol biosynthetic blockade. erg7A deficiency led to
the accumulation of upstream substrates (squalene and 2,3-oxidosqualene) as well
as downstream sterol intermediates, including lanosterol and eburicol. This
sterol perturbation conferred triazole hypersusceptibility to a laboratory
strain and restored susceptibility in resistant clinical isolates harboring
cyp51A or hmg1 mutations. We further validated these findings using Ro 48-8071,
a specific oxidosqualene cyclase inhibitor, and found that Ro 48-8071 exhibited
potent synergy with the long-tailed triazoles, itraconazole, and posaconazole.
Our findings demonstrate that targeting Erg7 sensitizes A. fumigatus to
triazoles, offering a potential combination therapy against resistant
isolates.IMPORTANCEAspergillus fumigatus is a mold pathogen and the major
etiologic agent of invasive aspergillosis. Treatment of invasive aspergillosis
is currently limited to only three classes of antifungals, the use of which is
hampered by toxicity and rising antifungal resistance rates. The front-line
therapeutic class, the triazoles, functions through the inhibition of ergosterol
biosynthesis, and the majority of clinical triazole resistance is caused by
target gene mutations impacting the sterol demethylase Cyp51. In this study, we
differentiate the biological roles of three putative A. fumigatus lanosterol
synthase paralogs as enzymes functioning upstream of the triazole target. We
provide strong genetic and pharmacological evidence that inhibition of this
upstream enzymatic step in the ergosterol biosynthetic pathway is synergistic
with triazoles and can resensitize resistant clinical isolates. The development
of lanosterol synthase inhibitors with fungus-specific activity is expected to
provide new stand-alone and/or combination therapies to improve outcomes of
invasive aspergillosis.

INTRODUCTION

Invasive aspergillosis (IA) caused by Aspergillus fumigatus remains a leading cause of mortality in immunocompromised patients (1). Triazoles, which target sterol C14α-demethylase (Cyp51), are the first-line treatment for IA. However, the emergence of triazole resistance increasingly compromises clinical outcomes (2). While amino acid substitutions and promoter tandem repeats in cyp51A are well-documented triazole resistance mechanisms in A. fumigatus (3), resistance independent of cyp51A is increasingly recognized. These include the overexpression of efflux pumps (4) and mutations in transcriptional regulators such as the CCAAT-binding complex HapE (5, 6). Significantly, mutations in the HMG-CoA reductase, Hmg1, have also emerged as a critical resistance determinant (7). This expanding resistance profile highlights the urgent need to identify novel antifungal targets that could function as stand-alone therapies or as effective combination therapies to improve triazole activity.

To address this challenge, we focused on identifying essential enzymes within the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway. Ergosterol is a critical component of fungal cell membranes, responsible for membrane fluidity, permeability, and the proper function of integral membrane proteins (8). As such, the ergosterol pathway has long served as a primary target for antifungal therapy. Clinically employed drug classes include allylamines targeting Erg1 (9), triazoles targeting Erg11/Cyp51, and polyenes that directly bind membrane sterols (10). Unlike in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and other yeasts, where Erg6 participates in later stages of the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway, A. fumigatus Erg6 converts lanosterol to eburicol (11). In doing so, A. fumigatus Erg6 directly synthesizes the Cyp51 substrate, thereby repositioning it to an early enzymatic step upstream of the triazole target. Our previous studies validating erg6 as an essential gene (12), alongside the validated targets Erg1 and Cyp51, suggest that this upstream biosynthetic module is critical for fungal survival. However, the lanosterol synthase, Erg7, which sits centrally within this cluster, remains uncharacterized in A. fumigatus.

Erg7, encoding lanosterol synthase (also known as oxidosqualene cyclase, OSC), catalyzes the cyclization of 2,3-oxidosqualene to lanosterol, forming the sterol ring core. While both fungal and mammalian genomes encode lanosterol synthase enzymes, plants predominantly divert 2,3-oxidosqualene to cycloartenol via cycloartenol synthase (13). This divergence suggests that targeting fungal Erg7 could also minimize phytotoxicity for agricultural applications. Moreover, the Erg7 function is highly conserved across the fungal kingdom. The lanosterol synthase from Pneumocystis carinii, an atypical fungal pathogen known for its reliance on host cholesterol, remains functionally active and complements S. cerevisiae erg7 mutants (14). This conservation implies that Erg7 is a promising broad-spectrum target. To date, Erg7 has been characterized as an essential enzyme in Candida albicans (15, 16) and S. cerevisiae (17–19). In this study, we genetically characterized the erg7 paralogs in A. fumigatus. We identified erg7A as the dominant gene and demonstrated that its deletion effectively reverses triazole resistance. Supporting our genetic validation, we further demonstrated that the specific lanosterol synthase inhibitor, Ro 48-8071, exhibits potent synergy with triazoles against resistant clinical isolates.

Three sequences exhibiting greater than 40% sequence identity were retrieved and designated Erg7A (XP_077660888.1), Erg7B (XP_751627.1), and Erg7C (XP_751356.1). As displayed in Fig. 1A, all three A. fumigatus paralogs share a highly conserved structural organization with the squalene cyclase N-terminal and C-terminal domains, which is identical to S. cerevisiae Erg7 and Homo sapiens lanosterol synthase (LSS) (Fig. 1A). To further determine the evolutionary relationships among these genes, we expanded our analysis to include a total of 29 orthologous sequences retrieved from a diverse range of fungal taxa. These included the genus Aspergillus as well as representatives from the Eurotiomycetes, Sordariomycetes, Basidiomycota, Saccharomycetes, and Homo sapiens to capture the broad evolutionary diversity (Table S3). Maximum likelihood phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that the three A. fumigatus Erg7 occupied distinct evolutionary positions (Fig. 1B). Specifically, AfErg7A clustered tightly with the lanosterol synthases of other filamentous fungi. AfErg7B formed a separate lineage, whereas AfErg7C was located at an isolated and basal position on the tree, highlighting the significant evolutionary divergence of this paralog. Phylogenetic tree separates Erg7A, Erg7B, and Erg7C into distinct clades.erg7A plays the primary functional role among the lanosterol synthase paralogsTo investigate the biological function of the erg7 paralogs in A. fumigatus, we constructed single deletion mutants for each gene (Δerg7A, Δerg7B, and Δerg7C) by using the CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing system. As shown in Fig. 2A, the Δerg7A mutant showed significantly reduced colony growth compared to the background strain, ΔakuB-pyrG+. In contrast, the Δerg7B and Δerg7C mutants exhibited colony morphologies comparable to the parental strain. To determine if the observed phenotypes were influenced by transcriptional compensation, the mRNA levels of the remaining paralogs were measured in each null mutant. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed that the expression of non-deleted erg7 paralogs remained unaltered in the single deletion backgrounds (Fig. S1A). To further evaluate whether the erg7 paralogs have functional redundancy, we then attempted to generate double deletion mutants. While Δerg7A/Δerg7C and Δerg7B/Δerg7C were successfully obtained (Fig. 2A), we were unable to recover a viable Δerg7A/Δerg7B mutant. Radial growth analyses revealed that the Δerg7A/Δerg7C double mutant maintained the Δerg7A single deletion phenotype, whereas the Δerg7B/Δerg7C double mutant remained similar to the parental strain (Fig. 2A). To explore the possibility that erg7A/erg7B double deletion is lethal, we constructed a conditional mutant in the Δerg7A background where the native promoter of erg7B was replaced by a doxycycline-repressible promoter (20). In this conditional expression strain, exogenous doxycycline caused a significant reduction in erg7B expression, confirming a functional promoter replacement (Fig. S1B). Correspondingly, growth of this mutant was progressively inhibited by increasing concentrations of doxycycline added to culture media, with near-complete inhibition observed at 0.25 μg/mL (Fig. 2B). This result confirms that deletion of erg7A and erg7B is synthetically lethal. Notably, the ∆erg7A/Tetoff-erg7B mutant displayed a wild-type phenotype in the absence of doxycycline, lacking the growth defect associated with erg7A deletion (Fig. 2B, left panel). This observation suggested that the doxycycline-repressible promoter may drive constitutive overexpression of erg7B in the absence of doxycycline and therefore compensate for the loss of erg7A. The overexpression of erg7B in the absence of doxycycline was confirmed by the quantitative RT-PCR analysis, and erg7B expression was found to be upregulated 60-fold (Fig. S1B). To further test the hypothesis that high levels of erg7B are sufficient to compensate for erg7A deficiency, we overexpressed erg7B in the Δerg7A background by replacing the native promoter of erg7B with the constitutive hspA promoter (21). As expected, this genetic manipulation also ameliorated the ∆erg7A phenotype (Fig. 2C). Conversely, overexpression of erg7C using the same strategy failed to rescue the growth defect of Δerg7A (Fig. 2C). Consistent with our deletion results, the individual overexpression of erg7A or erg7B had no effect on sterol profiles, which indicated that the excess lanosterol synthase activity does not perturb homeostasis (Table S5). In contrast, the overexpression of erg7C caused a dramatic metabolic shift characterized by a reduction in total ergosterol and the accumulation of two specific non-physiological sterol intermediates, protosta-17(20)Z,24-dien-3β-ol, to approximately 20% and protosta-17(20),24-dien-3β,29-diol (Table S5). This intermediate was absent in erg7A and erg7B overexpression strains. Previously, erg7C (Afu4g14770) has also been characterized as osc3 or helA and noted to be involved in the biosynthesis of the fusidane-type antibiotic, helvolic acid (24, 25). Thus, these results suggest that Erg7C primarily functions as a secondary metabolite synthase that diverts the substrate 2,3-oxidosqualene away from the ergosterol pathway.

To further elucidate the interplay between erg7A deficiency and triazole antifungals, we analyzed the sterol composition under voriconazole treatment. Voriconazole is the first-line treatment for IA and leads to ergosterol depletion accompanied by 14-methylergosta-8,24(28)-dien-3β,6α-diol accumulation (26). While this intermediate is often considered a toxic sterol in yeast, its toxicity in A. fumigatus remains controversial (27). In the parental background, voriconazole exposure led to a dramatic drop in cellular ergosterol levels, which fell from a baseline of 3.25 ± 0.43 to 0.27 ± 0.11 µg/mg dry weight (Fig. 3E). When Cyp51 was inhibited by voriconazole exposure, GC/MS analyses suggest the C14α-methylated intermediates are forced into the putative Erg3-dependent pathway to produce a toxic 3,6-diol (Fig. 4, indicated by red dashed lines). Notably, in the Δerg7A mutant, the accumulation of the 3,6-diol in the total sterol pool was lower compared to the wild type (4.03% vs. 9.17%) (Table S4). Despite this reduced toxic 3,6-diol accumulation, the Δerg7A mutant remained hypersusceptible to triazole stress, likely due to the combination effect of erg7A deletion and voriconazole-mediated Cyp51 inhibition to deplete cellular ergosterol to a lethal level of 0.11 ± 0.01 µg/mg dry weight (Fig. 3E). Taken together, these data support a model wherein, under non-stress conditions, sterol intermediate flux is mainly channeled toward the canonical pathway of ergosterol biosynthesis (Fig. 4). A minor branch pathway diverts fecosterol to produce ergosta-5,8,22-trienol (Fig. 4, indicated by black dashed lines), which constitutes approximately 2% of the total sterol pool. This side pathway was suppressed upon erg7A deletion as the percentage of alternative pathway intermediates decreased (Table S4).

DISCUSSIONIn this study, we characterized the lanosterol synthase paralogs in A. fumigatus and revealed Erg7A as the dominant paralog governing sterol homeostasis. Our findings demonstrate that although erg7B can compensate when artificially overexpressed, the deletion of erg7A creates a critical ergosterol biosynthetic blockage that becomes synthetically lethal when coupled with erg7B repression. This genetic defect not only compromises membrane robustness but also causes hypersensitivity to triazoles that override established resistance mechanisms. Furthermore, we clarified that the divergent paralog, here called Erg7C, functions in helvolic acid biosynthesis rather than a canonical lanosterol synthase (25). This functional divergence explains its inability to rescue the ∆erg7A growth defect.Contrary to the expectation that enzyme deletion depletes downstream products, our sterol profiles revealed a significant accumulation of the downstream intermediates, lanosterol and eburicol, in the absence of erg7A. The presence of these intermediates confirms that the minor paralog, Erg7B, remains active to convert substrates and maintain sufficient flux to prevent auxotrophy. However, the accumulation of lanosterol and eburicol implies a severe functional bottleneck at the subsequent enzymatic steps. We propose two reasons to drive this blockage. First, transcriptional analysis revealed that cyp51A expression is downregulated in the absence of erg7A. The fact that cyp51A is repressed together with erg7A deletion suggests a potential, yet unknown, regulatory interaction between Erg7A and Cyp51A. Beyond the transcriptional suppression, insights from yeast suggest that spatial compartmentalization could also contribute to this metabolic blockage. In S. cerevisiae, although Erg7p primarily localizes to LDs, a subpopulation of Erg7p is required at the ER to facilitate sterol synthesis (33–36). These studies uncovered that if Erg7p is restricted to lipid particles without physical interaction with Erg27p or other ER machinery, the pathway cannot function efficiently. Although A. fumigatus Erg7B localization remains unknown, the fact that the subsequent enzyme Erg6 is a known lipid droplet resident, whereas Cyp51 resides in the ER, implies that the conversion of eburicol to downstream intermediates requires a necessary contact between organelles for substrate channeling. Given the accumulation of lanosterol and eburicol in ∆erg7A, A. fumigatus Erg7A likely performs a critical bridging function between LDs and ER, similar to yeast Erg7p. In the absence of Erg7A, the LD-resident module (converting lanosterol to eburicol) likely becomes spatially isolated from the ER, creating a transport bottleneck that mimics enzymatic inhibition despite the presence of active Erg7B. Therefore, in addition to ergosterol depletion, the synthetic lethality caused by erg7A/B double deletion may be partly due to the paradoxical increase in these sterol intermediates. Our previous work suggests that lanosterol accumulation induces negative feedback regulation on ergosterol biosynthesis through the inhibition of Hmg1, a rate-limiting enzymatic step for isoprenoid production (7). In addition, Elsaman et al. have described toxic effects of eburicol accumulation that, at least partially, underpin the fungicidal activity of triazoles against Aspergillus (27).The emergence of triazole-resistant strains poses a significant threat to current antifungal therapies. Our findings highlight Erg7A as a potential drug target. Whether the resistance is driven by a downstream cyp51A G54R mutation that blocks drug binding or an upstream regulatory modulation with an hmg1 mutation, erg7A deficiency effectively restores susceptibility. This suggests that re-sensitizing multidrug-resistant strains by erg7A deletion is independent of the underlying resistance mechanism. The metabolic bottleneck created by removing erg7A in the upstream pathway has a severe negative impact on total ergosterol biosynthesis. This critical role is further highlighted by the transcriptional responsiveness of erg7 to antimicrobial stress. Previous studies have reported that erg7 expression is upregulated following exposure to polyoxometalates in C. albicans (37), indicating that the fungal cell responds to stress through modulating Erg7 levels. Consequently, Erg7 appears to be in the compensatory network for survival under stressful conditions. Therefore, targeting Erg7 represents a potential strategy for adjunctive therapy. The potential of Erg7 as a druggable target is well-supported by its pharmacological feasibility (32). Recent studies have successfully utilized virtual screening algorithms to repurpose the hormone calcitriol (used for the treatment of osteoporosis) as a potential inhibitor of C. albicans Erg7, as it shares structural features with the sterol substrate. This validates the druggability of Erg7 based on structure-based strategies (38).Another widely investigated Erg7 inhibitor is Ro 48-8071. This compound was originally characterized as an inhibitor of mammalian OSC (31), serving as a chemical lead as well as a valuable pharmacological probe. Our results demonstrated that although Ro 48-8071 displayed moderate monotherapy activity, it acted as a potent sensitizer to reverse triazole resistance in A. fumigatus clinical isolates. Notably, this synergistic interaction exhibited a distinct preference for long-tailed triazoles (e.g., itraconazole and posaconazole) over the compact ones (e.g., voriconazole and isavuconazole). Similar to genetic depletion of lanosterol synthase activity through erg7A deletion, treatment with Ro 48-8071 would be expected to not only induce accumulation of upstream pathway precursors, such as oxidosqualene, but also paradoxically downstream sterol-type precursors, specifically lanosterol and eburicol. Previous structural studies indicate that ergosterol promotes a condensed, thick, and highly ordered membrane bilayer due to its smooth planar ring. In contrast, lanosterol possesses a rough α-face caused by the C14 methyl group that prevents close interaction with phospholipid acyl chains. Consequently, lanosterol-rich membranes fail to pack lipid acyl chains effectively, leading to increased membrane disorder, reduced thickness, and a looser bilayer arrangement compared to the wild-type ergosterol-rich membranes (39). This structural disorder differentially affects triazole drugs based on their molecular size and shape. Long-tailed triazoles are highly lipophilic molecules that interact extensively with the lipid bilayer during cellular entry. The compromised membrane caused by Ro 48-8071 likely reduces the hindrance for these bulky molecules and facilitates their passive diffusion and intracellular accumulation. This aligns with our observation that the synergistic effect was most pronounced for lipophilic triazoles. However, our data employing the resistant clinical isolates DI15-97 and DI15-99 suggest that the synergistic effect of Ro 48-8071 can extend to compact triazoles as well. Furthermore, the synergism noted in resistant clinical isolates was found to be independent of the underlying resistance mechanism. Therefore, as proposed for mammalian cell applications, Ro 48-8071 could also serve as a lead structure for the development of new Erg7 inhibitors with anti-Aspergillus activity.In summary, this study identifies Erg7A as the primary functional paralog among three putative lanosterol synthases. Our results demonstrate that Erg7A is a potential vulnerability within the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway. Additionally, Erg7 inhibition creates a synthetic blockade that effectively overrides established triazole resistance mechanisms in A. fumigatus. The specific synergy between the Erg7 inhibitor Ro 48-8071 and long-tailed triazoles provides a promising strategy to combat multidrug-resistant A. fumigatus. Building on these findings, in vivo studies assessing Δerg7A mutant fitness in murine models of invasive aspergillosis, together with evaluation of Erg7 inhibitors such as Ro 48-8071 in combination with triazoles during infection, will be needed to support translation of this strategy to the clinical setting.

Contrary to the expectation that enzyme deletion depletes downstream products, our sterol profiles revealed a significant accumulation of the downstream intermediates, lanosterol and eburicol, in the absence of erg7A. The presence of these intermediates confirms that the minor paralog, Erg7B, remains active to convert substrates and maintain sufficient flux to prevent auxotrophy. However, the accumulation of lanosterol and eburicol implies a severe functional bottleneck at the subsequent enzymatic steps. We propose two reasons to drive this blockage. First, transcriptional analysis revealed that cyp51A expression is downregulated in the absence of erg7A. The fact that cyp51A is repressed together with erg7A deletion suggests a potential, yet unknown, regulatory interaction between Erg7A and Cyp51A. Beyond the transcriptional suppression, insights from yeast suggest that spatial compartmentalization could also contribute to this metabolic blockage. In S. cerevisiae, although Erg7p primarily localizes to LDs, a subpopulation of Erg7p is required at the ER to facilitate sterol synthesis (33–36). These studies uncovered that if Erg7p is restricted to lipid particles without physical interaction with Erg27p or other ER machinery, the pathway cannot function efficiently. Although A. fumigatus Erg7B localization remains unknown, the fact that the subsequent enzyme Erg6 is a known lipid droplet resident, whereas Cyp51 resides in the ER, implies that the conversion of eburicol to downstream intermediates requires a necessary contact between organelles for substrate channeling. Given the accumulation of lanosterol and eburicol in ∆erg7A, A. fumigatus Erg7A likely performs a critical bridging function between LDs and ER, similar to yeast Erg7p. In the absence of Erg7A, the LD-resident module (converting lanosterol to eburicol) likely becomes spatially isolated from the ER, creating a transport bottleneck that mimics enzymatic inhibition despite the presence of active Erg7B. Therefore, in addition to ergosterol depletion, the synthetic lethality caused by erg7A/B double deletion may be partly due to the paradoxical increase in these sterol intermediates. Our previous work suggests that lanosterol accumulation induces negative feedback regulation on ergosterol biosynthesis through the inhibition of Hmg1, a rate-limiting enzymatic step for isoprenoid production (7). In addition, Elsaman et al. have described toxic effects of eburicol accumulation that, at least partially, underpin the fungicidal activity of triazoles against Aspergillus (27).

The emergence of triazole-resistant strains poses a significant threat to current antifungal therapies. Our findings highlight Erg7A as a potential drug target. Whether the resistance is driven by a downstream cyp51A G54R mutation that blocks drug binding or an upstream regulatory modulation with an hmg1 mutation, erg7A deficiency effectively restores susceptibility. This suggests that re-sensitizing multidrug-resistant strains by erg7A deletion is independent of the underlying resistance mechanism. The metabolic bottleneck created by removing erg7A in the upstream pathway has a severe negative impact on total ergosterol biosynthesis. This critical role is further highlighted by the transcriptional responsiveness of erg7 to antimicrobial stress. Previous studies have reported that erg7 expression is upregulated following exposure to polyoxometalates in C. albicans (37), indicating that the fungal cell responds to stress through modulating Erg7 levels. Consequently, Erg7 appears to be in the compensatory network for survival under stressful conditions. Therefore, targeting Erg7 represents a potential strategy for adjunctive therapy. The potential of Erg7 as a druggable target is well-supported by its pharmacological feasibility (32). Recent studies have successfully utilized virtual screening algorithms to repurpose the hormone calcitriol (used for the treatment of osteoporosis) as a potential inhibitor of C. albicans Erg7, as it shares structural features with the sterol substrate. This validates the druggability of Erg7 based on structure-based strategies (38).

Another widely investigated Erg7 inhibitor is Ro 48-8071. This compound was originally characterized as an inhibitor of mammalian OSC (31), serving as a chemical lead as well as a valuable pharmacological probe. Our results demonstrated that although Ro 48-8071 displayed moderate monotherapy activity, it acted as a potent sensitizer to reverse triazole resistance in A. fumigatus clinical isolates. Notably, this synergistic interaction exhibited a distinct preference for long-tailed triazoles (e.g., itraconazole and posaconazole) over the compact ones (e.g., voriconazole and isavuconazole). Similar to genetic depletion of lanosterol synthase activity through erg7A deletion, treatment with Ro 48-8071 would be expected to not only induce accumulation of upstream pathway precursors, such as oxidosqualene, but also paradoxically downstream sterol-type precursors, specifically lanosterol and eburicol. Previous structural studies indicate that ergosterol promotes a condensed, thick, and highly ordered membrane bilayer due to its smooth planar ring. In contrast, lanosterol possesses a rough α-face caused by the C14 methyl group that prevents close interaction with phospholipid acyl chains. Consequently, lanosterol-rich membranes fail to pack lipid acyl chains effectively, leading to increased membrane disorder, reduced thickness, and a looser bilayer arrangement compared to the wild-type ergosterol-rich membranes (39). This structural disorder differentially affects triazole drugs based on their molecular size and shape. Long-tailed triazoles are highly lipophilic molecules that interact extensively with the lipid bilayer during cellular entry. The compromised membrane caused by Ro 48-8071 likely reduces the hindrance for these bulky molecules and facilitates their passive diffusion and intracellular accumulation. This aligns with our observation that the synergistic effect was most pronounced for lipophilic triazoles. However, our data employing the resistant clinical isolates DI15-97 and DI15-99 suggest that the synergistic effect of Ro 48-8071 can extend to compact triazoles as well. Furthermore, the synergism noted in resistant clinical isolates was found to be independent of the underlying resistance mechanism. Therefore, as proposed for mammalian cell applications, Ro 48-8071 could also serve as a lead structure for the development of new Erg7 inhibitors with anti-Aspergillus activity.

In summary, this study identifies Erg7A as the primary functional paralog among three putative lanosterol synthases. Our results demonstrate that Erg7A is a potential vulnerability within the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway. Additionally, Erg7 inhibition creates a synthetic blockade that effectively overrides established triazole resistance mechanisms in A. fumigatus. The specific synergy between the Erg7 inhibitor Ro 48-8071 and long-tailed triazoles provides a promising strategy to combat multidrug-resistant A. fumigatus. Building on these findings, in vivo studies assessing Δerg7A mutant fitness in murine models of invasive aspergillosis, together with evaluation of Erg7 inhibitors such as Ro 48-8071 in combination with triazoles during infection, will be needed to support translation of this strategy to the clinical setting.
