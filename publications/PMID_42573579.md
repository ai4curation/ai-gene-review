# A TMEM63B variant with enhanced mechanosensitive channel activity and acquired lipid scramblase function.

TMEM63B, a mechanosensitive ion channel (MSC), is associated with severe
neurodevelopmental disorders, including severe early-onset developmental and
epileptic encephalopathy. Structure-function studies have shown that TMEM63B
pathogenic variants, including V44M in transmembrane helix 0 (TM0) and T481N in
TM4, cluster near the hydrophobic neck region of the ion permeation pathway, a
region critical for gating and permeation. Notably, V44M and T481N convert
TMEM63B into constitutive phospholipid scramblases without obvious effects on
their MSC activity, revealing an unexpected channel-to-scramblase switch in
these variants. To further define the mechanistic basis of this phenomenon, here
we characterized I475del, a TM4 deletion variant near V44M and T481N. Unlike
V44M and T481N, the I475del channel exhibited basal leak currents, enhanced
mechanically activated currents, and elevated mechanosensitivity, altogether
supporting its classification as a bona fide gain-of-function MSC variant. Like
V44M and T481N, I475del also enabled constitutive phospholipid scramblase
activity. However, unlike these variants, I475del uniquely displayed further
potentiation of scramblase activity under hypotonic osmotic stress. In addition,
mutating the key residues that control gating conformational changes abolished
gain-of-function ion and lipid transport through I475del. Together, our results
support a stratified molecular model of TMEM63B channelopathies in which the
pathogenic variants progressively destabilize the hydrophobic gate, permitting
lipid permeation at the resting state followed by force-induced ion and lipid
cotransport. These findings advance the mechanistic understanding of TMEM63B
function and TMEM63B-associated disease and provide a framework for developing
therapeutic strategies targeting variant-specific pathologies.

INTRODUCTION

TMEM63s were initially characterized as a family of mammalian osmosensitive and mechanosensitive ion channels (MSCs) (Hou et al., 2014; Yuan et al., 2014), forming the largest known family of MSCs together with plant orthologs known as the OSCAs (Murthy et al., 2018a). In humans and higher mammals, there are three members, TMEM63A-C. TMEM63A and TMEM63B exhibited stretch-activated currents when heterologously expressed in naïve cells, establishing TMEM63s as MSCs (Murthy et al., 2018a). MSCs play crucial roles in physiological processes such as auditory transduction (Kawashima et al., 2011), balance (Kawashima et al., 2011), touch sensation (Ranade et al., 2014), proprioception (Woo et al., 2015), nociception (Murthy et al., 2018b), regulation of blood pressure (Zeng et al., 2018), and bladder control (Marshall et al., 2020). However, physiological roles of TMEM63s are only just being uncovered. TMEM63 proteins have been proposed to function in auditory (Du et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2024) and thirst sensation (Yang et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2025), pulmonary fluid (Chen et al., 2024) and insulin secretion (Tu et al., 2025), pancreatic acinar cell function (Tsvilovskyy et al., 2024), and cell motility (Tu et al., 2024) and migration (Minegishi et al., 2025). Pathologically, TMEM63A and TMEM63C are associated with hypomyelinating leukodystrophy type 19 (Yan et al., 2019; Halford et al., 2025) and hereditary spastic paraplegia (Tábara et al., 2022), respectively, whereas heterozygous de novo variants in TMEM63B have been identified in individuals presenting with neurodevelopmental disorders including severe early-onset developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE), with associated hematological abnormalities such as transfusion-dependent macrocytic anemia occurring in a subset of affected individuals (Vetro et al., 2023). Characterizations of these clinical variants have revealed unexpected, and at times, conflicting results regarding the resultant pathophysiologies.

Structurally, mammalian TMEM63 proteins function as 11-transmembrane domain (TMs, numbered 0–10) monomers containing a single, highly constricted ion conduction pathway formed primarily by TM0, TM3, TM4, TM5, and TM6 (Zheng et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024). They adopt a similar architecture to other members of the Transmembrane Channel-Scramblase (TCS) superfamily, formed together with the calcium-activated TMEM16 and the mechanosensitive TMC families (Le et al., 2021). In this architecture, the hydrophobic neck region of the pore plays an essential role in controlling gating and ion/phospholipid permeation (Le et al., 2019a; Arndt et al., 2022; Lowry et al., 2024). Interestingly, multiple clinical variants of TMEM63B cluster around the hydrophobic neck region, including the most common TMEM63B variant, V44M on TM0, as well as T481N on TM4, and a novel deletion variant, I475 deletion (I475del) also on TM4.

Interestingly, individuals carrying the TMEM63B V44M, T481N, or I475del variants exhibited broadly overlapping but clinically variable manifestations (Vetro et al., 2023). Profound global developmental delay and white matter abnormalities appear to be shared features. However, the neurological and hematological phenotypes differ substantially among variants. V44M carriers showed early seizure onset, often presenting initially as neonatal apneic episodes that progressed to multifocal epilepsy and cerebellar atrophy. Hematological abnormalities ranged from mild anemia and neonatal jaundice to severe, transfusion-dependent anemia. In contrast, the T481N carrier developed focal motor seizures at 2 months of age and showed white matter changes without cerebellar involvement, while maintaining a normal hematological profile. Finally, the I475del carrier presented with infantile spasms at 6 months of age and, uniquely, microcephaly, which co-occurred with severe, transfusion-dependent hemolytic anemia. These divergent clinical features raise the possibility that the three variants may differentially alter the biophysical properties of TMEM63B.

TMEM63B missense variants were initially reported as gain-of-function (GOF) alleles based on the observation of elevated leak currents and reduced response to hypotonic stimulation relative to the wildtype (WT) protein (Vetro et al., 2023). More recent characterization of the V44M and T481N variants, however, has called this into question, instead suggesting that these variants do not enable leaky ion conduction, but rather allow spontaneous transport of phospholipids—effectively converting TMEM63B into a constitutive phospholipid scramblase (Zheng et al., 2025). Cryo-EM structures of the equivalent variant in TMEM63A (V53M) confirmed that this substitution favors a lipid-open conformation for the protein, providing a structural explanation for the observed scrambling and absence of leak currents (Zheng et al., 2025). Additionally, TMEM63B-mediated scrambling has been reported by other groups (Miyata et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2024), though it is still unclear how this proposed function relates to its established role as an osmo- and mechanosensitive ion channel and it is not presently known how lipid scrambling might contribute to underlying disease pathophysiology. In particular, pathological variants have not yet been conclusively shown to affect mechanically activated functions of TMEM63B.

Here, we characterize the I475del variant of TMEM63B, demonstrating that I475del exhibits pressure-induced potentiation of ionic currents, establishing it as a variant with GOF MSC activity. Additionally, I475del displays spontaneous constitutive lipid scramblase activity similar to V44M and T481N. Strikingly, however, I475del uniquely enables osmolarity-induced potentiation of scramblase activity, revealing a previously unappreciated mechanistic coupling between the mechanosensitive gating of TMEM63B and its proposed scramblase function. This raises the possibility that mechanically activated lipid scrambling may represent an additional, underappreciated aspect of TMEM63B pathophysiology. Furthermore, we propose that the I475del variant enables an elusive ion- and lipid-open conformation, providing a stratification of functional effects caused by TMEM63B clinical variants. This molecular model informs both our mechanistic understanding of these proteins and future efforts to treat the underlying variant-associated diseases.

DISCUSSIONOur efforts reveal that the I475del variant is unique among characterized TMEM63B variants for promoting both ion and lipid permeation. Unlike V44M and T481N (Zheng et al., 2025), the I475del channel exhibits voltage-driven leak currents in the absence of applied pressure (Figure 1). Notably, the V44M and T481N missense variants were also shown to have reduced current amplitudes relative to WT, which was attributed to reduced plasma membrane localization (Zheng et al., 2025). Yet, in spite of reduced membrane localization for the I475del variant (Figure 6), we found increased current amplitudes, which were exacerbated under applied pressure (Figure 1). We ascribe these differences to the elevated pressure sensitivity of the I475del variant (Figure 2), a value that is likely underestimated due to the experiments being conducted at sub-saturating pressures. Importantly, we cannot rule out the possibility of contributions from altered ion conductance or open probability, and future experiments will be required to disentangle individual contributions. That said, our results firmly differentiate ion permeation for the deletion variant from the V44M and T481N missense variants (Zheng et al., 2025).On the other hand, we also demonstrate that like the previously characterized V44M or T481N variants (Zheng et al., 2025), I475del induces a channel-to-scramblase transition, rendering the mutant TMEM63B capable of constitutively permeating phospholipids (Figure 3). Scramblase activity is evidenced through the punctate pattern of PS exposure, which has been previously observed for other TMEM63 clinical (Zheng et al., 2025) and synthetic variants (Lowry et al., 2024). This unusual pattern of scramblase activity remains mysterious, especially given the uniform plasma membrane localization observed for the I475del variant (Figure 6). Like its ion channel activity, I475del (but not V44M or T481N) scramblase activity is potentiated in response to osmotic pressure changes resulting from hypotonic challenge (Figure 4), suggesting that the deletion destabilizes the hydrophobic neck region more dramatically than the missense variants. Finally, we connect I475del-induced functional effects to previously established TMEM63B conformational changes in the gating cycle (Miyata et al., 2024), showing that adding mutants to prevent these conformational changes also prevents ion channel and lipid scramblase activities attributed to the I475del variant (Figure 5) without being confounded by preventing their plasma membrane localization (Figure 6).Our study shows that overexpression of WT TMEM63B in HEK293T cells does not produce constitutive or hypotonicity-induced lipid scrambling (Figure 3 and Figure 4), calling into question recent studies proposing that WT TMEM63B itself functions as a lipid scramblase (Niu et al., 2024; Miyata et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2026). Instead, our results, together with prior work (Zheng et al., 2025), show that TMEM63B channelopathy variants, including V44M, T481N, I475del, exhibit constitutive lipid scrambling activity, suggesting that these variants destabilize the gate sufficiently to permit a baseline level of lipid permeation, even in the absence of mechanical stimulation. Relative to V44M and T481N, the gate-destabilizing effect of I475del appears substantially stronger, consistent with its gain-of-function effects on both ion and lipid permeability.We therefore propose a stratified model of TMEM63B channelopathies and scramblase-opathies (Figure 7), in which variant-induced destabilization of the hydrophobic gate region allows either lipid permeation alone (in the case of V44M, T481N), or both ion and lipid permeation (in the case of I475del) in the absence of applied pressure. Under mechanical stimulation, V44M and T481N retain WT-like ion permeation, albeit with reduced current amplitudes, whereas I475del shows potentiation of both ion and lipid permeation. We propose that the elevated gate destabilization caused by I475del may be caused by a shift in the register of TM4, broadly altering the hydrophobic balance along a key gating interface between TMs 0, 4, and 6—an interface which has previously been shown to play a crucial role in ion and lipid permeation for TMEM63 proteins (Lowry et al., 2024). In contrast, V44M and T481N may produce more localized perturbations at this hydrophobic interface, leading to milder gate destabilization.In summary, our findings support a stratified model of hydrophobic gate destabilization to explain the distinctive pathophysiology of TMEM63B-associated disease variants. This framework advances the mechanistic understanding of TMEM63B in health and disease, inspiring potential avenues toward variant-specific therapeutic strategies.

Electrophysiology and data analysis

Recordings were conducted with an Axopatch 200B amplifier, and signals were digitized at 10 kHz via an Axon Digidata 1550A (Molecular Devices, Inc., San Jose, CA). Recordings were performed at room temperature between 24 and 48 hours post-transfection using glass pipettes drawn from borosilicate capillaries (Sutter Instruments, Novato, CA). Pipettes were subsequently fire-polished using a microforge (Narishige, Amityville, NY) with resistances ranging from 2 to 4 MΩ in the bath solution.

All recordings were performed in the cell-attach patch clamp configuration. The bath solution contained (in mM): 140 KCl, 10 HEPES, 2 MgCl2, 10 glucose, pH 7.3 adjusted with KOH, while the pipette solution contained (in mM): 130 NaCl, 5 KCl, 10 HEPES, 10 TEA-Cl, 1 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, pH 7.3 adjusted with NaOH. Pressure-activated currents were elicited using a high-speed pressure clamp system (ALA Scientific Instruments, Farmingdale, NY, Cat. #HSPC-1). Where indicated, voltage protocols were run with and without pressure and mechanosensitive currents were isolated by subtracting the voltage-only traces from the voltage-plus-pressure traces.

Electrophysiological data analysis was conducted in Clampfit (Molecular Devices, Inc., San Jose, CA), Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA), and GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad, Boston, MA). Individual I-V curves were fitted with a Boltzmann function:

(1)
I(V)=Imax/min1+e−ZFV−V50RT

where Imax/min represents either the peak outward or inward current, Z the net charge movement during the closed to open state transitions, F the Faraday constant, and V50 the voltage of half-maximal current activation. Half-maximal pressure (P50) was calculated by fitting current-pressure (I-P) traces with a similar Boltzmann function:

(2)
I(P)=Imax1+eP50−Pκ

Where κ represents a slope factor.
