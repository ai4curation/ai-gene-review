Over 300 million people globally are affected by rare diseases, many of which
present predominantly with neurologic symptoms. Rare neurologic disorders pose
significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges including delayed diagnoses,
limited treatment options, and a shortage of specialists. However, advancements
in diagnostics, particularly next-generation sequencing and expansion of newborn
screening, have significantly shortened the time to diagnosis for many of these
disorders. Concurrently, the past decade has witnessed exponential development
of new treatments for rare neurologic diseases, with several approved gene
therapies and more trials under way. A range of targeted therapies now offers
hope for not only symptomatic management but also for disease modification. As
treatments transition from clinical trials to clinical practice, the
responsibility of identifying and monitoring patients may increasingly fall on
the general neurologists. This evolving therapeutic landscape highlights the
urgent need to enhance our understanding of this new class of medications and
the details on clinical eligibility and monitoring of patients with diseases
that have approved gene therapies. This article provides a comprehensive
overview of gene-targeted therapies currently available for neurologic
disorders, with a focus on their mechanisms, challenges, and post-treatment
considerations.

The approved lentiviral approaches are classified as ex vivo gene therapy because the vector is introduced into the patient's cells outside the body by autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (auto-HSCT). In general, the major risks from ex vivo gene therapy arise from the use of conditioning agents and the risk of viral genome integration into critical regions of the host DNA, which could lead to unanticipated pathogenicity, such as cancer, that could occur several years after the intervention. Auto-HSCT is associated with a 3%–5% risk of secondary hematologic malignancy at 5 years,6 although the risk may be higher for some gene therapies.7,8 It is likely that both the conditioning regimen and the specifics of the plasmid design play a critical role in the overall risk of future cancer. In addition, auto-HSCT gene therapy products are manufactured individually, which increases the time to treatment by months.

Contrarily, AAV vectors are in vivo gene therapies because the products are given directly to patients, which eliminates the need for individual manufacturing. They, however, carry additional challenges including the risk of host immunologic responses, which can result in clinical complications, such as thrombotic microangiopathy, cardiac involvement, and potentially fatal hepatitis.9

In addition, the in vivo delivery methods such as intrathecal, intracisternal magna, and intraparenchymal routes that aim to bypass the blood-brain barrier carry risks associated with their specific route of administration themselves. Each approach has different cellular tropism and varying side effect profiles, which, depending on each specific disease, may be more or less desirable.

For patients with disease phenotypes resulting from null mutations, there is also a risk that the body will react to the newly replaced protein product introduced through any of the abovementioned gene therapy approaches. Cross-reactive immunologic material status may be an important predictor in those at higher risk of poor response to gene therapy,10 although this is best understood in the context of enzyme replacement strategies.

Hepatotoxicity with transaminase elevation is believed to be secondary to cell-mediated immunity to the vector capsid.23 Across clinical trials, asymptomatic elevations have been observed in 90% of patients, typically beginning around day 7 and resolving by 2 months after treatment while on a steroid regimen.24,25 In postmarketing data, however, serious symptomatic cases of liver injury, including a few cases of liver failure and death, have been reported.25,26 Therefore, patients with transaminase levels or total bilirubin above twice the upper limit of normal, therefore, should not be dosed, except for isolated neonatal hyperbilirubinemia. Per current recommendations, patients should receive a steroid course beginning 24 hours before infusion and continue it for at least 1 month after dosing. Liver function tests and coagulation parameters should be monitored closely during this time. Given the use of steroids, consideration should be given to adjusting the vaccination schedule.While transient thrombocytopenia was observed within the first 2 weeks of treatment in clinical trials, a few cases of TMA were identified after marketing, some of which were fatal.25,27 In addition to platelet monitoring, patients should be followed closely for hypertension, bruises, seizures, and decreased urine output. Routine monitoring of troponin-I levels is also recommended, given asymptomatic elevations noted in trials and preclinical evidence of cardiac toxicity.25 While there is a theoretical risk for insertional mutagenesis, only one case of tumor without confirmed causal transgene insertion has been reported to date.28 Finally, as with other AAV-mediated therapies, vector shedding in body waste is known to occur during the first month. Therefore, routine precautions such as hand hygiene should be implemented.

Biochemical efficacy of these therapies has been demonstrated by measuring the percentage increase in expression of dystrophin compared with baseline while clinical efficacy has been illustrated with improvements in the 6-minute walk test (6MWT) and pulmonary function testing.44Delandistrogene moxeparvovec-rokl is a AAVrh74-based IV gene therapy containing a microdystrophin transgene, approved for ambulatory and nonambulatory patients older than 4 years with a confirmed gene mutation in the DMD gene. ConclusionRare neurologic diseases represent a significant area of hope, given the rapid advances in diagnostics and novel therapeutics. Current trials and preclinical studies are evaluating the role of cutting-edge gene therapy techniques such as recombinant AAVs with improved cell specificity, lipid nanoparticles, transferrin receptor ligand tagging for CNS uptake, CRISPR, and gene-editing approaches. Presymptomatic treatments in those identified through NBS and the potential for in utero treatment bring new horizons for altering the natural history of these diseases. Despite its promise, gene therapy also raises significant practical and ethical challenges. As exemplified by the withdrawal of eli-cel in Europe, the high cost of these therapies has emerged as a major barrier. Differential access to treatment has raised equity concerns. Ethical concerns are also important when considering experimental therapies. Addressing the rising knowledge gap, standardizing approaches to evaluate variants of uncertain significance, developing regulated monitoring protocols for presymptomatic patients, and creating dedicated research and clinical infrastructure are imperative to assess the long-term benefits and address the challenges of gene therapy.
