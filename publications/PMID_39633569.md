BACKGROUND: Severe malaria is associated with impaired nitric oxide (NO)
synthase (NOS)-dependent vasodilation, and reversal of this deficit improves
survival in murine models. Malaria might have selected for genetic polymorphisms
that increase endothelial NO signaling and now contribute to heterogeneity in
vascular function among humans. One protein potentially selected for is alpha
globin, which, in mouse models, interacts with endothelial NOS (eNOS) to
negatively regulate NO signaling. We sought to evaluate the impact of alpha
globin gene deletions on NO signaling and unexpectedly found human arteries use
not only alpha but also beta globin to regulate eNOS.
METHODS: The eNOS-hemoglobin complex was characterized by multiphoton imaging,
gene expression analysis, and coimmunoprecipitation studies of human resistance
arteries. Novel contacts between eNOS and hemoglobin were mapped using molecular
modeling and simulation. Pharmacological or genetic disruption of the
eNOS-hemoglobin complex was evaluated using pressure myography. The association
between alpha globin gene deletion and blood pressure was assessed in a
population study.
RESULTS: Alpha and beta globin transcripts were detected in the endothelial
layer of the artery wall. Imaging colocalized alpha and beta globin proteins
with eNOS at myoendothelial junctions. Immunoprecipitation demonstrated that
alpha globin and beta globin form a complex with eNOS and cytochrome b5
reductase. Modeling predicted negatively charged glutamic acids at positions 6
and 7 of beta globin to interact with positively charged arginines at positions
97 and 98 of eNOS. Arteries from donors with a glutamic acid-to-valine
substitution at beta globin position 6 (sickle trait) exhibited increased
NOS-dependent vasodilation. Alpha globin gene deletions were associated with
decreased arterial alpha globin expression, increased NOS-dependent
vasodilation, and lower blood pressure. Mimetic peptides that targeted the
interactions between hemoglobin and eNOS recapitulated the effects of these
genetic variants on human arterial vasoreactivity.
CONCLUSIONS: Alpha and beta globin subunits of hemoglobin interact with eNOS to
restrict NO signaling in human resistance arteries. Malaria-protective genetic
variants that alter the expression of alpha globin or the structure of beta
globin are associated with increased NOS-dependent vasodilation. Targeting the
hemoglobin-eNOS interface could potentially improve NO signaling in diseases of
endothelial dysfunction such as severe malaria or chronic cardiovascular
conditions.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Alpha thalassemia silent carriers and individuals with sickle cell trait share a common vascular phenotype of increased NOS-dependent inhibition of arterial vasoconstriction.

Genetic variants that arose because they reduce the severity of malaria now contribute to heterogeneity in vascular function and vascular disease risk in human populations.

Human arterial vasoconstriction can be inhibited by targeting hemoglobin–endothelial NOS interactions with molecules that mimic key structures of hemoglobin but lack heme.

One of the ways arterial endothelial cells regulate blood flow is through enzymatic synthesis and release of nitric oxide (NO), a free radical that activates soluble guanylate cyclase in the neighboring smooth muscle to initiate a biochemical cascade culminating in arterial dilation.1 Initially modeled as a gas that passively diffused across cell membranes,2–4 NO signaling is now recognized to occur in a more directed fashion in resistance arteries. The directionality of NO is in part a result of polarized endothelium in resistance arteries that contain myoendothelial junctions where NO synthesis is colocalized with channels that facilitate the transit of NO to nearby targets in smooth muscle.5–9 The exchange of NO, ions, and other small molecules across the myoendothelial junction couples vasodilatory signals with vasoconstrictive signals to enable rapid changes in artery diameter while maintaining overall vascular homeostasis.10–15

Recently, a new mechanism of vasoregulation was discovered whereby the alpha subunit of hemoglobin regulates the release of NO through direct physical and chemical interactions with endothelial NO synthase (eNOS).16 Alpha globin catalyzes the reaction of NO with molecular oxygen to form nitrate and prevent NO from crossing the cell membrane to its smooth muscle receptors.17 Disruption of the interactions between alpha globin and eNOS increases NO signaling, dilates arteries, and lowers blood pressure in cellular and preclinical models.18–21 Arteries from hypertensive donors dilated in response to treatment with an alpha globin mimetic peptide ex vivo,19 and individuals heterozygous for an alpha globin gene deletion had increased flow-mediated dilation, suggesting that alpha globin plays a role in regulating vasodilation in humans.22 Population-based studies revealed the alpha globin gene deletion to be associated with protection against chronic and end-stage kidney disease,23 but it was not consistently associated with blood pressure, hypertension, or ischemic stroke.24–26

To better understand the evolving role of endothelial alpha globin in human arterial vasoregulation, we sought to characterize the expression, binding partners, and subcellular localization of alpha globin in resistance arteries obtained from human omental and subcutaneous adipose tissues. We found that human arteries express not only the alpha but also the beta subunit of hemoglobin. We used molecular modeling and molecular dynamics simulation to understand the revised hemoglobin-eNOS signaling complex and designed mimetic peptides that targeted the newly discovered interactions between beta globin and eNOS. We demonstrate that disrupting eNOS–beta globin or eNOS–alpha globin interactions increases NO signaling and inhibits vasoconstriction of human arteries. Last, we study individuals heterozygous for genetic variants in either the alpha or beta globin genes to understand the effects of these mutations on arterial vasoregulation and blood pressure. We focused on heterozygous individuals to avoid the potentially confounding effects of anemia, hemolysis, and NO scavenging that can lead to chronic vasculopathy in people living with homozygous sickle cell disease.27–29

Based on the studies presented herein, we propose that sickle cell trait, in which an amino acid substitution disrupts the interactions of beta globin with eNOS, and alpha thalassemia, in which vascular expression of alpha globin is decreased, both converge on a common phenotype of increased NO synthase (NOS)–dependent feedback vasodilation. This finding offers new insight into the vascular physiology of 2 common human hemoglobinopathies and highlights a potential mechanism by which sickle cell trait and alpha thalassemia might confer protection against severe malaria,30–35 a disease characterized by impaired NO bioavailability and endothelial dysfunction.36–42 Furthermore, these studies highlight specific structures, some of which may have been selected by malaria for their critical role in regulating NO signaling, that could potentially be targeted to treat severe malaria and other diseases in which endothelial NO signaling is impaired.

Within the ROI, the autofluorescent globin complexes were identified as surface objects, based on fluorescence signals in channels 2, 3, and 4, and the fluorescence intensity and volume of each were calculated. Molecular Dynamics SimulationModels of the human eNOS oxygenase domain homodimer and hemoglobin heterotetramer were first built from their crystal structures (PDB 3NOS and 6BB5), subjected to short (5 ns) molecular dynamics simulations, and then assembled into an eNOS-hemoglobin complex by overlaying each monomer on their predicted interaction mode. This initial conformation was used in all subsequent simulations, including the beta globin E6V mutant, which was created by replacing E for V. Residues 1 to 66 and 481 to 1203 of the eNOS protein, absent in the crystal, were not modeled. AlphaFold2 showed a poor prediction score for the proline-rich 1 to 66 segment, possibly because of its intrinsic disorder. Because this segment is at the predicted eNOS/hemoglobin interface, the N-term of the modeled eNOS (at K67) was acetylated to avoid possible electrostatic artifacts; all other termini in the complex were uncapped. Assuming Asp– and Glu– unprotonated and Arg+ and Lys+ protonated, the protonation state of His was set so that the total charges of the monomers and the complex were minimized (His+ in eNOS and His0 in hemoglobin); the complex was neutralized by the addition of 6 Cl− ions (4 in the E6V mutant), and 112 K+ and 112 Cl− ions were added to mimic near-physiological electrolyte concentration. The ions were randomly distributed in the water phase after the complex was solvated and overlapping water molecules removed. All bond lengths involving hydrogen atoms were constrained with the SHAKE algorithm, and an integration step of 2 fs was used. The temperature and pressure were maintained with the Hoover thermostat, using a mass of 103 kcal mol−1 ps2, and with the Langevin piston method, with mass and collision frequency of 400 atomic mass units and 20 ps−1. After standard protocols of heating and equilibration, a productive phase of 30 ns was conducted and analysis performed over the last 20 ns. Three independent simulations were performed for the HbA and HbS eNOS-hemoglobin complexes. Soft definitions of H-bond/salt-bridge and hydrophobic/nonpolar interactions are based on a distance (δ) criterion between donor and acceptor atoms (δAD<4 Å, where AD indicates acceptor-donor) and side-chain carbon atoms (δCC<4.8, where CC indicates carbon-carbon), respectively. This revealed distinct autofluorescent puncta distributed in the wall of the human arteries that were absent from murine arteries imaged in the same fashion (Figure 1A through 1D). The broad-spectrum autofluorescent emissions, detectable across 4 detector channels ranging from 393 to 717 nm, were characteristic of hemoglobin under multiphoton excitation.57 When the arterial wall puncta were compared against RBC intentionally retained in the arterial lumen (Figure 1E and 1F), the emission wavelengths were similar; however, the arterial wall puncta were distinguishable from RBC and RBC clusters based on their smaller size (median [25th, 75th]: 16 [7, 30] versus 212 [109, 320] µm3; P<0.0001) and greater intensity (107 [96, 118] versus 34 [33, 36] arbitrary brightness units; P<0.0001; Figure S1). Beta globin E6 is the site of the sickle cell mutation, which confers resistance to severe malaria in heterozygous form, but in homozygous form facilitates polymerization of deoxyhemoglobin in the red blood to cause sickle cell disease. To understand the potential consequences of the glutamic acid to valine substitution (E6V) on hemoglobin-eNOS interactions in the vascular endothelium, we carried out molecular dynamics simulations of hemoglobin S or hemoglobin A with eNOS. Simulation confirmed the critical intermolecular contacts identified by the docking algorithm between negatively charged glutamic acids at positions 6 (E6) and 7 (E7) and positively charged arginines of eNOS (R97, R98, K67, R98) (Figure 7A through 7D). Introduction of the sickle cell mutation, which replaces negatively charged glutamic acid at beta globin position 6 with nonpolar valine (E6V), disrupted the interactions with R97 and R98 of eNOS1 and between E7 of beta globin and R97 of eNOS2. The only strong interactions that remained were between eNOS1 R112 and alpha globin1 E23, whereas new hydrophobic/nonpolar interactions developed to partially compensate for the loss of E6 (Videos S1 and S2; Figures S9 and S10). Several proline residues, particularly in eNOS, contribute to these interactions. K67 might interact electrostatically with E6, and thus be affected by the E6V variant; however, the lack of structures for amino acids 1 to 66 of eNOS limits the evaluation of K67. Overall, the E6V variant was associated with partial detachment and reorientation of hemoglobin relative to eNOS resulting in new positions of the heme groups relative to one another. This raised the question of whether the structural changes introduced by the sickle cell mutation would affect the activity of the hemoglobin-eNOS complex and alter vasoreactivity of human arteries.

The ability of hemoglobin to restrict the release of NO may be dependent on noncovalent interactions between the eNOS oxygenase domain and specific amino acids on both the alpha and beta subunits. Most striking are the glutamic acids at positions 6 and 7 of beta globin, predicted to interact with arginines at positions 97 and 98 of the eNOS oxygenase domain. Arteries from individuals who carry a glutamic acid to valine substitution at position 6 of beta globin, also known as sickle cell trait, exhibit a phenotype of increased NOS-dependent feedback vasodilation. We used both human genetic and pharmacological approaches to independently target the alpha and beta subunits of hemoglobin and to investigate the role of endothelial hemoglobin as a restrictor of NOS-dependent feedback vasodilation. Targeting hemoglobin-eNOS interactions with either the alpha or beta globin mimetic peptide significantly diminished the phenylephrine vasoconstriction response of human resistance arteries in a manner that was entirely NOS-dependent. In contrast, the diminished vasoconstrictive responses to phenylephrine associated with the globin genetic variants alpha thalassemia and sickle cell trait were only partially restored by NOS inhibition. This suggests that other vasoregulatory pathways in addition to NOS-dependent signaling may be involved in determining the vascular phenotypes associated with these globin gene variants.

To extend this mechanism from the context of isolated arteries to the level of blood pressure in a population, we examined the relationship between blood pressure and alpha globin genotype among Cambodian children where the alpha globin gene deletion is common. We found the alpha globin gene deletion to be associated with lower systolic and diastolic blood pressure in analyses that adjusted for 2 major determinants of blood pressure in children: age and height. This finding contrasts with a study of Kenyan adolescents that found no association between alpha globin genotype and systolic or diastolic blood pressure64 and a study in adults that found no association between alpha globin genotype and clinical hypertension.25 The association of alpha globin gene deletions with blood pressure among young children but not among adolescents or older adults might be explained by the decline of vascular NO signaling in resistance arteries with age or the predominance of other blood pressure determinants later in life.65 Alpha globin gene deletions have also been associated with lower prevalence of chronic kidney disease and with lower incidence of progression to end-stage kidney disease among African American adults.23 The current study provides a potential mechanism, enhanced NO release and resistance to alpha adrenergic vasoconstriction, by which the progression of kidney disease might be prevented or slowed in individuals with alpha globin gene deletions.

Severe malaria is associated with endothelial dysfunction and decreased endothelial NO bioavailability,40–42,66–69 and clinical trials of NO precursors such as arginine or inhaled NO have been carried out with the goal of restoring endothelial function and reducing malaria severity.70–72 The discovery that endothelial NO signaling is enhanced by the beta globin E6V variant and by alpha thalassemia links these known malaria-protective variants to an established pathophysiology of malaria involving impaired NO signaling and endothelial dysfunction. This highlights a potential mechanism through which these globin variants may protect against severe malaria.
