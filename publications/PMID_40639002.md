Toll-like receptor (TLR)-mediated recognition of specific ligands is critical
for initiating innate immunity against Eimeria infections. However, the
avian-specific TLR15 has unclear immunological functions, including its specific
ligands and role in anti-coccidia immunity. In this study, an aspartyl protease
from Eimeria tenella (EteASP) was identified as a potential TLR15 ligand by
co-immunoprecipitation. Two distinct TLR15-expressing innate immune cell
populations, macrophages and dendritic cells, were employed as models to
investigate the immune responses triggered by recombinant EteASP protein
(rEteASP). In macrophages, rEteASP induced robust immune activation, associated
with the stimulation of both NF-κB and MAPK pathways. This activation correlated
with increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α),
nitric oxide, and reactive oxygen species, alongside enhanced phagocytosis.
Furthermore, rEteASP promoted classical dendritic cell activation in chicken
bone marrow-derived dendritic cells, marked by stellate morphology and
upregulated CD11c and MHC II surface expression. The activation patterns in
chBMDCs mirrored those in macrophages, characterized by increased production of
immune mediators. A mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR) further demonstrated that
rEteASP-activated dendritic cells efficiently triggered naive T cell activation,
driving a shift towards balanced Th1/Th2-type cellular immune response.
Collectively, these findings establish EteASP as a potent immunomodulator
capable of activating key innate immune effector functions in macrophages and
dendritic cells, and bridging to adaptive immunity. Our data implicate TLR15 as
a potential receptor contributing to these responses, broadening the
investigation of TLR15 ligands in protozoa and offering novel insights into
Eimeria-host immune interactions. However, the potential involvement of
additional pattern recognition receptors in EteASP recognition and the
definitive role of TLR15 requires further investigation.

Introduction

Avian coccidiosis is a severe intestinal parasitic disease caused by one or multiple Eimeria species, representing a major threat to the poultry industry. Coccidia-infected chickens typically show symptoms including anorexia, lethargy, and white watery or hemorrhagic diarrhea, leading to reduced weight gain and declines in overall production performance (Dalloul and Lillehoj, 2006). In severe cases, chicken coccidiosis result in mortality rates as high as 80 %, with annual economic impact of the disease escalating to a staggering £10.4 billion globally (Blake et al., 2020). Current control of avian coccidiosis relies primarily on pharmacological interventions. However, escalating issues of Eimeria strain resistance and drug residues necessitate novel control strategies. Immunoprophylaxis offers a promising alternative, requiring deeper understanding of host immune mechanisms against Eimeria for effective vaccine development. While existing research focuses predominantly on adaptive immunity, innate immune responses remain inadequately characterized.

As the first line of host defense, innate immunity provides rapid, broad-spectrum anti-infectious immunity via pattern recognition receptor (PRR)-mediated signaling cascades, while simultaneously orchestrating the initiation of specific adaptive immune responses. PRRs, especially the Toll-like receptor (TLR)-mediated activation of macrophages and dendritic cells, are considered a critical bridge linking between innate and adaptive immunity (Akira et al., 2001). Macrophages and dendritic cells recognize target antigens via diverse TLRs localized on cell surfaces or intracellular membranes, thereby activating pivotal downstream signaling pathways and facilitating antigen presentation to effector cells. The resulting cascade drives the expression and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and immune mediators, playing a crucial role in the early defense against parasitic infections and the regulation of adaptive immunity (Akira et al., 2001). For example, the lipophosphoglycan (LPG) and glycoprotein-lipid complexes of Leishmania parasites respectively activated mammalian TLR2 and TLR4, triggering the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IFN-γ) and pathogen-killing mediators like nitric oxide (NO) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) in NK cells and macrophages (Becker et al., 2003; Hawn et al., 2002; Whitaker et al., 2008; Colmenares et al., 2001). Similar phenomena have been observed in the early infections by Plasmodium and Toxoplasma gondii (T. gondii) (Wu et al., 2010a; Yarovinsky et al., 2005; Koblansky et al., 2013). It is evident that TLR-mediated recognition of parasites and their components by macrophages and dendritic cells is essential for triggering the early immune response to parasite infections. However, the complex nature of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) often means they can be recognized by multiple PRRs, either independently or cooperatively.

TLRs belong to an ancient family of PRRs, the TLR homologs across species recognize conserved ligand profiles. However, certain species have developed unique TLRs as adaptive responses to the distinct selective pressures of their specific environment. TLR15 is a TLR exclusive to the immune system of birds and reptiles, where it may play a role in coordinating the immune response to a range of avian-specific diseases. For instance, chicken TLR15 expression is significantly upregulated in response to Salmonella Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium) stimulation in vivo and in vitro experimental models (Higgs et al., 2006). Furthermore, TLR15 expression in chicken macrophages and chondrocytes was significantly upregulated upon stimulation with a C-terminal diacylated lipopeptide (MDLP) from Mycoplasma synoviae (Oven et al., 2013). In chicken coccidiosis, invasion by Eimeria tenella (E. tenella) sporozoites led to a similar upregulation of the transcription level of TLR15 in chicken macrophages and heterophils (Zhou et al., 2013; Sandholt et al., 2021). These findings suggest that TLR15 is upregulated during infections with various poultry-related pathogens, highlighting its potential role in pathogen recognition. Notably, the definitive official ligand for TLR15 remains unknown. Current evidence tentatively links its activation to fungal-origin heat-stable proteases, but conclusive ligand-receptor validation remains pending (de Zoete, et al., 2011; Boyd et al., 2012; Voogdt et al., 2018).

In the present study, a eukaryotic aspartyl protease (named EteASP) from E. tenella was identified through co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) as a potential ligand interacting with TLR15. Subsequent mechanistic investigations in macrophages and dendritic cells revealed that the recombinant protein form of this Eimeria component (rEteASP) demonstrated robust immunostimulatory potency. Specifically, rEteASP induced the activation of various innate immune responses and triggered Th1-type cellular immunity, which plays a key role in coccidiosis.

Production performance losses and mortality caused by avian coccidiosis remain a significant impediment to the sustainable development of the global poultry industry. During the early phase of Eimeria infection, host PRRs act as molecular sentinels to detect specific parasite components, initiating a tightly coordinated signaling cascade for early pathogen containment and the priming of adaptive immunity. As pivotal PRRs, mammalian TLRs initiate anti-parasitic immunity against Leishmania, T. gondii, and Plasmodium infections through recognition of distinct parasite-derived components (Becker et al., 2003; Wu et al., 2010a; Yarovinsky et al., 2005; Koblansky et al., 2013). However, the specific TLR ligands involved in Eimeria-chicken interactions remained uncharacterized. This study identified an aspartyl protease from E. tenella (EteASP) as a candidate ligand interacting with TLR15 using Co-IP and elucidated its significant intrinsic immunostimulatory capacity in chicken macrophages and chBMDCs. These findings expand the exploration of potential ligands for TLR15, provide insights into potential mechanisms of TLR15 engagement, and establish EteASP as a potent immunomodulatory component that could inform the rational design of novel anticoccidial strategies.

The candidate TLR15-interacting protein EteASP (annotated as full-length Eimepsin III) exhibits significant homology to T. gondii TgASP3, a protease facilitating host cell entry and egress (Dogga et al., 2017). Its stage-specific expression across E. tenella infectious stages supports a potential role in host-parasite interactions (Katrib et al., 2012). The observed Co-IP interaction with TLR15 suggests a potential mechanism by which the host might detect this virulence-associated protease.

Functional characterization revealed that EteASP induces potent innate immune activation. In macrophages, EteASP was associated with the activation of canonical NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades, driving a robust pro-inflammatory response characterized by the upregulation of key mediators (IL-1β, CXCLi1/2, iNOS) and enhanced phagocytic capacity. Intriguingly, certain cytokines (IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, TNF-α) exhibited a concentration-dependent induction pattern. This dose-response relationship suggests sophisticated immune regulation, potentially acting as a safeguard against excessive inflammation at lower ligand concentrations or during early infection, while enabling potent effector functions at higher pathogen loads. This may involve more complex signaling regulation, such as feedback mechanisms or differential activation thresholds, which require further investigation.

In chBMDCs generated using established protocols, EteASP similarly induced maturation (upregulating CD40, CD80, CD83, CD86, MHC II, and CD11c) and secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators (van den Biggelaar et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2010b). Most significantly, EteASP-activated chBMDCs effectively primed spleen T cells towards a mixed Th1/Th2 cytokine profile (elevated IL-2, IFN-γ, and IL-4) and expanded both CD4+ and CD8α+
T cell subsets. While these responses align with established Th1/Th2-mediated protective immunity against avian coccidiosis, potential contributions from the 19 % non-CD3 spleen cells (primarily B and NK cells) in co-cultures should nonetheless be considered (Vermeulen, 1998; Bessay et al., 1996; Belli et al., 2004; Constantinoiu et al., 2008). Such cells may modestly elevate IFN-γ (NK-derived) or IL-4 (B cell-derived) background levels. Future studies employing CD3-beads sorting of CD3+
T cells could preclude this interference to refine cellular results. Collectively, our findings establish EteASP as a potent inducer of innate and adaptive immunity in chickens, offering robust protection against coccidiosis.

Notably, while both EteASP and LPS (TLR4 agonist) induced robust DC maturation, distinct patterns in cytokine induction (differential regulation of IFN-γ and TLR15 transcripts) were observed. This suggests potential differences in signaling intensity, kinetics, or preferential engagement of downstream adaptor molecules between the pathways activated by these stimuli in avian DCs. Understanding these nuanced differences could reveal specialized roles for different PRR pathways in orchestrating the avian immune response to diverse pathogens.

Crucially, while our data identify an interaction between EteASP and TLR15 and utilize TLR15-expressing cells, the definitive role of TLR15 in mediating the observed immune effects remains to be established and necessitates further investigation. It is essential to consider that immune cells express a diverse repertoire of PRRs. Therefore, the robust immunostimulatory activity of EteASP demonstrated here could be mediated by TLR15 acting alone, in concert with other PRRs (such as other TLRs, NOD-like receptors, or C-type lectin receptors). This potential for multiple recognition pathways is biologically plausible, given the complex nature of many parasite-derived PAMPs. To fully elucidate the recognition mechanisms and define the functional contribution of TLR15, key future directions include: (1) Definitively validating the necessity of TLR15 for the immune effects using genetic knockdown/knockout models; (2) Screening for other potential EteASP-interacting receptors; and (3) Assessing the physiological relevance in vivo, specifically determining if TLR15 deficiency impairs responses to EteASP or impacts E. tenella parasite control, and evaluating whether EteASP contributes to protective immunity as a vaccine component. Addressing these questions is crucial for translating the discovery of EteASP's immunomodulatory potential into practical applications.

In conclusion, this study identifies EteASP as a potent immunomodulatory component of E. tenella capable of activating key innate immune pathways and driving a protective Th1/Th2-balanced adaptive immune response. Our data implicate TLR15 as a candidate receptor potentially contributing to these effects based on co-immunoprecipitation and the use of TLR15-expressing cells. This work significantly advances the investigation of potential ligands for avian TLR15 and characterizes a novel parasite-derived immunostimulant. The potent and balanced immune response elicited by EteASP underscores its considerable potential as a candidate for developing novel anti-coccidial strategies. Future research should focus on genetically validating the functional contribution of TLR15, elucidating the precise molecular mechanism of EteASP recognition (including the potential involvement of other PRRs), and evaluating the efficacy of EteASP-based strategies in Eimeria-challenge models.
