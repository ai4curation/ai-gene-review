The mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC) governs the entry of pyruvate-a central
metabolite that bridges cytosolic glycolysis with mitochondrial oxidative
phosphorylation-into the mitochondrial matrix1-5. It thus serves as a pivotal
metabolic gatekeeper and has fundamental roles in cellular metabolism. Moreover,
MPC is a key target for drugs aimed at managing diabetes, non-alcoholic
steatohepatitis and neurodegenerative diseases4-6. However, despite MPC's
critical roles in both physiology and medicine, the molecular mechanisms
underlying its transport function and how it is inhibited by drugs have remained
largely unclear. Here our structural findings on human MPC define the
architecture of this vital transporter, delineate its substrate-binding site and
translocation pathway, and reveal its major conformational states. Furthermore,
we explain the binding and inhibition mechanisms of MPC inhibitors. Our findings
provide the molecular basis for understanding MPC's function and pave the way
for the development of more-effective therapeutic reagents that target MPC.

## Full Text

Cells derive energy from core metabolic reactions segregated across two compartments: glucose breakdown via glycolysis in the cytosol and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production through oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) within mitochondria. At the heart of these processes is the import of pyruvate—the end product of glycolysis in cytosol and the major substrate for the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle in mitochondria—from the cytosol into the mitochondrial matrix7. This critical function is performed by the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC), located on the inner mitochondrial membrane1. MPC is thus central to cellular energetics, mediating a critical branchpoint in metabolism: either translocating pyruvate into mitochondria for ATP production and other biosynthesis pathways or keeping pyruvate in cytosol for anabolic processes2-5. Highlighting its critical role in pyruvate metabolism, MPC has been shown to be key in managing ATP production, regulating gluconeogenesis, and shaping amino acids and lipids synthesis5,6,8.

Despite the functional recognition and characterization of MPC over half a century ago1,9, its molecular identity remained unknown until 2012, when two pioneering and complementary studies identified MPC1 and MPC2 as its core components2,3. These paralogous mitochondrial proteins, highly conserved across eukaryotes (Extended Data Fig. 1), are essential and sufficient to give rise to MPC activity10,11. They are distinct from the SLC25 mitochondrial carrier family, constituting a new solute carrier family, SLC54 (ref.12). The identification of MPC ushered in an explosion of research focused on its physiological functions, pharmacology, and pathophysiology4,6,8. Nonetheless, fundamental properties of MPC, such as the stoichiometry and oligomerization state of MPC1 and MPC2, remain unresolved2,8,13,14. Even the topology of MPC1 has not been settled. Protease protection assays suggest MPC1 has two transmembrane helices (TMs), compared with MPC2’s three14,15, but structural predictions indicate both are 3-TM proteins8,16,17. The structure of MPC is needed to define the architecture of this transporter family, elucidate the substrate translocation pathway, and gain insights into MPC's transport mechanism.

MPC activity is vital for maintaining the delicate balance between glycolysis and OXPHOS, which is essential for normal physiology. Dysregulation of MPC is a hallmark of diverse pathologies. For example, MPC activity is suppressed in various cancers5,18, promoting aerobic glycolysis (called the Warburg effect) and tumor proliferation. Furthermore, in cardiovascular systems, MPC dysfunction causes pathological cardiac remodeling19,20.

Given MPC’s pivotal role in pyruvate utilization and metabolism regulation, developing strategies to modulate MPC is a priority. Inhibitors like UK5099, the gold-standard MPC blocker and an α-cyano-cinnamate derivative, have been instrumental in characterizing MPC’s functions over the past 50 years1,21. Numerous additional MPC inhibitors have been identified or developed, opening promising therapeutic avenues for metabolic disorders and chronic degenerative diseases, including type II diabetes22,23, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)23-25, neurodegeneration26-28, specific cancers29, and hair loss30. Notably, MPC is the direct target of thiazolidinediones (TZDs), FDA-approved insulin sensitizers for type-2 diabetes, highly effective in managing blood glucose levels and preserving β-cell functions31-33. Despite significant restrictions due to off-target side-effects, TZDs remain widely prescribed33,34. Thus, enhancing their specificity would have profound implications. Promising new-generation TZDs better targeting MPC have been in phase 2B clinical trials for type II diabetes and NASH25,31,35. Mechanistically, inhibiting MPC suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis, reduces blood glucose levels36,37, and offers protection against NASH23,24. Despite intense studies, how inhibitors bind MPC and inhibit its activity remains unclear. Such knowledge is essential for developing highly specific and selective MPC inhibitors to fully realize their therapeutic potential.

Here we report cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of human MPC in two conformational states and in complex with inhibitors and substrate. These structures, together with functional studies, reveal MPC’s architecture, substrate recognition, translocation pathway, and inhibition mechanisms.

Overall structure

In purified human MPC, MPC1 and MPC2 show a roughly 1:1 stoichiometry based on SDS-PAGE analysis (Extended Data Fig. 2a). These structural studies were facilitated by an MPC-specific nanobody and a nanobody-binding Legobody39 as fiducial markers, along with the inhibitors, which significantly improved MPC’s thermostability.

Structures of MPC in detergent micelles and nanodiscs reveal the same heterodimer architecture, consisting of one copy each of MPC1 and MPC2, oriented uniformly relative to the membrane. The TM helices of MPC1 and MPC2 superimpose fairly well onto each other (Fig. 1c), demonstrating structural conservation despite a relatively low sequence similarity (~24% identity). Accordingly, both MPC1 and MPC2 contain three TMs, in contrast to the previous proposals that they differ in topology with two and three TMs, respectively14,15. Our findings corroborate the 3-TM hypothesis.

Each MPC subunit features a triple-helix bundle (THB), in which TM3 sits between and interacts with both TM1 and TM2, resulting in no direct contact between TM1 and TM2 (Fig. 1b-d). TM3 connects to TM2 through a short loop and tightly packs against it, while its interaction with TM1 is looser, potentially facilitating conformational switches (Fig. 1b). Overall, the 1-3-2 TM arrangement of MPC1 and MPC2 resembles that of the protomer of sugar transporter SemiSWEET, which, in contrast, forms a symmetrical homodimer. This indicates that the 1-3-2 triple helix bundle may be a basic unit for constructing diverse transporters.

MPC1 and MPC2 contain an N-terminal helical element, αLH, preceding TM1, and an elongated C-terminal TM3 (Fig. 1b-c). The αLH is amphiphilic and positioned parallel to the membrane, presumably associating with it, which may provide structural support or facilitate sensing the membrane environment40-42. The αLH does not contact the rest of the protein and is not well resolved, suggesting potential flexibility relative to the main part of the protein.

MPC1-MPC2 heterodimerThe three TMs of MPC1 or MPC2 are apparently insufficient to enclose a substrate translocation pathway. Instead, MPC1 and MPC2 form a heterodimer with a pseudo two-fold symmetry axis perpendicular to the membrane (Fig. 1f). At the dimer interface, TM1 of MPC1 interacts with TM2 of MPC2 on one side, while MPC1’s TM2 packs against MPC2’s TM1 on the other side (Fig. 1g). In contrast, TM3 of MPC1 and MPC2 are well separated from each other and do not make direct contact with the other subunit. Altogether, the six TMs of MPC1 and MPC2 surround a putative substrate translocation pathway at the center. In yeast MPC, the loops that connect TM1 and TM2 in both subunits are accessible for modification by dye that does not permeate the inner membrane14. A solvent-accessible cavity extends from the matrix surface into the membrane. On the intermembrane side, the L1-2 loops of MPC1 and MPC2 (connecting TM1 and TM2) run in opposite directions, interacting closely to seal the intermembrane surface and contributing significantly to the dimer interface. This gives rise to a bucket-like overall backbone architecture, with its opening facing the matrix side.

Cytosol-facing substrate-bound stateWT MPC was captured in a matrix-facing conformation in detergent micelles or nanodiscs. To gain insights into its alternating transport mechanism, we sought to capture its cytosol-facing conformation. We hypothesized that introducing a tryptophane residue at a location expected to be more open in a cytosol-facing conformation than in a matrix-facing conformation could favor or trap the transporter in a cytosol-facing conformation. Similar approaches have been employed to capture alternate conformations in other transporters45,46. Inspired by structures of SemiSWEET47,48, we introduced tryptophan point mutations at the MPC1/MPC2 interface. One such mutation, V74WMPC2, located away from the substrate-binding pocket (described below), successfully trapped MPC in a cytosol-facing conformation (Fig. 5a), where a cavity accessible from the cytosolic side reaches deep into the transporter. Consistent with its role in conformational trapping, which likely stalls the transport cycle, this variant shows minimal transport activity in the yeast growth assay (Extended Data Fig. 3h).MPC structures in both matrix-facing and cytosol-facing conformations help define the putative substrate translocation pathway across the membrane. The central cavities of both conformations overlap around the central axis, closer to the matrix side. This overlapped space, presumably the substrate binding pocket, can be accessed from either side of membrane, providing a basis for alternating access transport. Overall, the electrostatic surface of the substrate translocation pathway is positive, becoming highly positive around the proposed substrate-binding pocket (Fig. 5c). This positively charged environment is compatible with accommodating the substrate pyruvate, an acid that should be negatively charged under physiological conditions.In the cytosol-facing conformation structure, determined with 10 mM pyruvate (substrate), a strong non-proteinaceous density occupied the proposed substrate-binding pocket (Fig. 5b). This density was absent in the matrix-facing conformation structure, determined without substrate (in the presence of inhibitor). Its strategic location should allow alternating access to either side of membrane in the cytosol- or matrix-facing conformation. Moreover, its dimension matches well with that of pyruvate, which shows excellent compatibility with the surrounding chemical environment. We thus tentatively assign a pyruvate there. Nonetheless, the nearly symmetrical shape of pyruvate precludes an unambiguous assignment of its orientation, which we modeled in consideration of its chemical environment. In particular, pyruvate’s carboxyl group forms a salt bridge with the side chain of K49MPC2, which extends towards the pyruvate from the cytosolic side. K49MPC2 is stabilized by the nearby N100MPC2. Y62MPC1 and potentially H84MPC1 are also in range to directly interact with pyruvate's carboxyl group. Below K49MPC2, the pyruvate is surrounded by F66MPC1, L80MPC1, W82MPC2, and L96MPC2 (Fig. 5b). Cytosol-facing substrate-bound state

WT MPC was captured in a matrix-facing conformation in detergent micelles or nanodiscs. To gain insights into its alternating transport mechanism, we sought to capture its cytosol-facing conformation. We hypothesized that introducing a tryptophane residue at a location expected to be more open in a cytosol-facing conformation than in a matrix-facing conformation could favor or trap the transporter in a cytosol-facing conformation. Similar approaches have been employed to capture alternate conformations in other transporters45,46. Inspired by structures of SemiSWEET47,48, we introduced tryptophan point mutations at the MPC1/MPC2 interface. One such mutation, V74WMPC2, located away from the substrate-binding pocket (described below), successfully trapped MPC in a cytosol-facing conformation (Fig. 5a), where a cavity accessible from the cytosolic side reaches deep into the transporter. Consistent with its role in conformational trapping, which likely stalls the transport cycle, this variant shows minimal transport activity in the yeast growth assay (Extended Data Fig. 3h).

MPC structures in both matrix-facing and cytosol-facing conformations help define the putative substrate translocation pathway across the membrane. The central cavities of both conformations overlap around the central axis, closer to the matrix side. This overlapped space, presumably the substrate binding pocket, can be accessed from either side of membrane, providing a basis for alternating access transport. Overall, the electrostatic surface of the substrate translocation pathway is positive, becoming highly positive around the proposed substrate-binding pocket (Fig. 5c). This positively charged environment is compatible with accommodating the substrate pyruvate, an acid that should be negatively charged under physiological conditions.

In the cytosol-facing conformation structure, determined with 10 mM pyruvate (substrate), a strong non-proteinaceous density occupied the proposed substrate-binding pocket (Fig. 5b). This density was absent in the matrix-facing conformation structure, determined without substrate (in the presence of inhibitor). Its strategic location should allow alternating access to either side of membrane in the cytosol- or matrix-facing conformation. Moreover, its dimension matches well with that of pyruvate, which shows excellent compatibility with the surrounding chemical environment. We thus tentatively assign a pyruvate there. Nonetheless, the nearly symmetrical shape of pyruvate precludes an unambiguous assignment of its orientation, which we modeled in consideration of its chemical environment. DiscussionOur structures show that MPC1 and MPC2 form a heterodimer with the substrate translocation pathway at the center of the dimer interface, defining the basic functional unit and architecture of this important transporter family. Furthermore, our results resolve controversies about MPC's oligomerization and topology. Our structures reveal that both MPC1 and MPC2 are composed of three TMs, showing an interesting parallel to the SemiSWEET sugar transporter, a dimer of triple helix bundles. In contrast to the symmetric homodimer of SemiSWEET, however, MPC is composed of two homologous but distinct subunits, both involved in substrate binding. Given the asymmetric nature of pyruvate, MPC1 and MPC2 supply distinct residues to coordinate the substrate. We note that MPC1 or MPC2 homodimers could not provide the same coordination sites as the MPC1-MPC2 heterodimer, highlighting the structural requirement of heterodimer formation. Interestingly, phylogenic analyses suggest that, despite limited overall sequence similarity between MPCs and SemiSWEET, MPC2 shows greater similarity to SemiSWEET, with conservation at several positions, while MPC1 lacks such conservation (Extended Data Fig. 9). For instance, in placental mammals, MPC1L (~50% identity to MPC1) is specifically expressed in testis, while in yeast, MPC3 (~80% identical to MPC2) is expressed under oxidative conditions14,15. Based on our structural analyses and their ability to functionally substitute in the MPC complex (MPC1L for MPC1 in placental mammals; MPC3 for MPC2 in yeast), we propose that the alternative MPC complexes formed by these proteins likely adopt a similar heterodimer architecture as the canonical MPC complex. Previous biochemical studies are consistent with this proposal10, and our structural observations and sequence comparisons show that key residues for substrate binding and gate formation are highly conserved in these paralogues (Extended Data Fig. 1). This provides a molecular explanation for intriguing observations made nearly 50 years ago, that UK5099 acts as a non-competitive inhibitor for mitochondrial uptake of pyruvate (non-permeable to the mitochondrial membrane) but a competitive inhibitor for substrates capable of diffusing across the membrane21,43. METHODSStructure determination strategyTo facilitate structural investigation of MPC, we carried out systematic biochemical characterizations of MPCs from over 50 species, using a variety of expression systems and strategies, such as co-expression, covalent fusion, and in vitro assembly. We found that co-expressing MPC1 and MPC2 in HEK293 cells yielded the best outcome and decided to focus on human MPC, given its direct medical relevance. Molecular Docking of MPC InhibitorsMPC inhibitors (GW450863X, GW604714X, SR-01000197229 (referred to as compound 2 in ref11), and STK177541 (referred to as compound 12 in ref11)) were prepared for molecular docking studies using the LigPrep tool71 of Schrödinger suite 2023-2 (Schrödinger, New York, NY, USA). The inhibitors’ SDF files were converted into energy-minimized 3D conformations utilizing the OPLS4 force field72. Hydrogen atoms were added, and all possible ionization states and tautomeric forms were generated at a pH of 7.0 ± 2 using Epik73, with the desalt option enabled. The structure of MPC with AKOS was processed using Maestro Schrödinger’s Protein Preparation Wizard, which involved adding missing hydrogen atoms, correcting metal ionization states, and removing water molecules located >5 Å away from protein residues. Subsequently, the protein structure was optimized by predicting the pKa values of ionizable residues with PROPKA74, followed by restrained energy minimization using the OPLS4 force field. A receptor grid (size 10 × 10 × 15 Å) was generated around the AKOS site using Glide’s Receptor-Grid-Generation tool. MPC inhibitors were docked into this grid using Schrödinger’s Ligand Docking tool71, applying a van der Waals radii scaling factor of 0.8 for non-polar atoms and a partial charge cut-off of 0.15. The initial docking was performed using the standard precision (SP) protocol, followed by a more rigorous extra precision (XP) protocol with flexible ligand sampling to refine the results.
