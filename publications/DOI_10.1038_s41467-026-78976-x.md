# Electrode-associated growth reveals structure of OmcZ-type cytochrome nanowires in anaerobic methanotrophic archaea
**Authors:** Martijn Wissink, Cong Shen, Jiaqi Wang, Reinier A. Egas, Phoenix Smid, Felix J. Elling, Kevin W. Becker, Martin Pabst, Mike S. M. Jetten, Jun Liu, Nikhil S. Malvankar, Cornelia U. Welte

Electrode-associated growth reveals structure of OmcZ-type cytochrome nanowires in anaerobic methanotrophic archaea Received: 18 March 2026

Accepted: 5 October 2026

Anaerobic methanotrophic (ANME) archaea are key regulators of methane emissions in anoxic environments, yet their physiology remains poorly understood due to the lack of axenic cultures. Here, we show that cultivation on poised electrodes yields near-axenic cultures (95.8% relative abundance) of a freshwater ANME, for which we propose the name ‘Candidatus Methanoperedens vercellensis’. We identified key physiological properties of ‘Ca. M. vercellensis’: doubling times of 4.6 days at 30 °C, menaquinone as respiratory electron carrier, the inability to oxidize ethane, and preferential reduction of iron oxides over nitrate after cultivation on electrodes. A 3.3 Å cryo-electron microscopy structure of filaments displayed on the archaeal surface revealed polymerized OmcZ-type cytochrome nanowires, which are homologous to OmcZ nanowires essential for long-range (>10 μm) extracellular electron transfer (EET) in Geobacter sulfurreducens. The archaeal OmcZ-type nanowires include dense, collinear stacking of protected hemesakin to bacterial OmcZ nanowires, but with post-translational modification, thus yielding morphology distinct from bacterial OmcZ nanowires. Notably, in contrast to bacterial OmcZ, archaeal OmcZ-type nanowires do not form interconnected networks, and hemes are less solvent-exposed, which can protect them from the environment. Surprisingly, these archaea assemble OmcZ into nanowires even in the apparent absence of prolyl isomerase involved in folding bacterial OmcZ. This work establishes a model system for molecular understanding of nanowire-mediated EET in archaea.

oxidation to sulfate reduction6,7 whereas in freshwater ecosystems, ANME of the genus “Candidatus Methanoperedens” couple methane oxidation to the reduction of alternative electron acceptors, including nitrate, and iron and manganese oxides8–11. Despite their global importance, in-depth physiological studies of ANME remain scarce. This is largely due to the absence of axenic cultures, which necessitates studying ANME within complex microbial communities, combined with inherently slow growth rates ranging from 19 days to 7.5 months12–15. Although next-generation sequencing has greatly expanded our understanding of ANME metabolic potential, genomic data alone provide an incomplete picture of function16–18. For example, ANME genomes encode five- to eight-fold more multiheme c-type cytochromes (MHCs) than their methanogenic relatives16, yet the physiological roles of these proteins remain poorly understood. In marine systems, high rates of anaerobic methane oxidation are proposed to be enabled by direct interspecies electron transfer (DIET) mediated by MHCs and conductive “nanowires” between ANME and SRB6,7. However, the exact molecular components and pathways underlying extracellular electron transfer (EET) and methane oxidation in ANME remain largely unresolved. To overcome these limitations and directly probe the mechanisms underlying EET in ANME, bioelectrochemical systems (BESs) have emerged as a powerful experimental platform19. By providing a poised electrode as an alternative electron acceptor, a selective environment is created that enables a controlled investigation of metabolic processes. Applying this approach to freshwater ANME-2d “Ca. Methanoperedens” has demonstrated methane-dependent current production and implicated MHCs as key components for EET20–22. Functional nanowires involved in EET have not yet been demonstrated in archaea. A recent study reported cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of cytochrome filaments from Pyrobaculum calidifontis (ECNPc) and Archaeoglobus veneficus (ECNAv)23. However, the physiological roles of these filaments remain unresolved, as these archaea were neither grown on electrodes nor with extracellular electron acceptors, and no intact cell-attached filaments have been reported. In contrast, our current understanding of the role of MHCs in microbial EET mechanisms originates predominantly from studies of electroactive bacteria, particularly Shewanella and Geobacter species. Shewanella species use the Mtr respiratory pathway, which includes outer membrane cytochrome proteins for the reduction of extracellular electron acceptors, including electrodes24. In Geobacter species, conductive surface-displayed nanowires play a key role in EET. Although their molecular identity was long debated, accumulating evidence now supports polymerized MHCs of OmcS25,26 and OmcZ27,28 as the primary components of nanowires for EET. Additionally, in omcS-deficient mutant strains, OmcE can polymerize into nanowires in vitro29, although the physiological relevance of these structures remains unclear. Out of these MHCs, OmcZ is the only outer-surface nanowireforming cytochrome known to be essential for long-range (>10 µm) EET in microbial communities grown on electrodes27,30. Among reported cytochrome nanowires, OmcZ uniquely features linear, compact heme arrangements that confer high conductivity27,30. Notably, OmcZ homologs seem more widespread in archaea than in bacteria and are frequently found in genomes of “Ca. Methanoperedens” and in giant (>1 Mb) extrachromosomal elements called Borgs that are associated with “Ca. Methanoperedens” genomes31,32. Moreover, genomic neighborhoods of OmcZ homologs often encode OzpA (OmcZ protease) homologs for nanowire maturation33. Consistent with these genomic findings, OmcZ homologs are actively expressed in “Ca. Methanoperedens” during metal-dependent respiration: a 44% identity OmcZ-type homolog was highly expressed during Fe(III) reduction in “Ca. Methanoperedens nitroreducens”22, and a 21% identity homolog was among the most abundant transcripts during Fe(III)

reduction in “Ca. Methanoperedens ferrireducens”8. Similarly, OmcZ homologs were among the top-expressed genes when “Ca. Methanoperedens” was found in electrode-associated microbial communities21. Despite these transcriptomic-level observations, it remains unclear whether these archaeal OmcZ homologs assemble into functional nanowires or how they contribute to EET. In this study, we employed BESs to cultivate “Ca. Methanoperedens” under controlled electrochemical conditions and obtained a near-axenic culture (95.8% relative abundance) of “Candidatus Methanoperedens sp.” Vercelli strain 1, for which we propose the name “Candidatus Methanoperedens vercellensis”. This strain harbors a large plasmid, but no Borg-like extrachromosomal elements34. Using this near-axenic culture, we determined key physiological parameters and found that EET-performing archaeal cells produce OmcZ-type cytochrome nanowires. Cryo-EM reveals structural features that we hypothesize may be optimized for high electron conductivity and efficient EET, distinct from Geobacter sulfurreducens OmcZ.

Results Bioelectrochemical cultivation yields a near-axenic anaerobic methanotroph culture “Ca. Methanoperedens” was cultivated in duplicate BESs with methane as the sole electron donor and a carbon felt electrode as the sole electron acceptor for 352 consecutive days. Every ~70 days, the electrodes were subcultured into new BESs, showing continued exponential current production, signifying microbial growth (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). The methane dependency of the produced current was very high, reaching 97% (Fig. 1b), indicating that essentially all current was produced through methane oxidation. The remaining current in the absence of methane could originate from switching to oxidation of intracellular storage compounds such as polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) by “Ca. Methanoperedens”35. At 280 days, the coulombic efficiency was 93 ± 8% (mol electrons generated/mol methane oxidized) (Supplementary Fig. 2A), far exceeding the previously reported 17%21, indicating that all the consumed methane is essentially converted into current, with only a small percentage of electrons used for anabolic processes. To assess the microbial community structure during cultivation, we performed metagenomic sequencing of the bioreactor inoculum and BES cultivations at days 67, 119, and 207. Combining short- and long-read sequencing enabled us to obtain a complete genome sequence of “Ca. Methanoperedens sp.” Vercelli Strain 1 (Supplementary Table 1), consisting of a circular chromosome of 3.41 Mbp. Together with the circular chromosome, the 156 Kbp plasmid HMp_v2 was found at a 1:1 ratio to the genome, in line with previous investigations of extrachromosomal elements associated with this strain34. Closing the genome revealed an additional 60 protein-coding sequences, including five predicted multiheme c-type cytochromes (MHC; ≥3 CxxCH, C3xCH, or CxxCK motifs). Having closed its genome, we now propose the name “Ca. M. vercellensis” for this strain. The bioreactor inoculum contained 9.7% “Ca. M. vercellensis” based on read mapping to metagenome-assembled genomes (MAG) (Fig. 1c). The two biological replicates had a relative abundance of “Ca. M. vercellensis” including plasmid HMp_v2 of 55% and 73% after 67 days, of 96% and 91% after 119 days, and of 94% and 87% after 207 days, indicating a stabilization of community composition and establishment of a growing, near-axenic “Ca. M. vercellensis” culture after 119 days. At the same time, the well-known electrogen Geobacter sp. was present at 18% and 4.2% relative abundance in both replicates after 67 days, compared to 8.5% in the bioreactor inoculum. However, after 119 days, Geobacter sp. were absent from the microbial community, which was confirmed by negative-stain TEM images (Supplementary Fig. 3). Geobacter sp. likely established a biofilm in the BES before the addition of the bacteria-suppressing antibiotics, and necromass DNA accounted for its initial detection in the metagenomics dataset.

Apart from “Ca. M. vercellensis”, another microbe, a Bosea sp. bacterium, increased substantially in relative abundance during cultivation in BESs to reach the highest observed relative abundance of 6.2% in BES2 after 207 days (Fig. 1c). The Gram-negative genus Bosea is strictly aerobic and can oxidize various carbon sources and amino acids36–38. The Bosea MAG obtained from the electrodes did not encode any characterized EET pathways (OmcS, OmcZ, OmcE, Mtr) or methane activation machinery (MCR, pMMO, sMMO). It likely thrives on trace oxygen amounts leaking into the BES, as well as carbon exudates or necromass from “Ca. M. vercellensis”. Therefore, it is unlikely

that Bosea sp. directly participates in methane oxidation on the electrode; it might, however, provide unknown essential metabolites to “Ca. M. vercellensis”.

Following the initial BES incubations at room temperature (22.2 °C), we investigated the effect of elevated temperatures on current production. Implementing temperature control not only stabilized the signal by reducing current fluctuations caused by diurnal temperature changes (Supplementary Fig. 2B, C) but also revealed a temperature-dependent response: increasing the incubation temperature to 30 °C reduced the current doubling time to 5.5 ± 0.9 days (Fig. 2a–c, Supplementary Fig. 2D–G). When plotting doubling time as a function of temperature, a general trend of faster growth at higher temperatures was observed, with an optimum at 30 °C (Fig. 2d). Methanogens of the Methanosarcinales order typically employ methanophenazine as a redox shuttle in their electron transport chains39. Although Methanoperedenaceae belong to the same order, previous analyses have not detected methanophenazine in enrichment cultures40, and the corresponding biosynthesis pathway remains unresolved, leaving its presence in the genome of “Ca. M. vercellensis” uncertain. Instead, the futalosine pathway for menaquinone synthesis is encoded in the genome (Supplementary Data 1). Consistent with this genomic prediction, lipidomic analysis on the electrode-associated community revealed no detectable methanophenazine, whereas menaquinone was identified as the dominant membrane-integral redox carrier (Fig. 2e, f, Supplementary Table 2), indicating that it functions as the principal electron shuttle in “Ca. M. vercellensis”. A minor fraction of the detected quinones consisted of ubiquinones (Fig. 2e, g), which likely originate from Bosea sp., the second most abundant organism in the community. After methane, ethane is the second most abundant hydrocarbon in natural gas escaping from cold seeps and hydrothermal vents41. Previous studies have hypothesized that methanotrophic MCRs are incapable of oxidizing ethane42, which was tested by replacing methane with ethane in a BES. Ethane addition did not lead to an increase in current beyond the baseline established after argon flushing, indicating that “Ca. M. vercellensis” cannot metabolize ethane as an electron donor (Supplementary Fig. 2H, I). After resuming regular flushing with methane, the level of current re-established as before the experiment, indicating that the addition of ethane had not irreversibly inhibited the metabolism of “Ca. M. vercellensis”. To investigate how electrode respiration changes electron acceptor preference in “Ca. M. vercellensis”, we compared methane oxidation rates with different electron acceptors between the original bioreactor enrichment culture and the electrode-associated community after 289 days of electrode-associated growth. The original bioreactor culture exhibited higher methane oxidation rates with nitrate than with ferrihydrite (an iron mineral) (Fig. 2h). In contrast, after growth on electrodes, the “Ca. M. vercellensis” culture preferred ferrihydrite over nitrate as an electron acceptor, suggesting a preference for EET to insoluble electron acceptors (Fig. 2i). Notably, the electrodegrown culture did not resume methane oxidation in the presence of nitrate during the 8-day incubation period. In the absence of genomic changes, this may indicate that switching back to nitrate respiration is a slow regulatory process, or that nitrite accumulation, resulting from the loss of nitrite-scavenging partners (Supplementary Data 1; genomes encoding dissimilatory nitrite reductases in the inoculum but absent in the BESs were Gallionellaceae, Geobacter, Casimicrobiaceae, Phycisphaerales), inhibits nitrate-dependent methane oxidation in the electrode-adapted community.

The discovery and structure of OmcZ-type nanowires from electrode-grown “Ca. M. vercellensis”

The genome of “Ca. M. vercellensis” encodes 39 MHCs, several of which are organized in genomic clusters. Two such clusters contain homologs of known cytochrome nanowires (Fig. 3a). One cluster encodes two homologs of Geobacter sulfurreducens OmcZ (OmcZGs),

sharing 34% amino acid identity (99% coverage, E-value 7 × 10−73) and 29% amino acid identity (74% coverage, E-value 2 × 10−39), respectively. These proteins were designated OmcZ1Mv (gene ID: DOEMACJB_03675) and OmcZ2Mv (DOEMACJB_03676) and are flanked by two genes encoding serine proteases (Fig. 3a), consistent with the presence of OmcZ-specific proteases that are essential for OmcZ cleavage and nanowire maturation27,33. Further downstream, the genomic neighborhood encodes Type II/IV secretion system proteins and Type IV pili subunits (DOEMACJB_03667-03670) which may be involved in secretion of OmcZ as well as anchoring to the cytoplasmic membrane. A second cluster encodes a 4-heme cytochrome (DOEMACJB_01452) with 29% identity (86% coverage, E-value 3 × 10−12) to the archaeal extracellular cytochrome nanowire (ECN) of A. veneficus and was therefore designated ECNMv. Negative-stain TEM images revealed abundant filaments extending from the cell surfaces of the electrode-grown samples (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Fig. 3). When purified, both Coomassie blue (Fig. 3c) and heme-stain gels (Fig. 3c) showed a single major band at approximately 70 kDa. These filaments exhibited the characteristic sinusoidal feature of cytochrome nanowires (Fig. 3d)25–27. Mass spectrometry data revealed OmcZ2Mv as the most abundant cytochrome in both the gel bands and the electrode biofilm (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B; Supplementary Data 2). Other c-type cytochromes were present at very low abundances (Supplementary Data 2). In addition to high protein abundance in the near-axenic “Ca. M. vercellensis” culture, OmcZ2Mv was one of the most highly expressed genes in RNA-seqdata from electrode-associated “Ca. M. vercellensis” in a previous study, although it appeared truncated without heme-binding motifs in a fragmented genome21. Another type of filament was observed at low abundance that appeared thicker in negative-stain TEM images (Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). However, this filament was not detected in cryo-EM images. While OmcZ1Mv was barely detected in the mass spectrometry data, the presence of this thicker filament coincided with ECNMv being the second most abundant cytochrome in the electrode medium at a 1:20 ratio compared to OmcZ2Mv (Supplementary Data 2). Although the identity of this filament remains unresolved, its absence from cryo-EM images suggests that OmcZ2Mv is the dominant nanowire involved in electrode-associated EET in “Ca. M. vercellensis”. The dense networks of filaments in cryo-EM images (Fig. 3e) enabled the reconstruction of an atomic model of OmcZ2Mv nanowires. The structure was resolved at 3.3 Å (Supplementary Figs. 5, 6) and revealed a helical assembly with a rise of 58 Å and a twist of 159° per protomer (Fig. 3f). Similar to OmcZGs, OmcZ2Mv undergoes proteolytic processing in which the C-terminal domain (~30 kDa) is cleaved off, yielding a nanowire-forming N-terminal part of 330 residues containing eight canonical c-type hemes (Fig. 3g, h; Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, the nanowire-forming part of OmcZGs comprises 258 residues and includes one heme bound by a non-canonical motif (CX14CH). In both homologs, about 80% of the structure is composed of turns and loops (Fig. 3g; Supplementary Table 3). Previous structural studies of OmcZGs required alkaline conditions (ethanolamine buffer, pH 10.5) to prevent extensive filament aggregation in vitro27,28. In contrast, here the native structure of OmcZ2Mv nanowires was directly resolved under physiological conditions (HEPES-buffered growth medium, pH 7.25), without precipitation by ammonium sulfate.

Firstly, several hemes in OmcZ2Mv are displaced compared to OmcZGs (Fig. 4a, b; Supplementary Fig. 7A–F), especially at the bottom part of the protomer where Heme II and III are shifted by about 4 Å (Fig. 4c, d). Secondly, some hemes are not in a planar conformation, which impacts their redox potential and nanowire conductivity43 (Fig. 4c, d; Supplementary Fig. 7A–F). Thirdly, OmcZ2Mv has reduced edge-to-edge distances of hemes compared to OmcZGs (Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Table 4). Together, these structural features might be optimizations for high conductivity in OmcZ2Mv nanowires, potentially enabling efficient long-range EET. OmcZ2Mv possesses an expanded protein scaffold, with ~30% more residues than OmcZGs (Supplementary Tables 3, 5), primarily in the regions between binding sites of Heme III and IV, and Heme VI and VII (Fig. 4e, f). These expansions substantially alter the solvent

exposure of hemes. In OmcZGs, Heme I and III, and the branched Heme VI are largely solvent exposed, whereas in OmcZ2Mv these hemes are buried within pockets formed by the expanded loops (Fig. 4g–j). This heme insulation potentially reduces electrical current leakage and nanowire aggregation by preventing hydrophobic interactions between exposed hemes. Furthermore, the expanded loop between Heme VI and VII increases the interfacial area between adjacent protomers from ~1200 Å² to ~1400 Å², stabilizing inter-subunit interactions and increasing filament stability (Fig. 4j). OmcZ-family proteins share key structural features Despite originating from different domains of life and sharing low sequence identity, bacterial OmcZGs and archaeal OmcZ2Mv share a highly conserved molecular architecture. Aligning 90 OmcZ-family proteins from both bacteria and archaea revealed four highlyconserved short sequence regions that form a “core quadrangle” surrounding Heme V and Heme VIII (Fig. 5a): Region 1 (DGHYV/IP, residues 149-154), Region 2 (L/IHNIE/Q, residues 289-293), Region 3 (GXGHI/LG, residues 311–316), and Region 4 (DCXGCH, residues 320-325).

Furthermore, we identified a spatially conserved set of 91 residues, encompassing these four regions, with a root-mean-square deviation of 0.98 Å (Fig. 5b). These residues cluster predominantly on the upper half of the protomer and include multiple histidine residues coordinating Heme I, IV, V, VII, and VIII (Fig. 5b). Notably, the regions surrounding the triangular arrangement of Heme V, VII, and VIII display the highest degree of structural conservation, underscoring their central role in maintaining the specific architecture of OmcZ-family proteins. Because loops and turns dominate OmcZ-family proteins, only a few conserved structural features are responsible for organizing the heme chain and maintaining nanowire integrity. Central to this organization are three conserved histidine-histidine (HH) motifs that link adjacent heme pairs in both OmcZ2Mv and OmcZGs together. Specifically, H112-H113 (H96-H97 in OmcZGs) coordinates Hemes I and IV (Fig. 5c), H200-H201 (H182-H183) coordinates Hemes VI and VII (Fig. 5d), and H45-H46 (H40-H41) coordinates Hemes II and III (Fig. 5e, f). Additional interactions further stabilize these heme pairs. For Heme II and III, the HH motif is complemented by salt bridges between the propionate groups of Heme II and R80 (R85), located immediately upstream of the Heme III binding motif (RNCHDCH) (Fig. 5e, f). A similar dual-sided stabilization is observed for the Heme VII-VIII pair. On one side, Heme VII is linked to Heme VIII via a short α-helix bearing histidines H284 (H209) and H290 (H215) at opposite ends. On the other side, a salt bridge is formed between the propionate group of Heme VIII and R264 (R203), further stabilizing this heme pair (Fig. 5g, h). Heme VIII is also structurally coupled to Heme V through an α-helix on the conserved Region 4 that contains the Heme VIII-binding motif, while the Heme V-coordinating histidine H314 (H241) on the conserved Region 3 precedes this α-helix (Fig. 5i, j). Together, these interactions position Heme VII as a central point within the heme chain: Heme VII adopts an approximately parallel orientation relative to Hemes IV, V, and VI, and is connected to adjacent hemes through both HH motifs and α-helical linkers. AlphaFold predictions of full-length OmcZ proteins indicate that the cleaved C-terminal regions adopt β-sandwich folds resembling immunoglobulin-like domains, with three such domains in OmcZ2Mv and two in OmcZGs (Supplementary Fig. 10A, B). In both proteins, the cleavage sites are located in a loop separating the nanowire-forming Nterminal region from the C-terminal region containing β-sandwich folds (Supplementary Fig. 10C, D). These sites follow bulky aromatic residues and are sandwiched by small amino acids, indicating a general mechanism for the maturation of OmcZ-type nanowires27,44.

OmcZ2Mv presents a broad distribution in ANME archaea and Borgs Archaeal OmcZ homologs are predominantly found in ANME archaea, determined from analysis of the NCBI ClusteredNR database. A systematic analysis of 212 MAGs of ANME archaea (Supplementary Fig. 11A) revealed that OmcZ homologs are widespread across the ANME-2 clade with a prevalence of 20–70% within the subclades 2a-d, but occur rarely in ANME-1 and are absent from ANME-3 MAGs (Supplementary Fig. 11B). Notably, gene copy number expansion of OmcZ was observed exclusively in ANME-2d, the freshwater lineage represented by the genus “Ca. Methanoperedens”, whereas ANME-2abc lineages only encode a single copy (Supplementary Fig. 11C). Similar gene expansions were also observed in Borg elements associated with “Ca. Methanoperedens” (Supplementary Fig. 11C), expanding previous reports of OmcZ homologs on Borg elements31,32. By including bacterial homologs in the dataset, OmcZ-family proteins could be classified into six major phylogenetic groups (Fig. 6a). Groups I-IV consist of OmcZ homologs found in ANME-2d and Borg elements only, Group V comprises ANME-1 and ANME-2abc lineages, and Group VI contains bacterial OmcZ homologs (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 12). The OmcZ2Mv nanowire clusters most closely

with Group II, sharing 82.1%, 62.3%, and 62.6% protein sequence identity with Borg Amethyst_249, Borg Emerald_171, and the “Ca. Methanoperedens” host of Borg Black_172, respectively (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 12). In contrast, OmcZ2Mv is evolutionarily distant from the bacterial OmcZGs, which belongs to Group VI, as well as from OmcZ1Mv, the second OmcZ homolog encoded by “Ca. M. vercellensis”, which falls within Group IV (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 12). ANME-2d archaea and Borg elements frequently encode multiple OmcZ homologs from different phylogenetic groups (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 11D), suggesting a differential use of OmcZ variants under different environmental or metabolic conditions. Archaeal OmcZ gene clusters commonly co-occur with serine peptidases (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 11D), consistent with a role analogous to Geobacter OmcZ-cleaving protease OzpA that facilitates protomer polymerization27,33. The OmcZ groups also differ markedly in protein size and glycosylation potential. OmcZ homologs from Groups I-II consist of substantially more amino acid residues than the other groups (Supplementary Fig. 11E). Additionally, OmcZ proteins from ANME-2d and Borg elements exhibit the highest percentage of N-glycosylation motifs, exceeding that of OmcZGs by more than fivefold (Supplementary Fig. 11F). For example, Group I OmcZ from Borg Amber_301 encodes 16 motifs across 372 residues making up ~12% of the protein (Supplementary Fig. 9C), indicating that N-glycosylation may represent a specific evolutionary adaptation. Together, this analysis supports the widespread distribution and diversification of OmcZ homologs across ANME lineages, with OmcZ2Mv nanowires representing a structural framework for EET in anaerobic methanotrophs. Moreover, its close evolutionary relationship with Borgencoded OmcZ proteins provides new insight into the potential physiological roles of these enigmatic extrachromosomal elements.

Discussion This study establishes “Ca. Methanoperedens vercellensis” (formerly “Ca. Methanoperedens Vercelli Strain 1”) as a near-axenic model organism for investigating electrode-associated growth and EET in anaerobic methanotrophic archaea. EET-performing cells predominantly produced OmcZ2Mv nanowires, for which we determined a 3.3 Å-resolution cryo-EM structure revealing features that could help explain high electron conductivity and efficient EET. Within 119 days of incubation in a BES, the relative abundance of “Ca. M. vercellensis” increased from 9.7% in the inoculum to 95.8%, resulting in a near-axenic culture. To our knowledge, such a high level of enrichment has not been reported previously and highlights the strong selective advantage of electrode-associated growth together with a mix of bacteria-suppressing antibiotics. Furthermore, the absence of soluble electron acceptors such as nitrate eliminated the need for facultative syntrophic partners, such as denitrifying anaerobic methane oxidizing bacteria or anaerobic ammonium oxidizing bacteria, to mitigate toxic nitrite accumulation45,46. We demonstrated that BESs provide a powerful platform to study ANME physiology by deriving current-based doubling times of 8.1 ± 1.1 days at 22.2 °C, which shortened to 5.5 ± 0.9 days at 30 °C. This temperature-dependent increase in growth rate corresponds to an apparent Q10 of 1.6. This doubling time of “Ca. M. vercellensis” is less than half of the previously measured doubling times for Methanoperedenaceae (19 days at 22 °C)13, and substantially shorter than reported values for marine ANME species12,14,15. The rapid recovery of current after replacing ethane with methane further indicates that ethane is not inhibitory, contrasting with previous hypotheses that non-methane hydrocarbons inhibit ANME archaea47. The detection of menaquinone as the membrane-integral electron carrier in “Ca. M. vercellensis” is consistent with earlier work40 and highlights a fundamental metabolic divergence among ANME clades. Freshwater ANME-2d encode the futalosine pathway for menaquinone biosynthesis, whereas their marine relatives, ANME-2abc and ANME-3, lack this pathway and are instead thought to rely on methanophenazine16. This distinction is notable given the substantial difference in redox potential between menaquinone and methanophenazine (E0’ = −80 mV and −165 mV for menaquinone-8 and methanophenazine, respectively). Prolonged electrode-associated growth markedly altered the respiratory physiology of “Ca. M. vercellensis”, which did not resume nitrate-dependent methane oxidation within 1 week, despite the absence of genomic changes, suggesting a tight regulatory control of nitrate reductase expression rather than genetic loss. In contrast, methane oxidation readily occurred in the presence of ferrihydrite, indicating that the MHCs facilitating EET to the electrode can also mediate electron transfer to insoluble metal oxides. This apparent overlap in electron transfer capability contrasts with transcriptomic data from enrichment cultures of “Candidatus Methanoperedens nitroreducens”, where distinct subsets of MHCs were expressed during iron reduction versus electrode respiration22. Here, we report the structure of archaeal OmcZ nanowires at 3.3 Å resolution obtained by native purification under physiologically relevant conditions. In contrast, previously reported structures of bacterial OmcZGs nanowires were solved in highly alkaline pH 10.5 ethanolamine buffers27,28, conditions known to induce conformational rearrangements in c-type cytochromes48. Moreover, earlier OmcZ nanowire structures were resolved at 3.5 and 4.0 Å resolution, limiting the visualization of fine structural features27,28. Comparison of OmcZ2Mv with OmcZGs revealed three major distinctions in the organization of the heme chain, the nanowire surface architecture, and the morphology of filaments. First, the heme chain in OmcZ2Mv exhibits shorter edge-to-edge distances than OmcZGs, a feature that exponentially enhances electron transfer rates49. Previous studies on OmcS and OmcZ nanowires have shown that more compact heme arrangements can increase electrical conductivity by up to three orders of magnitude26,30. By analogy, the reduced inter-heme spacing in OmcZ2Mv possibly supports higher electrical conductivity than in OmcZGs. Additionally, OmcZ2Mv nanowires exhibit non-planar heme conformations. Apart from shortening inter-heme distances, such heme distortions, which are common among cytochromes, are known to modulate redox potentials, thereby lowering energetic barriers for electron transfer50–52. The non-planar heme geometries in OmcZ2Mv likely arise from structural constraints imposed by the heme-binding motifs, together with stabilizing salt bridges to heme propionate groups. Moreover, shifted heme propionate groups, like those observed in OmcZ2Mv, have been associated with increased heme stability53. Second, the heme chain in OmcZ2Mv nanowires is more insulated than in OmcZGs. In OmcZGs, solvent-exposed hemes can promote nanowire aggregation and facilitate inter-nanowire electron transfer. In contrast, the expanded inter-heme regions in OmcZ2Mv shield the heme chain, likely reducing current leakage, and limiting filament aggregation and flexibility. This insulation is further enhanced by large extra densities observed on ten surface-exposed asparagine residues within N-glycosylation motifs (Fig. 6). Similar densities have been reported for OmcE and ECNAv nanowires23,29, but in much lower abundance and without defined motifs.

N-glycosylation is a common feature of archaeal proteins and may serve several stabilizing and organizational roles in OmcZ2Mv nanowires54,55. Notably, eight out of the ten modified asparagines on OmcZ2Mv are located on turns, and four follow aromatic residues, a context previously shown to enhance protein stability56, potentially compensating for the missing prolyl isomerase in the OmcZ gene cluster (Fig. 6). Moreover, because most N-glycosylation motifs are positioned near hydrophobic regions and hydrophobic interactions dominate protomer interfaces, these putative modifications may suppress nonspecific protomer-protomer contacts while favoring controlled, end-to-end self-assembly. The large size of the protruding densities could further act as barriers that prevent filament bundling, analogous to glycosylation-mediated archaellum organization in Halobacterium salinarum57. Finally, because OmcZ-type nanowires must associate with the cell surface to enable electron transfer, the putative N-glycosylation may also facilitate anchoring of OmcZ2Mv nanowires to the archaeal cell envelope. Third, the nanowire morphology of OmcZGs and OmcZ2Mv differs substantially. Whereas OmcZGs nanowires aggregate extensively and densely fill the extracellular space between bacterial cells and electrodes, OmcZ2Mv forms straighter, less densely packed filaments (Fig. 6). Given the relatively slower growth of “Ca. M. vercellensis” compared to G. sulfurreducens, evolutionary selection may favor a more efficient nanowire architecture that supports long-range electron transfer without extensive aggregation. In G. sulfurreducens, this role is fulfilled by straight, conductive OmcS nanowires. However, OmcS-type nanowires and their accessory genes are absent in archaea58. It could therefore be possible that OmcZ2Mv nanowires have convergently evolved morphological and functional features resembling those of bacterial OmcS nanowires. Despite several distinctions between OmcZ2Mv and OmcZGs, key design principles are conserved across the OmcZ family, even at low (~30%) sequence identity. The OmcZ-family proteins exhibit a remarkably conserved molecular architecture centered on a “core quadrangle” surrounding hemes V and VIII, which represents the most invariant region across all analyzed homologs. Structural alignment revealed that this conserved core anchors the organization of the heme chain and reinforces nanowire integrity. In the absence of extensive secondary structures, heme positioning and connectivity are maintained by a limited set of conserved motifs, most notably three pairs of consecutive histidine residues that coordinate adjacent heme pairs, reinforced by arginine-mediated salt bridges to heme propionate groups. Together, these conserved interactions position Heme VII as a central hub within the chain and highlight how OmcZ-family nanowires preserve a shared conductive architecture despite sequence divergence. A systematic phylogenetic analysis across ANME archaea revealed that OmcZ homologs are widespread among ANME-2 lineages, but rare in ANME-1 and absent in ANME-3. These homologs cluster into six distinct phylogenetic groups that differ in protein length and number of glycosylation motifs (Supplementary Fig. 11B). Notably, only freshwater ANME-2d, represented by the family Methanoperedenaceae, harbor multiple OmcZ homologs within a single genome. This pattern suggests that OmcZ gene expansion may contribute to niche adaptation and enhanced metabolic flexibility in ANME-2d. Although “Ca. M. vercellensis” encodes a large plasmid without OmcZ homologs, OmcZ genes are frequently found on giant (>1 Mb) extrachromosomal elements known as Borgs that are associated with other “Ca. Methanoperedens” genomes31,32. Borgs encode numerous metabolic genes linked to methane oxidation that could augment host metabolism. Supporting this notion, a Borg-encoded OmcZ homolog was found to be expressed at higher levels than their chromosomal counterparts32. Together, these observations support the notion that maintaining multiple OmcZ variant from distinct phylogenetic groups is advantageous for Methanoperedenaceae.

Beyond freshwater ANME-2d, cytochrome genes were upregulated when marine ANME lineages couple methane oxidation to sulfate reduction through DIET with SRB via conductive cytochrome nanowires6,7. Although the molecular identity of these nanowires remains unresolved, marine ANME-2abc frequently encode and express OmcZ homologs (Supplementary Fig. 11B)16,59. This widespread presence raises the intriguing possibility that, in addition to long-range EET to electrodes described here, OmcZ-type nanowires also facilitate DIET to SRB partners. Such nanowire-mediated DIET could help explain the rapid metabolism without diffusible intermediates6 and filamentous structures7 reported in prior studies. Our study has several limitations. Although we obtained a nearaxenic electrode-grown culture of “Ca. M. vercellensis”, lowabundance community members remained present and may still influence its physiology during electrode-associated growth. Moreover, the relatively slow growth rates of this archaeon, combined with the limited scale of the BESs, restricted the amount of biomass available and prevented extensive biochemical and physicochemical characterization of the nanowires. While additional nanowire candidates were observed by negative-stain TEM and detected by mass spectrometry, insufficient particle numbers precluded high-resolution cryoEM structure determination. Furthermore, the chemical identity of the extra densities observed on surface-exposed asparagine residues could not be directly verified, leaving the proposed N-glycosylation tentative. Finally, the lack of established genetic tools for “Ca. M. vercellensis” currently prevents direct genetic investigation of OmcZ2Mv nanowire function or systematic testing of the physiological roles of specific structural features and surface modifications.

Quinones were detected by scanning from m/z 150 to 2000, followed by data-dependent MS2 (isolation window 1.2 m/z) using resolving powers of 140,000 and 17,500 (FWHM at m/z 200) in full-scan MS and MS2, respectively. Optimal fragmentation of quinones was achieved using a stepped normalized collision energy (NCE) of 15,

25, and 50. A mass calibration was performed using the Thermo Scientific Pierce LTQ Velos ESI Positive Ion Calibration Solution prior to analysis. Quinones were identified by retention time, accurate molecular mass, and MS2 fragment spectra74,75. Quinones were quantified by comparing their peak area to dilution series of MK7:7 and UQ10:10 standards.

Biofilm activity assays Activity assays with “Ca. M. vercellensis” enriched biofilms from BESs were performed after batch 4 in 60 mL serum bottles with 20 mL anoxic BES medium. In the anaerobic hood (97% N2, 3% H2), approximately one-sixteenth of the electrode biofilm of two different BESs was combined per bottle. The bottles were stoppered, the headspace was flushed with N2/CO2 (9:1) for 5 min, and pressurized to 1.1 bar. Subsequently, 10 mL 13C-CH4 was added, and the bottles were incubated at 26 °C and 200 rpm. In biological duplicate nitrate (250 µM), ferrihydrite (100 µM) and a control without electron acceptor were incubated for 8–9 days. Biofilm activity from BESs were compared to bioreactor activity using 120 mL serum bottle subcultures of 60 mL (0.13 g dry weight) bioreactor samples in biological triplicate. Bioreactor biomass was washed three times in BES medium and made up to a final volume of 40 mL. Subsequent handling was identical to the BES biofilm bottle incubations, except that 20 mL 13C-CH4 was added instead of 10 mL. Nitrate and nitrite concentrations were checked twice a day using colorimetric test strips (detection limit 10 mg L−1, MQuant, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany).

Protein phylogenetic analysis A local protein database of MAGs with clear taxonomic classification from the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB R220) was used to search for OmcZ homologs using blastp. OmcZ homolog proteins were extracted from the MAGs with the e-value cutoff of 1e-20 and manual curation. In combination with homologs from the NCBI Clustered NR database and published literature, 90 OmcZ homolog proteins were identified across bacteria, archaea, and Borg elements. To obtain the sequences of the nanowire-forming OmcZ, the signal peptides were removed after the cleavage sites predicted by SignalP v576, and the selfinhibitor C-part was removed after the cleavage sites of the accessory serine protease, which were chosen at the corresponding positions to the cleavage sites in G. sulfurreducens OmcZGs (after WFGN)27 and “Ca. M. vercellensis” OmcZ2Mv (after GWAG) from the multisequence alignment. This alignment also provides the conserved regions in Fig. 5A. The unrooted protein phylogeny tree of nanowire-forming OmcZ in Fig. 6 was calculated using the maximum likelihood method with 1000 bootstrap replications in MEGA-X (https://megasoftware.net/) and plotted in iTOL77.

Nanowire purification The yields of nanowires were examined during different purification steps of electrode-grown “Ca. M. vercellensis” samples: (1) the electrode-grown biofilm, HEPES based buffer, pH 7.25; (2) the supernatant of (1) after 21300 g spin; (3) the pellet of (1) after 21300 g spin and resuspended in 50 mM Tris 50 mM NaCl pH 8.0 buffer; (4) the 10-fold concentrated fraction (2). Protein mass spectrometry Both samples from electrode-grown culture medium and carefully excised ~70 kDa SDS-PAGE gel bands were sent to the Keck Mass Spectrometry & Proteomics Resource at Yale University. Subsequent proteolytic digestion following reduction and alkylation were carried out, after which peptides were analyzed using a Waters Acquity M-class UPLC system coupled to a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer. Peptides were separated over a 0–90 min gradient. Solvent A consisted of H₂O containing 0.1% formic acid, and solvent B consisted of acetonitrile in H₂O with 0.1% formic acid. The Orbitrap mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, acquiring peptide signals from m/z 300–1700 at a resolution of 70,000 in full MS mode, with a maximum ion injection time of 45 ms and an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 3 × 10⁶. The top 20 precursor ions were selected for MS/MS analysis and fragmented using higher-energy collisional dissociation. MS/MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 17,500 with an AGC target of 1 × 10⁵, a maximum injection time of 100 ms, an isolation width of 1.7 m/z, and a NCE of 28. Singly charged ions and ions with charge states ≥7 were excluded from fragmentation. Peptide match was preferred, and dynamic exclusion was set to 20 s. Mass spectrometric raw data were analyzed against a protein database derived from genome sequencing using PEAKS Studio X (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Waterloo, Canada). Database searches were performed with a precursor mass tolerance of 20 ppm and a fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.02 Da, allowing up to 3 missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation was included as a fixed modification, and methionine oxidation, as well as asparagine and glutamine deamidation, were included as variable modifications. Peptidespectrum matches were filtered to a 1% false discovery rate (FDR), and protein identifications supported by at least two unique peptides were considered significant. Results from PEAKS were exported to proteins.csv, listing the identified proteins.

Model building of OmcZ2Mv nanowires The amino acid sequence of OmcZ2Mv was retrieved from the closed genome sequence from this work and used as input for AlphaFold381 to generate predicted structural models. The predicted models were fitted into the cryo-EM map using ChimeraX82, followed by real-space refinement in PHENIX83 and Coot84. The final protomer models exhibited excellent stereochemistry, as evaluated by PHENIX. To better refine heme-interacting areas at this resolution, bond/angle restraints for the heme molecule itself (His-Fe) and Cys-heme thioester bonds were restricted based on the geometries obtained in the reported structure of OmcZ nanowires from Geobacter sulfurreducens (PDB 7LQ5). Phenix reported a map-to-model correlation coefficient of 0.85, which served as robust cross-validation metric for evaluating map quality and the accuracy of helical symmetry parameters. The helical OmcZ2Mv filament was modeled in Phenix by fitting 3 subunits of OmcZ2Mv filament monomers into the helical cryo-EM density map. UCSF ChimeraX82 was utilized for surface rendering of cryo-EM density maps and for segmentation in all visual presentations of structures. All the structures were validated in PHENIX83 using the Comprehensive validation tool, and the map quality was assessed using the Mtriage tool in PHENIX83, as summarized in Supplementary Table 6. Structural models of full-length OmcZGs and OmcZ2Mv were predicted by AlphaFold381. ChimeraX82 was used for all visual exhibitions of structures.
