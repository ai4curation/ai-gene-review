Sevenless (Sev) is a Drosophila receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) required for the
specification of the R7 photoreceptor. It is cleaved into α and β subunits and
binds the ectodomain of the G-protein-coupled receptor bride of sevenless
(Boss). Previous work showed that the Boss ectodomain could bind but not
activate Sev; rather, the whole seven-pass transmembrane Boss was required.
Here, we show that Sev does not need to be cleaved to function and that a
single-pass transmembrane form of Boss activates Sev. We use cryo-electron
microscopy and biophysical methods to determine the structural basis of ligand
binding and pH-dependent dimerization of Sev, and we discuss the implications in
the process of Sev activation. The Sev human homolog, receptor oncogene from
sarcoma 1 (ROS1), is associated with oncogenic transformations, and we discuss
their structural similarities.

INTRODUCTION

Receptor Tyrosine Kinases (RTKs) are transmembrane (TM) receptors that play a variety of biological functions critical to animal development and physiology.1,2 The ROS family of RTKs has representatives in mammals, birds, insects and nematodes; it includes the largest known RTKs, exceeding the size of the Eph receptors.3,4 In humans it is represented by Receptor Oncogene from Sarcoma 1 (ROS1), which is expressed in a number of tissues, but the biological roles it plays are generally not well understood and the protein is best known for the oncogenic transformations it engenders.5–7 In flies it is represented by Sevenless (Sev) which functions in the highly characterized signaling mechanism that specifies the R7 photoreceptor.8–12

The majority of RTKs are monomeric TM receptors in which ligand binding to the extracellular domain (ECD) induces dimerization that brings the intracellular domains (ICDs) of the dimer into proximity, and the tyrosine kinase (TK) domain of one phosphorylates ICD tyrosine residues of the other to activate downstream biological processes.1,2 Some monomeric RTKs can spontaneously dimerize in a ligand-independent manner when expressed at high levels, and their expression levels need to be tightly controlled. Other RTKs, such as the Insulin Receptor (IR) are preorganized into multimeric structures that shift conformation upon ligand binding to achieve activation.13–16 Sev is expressed at unusually high levels to allow it to hyper-activate the MAPK transduction pathway in the R7 precursor cell11, but it is strictly ligand-dependent, only activating when its ligand Bride of Sevenless (Boss) is present.17–19 Earlier work identified the cleavage of the Sev primary polypeptide into α and β subunits, and their assembly into a potential (αβ)2 dimer reminiscent of the IR.20 Such a structure would allow Sev to be expressed at high levels but activate only upon ligand-induced conformational changes. However, we demonstrate here that cleavage is not necessary for Sev function, and that Sev has a unique structure that differs substantially from the IR.

Sev has other unusual features, notably that its ligand Boss is an obligate TM protein expressed in the neighboring R8 cell, and that the Sev/Boss complex is internalized into the R7 precursor, suggesting an unusual mechanism for Sev activation.17,18,21 Boss is a G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) and its extracellular domain (ECD) binds to Sev.22 Importantly, secreted BossECD binds to but does not activate Sev.23 Previous work suggested that all seven TM helices of native Boss were required for Sev activation, but here we document that a single-TM form of Boss is adequate. When Boss and Sev interact, they are both integral plasma membrane proteins, and the entire Sev/Boss complex is internalized into the R7 precursor by either phagocytosis or trans-endocytosis.18 The Sev and Boss proteins later accumulate in large multi-vesicular bodies (MVBs) visually prominent in R7 precursors.17,18 This raises two significant questions: why does Boss need to be a TM protein to activate Sev? And, how does this relate to the internalization of the Sev/Boss complex into vesicular compartments within the R7 precursor?

Here we use cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) to determine structures of the SevECD both in the ligand-free form and in complex with BossECD. We find that at Drosophila extracellular pH SevECD is mostly monomeric, but dimers become the predominant species at lower pH. This occurs whether BossECD is present or absent, and notably as the pH is lowered BossECD loses affinity for SevECD. These data suggest the possibility of a pH-dependent mechanism of Sev activation.

The only reported case of a pH-dependent RTK is the insulin receptor-related receptor (IRR) which experiences a ligand-independent conformational transition upon sensing alkaline pH in the extracellular environment.24,25 Here we suggest a different pH-dependent mechanism for Sev, in which dimerization and activation is triggered by the acidic pH of an internal compartment. Our analysis of Sev suggests that it has significant structural homology with its human counterpart ROS1. We discuss the extent to which our findings on the molecular and structural features of Sev are conserved in ROS1, despite hundreds of millions of years of evolution.

RESULTSSev does not require cleavage to function in vivoSev has a large ECD comprising a helical domain (HD), nine fibronectin type III domains (F), and three β-propeller domains (P); based on sequence, there is a furin cleavage site formed by nine arginine residues (2038–2046) (Figure 1A). Previous work showed that furin cleavage generated two protein chains: the α subunit, which includes most of the ECD, and the β subunit, which includes the TM region and the ICD, and that these likely assemble into an (αβ)2 dimer similar to the IR.20 Sev works in concert with the Drosophila EGF receptor (DER) to activate the MAPK transduction pathway in the R7 precursor. While DER can be activated by ligand-independent dimerization resulting from high expression levels, Sev activation requires ligand binding even when highly expressed.26,27 The inferred IR-like structure of Sev provided a mechanistic explanation for this behavior: it was predicted to assemble into an inert (αβ)2 dimer in which ligand binding would trigger activation by conformational changes.To evaluate this model, we removed the furin cleavage site producing a sev coding sequence lacking residues 2038–2046 (sevΔfur) and transformed it into sevenless-null flies (sev°) under the sev transcriptional control elements ([sev]sevΔfur) and examined R7 specification in adult eyes. In wild-type (WT) eyes, each ommatidium has a single central R7 photoreceptor (red arrows in Figure 1B, left panel) and in sev° eyes no ommatidia have R7s (Figure 1B, middle panel), but when [sev]sevΔfur was introduced to the sev° background, R7s were restored to all ommatidia (red arrows in Figure 1B, right panel). Thus, the SevΔfur protein fully rescues the absence of native Sev and suggests that the cleavage of Sev into α and β subunits is not required for its function. To evaluate whether there is a redundant cleavage site that can substitute in the absence of the defined furin site, we performed western blots on developing eyes in which N terminally Myc-tagged versions of Sev were transformed into flies under UAS transcriptional control and overexpressed in developing eye discs in a sev° background using GMR.Gal4 (Figure S1A). We observed two Sev bands in the overexpressed WT Sev: one for intact Sev and one for the Sev α subunit. When SevΔfur was similarly overexpressed, we observed a single band for the intact Sev protein, suggesting the absence of cryptic cleavage sites.The structure of SevECD monomer reveals a unique foldTo evaluate the structure of Sev, we expressed in insect cells the SevECD (residues 123–2119) lacking the furin cleavage site with a C-terminal His-tag and a Strep-tag appended for purification purposes. The purification was carried out by metal affinity chromatography followed by Strep-tag affinity chromatography and size exclusion. We observed excellent expression, purity, and monodispersity (Figure S1B–D), ideal for structural studies. We then used single-particle cryo-EM to determine the structure of monomeric SevECD at 2.78 Å resolution (Figure 1C, Figure S2). The cryo-EM analysis revealed a highly homogenous set of particles with dimensions 148 Å × 70 Å. The density map allowed us to model residues 206–1287, running from the HD domain down to the F4 domain, which form a unique architecture not observed in any other protein family. We named it the “Sevenless fold”. C-terminal to this, the protein structure could not be resolved; neither could the first N-terminal ~100 residues. The HD, F1 and F2 domains extend on one side of the Sevenless fold up to the P1 domain vertex, where the protein chain turns and extends on the other side of the molecule down to the F4 domain, proximal to the N-terminal HD domain. Multiple disulfide bonds are visible in the cryo-EM map: five in HD (two of them are shown in Figure S2), one in F1 and one in P2, all apparently involved in intra-domain stabilization.Sequence non-adjacent inter-domain contacts are non-covalent and observed between HD and P2 and between HD and F4 (Figure 1D). Residues Phe231, Leu232, and Met292 in HD interact with Arg1019, Val1023 and Leu1034 in P2 through hydrophobic contacts; a salt bridge between Glu227 and Arg1019 and a main chain hydrogen bond between the amino group of Leu232 and the carbonyl of Arg1032 outline the hydrophobic interactions. A similar chemistry is observed in the interface between HD and F4, where the hydrophobic side chains of Phe235, Ile237 and Lys247 in HD interact with Leu1236, Leu1243 and the aliphatic chain of Arg1234 in F4. Lys247 and Glu234 in HD are also involved in salt bridges with Glu1238 and Arg1234 respectively (Figure 1D). However, native Sev is unlikely to experience alkaline pH since the extracellular pH in Drosophila is between 6 and 7.28,29 To understand the structural basis of Boss binding to Sev, we determined the cryo-EM structure of SevECD in complex with BossECD (Figures S3 and S4). The cryo-EM dataset collected at pH 6.6 yielded the highest resolution structure for the SevECD/BossECD complex. The particle set was highly heterogeneous, with multiple 3D classes representative of monomeric and dimeric forms of both SevECD and BossECD (Figure S3). Most of the SevECD particles were in the monomeric form, and we determined the structure of monomeric SevECD in complex with monomeric BossECD at 3.09 Å resolution (Figure 2A, left panel). Equal amounts of BossECD monomer and dimer were observed. To maximize signal, we merged the particle sets of BossECD monomer and BossECD dimer in a single refinement, masking out the Boss protomer not bound to Sev. The map revealed that BossECD binds to the F3 domain of SevECD through the interaction of β-hairpin loops from each molecule. Residues 475–494 of Boss interact with F3 by extending the two β-sheets of the domain with two parallel β-strands (Figure 2A, middle panel). The Sev-binding region of BossECD and the external strands of SevECD interact through their main chain atoms, with additional hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds that involve side chains. Two key leucine residues, Leu489Boss and Leu491Boss, bind to the hydrophobic pocket of F3 outlined by residues Phe876Sev, Ile878Sev and Phe886Sev; His480Boss is also involved in this hydrophobic interaction. Residues exposed to the external part of the Sev-binding region are mostly involved in polar interactions: Tyr481Boss forms a hydrogen bond with Arg879Sev, and Glu490Boss forms a salt bridge with Arg890Sev and a hydrogen bond with Gln889Sev (Figure 2A, right panel). Structural and biophysical characterization of BossECDThe sequence of Boss predicts a ~100 kDa protein with a ~60 kDa N-terminal ECD, seven TM helices and a ~14 kDa ICD. In the cryo-EM dataset of SevECD in complex with BossECD at pH 6.6 we observed two forms of BossECD: monomeric and dimeric. Using 3D classification, we isolated dimeric BossECD particles and aligned them in masked local refinement to determine the structure of dimeric BossECD in the Sev-bound form at 3.19 Å resolution (Figure 3, Figure S3). The N-terminal ECD of Boss is arranged in two distinct domains that we named based on their position relative to the TM insertion. The membrane-distal domain (MDD) includes residues 35–235 and residues 337–439. The N-terminal ~100 amino acids of Boss were too flexible to be modeled in our cryo-EM structure. The MDD contains several α-helices surrounding one β-sheet and is glycosylated at residue Asn183. The membrane-proximal domain (MPD) is smaller, formed by residues 236–336 and 440–530. The MPD has a high β-strand content, resulting in two main β-sheets, one in each protomer, that create a platform-like structure adjacent to the predicted TM insertion (Figure 3A). There are two visible glycosylation sites in the MPD, at residues Asn474 and Asn485. Interestingly, both the Sev-binding region and the dimerization region are part of the MPD.The inter-protomer interface is formed by a “strand-swap” region where residues 521–529 form two β-strands that swap position relative to the bulk of the N-terminal region, each sandwiched between two strands of the partner protomer (Figure 3B). The dimer interface shows a symmetric architecture mostly stabilized by hydrogen bonds between main chain atoms of each strand and hydrophobic contacts that involve residues Trp523, Ile525 and Met527 and their C2-symmetric counterparts. These hydrophobic interactions are further stabilized by the presence of Leu266 at the top of the dimer interface on the side of the sheet facing the rest of the ECD (Figure 3C). The other side of the sheet is rich in charged residues (Figure S5A), which could be involved in interactions with the external polar heads of the membrane phospholipids. To validate these structural results, we expressed and purified two BossECD variants with dramatic changes in chemical nature, Leu266Asp and Ile525Asp, and used analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC) to measure their homodimerization affinity compared to WT BossECD (Figure 3D, Figure S6A). For both variants, we measured a higher KD than the WT, indicative of a lower inter-protomer affinity and disruption of the dimer, consistent with our structural results. We also investigated the effect of pH on the dimerization of WT BossECD using AUC at pH 7.6, 6.6 and 5.6 (Figure 3E, Figure S6B) and mass photometry at pH 7.6, 6.6, 5.6 and 4.6 (Figure S6C). These results suggest that Boss is mostly dimeric at pH ≥6.6, with a decreased stability at more acidic pH values in the concentration range used for AUC (9–3 μM). Mass photometry data showed a single prevalent BossECD population in the pH range 7.6–4.6, with a measured molecular weight between 99–78 kDa consistent with a monomeric form of BossECD at the low concentration used for this analysis (30 nM).

At pH 6.6, the SevECD homodimer accounted for 4% of the particles in the dataset of SevECD/BossECD, and 13% in the dataset of ligand-free SevECD, and the percentage increased as the pH was decreased, rising to 85% at pH 4.6 (Figure S5B and S7). The dimer structure was determined at 3.06 Å resolution, revealing two SevECD protomers assembled in a C2-symmetric molecule of dimensions 215 Å × 102 Å (Figure 5A, Figure S7). Dimeric SevECD at pH 6.6 allowed us to model residues 206–1729, including domains F5, P3 and F6, that could not be resolved in monomeric SevECD. Domains F7, F8 and F9, the very C-term of SevECD, were disordered in our structure at pH 6.6. However, cryo-EM analysis of a dataset collected at pH 4.6 revealed a 3D class representative of ligand-free SevECD dimer where we could observe low-resolution density for domains F7 and F8. We used particle subtraction and masked local refinement to align the SevECD dimer particles on domains F6–F9, and then merged it with the overall map aligned on the rigid top region of the molecule. The composite map revealed a T-shaped dimer where the top portion of the assembly is formed by two interacting Sevenless fold structures and a stalk region comprising C-terminal domains F6–F9, which protrudes from the C2 center of symmetry in the direction of where the membrane bilayer would be in the native context (Figure 5B). The reconstruction allowed us to model additional domains F7 and F8 as poly-Ala.Similar to the monomeric form, the SevECD dimer contains intra-domain disulfide bonds, one in P3 and two in F6; there are no disulfide bonds between the two SevECD protomers. SevECD dimerization is mostly mediated by hydrogen bonds and salt bridges between domains F1, F5 and P3 (Figure 5C). The interface between F1 of one protomer and F5 of the other is dominated by main chain hydrogen bonds between two external antiparallel β-strands, further stabilized by side chain interactions that involve histidine residues: the carboxyl group of Asp386B is sandwiched between Arg1297A, and His1390A; Arg1297A also interacts with the main chain carbonyl of Val392B. On the opposite site of the sheet, Tyr385B is involved in a hydrogen bond with His1371A, while another tyrosine residue Tyr341B forms a hydrogen bond with the carboxamide group of Gln1373A. The F1B domain also interacts with the P3A domain through a hydrogen bond between the main chain carbonyl of Leu396B and the side chain amino group of Arg1648A, which is also in the proximity of residue Asp384B (Figure 5C).The interactions observed in the dimer interface involve two histidine residues. The side chain imidazole ring of histidine can exist in three chemical forms: two distinct neutral tautomeric forms in equilibrium, with hydrogens at either the Nδ1 or Nε2 nitrogen atom (Nδ1-H, Nε2-H) and one positively charged protonated form, with hydrogens at both Nδ1 and Nε2 (Figure S5C).30 The pKa of the imidazole ring of histidine is 6.3, although it can vary substantially within proteins.31.The relative abundance of histidine’s chemical forms depends on the pH of the environment: at pH > pKa the neutral tautomeric forms are prevalent, while at pH < pKa the protonated form is prevalent. The analysis of the protonation states of His1390 and His1371 using resonance structures and histidine’s tautomeric equilibrium can provide insights into the pH-dependent stabilization of the Sev dimer (Figures S5D–G). At pH < pKa of His1390, Asp386B could be stabilized by two possible salt bridges, with Arg1297A and with protonated His1390A (Figure S5D). At pH > pKa of His1390, Asp386B could only form one salt bridge with Arg1297A, while hydrogen bonding may still be possible with one of the two tautomeric forms of unprotonated His1390A (Figure S5E). Acidic pH induces Sev dimerization

In our cryo-EM datasets collected at pH ≤6.6, we observed SevECD in both its monomeric and dimeric forms. At pH 6.6, the SevECD homodimer accounted for 4% of the particles in the dataset of SevECD/BossECD, and 13% in the dataset of ligand-free SevECD, and the percentage increased as the pH was decreased, rising to 85% at pH 4.6 (Figure S5B and S7). The dimer structure was determined at 3.06 Å resolution, revealing two SevECD protomers assembled in a C2-symmetric molecule of dimensions 215 Å × 102 Å (Figure 5A, Figure S7). Dimeric SevECD at pH 6.6 allowed us to model residues 206–1729, including domains F5, P3 and F6, that could not be resolved in monomeric SevECD. Domains F7, F8 and F9, the very C-term of SevECD, were disordered in our structure at pH 6.6. However, cryo-EM analysis of a dataset collected at pH 4.6 revealed a 3D class representative of ligand-free SevECD dimer where we could observe low-resolution density for domains F7 and F8. We used particle subtraction and masked local refinement to align the SevECD dimer particles on domains F6–F9, and then merged it with the overall map aligned on the rigid top region of the molecule. The composite map revealed a T-shaped dimer where the top portion of the assembly is formed by two interacting Sevenless fold structures and a stalk region comprising C-terminal domains F6–F9, which protrudes from the C2 center of symmetry in the direction of where the membrane bilayer would be in the native context (Figure 5B). The reconstruction allowed us to model additional domains F7 and F8 as poly-Ala.

Similar to the monomeric form, the SevECD dimer contains intra-domain disulfide bonds, one in P3 and two in F6; there are no disulfide bonds between the two SevECD protomers. SevECD dimerization is mostly mediated by hydrogen bonds and salt bridges between domains F1, F5 and P3 (Figure 5C). The interface between F1 of one protomer and F5 of the other is dominated by main chain hydrogen bonds between two external antiparallel β-strands, further stabilized by side chain interactions that involve histidine residues: the carboxyl group of Asp386B is sandwiched between Arg1297A, and His1390A; Arg1297A also interacts with the main chain carbonyl of Val392B. On the opposite site of the sheet, Tyr385B is involved in a hydrogen bond with His1371A, while another tyrosine residue Tyr341B forms a hydrogen bond with the carboxamide group of Gln1373A. The F1B domain also interacts with the P3A domain through a hydrogen bond between the main chain carbonyl of Leu396B and the side chain amino group of Arg1648A, which is also in the proximity of residue Asp384B (Figure 5C).

The interactions observed in the dimer interface involve two histidine residues. The side chain imidazole ring of histidine can exist in three chemical forms: two distinct neutral tautomeric forms in equilibrium, with hydrogens at either the Nδ1 or Nε2 nitrogen atom (Nδ1-H, Nε2-H) and one positively charged protonated form, with hydrogens at both Nδ1 and Nε2 (Figure S5C).30 The pKa of the imidazole ring of histidine is 6.3, although it can vary substantially within proteins.31.The relative abundance of histidine’s chemical forms depends on the pH of the environment: at pH > pKa the neutral tautomeric forms are prevalent, while at pH < pKa the protonated form is prevalent. However, this class represents only a small fraction of the total particles and could not be used for 3D refinements, but may under-represent the true frequency of such complexes in vivo where the Boss dimer is membrane-stabilized. Another possibility is the formation of molecular clusters of Boss and Sev dimers between the two cell membranes, as occurs in Eph/ephrin interactions.39 However, structural modeling suggests that this is topologically unfavorable and therefore unlikely (Figure 7C).

Potential mechanisms of Sev activation

Mass photometry data indicate that Sev dimerization depends on acidic pH and can occur in the absence of Boss. The structure of the SevECD dimer at pH 4.6 revealed a stalk region formed by domains F6–F9, directly N-terminal to the transmembrane insertion in full-length Sev. The F9 domains, at the C-terminus of our construct, were too disordered to be modeled, nevertheless, the cryo-EM map suggested that in the stalk that projects to the membrane insertion, the two protomers are tightly apposed. By inference then, in the full-length Sev the two TM domains and ICDs should be proximate, potentially allowing the mutual tyrosine phosphorylation of the ICDs that define receptor activation.

Generally, when ligand binds a classical RTK at the cell surface, it induces dimerization and activation. This does not appear to be the case with Sev; rather Sev dimers form much more readily at a pH significantly lower than that of the extracellular matrix. Furthermore, Sev dimerization at low pH occurs in a Boss-independent manner. Thus, rather than acting to directly dimerize Sev, we suggest a possible role for Boss in directing the Sev/Boss complex into internal compartments with low pH where Boss may release Sev which then dimerizes and activates (Figure 7A). In this model, the transmembrane nature of Boss plays a key, but undefined role in directing the internalization of the Sev/Boss complex.

However, we note that our in vitro experiments use a soluble form of BossECD, whereas in vivo, BossECD interacts with Sev as part of an integral membrane protein. Given this proviso, we cannot discount the possibility that under these conditions Boss behaves differently in its interactions with itself and with Sev. This raises other possible mechanisms by which Sev activation could occur. For example, dimeric Boss may engender Sev dimerization and activation at the plasma membrane in a manner typical of RTK/ligand interactions (Figure 7B). Indeed, we observed a tetrameric arrangement in one 2D class average obtained for the cryo-EM dataset of SevECD/BossECD at pH 6.6 (Figure S3, 2D classification), where two Sev monomers are apparently bound to dimeric Boss, with each Sev monomer bound to a Boss protomer. However, this class represents only a small fraction of the total particles and could not be used for 3D refinements, but may under-represent the true frequency of such complexes in vivo where the Boss dimer is membrane-stabilized. Another possibility is the formation of molecular clusters of Boss and Sev dimers between the two cell membranes, as occurs in Eph/ephrin interactions.39 However, structural modeling suggests that this is topologically unfavorable and therefore unlikely (Figure 7C).

Conservation of ligand-binding structures in Sev and ROS1Boss proteins are found throughout insects, but their role as the Sev ligand is likely restricted to Diptera. Boss has not yet been identified in vertebrates, but ROS1 has been recently reported to bind the secreted neural EGF-like 2 protein (NELL2) and its ligand, NELL2-interacting cofactor for lumicrine signalling (NICOL).40,41 We document a striking conservation between the Boss-binding site of Sev, the F3 domain, and the corresponding F3 domain of ROS1. Naively, this suggests a conservation of the binding site, but we note that Boss and NELL2 are very different in their structures and their sequence alignment does not show significant conservation, suggesting that the molecular basis of the interaction between ROS1 and NELL2 is likely diverged from that of Sev/Boss. Furthermore, since the ROS1 ligand is a secreted protein, any phagocytotic-like internalization of the ligand/receptor complex as is observed for Boss/Sev is unlikely. Given the extensive structural homology between Sev and ROS1, a pH-dependent mechanism of ROS1 dimerization and activation is a possibility, although the residues primarily involved in Sev dimerization are not conserved in ROS1.

ConclusionWe report here the structural basis of ligand binding and dimerization of Sev, the only pH-dependent RTK reported to dimerize at acidic pH. The only other example of a pH-dependent RTK is the IRR, activating at extracellular alkaline pH mainly in kidney, stomach and pancreas.24 With Sev, however, we speculate that appropriate pH for activation is provided in a specialized internal compartment rather than at the plasma membrane. Unlike Sev, the IRR is pre-assembled into (αβ)2 dimers, with high extracellular pH causing a conformational change in the structure of the ECD, similarly to the IR. With Sev, we suggest a different mechanism whereby Sev monomers dimerize and activate only in internal compartments at low pH. LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDYOur results provide a structural and functional description of the Sev/Boss systems, but there are limitations to our conclusions. For the structure of SevECD homodimer, the C-terminal region of our construct (F9 domain) could not be modeled in our cryo-EM reconstruction, likely due to intrinsic flexibility. The F7 and F8 domains were modeled as poly-Ala since the cryo-EM map did not allow modelling of the side chains. In terms of biological function, we suggest models based on our molecular characterization of the SevECD/BossECD complex and the documented internalization of the complex into the R7 precursor, but we do not provide experimental evidence for the exact mechanism of Sev activation. We do not clarify the molecular details of the Sev/Boss internalization process, which involve other proteins and go beyond the scope of this paper.

For SevECD in complex with BossECD, purified BossECD was added to purified SevECD in a 2:1 molar ratio (final concentration 0.26 mg/mL BossECD and 0.5 mg/mL SevECD) and incubated on ice for 30 min in three different buffers: 1) 10 mM Bis-Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 6.6, 2) 20 mM sodium acetate, 150 mM NaCl, pH 5.6 and 3) 20 mM sodium acetate, 150 mM NaCl, pH 4.6. The pH of the samples was confirmed using pH-indicator paper. Cryo-EM grids for all samples were prepared by applying 2 μL of sample to a freshly glow-discharged gold UltrAuFoil grid (R 0.6/1 300 mesh); the sample was vitrified in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV with blot force 0, no wait time, blot time 4 s for ligand-free SevECD, and blot time 6 s for the SevECD/BossECD complex. Cryo-EM images were collected using the Leginon software42 installed on a Titan Krios electron microscope operating at 300 kV, equipped with a Gatan K3-BioQuantum direct detection device. The total dose was fractionated over 50 raw frames for 2.5 s.

Patch motion correction, local motion correction, patch CTF estimation, particle picking and extraction, 2D and 3D classification, ab initio model generation, 3D refinements, local refinements, particle subtraction and local resolution estimation for all datasets were carried out in cryoSPARC 3.4.43 C2 symmetry was applied to the final 3D refinement of the SevECD dimer and the local refinement of the BossECD dimer. Final maps were density-modified using deepEMhancer44 to support model building.

The individual domains of SevECD and the BossECD monomer were modeled using Alphafold structure predictions32 as initial templates. Their refined coordinates were then used to model the SevECD dimer and the BossECD dimer structures. The structure of SevECD in complex with BossECD was modeled using the SevECD monomer structure and the BossECD monomer Alphafold prediction. The structure of the BossECD dimer was modeled using the refined coordinates of the BossECD monomer structure obtained for the SevECD/BossECD complex. Domains F7 and F8 in the SevECD dimer structure were modeled as polyAla, except for the glycosylation sites; the glycan density was used to orient the domains into the cryo-EM density. Automated and manual model building were iteratively performed using real space refinement in Phenix45 and Coot46, respectively. Geometry validation and structure quality assessment were performed using Phenix.45 Structural analysis and figure preparation were carried out using PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.0 Schrӧdinger, LLC), UCSF Chimera47 and ChimeraX.48 A summary of the cryo-EM data collection, reconstruction and refinement statistics is shown in Table S1.

The binding assays were performed using the same assay parameters as described above. Attempts to reproduce the binding assays at pH 5.2 and 4.6 did not yield reproducible results, regardless of the use of a Series S CM3 chip or Series S C1 chip lacking the negatively charged dextran layer. We also tested BossECD binding to SevECD using different immobilization strategies and buffer conditions to overcome this problem. Along with the amine coupling immobilization of SevECD, we assessed SevECD capturing on a Streptactin-coated chip. At pH 5.2 and 4.6 we tested higher ionic strength (300 mM NaCl instead of 150 mM NaCl) with the purpose of disrupting the observed non-specific interaction of Boss with the empty flow cells. Unfortunately, in all cases we still observed binding of BossECD to the empty reference surface, preventing us from obtaining accurate results at pH <5.6. We interpreted these results based on the isoelectric point (pI) of BossECD. Based on the amino acid sequence, the pI of Boss is 5.25, meaning that at pH <5.25, BossECD is positively charged and can interact with the negatively charged dextran layer of the SPR chip, explaining the indiscriminate binding of BossECD to the surface with and without immobilized SevECD.

Sedimentation equilibrium measurements using analytical ultracentrifugationExperiments were performed in a Beckman XL-A/I analytical ultracentrifuge (Beckman-Coulter, Palo Alto CA, USA), utilizing six-cell centerpieces with straight walls, 12 mm path length and sapphire windows. All proteins were dialyzed over-night and then diluted in either 10 mM Bis-Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 6.6, or 10 mM Bis-Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 5.6, or 10 mM Tris, 150 NaCl mM, pH 7.6. Samples were diluted to 0.75, 0.57 and 0.28 mg/mL in channels A, B and C, respectively. Dilution buffers were used as blank. All samples were run at four speeds. The speeds were 11000, 14000, 17000, and 20000 rpm for all BossECD proteins, and 6000, 7500, 9000 and 10500 rpm for all SevECD proteins. The lowest speed was held for 20 h after which four measuring scans were taken with 1 h interval, the second lowest speed held for 10 h, followed by four scans as above, the third lowest and the highest speed performed identically as the second lowest speed. All measurements were done at 25 °C, and detection was by interference at 675 nm. Solvent density and protein v-bar were determined using the program SednTerp49 (Alliance Protein Laboratories, Corte Cancion, Thousand Oaks, CA, USA). For calculation of dimeric KD and apparent molecular weight, all useful data were used in a global fit, using the program SedPhat50, obtained from National Institute of Health (www.nibib.nih.gov). Attempts to perform the same experiment in a buffer of 20 mM sodium acetate, 150 mM NaCl, pH 4.6 failed to yield data that could be used to determine a KD, as a result of protein precipitation during a prolonged experiment (> 20 hours) at low pH.

Mass photometryMass photometry (MP) experiments were performed using a Refeyn One MP photometer (Refeyn Ltd.). Samples were evaluated with microscope coverslips (cover glass thickness 1 ½ 24 × 50 mm, Corning). Briefly, the coverslips were washed with Milli-Q water and isopropanol, then silicone templates were placed on top of dried coverslip to form reaction chambers. The instrument was calibrated using NativeMark Protein Standards (NativeMark™ Unstained Protein Standard, ThermoFisher). Measurements were performed at four different pH values: 7.6, 6.6, 5.6 and 4.6. For each acquisition, 10 μL of fresh room temperature buffer was pipetted into a well, the focal position was identified and locked; 1 μL of SevECD at a concentration 370 nM (final concentration after dilution 37 nM) or BossECD at a concentration of 300 nM (final concentration after dilution 30 nM) was added to the well and thoroughly mixed. MP signals were recorded for 60 s to allow detection of at least 2 × 103 individual binding events. Data was collected using AcquireMP (Refeyn Ltd. 172 v2.3) and analyzed using DiscoverMP (Refeyn Ltd. 172 v2.3).51
