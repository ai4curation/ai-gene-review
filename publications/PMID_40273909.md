Virtually all individuals aged 65 or older develop at least early pathology of
Alzheimer's disease (AD), yet most lack disease-causing mutations in APP, PSEN,
or MAPT, and many do not carry the APOE4 risk allele. This raises questions
about AD development in the general population. Although transcriptional
dysregulation has not traditionally been a hallmark of AD, recent studies reveal
significant epigenomic changes in late-onset AD (LOAD) patients. We show that
altered expression of the LOAD biomarker phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH)
modulates AD pathology in mice and human brain organoids independent of its
enzymatic activity. PHGDH has an uncharacterized role in transcriptional
regulation, promoting the transcription of inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa-B
kinase subunit alpha (IKKa) and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) in astrocytes,
which suppress autophagy and accelerate amyloid pathology. A
blood-brain-barrier-permeable small-molecule inhibitor targeting PHGDH's
transcriptional function reduces amyloid pathology and improves AD-related
behavioral deficits. These findings highlight transcriptional regulation in LOAD
and suggest therapeutic strategies beyond targeting familial mutations.

INTRODUCTION

Late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (LOAD) often occurs in individuals with seemingly “normal” genomes without known mutations of familial AD (FAD) and primary tauopathies, including APP, PSEN, or MAPT mutations.1–3 Moreover, more than half of LOAD patients do not carry the APOE4 risk allele, and even fewer carry the TREM2 risk allele, indicating that the development of LOAD is not tied to a single genetic factor.1,4 Yet, nearly all individuals aged 65 or older develop some level of AD pathology.1,4 The lack of a deterministic genetic marker underscores the importance of identifying epigenetic biomarkers, which are particularly valuable for early diagnosis and understanding how LOAD can develop in the general population.

Phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) stands out among AD biomarkers. While other biomarkers, including β-amyloid (Aβ) and phosphorylated Tau (p-Tau), involve posttranslationally processed, modified, or aggregated protein forms,5,6 PHGDH’s protein and RNA expression levels both correlate with LOAD.7 These correlations have been reproduced in several patient cohorts and across different brain regions. Specifically, elevated PHGDH protein levels were found in the hippocampus,8 prefrontal cortex, and precuneus of LOAD patients.7,9 Importantly, PHGDH protein levels in the hippocampus correlated with Braak stages10,11 and the individual’s cognitive impairment.11 Furthermore, a consistent increase in PHGDH RNA levels was reported in three LOAD cohorts.12,13 Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) analysis confirmed PHGDH levels were specifically increased in the astrocytes of LOAD patients at the early phase of the disease.11,14 Additionally, the usefulness of PHGDH as a biomarker is demonstrated by the elevated levels of PHGDH’s extracellular RNA (exRNA) in the blood plasma of LOAD patients.7,15 Importantly, PHGDH exRNA levels in blood serum increase in patients prior to clinical diagnosis, suggesting plausible early diagnosis with this biomarker.7

Moreover, linkage studies implicated PHGDH in LOAD, as the gene is located within the strongest LOAD linkage region identified in a meta-analysis involving 2,206 affected individuals from 785 families.16

The aforementioned associations raised the question of whether PHGDH plays a causal role in LOAD. We found that overexpression and underexpression of PHGDH promotes and suppresses amyloid pathology, respectively, establishing a causal relationship. Importantly, we observed this causal relationship in a human brain organoid (BO) model of LOAD (LOAD-BO) devoid of FAD (APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2), tauopathy (MAPT), and APOE mutations,17 suggesting an unknown regulatory function of PHGDH in the pathophysiology of LOAD.

To investigate the underlying mechanism, we began by analyzing PHGDH’s known function. PHGDH is an enzyme required for serine synthesis.18 In the brain, PHGDH is specifically expressed in glial cells including astrocytes, where serine production occurs.18 In mice, PHGDH knockout (KO) disrupts the supply of serine, an essential metabolite and a precursor for D-serine, a key neuromodulator in neurotransmission. Furthermore, astrocyte-specific conditional KO of PHGDH impairs neuronal circuit integrity.18,19 Similarly, human PHGDH mutations lead to embryonic death or serine deficiency disorders exemplified by brain developmental defects (OMIM: 601815).20 Thus, adequate levels of wild-type (WT) PHGDH proteins are indispensable for normal development. To our surprise, our experiments failed to establish the requirement of PHGDH’s enzymatic function in its role of promoting AD pathology. These negative results led us to explore and eventually identify an uncharacterized function of PHGDH in transcriptional regulation that is required for promoting AD pathology.

RESULTSPHGDH overexpression induces Aβ in transgenic and WT miceWe first examined whether elevated PHGDH expression in astrocytes influences AD-related pathology in mice. To address this, we performed unilateral injections of an adeno-associated virus (AAV9)-encoding FLAG-tagged human PHGDH driven by the GFAP promoter (AAV9-GFAP-hPHGDH-FLAG-GFP) into the dentate gyrus (DG) of one hemisphere (“test side”) of 3xTg-AD mice—a triple-transgenic model of FAD.21 The opposite hemisphere (“control side”) received a control virus expressing GFP alone (AAV9-GFAP-GFP). Immunostaining confirmed that nearly all AAV-transduced cells (GFP+) were GFAP+ astrocytes, validating the specificity of astrocyte-targeted transduction (Figures S1A and S1B). Also, 1 month post-injection, the test side displayed elevated Aβ and GFAP levels compared with controls, while p-Tau levels remained unchanged in 3xTg-AD mice (Figures S1C and S1D). These results demonstrate that astrocytic PHGDH overexpression can induce Aβ accumulation in mice carrying FAD mutations.To further explore whether PHGDH promotes Aβ level in a normal genetic context, we induced astrocyte-specific PHGDH overexpression in WT mice. We unilaterally injected AAV9-GFAP-hPHGDH-GFP into the DG of one hemisphere (test side) in 6-month-old WT C57BL/6J mice, with the opposite hemisphere receiving the control AAV (AAV9-GFAP-GFP). At 7 months of age, AAV-expressing cells (GFP+) were again GFAP+ astrocytes, confirming the specificity of the viral construct (Figures S1E and S1F). As expected, western blot analysis revealed low Aβ levels on the control side (Figures S1G and S1H), while the test side showed elevated Aβ and GFAP levels (Figures S1G and S1H). These findings demonstrate that PHGDH overexpression driven by a GFAP promoter induces Aβ accumulation, even in the absence of FAD mutations. This is consistent with the elevated astrocyte PHGDH levels observed in LOAD patients, regardless of APOE4 status.7,8,11 However, no differences in p-Tau levels were observed between the test and control sides (Figures S1G and S1H), suggesting that changes in PHGDH expression alone are insufficient to fully replicate all aspects of LOAD pathology.To further validate these findings, we generated PHGDHtetO/+::GFAP-rtTAM2+/− double-transgenic mice by crossing PHGDHtetO mice22 with GFAP-rtTAM2 mice, enabling doxycycline (dox)-inducible PHGDH expression under the control of the GFAP promoter (Figures S1I and S1J). PHGDH expression was induced in 8-month-old PHGDHtetO/+::GFAP-rtTAM2+/− mice (test group), which were analyzed at 10 months of age. Age-matched control groups included PHGDHtetO/+::GFAP-rtTAM2+/− mice without dox and PHGDHtetO/+::GFAP-rtTAM2 −/− mice with dox.Western blot analysis confirmed increased PHGDH levels in the hippocampi of the test group compared with both control groups (Figures S1L and S1M). Immunostaining further validated the co-expression of PHGDH and GFAP in the same cells across all three groups, demonstrating that the increased PHGDH expression was driven by the GFAP promoter (Figure S1K).Notably, Aβ levels were significantly elevated in the test group compared with the control groups, while no differences in p-Tau levels were observed (Figures S1L and S1M). These findings align with results from AAV-induced PHGDH overexpression, indicating that PHGDH overexpression promotes Aβ elevation without requiring disease-causing mutations in APP, PSEN, MAPT, or the APOE4 risk allele. However, PHGDH overexpression alone is insufficient to fully replicate all aspects of AD pathology.Elevated PHGDH expression in astrocytes of LOAD-BOsTo investigate whether PHGDH modulates Aβ levels in a normal human genetic context, we utilized human BOs derived from human embryonic stem cells (hESCs).17 These BOs, which lacked FAD, MAPT, or APOE mutations, exhibited significant Aβ aggregates and other pathologies associated with LOAD after exposure to 10% human blood serum—used to simulate blood-brain barrier (BBB) leakage in the aging brain. This established a robust BO model for LOAD.17We successfully reproduced this process by differentiating H1 hESCs into BOs through embryoid body formation, neuroepithelial induction, expansion, and maturation over 130 days (Figure S2A). Our 130-day BO culturing protocol extended the original method17 by 30 days to enhance astrocyte maturation. These 130-day-old BOs were then cultured in either normal media (NM) or 10% human serum (Serum+) for 12 days before being harvested at day 142 for analysis (Figure S2A). Both NM- and serum-treated BOs expressed the neuronal marker MAP2 and the astrocyte marker GFAP (Figure S2B). Consistent with previous findings,17 serum-treated BOs (Serum+) exhibited Aβ aggregates and elevated p-Tau levels (Figures S2C–S2E). Treatment with a β-secretase inhibitor (Serum+/BACE-I) reduced Aβ aggregates, while treatment with a GSK-3 inhibitor (Serum+/GSK3-I) reduced p-Tau levels (Figures S2C–S2E).We refer to these 142-day-old NM- and serum-treated BOs as normal BOs and LOAD-BOs, respectively. PHGDH co-localized with GFAP, marking astrocytes in the outermost layer of both normal BOs and LOAD-BOs (Figure S2F), confirming astrocytic production of PHGDH in this system. Notably, PHGDH expression was significantly higher in the GFAP+ regions of LOAD-BOs, compared with those in normal BOs (Figures S2F and S2G), consistent with the elevated PHGDH levels observed in astrocytes during early-stage LOAD in patients.11 This upregulation of PHGDH in LOAD-BOs was reversed by Serum+/BACE-I but not by Serum+/GSK3-I (Figures S2F and S2G), suggesting that astrocytic PHGDH elevation in LOAD-BOs is more closely linked to Aβ pathology than p-Tau pathology.Suppression of astrocytic PHGDH reduces Aβ aggregates and synaptic loss in LOAD-BOWe investigated whether astrocytic expression of PHGDH influences AD-related characteristics in LOAD-BO. To this end, we developed two PHGDH-targeting short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs), shRNA1 and shRNA2, and validated their efficacy in knocking down PHGDH mRNA in HEK293T cells (Figure S3A). These shRNAs, along with a scramble control (Scr) containing a GFP gene and a GFAP promoter, were incorporated into AAVs for astrocyte-specific knockdown (KD) of PHGDH. On day 133, three days after serum exposure, AAV-shRNA1, AAV-shRNA2, and AAV-scramble were individually administered to serum-exposed BOs (Serum+/shRNA1, Serum+/shRNA2, and Serum+/Scr groups), while AAV-scramble was applied to normal BOs (NM/Scr group) (Figure S3B). Nearly all (~99%) GFP+ cells were GFAP+ in every experimental group, confirming AAVs’ specificity for astrocytes (Figures S3C and S3D). Conversely, approximately 60% of GFAP+ were GFP+ in every group, confirming the efficacy of AAV transduction (Figure S3E). Furthermore, both Serum+/shRNA1 and Serum+/shRNA2 effectively reduced PHGDH levels in GFP+ cells, compared with Serum+/Scr, confirming the efficacy of these AAV-shRNAs (Figure S3F).Compared with Serum+/Scr, both Serum+/shRNA1 and Serum+/shRNA2 significantly reduced the number of Aβ aggregates (Figures 1A and 1B), the Aβ coverage area (Figure S3G), and the total amount of Aβ (Figures S3H and S3I), without affecting p-Tau levels (Figures S3H–S3K). Furthermore, compared with NM/Scr, Serum+/Scr BOs showed a decrease in synaptic marker (SYN1) puncta (Figures 1A and 1C) and an increase in activated CASPASE-3 (aCASP3+) cells (Figures 1A and 1D), consistent with the expected synaptic loss and apoptosis increase in LOAD-BOs, compared with normal BOs. Additionally, both Serum+/shRNA1 and Serum+/shRNA2 increased the number of SYN1 puncta and decreased aCASP3+ cells, compared with Serum+/Scr (Figures 1A, 1C, and 1D). In conclusion, suppression of astrocytic PHGDH reduces Aβ aggregates, synaptic loss, and apoptosis, without affecting p-Tau levels in LOAD-BOs.Pathological worsening is mediated by PHGDH independent of its enzymatic activityWe sought to determine whether the pathological changes mediated by PHGDH expression depend on its enzymatic function. For this purpose, we utilized a mutation (R236Q) on the active site of PHGDH, generating an enzymatic-dead (ED) form of PHGDH (PHGDH-ED).23 WT PHGDH (PHGDH-WT) and PHGDH-ED, together with the GFP gene, were incorporated into AAVs driven by a GFAP promoter (AAV-PHGDH-WT and AAV-PHGDH-ED). We administered AAV-PHGDH-WT, AAV-PHGDH-ED, and the control AAV-GFP to 130-day BOs at the onset of serum exposure (Serum+/PHGDH-WT, Serum+/PHGDH-ED, Serum+/GFP) and also applied the control AAV-GFP to 130-day BOs without serum exposure (NM/GFP) (Figure S4A). All GFP+ cells are also GFAP+, demonstrating the specificity of AAV expression to astrocytes (Figure S4B). Furthermore, both AAV-PHGDH-WT and AAV-PHGDH-ED exhibited higher PHGDH levels in GFP+ cells, compared with the AAV-GFP control, confirming efficacy of AAV-mediated PHGDH overexpression (Figure S4C). As anticipated, Serum+/GFP BOs displayed an increased number of Aβ aggregates and Aβ coverage areas, decreased SYN1, and elevated aCASP3 levels, compared with NM/GFP BOs (Figures 1E–1H and S4A). In comparison with Serum+/GFP, Serum+/PHGDH-WT further exacerbated Aβ accumulation, synaptic loss, and apoptosis (Figures 1E–1H and S4D–S4F), indicating that increased astrocytic PHGDH exacerbates these AD-related pathological changes in LOAD-BOs. Consistent with the results of PHGDH KD experiments, PHGDH overexpression did not induce changes in p-Tau levels (Figures S4E–S4H). In summary, upregulation of PHGDH expression in astrocytes worsens AD-related pathological changes, while downregulation alleviates these changes, consistent with our in vivo findings.To our surprise, Serum+/PHGDH-ED also exacerbated Aβ accumulation, synaptic loss, and apoptosis, compared with Serum+/GFP, with no statistical difference observed between Serum+/PHGDH-ED and Serum+/PHGDH-WT (p = 0.56 for number of Aβ aggregates, 0.57 for Aβ coverage area, 0.97 for total Aβ level, 0.79 for SYN1, 0.44 for aCASP3, t tests) (Figures 1E–1H and S4D–S4F). Thus, mutating PHGDH’s active site does not ameliorate the pathological changes induced by PHGDH overexpression.Nuclear-localized PHGDH regulates gene expressionCertain catalytic enzymes possess secondary, non-catalytic functions known as moonlighting functions.24,25 Our immunofluorescence and western blot analysis confirmed cytosolic localization of PHGDH in hESCs (H1) and human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T), consistent with its role in serine synthesis (Figures S5A and S5B). Intriguingly, PHGDH exhibited both cytosolic and nuclear localization in astrocytes of 142-day normal human BOs, mouse neural stem cells (mNSCs), and mNSC-derived astrocytes (Figures S5A and S5B), suggesting nuclear localization of a subset of PHGDH in astrocytes and NSCs. Coincidentally, recent reports have indicated PHGDH localization in both cytosol and nuclei of several cancers.23,26Nuclear localization of PHGDH prompted us to investigate whether PHGDH associates with chromatin. We conducted chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) on PHGDH, using immunoglobulin G (IgG) as a control. ChIP-seq was performed on three experimental groups: NM/Scr, Serum+/Scr, and Serum+/shRNA1 (hereafter referred to as Serum+/KD), with two biological replicates in each group (Figure 2A; Table S1). Peaks were identified from the entire dataset by initially calling peaks from each replicate and then identifying reproducible peaks called from at least two replicates, irrespective of the experimental group. This analysis yielded 4,401 reproducible peaks, referred to as PHGDH peaks, forming the basis of subsequent analysis. Most (69.5%) PHGDH peaks were located in promoters, showing a 12-fold enrichment, compared with genomic background (p < 2.2e–16, chi-squared test) (Figures 2B and 2C), indicating preferential association of PHGDH with promoters.As a validation step, we subjected PHGDH peaks exhibiting higher PHGDH/IgG ChIP signal in Serum+/Scr than in NM/Scr (serum-induced peaks) to functional enrichment analysis. Genomic Regions Enrichment of Annotations Tool (GREAT) identified “astrocyte differentiation” as the most significant function associated with these serum-induced peaks (p < 2.7e–6) (Figure S5C), aligning with astrocyte-specific expression of PHGDH. Furthermore, Serum+/KD-reduced ChIP signals on most (89.7%) serum-induced peaks, compared with Serum+/Scr (p < 2.2e–16, chi-squared test) (Figure S5D), consistent with our expectation that astrocyte-specific suppression of PHGDH reduces PHGDH-chromatin association in LOAD-BOs.A total of 1,050 PHGDH peaks within promoters (promoter peaks) exhibited at least 1.5-fold reduction of ChIP signals in Serum+/KD, compared with Serum+/Scr (KD-affected promoter peaks). Functional enrichment analysis of these KD-affected promoter peaks revealed 12 function terms. The top-ranked terms included AD (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes [KEGG]: HSA05010), Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, followed by terms such as ribosome, mTOR signaling pathway, protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, and autophagy, all implicated in neurodegenerative diseases (Figure S5E). These findings suggest a correlation between PHGDH-bound promoters in LOAD-BO and neurodegenerative diseases.To assess whether PHGDH has a transcriptional regulatory function, we randomly selected eight promoter regions affected by PHGDH KD to examine the regulation of their corresponding genes: DVL2, FZD4, NEDD4L, NFKBIB, PIDD1, RRAGA, RRAGD, and WNT7A. In U87MG astrocytoma cells, overexpression of WT PHGDH (PHGDH-WT), compared with the control vector, led to upregulation of five genes (DVL2, NEDD4L, NFKBIB, PIDD1, and RRAGD), downregulation of RRAGA, and no significant effect on FZD4 and WNT7A expression (Figure S5F). These findings demonstrate PHGDH’s ability to both induce and suppress gene expression.To test whether these PHGDH overexpression-induced gene expression changes depend on PHGDH’s nuclear localization, we engineered a PHGDH variant with a nuclear export signal (NES) at its N terminus (PHGDH-NES) and validated reduced nuclear localization of PHGDH-NES (Figure S5G). PHGDH-NES reversed the transcriptional changes induced by PHGDH-WT on every PHGDH-induced and -suppressed gene, while leaving the non-responsive genes unaffected (Figure S5F). In contrast, PHGDH-ED elicited similar directional changes in gene expression as PHGDH-WT across all five PHGDH-induced genes and the PHGDH-suppressed gene (Figure S5F). Collectively, these findings suggest that PHGDH’s impact on gene expression is contingent upon its nuclear localization but does not require its enzymatic activity.PHGDH’s interaction with chromatin relies on its helix-helix-turn-helix domainPHGDH contains an NAD binding domain responsible for binding oxidized nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) (Figure S6A).17,27 In the AlphaFold folded PHGDH structure, we observed a helix-helix-turn-helix (HHTH) subdomain within the nucleotide binding domain, spanning from amino acid (aa) 103 to 165 (63 aa), which exhibits structural similarity to the three amino acid loop extension (TALE) homeodomain, a DNA-binding structural motif (Figures S6A–S6C).To evaluate the dependence of PHGDH’s chromatin binding ability on the HHTH domain, we deleted this domain from PHGDH to create a variant named PHGDH-dHHTH. AlphaFold3 analysis suggests that the deletion of the HHTH domain has little impact on the overall structure of the PHGDH protein (Figures S6D and S6E). We expressed PHGDH-WT and PHGDH-dHHTH in U87MG astrocytoma cells. Using ChIP-qPCR with an anti-FLAG antibody targeting the FLAG tag in both PHGDH-WT and PHGDH-dHHTH, along with an IgG antibody, we observed significantly reduced ChIP signals on all tested promoters with PHGDH-dHHTH, compared with PHGDH-WT (Figure 2D). Notably, there were no differences in ChIP signals between PHGDH-WT and PHGDH-dHHTH on the control genes (GAPDH and ACTB) (Figure 2D). These data suggest that PHGDH’s association with chromatin depends on its HHTH domain. Furthermore, PHGDH-dHHTH reversed the transcriptional changes induced by PHGDH-WT on all the five PHGDH-induced genes and the PHGDH-suppressed gene (Figure 2E). Thus, PHGDH’s capacity to modulate gene expression relies on its HHTH domain.PHGDH’s role in transcriptional regulation is dependent on specific DNA sequencesMotif analysis of PHGDH ChIP-seq peaks unveiled several enriched DNA motifs (p value cutoff = 1e–15) (Figure S6F). The top-ranking motif (sequence consensus: CCAAT(C/G)) exhibited sequence similarity with the DNA recognition motif of human PBX1 (JASPAR ID: MA0070.1) (Figure S6G), which recognizes DNA via its TALE homeodomain.28 Given the structural resemblance between PHGDH’s HHTH domain and TALE homeodomain and the sequence similarity of the CCAAT(C/G) motif with TALE homeodomain’s DNA recognition motif, we selected this top-ranking motif from PHGDH ChIP-seq for further analysis.We employed a dual luciferase assay to evaluate whether PHGDH can influence the activities of the promoters (−1,500 to +500 bp of the transcription start site [TSS]) of the five PHGDH-induced genes and the PHGDH-suppressed gene. In U87MG cells, we co-transfected a firefly reporter driven by the respective promoter sequence to be tested, along with a renilla reporter, and either PHGDH-WT or PHGDH-ED. PHGDH-WT activated all the promoters of the five PHGDH-induced genes and repressed the promoter of the PHGDH-suppressed gene (RRAGA) (Figure S6H). These findings align with the direction of expression changes observed in the endogenous genes when PHGDH-WT was overexpressed (Figure 2E). Notably, PHGDH-ED elicited the same directional expression changes as PHGDH-WT across all tested promoters (Figure S6H), implying that PHGDH’s active site is dispensable for driving promoter activity.Out of the six tested promoters, four of them (DVL2, PIDD1, RRAGA, and RRAGD) each contain one instance of the CCAAT(C/G) motif, while the other two (NEDD4L and NFKBIB) contain two instances of this motif, referred to as NEDD4L-1, NEDD4L-2, NFKBIB-1, and NFKBIB-2, resulting in a total of eight motif instances (Table S2). To investigate the role of these motifs, we generated eight mutant promoters, each containing one mutated motif instance, achieved by converting the three central bases from AAT to CCG (Table S2). We then compared each mutant promoter (cis-Mut) with the WT promoter (cis-WT) in cells expressing either PHGDH-WT or PHGDH-dHHTH. In the promoters of PHGDH-induced genes (DVL2, RRAGD, NEDD4L, NFKBIB, and PIDD1), all cis mutations (DVL2, RRAGD, NEDD4L-1, NEDD4L-2, NFKBIB-1, and NFKBIB-2) except one (PIDD1) reduced the promoter activity (Figure 2F). The mutation of the PIDD1 promoter likely missed the actual DNA recognition site, given the presence of other DNA motifs in the PHGDH ChIP-seq peaks. Conversely, in the promoter of the PHGDH-suppressed gene (RRAGA), the cis mutation increased the promoter activity (Figure 2F), confirming the suppression depends on this CCAAT(C/G) motif.We employed a combinatorial experimental design involving trans deletion and cis mutation to investigate whether PHGDH’s HHTH domain influences gene expression independently of the CCAAT(C/G) motif (Figure 2F). Deletion of the HHTH domain (PHGDH-dHHTH) decreased the activities of WT promoters (cis-WT) compared with PHGDH-WT on all five PHGDH-induced genes, while it increased the activity of the PHGDH-suppressed promoter (Figure 2F). At the 130th day, the BOs were subjected to either normal medium (Serum-) (included in the kit of STEMCELL Technologies, 08570), or normal medium with added human serum (Thermo Fisher, BP2525100) where human serum accounts for 10% in concentration of the mixture (Serum+). Culture medium was changed every 3 days. BOs were harvested on the 142th day for characterization.

In the inhibitor treatment experiments, the Serum+ medium is added with 5 μm BACE Inhibitor IV (Sigma, 565788), or 1 μm GSK3 inhibitor CHIR99021 (Sigma, SML1046) on the 130th day. Culture medium was changed every 3 days. BOs were harvested on the 142th day for characterization.

In the astrocyte-specific PHGDH overexpression experiments, on the 130th day, three BOs were placed in each well with 3 ml of either Serum- or Serum+ medium of a 6-well plate. A PHGDH expressing AAV (20 μL solution with the AAV concentration of 1×1013 TU/mL) was added to each well. Culture medium was changed every 3 days. BOs were harvested on the 142th day for characterization.

In the astrocyte-specific PHGDH knockdown experiments, on the 130th day, three BOs were placed in each well with 3 ml of medium of a 6-well plate. Serum- or Serum+ medium was applied. Culture medium was changed every 3 days. On the 133th day, shRNA expressing AAV (20 μL solution with the AAV concentration of 1×1013 TU/mL) was added to each well. BOs were harvested on the 142th day for characterization.

For IKKa and HMGB1 knockdown experiments, three 130-day BOs were placed in each well of a 6-well plate containing 3 ml of medium supplemented with 10% human serum. Control AAV and PHGDH overexpression AAV were introduced on day 130. Three days later, 100 pmol of siRNAs were transfected to each well using 10 uL of lipofectamine 3000 according to the standard protocol. The brain organoids were harvested on day 142. Scramble siRNA (Life Technologies, 4404021) and siRNA against IKKa (Life Technologies, AM16704, ID#: 112) and HMGB1 (Life Technologies, 4392421, ID#: s20254).

For small molecule treatments, three 130-day BOs were placed in each well of a 6-well plate containing 3 ml of either Serum- or Serum+ medium. Culture medium was changed every 3 days. On the 136th day, we supplied each culture medium with 3 μL of DMSO, 3 μL of IPA (10 mM) (Sigma, 57400, CAS#: 830–96-6), or 3 μL of NCT-503 (10 mM) (Sigma, SML1659, CAS#: 1916571–90-8), and treatment continued for 6 days. For L-serine treatment, considering regular DMEM/F12 medium contains 26.25 mg/L L-serine (Sigma, S4311, CAS#: 56–45-1), we add 50% more L-serine (13.125 mg/L) to the medium to reach the concentration of 39.375 mg/L. L-serine treatment started on the 136th day and lasted for 6 days. On the 142nd day, brain organoids were harvested for characterization.

Barnes MazeThe Barnes maze consisted of a 75 cm PVC disc with 20 evenly spaced holes, elevated 2–3 feet above the floor. An opaque black escape box (19 × 8 × 7 cm) was placed under one hole, with spatial cues arranged around the room. On the first day, mice were habituated to the maze and guided to the escape hole. Training took place on days two and three, using an 80 dB buzzer and 400 lux light to encourage escape behavior. During each trial, mice were placed inside an opaque cylinder in the center of the maze for 15 seconds to ensure random orientation. Once the cylinder was lifted, the buzzer was activated, and the mice were given 5 minutes to explore the maze. If the mouse found the target hole and entered the escape box, it remained for 1 minute before being returned to the holding cage. If not, the mouse was guided with a glass beaker, and if it still didn’t enter after 3 minutes, it was manually placed in the escape box for 1 minute. Three trials were completed: one on day two and two on day three. After a rest day, the test on day five removed the escape box. Mice were again placed inside the cylinder for 15 seconds, the buzzer was activated, and they were given 5 minutes to explore. Key parameters (Primary Latency, Distance, Speed, and Strategy) were recorded and analyzed using ANY-maze software.
