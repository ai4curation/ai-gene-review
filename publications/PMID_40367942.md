The α2δs are a family of extracellular synaptic molecules that are auxiliary
subunits of voltage-gated Ca2+ channel (CaV) complexes. They are linked to brain
disorders and are drug targets. The α2δs are implicated in controlling synapse
development and function through distinct CaV-dependent and CaV-independent
pathways. However, the mechanisms of action remain enigmatic since synapses
contain mixtures of α2δ isoforms in the pre- and postsynaptic compartments. We
developed a triple conditional knockout mouse model and demonstrated the
combined selective presynaptic ablation of α2δs in vivo in a developing
mammalian glutamatergic synapse. We identified presynaptic α2δs as positive
regulators of Munc13-1 levels, an essential neurotransmitter release protein. We
found that mammalian synapse development, presynaptic CaV2.1 organization, and
the transsynaptic alignment of presynaptic release sites and postsynaptic
glutamate receptors are independent of presynaptic α2δs. Therefore, our results
define presynaptic α2δ regulatory roles and suggest a new α2δ role in
controlling synaptic strength and plasticity.

Introduction

Synapses are the fundamental unit of information transfer in the central nervous system (CNS)1,2. They are composed of highly complex molecular machinery that tightly regulates synaptic development, transmission, and plasticity to control neuronal circuit output3. It has emerged that extracellular molecules in the synaptic cleft are key molecules controlling synapse formation, development, and synaptic transmission with human mutations in these molecules correlated with human neurological disorders4,5. However, the regulatory roles of many of these molecules and their importance in a native environment remain unknown.

The α2δ proteins are glycosylated extracellular auxiliary subunits of voltage-gated Ca2+ channels (CaV)6 conserved across species and are drug targets. In mammals the α2δ family consists of four isoforms (α2δ1–4) which are encoded by four separate genes, CACNA2D1–44,7. The α2δ1–3 isoforms are highly expressed throughout the CNS with the vast majority of synapses containing a mixture of α2δ1–38,9 located in the pre and postsynaptic compartments4. The α2δ4 isoform is restricted to the retina with negligible levels in the brain8,9. At mammalian CNS synapses with conventional active zones (AZs), the α2δs are implied to have distinct roles that are CaV2 dependent and independent10. These CaV2 independent pathways are proposed to control synapse formation, development, synaptic ultrastructure, transsynaptic alignment of the pre and post synaptic compartment, and axon growth, while CaV2 dependent pathways include CaV2 trafficking, presynaptic organization, and regulation of biophysical properties4,11. Finally, the α2δs may have distinct regulatory roles during different developmental periods4,10,11.

While the α2δs are important molecules, the precise regulatory steps of the α2δs and whether these steps are specific to the pre or postsynaptic compartment in native neuronal circuits are largely unknown. Although α2δ knockout (KO) mouse models have provided valuable information11,12, the isoforms are deleted throughout development and phenotypes observed may be due to compensatory mechanisms. Additionally, these models do not allow for separation of pre or postsynaptic mechanisms of action. While α2δ−1 conditional KO (CKO) mice exist13,14, synapses express various combinations of α2δ1–3. Currently, global α2δ2 KO15 and α2δ3 KO16 mouse models exist. However, the α2δ2, α2δ3 double KO mice suffer from perinatal lethality issues17,18. Although studies have been carried out in in vitro cell culture systems which have yielded important information18,19, these systems do not recapitulate the native environment of neuronal circuits and their development. Thus, cell-type specific and pre and postsynaptic mechanisms of action remain vague. Finally, C. elegans and Drosophila model systems have provided valuable insights16,20,21. However, whether these mechanisms are conserved in mammalian synapses is unclear. Taken together, how α2δs regulate synapse formation, development, and neuronal circuit assembly remains largely unsolved.

To overcome these prior issues and provide definitive answers to these long-standing questions, we took a new direction. We created a triple Cacna2d1,2,3 CKO mouse model which allows for cell-type specific and temporal ability to ablate the combination of α2δ1, α2δ2, and α2δ3 in a native neuronal circuit. With this mouse line, we were able to selectively ablate α2δ1–3 in vivo in the presynaptic compartment of a developing mammalian glutamatergic synapse. Using a combination of functional, molecular, and morphological approaches, we identified presynaptic α2δs are positive regulators of Munc13–1 levels, an essential neurotransmitter release protein. We found mammalian synapse development, presynaptic CaV2 organization, and transsynaptic alignment of presynaptic release sites and postsynaptic glutamate receptors are independent of presynaptic α2δs. Therefore, our results define presynaptic α2δ regulatory roles and suggest a new α2δ role in controlling synaptic strength and plasticity.

ResultsPresynaptic α2δ1, α2δ2, and α2δ3 are not essential for synapse formation or active zone ultrastructural organizationTo address the presynaptic roles of the α2δs in synapse formation and function in a native mammalian neuronal circuit, we created a Cacna2d1, Cacna2d2, Cacna2d3 (Cacna2d1,2,3) CKO mouse line (Fig 1A, Fig S1, 2). The Cacna2d1,2,3 CKO mouse overcomes the current limitations of Cacna2d models by allowing cell-type specific and temporal ablation of α2δ1, α2δ2, and α2δ3 in a native neuronal circuit. To determine the presynaptic roles of α2δ1–3 in synapse formation and function, we utilized the glutamatergic calyx of Held/Medial Nucleus of the Trapezoid Body neuron (MNTB) synapse located in the ascending auditory pathway. The calyx of Held is the sole input that drives AP spiking in the MNTB22 (Fig 1B) and undergoes a well-characterized developmental process during neuronal circuit maturation. Additionally, the molecular mechanisms at the calyx of Held controlling synaptic function and individual AZ ultrastructure are like many CNS synapses22–24.Since globular bushy cell (GBC) axons in the cochlear nucleus (CN) give rise to the calyx of Held22, we determined Cacna2d1-3 expression in the CN. We performed in situ hybridization (ISH) using BaseScope probes specific to the Cacna2d1-3 floxed exons (Fig S2). We found CN cells expressed Cacna2d1, Cacna2d2, and Cacna2d3, but in comparison Cacna2d1 expression was lower (Fig S2E, F). Subsequently, we injected HdAd Cre into the postnatal day (P)1 CN to create a Cacna2d1,2,3−/− calyx of Held and performed analysis at P18–23 when the calyx of Held is mature and mice have adult-like hearing (Fig 1B)25–29. We chose P1 as the GBC axon has reached the MNTB and avoids potential roles of α2δs in axon guidance and generation22. Since current Cacna2d2-3 antibodies have severe limitations for specific detection of α2δs in immunohistochemistry (IHC)12,30, we performed BaseScope in the Cre injected CN and found that in transduced cells Cacna2d1, Cacna2d2, and Cacna2d3 mRNA was undetectable. To further verify, we performed PCR analysis on mRNA extracts from Cacna2d1-3 primary cortical neurons infected with HdAd Cre. We found that Cre expression resulted in efficient excision of the floxed alleles (Fig S1A,B). We characterized the Cacna2d1,2,3 floxed mRNA by sequencing the floxed region after Cre excision. Sequencing confirmed excision occurred, and the sequence matched the predicted sequence for each isoform after excision, with the predicted TGA stops downstream (Fig S1C–E). To rule out that Cacna2d4 expression was upregulated in the Cacna2d1,2,3−/− cells, we performed ISH using RNAScope probes specific to Cacna2d4 on the Cacna2d1,2,3−/− cells in the CN (Fig S2G). We found an absence of Cacna2d4 mRNA in Cacna2d1,2,3−/− cells. Based on this data, we conclude that all three Cacna2d isoforms can be ablated in vivo and in vitro in the Cacna2d1,2,3 CKO mouse line and Cacna2d4 expression is not upregulated.Subsequently, we analyzed how the combined presynaptic loss of α2δ1–3 impacted calyx of Held morphology and presynaptic ultrastructure. Volumetric reconstruction of confocal z-stack images revealed no difference in the surface area or volume of the presynaptic terminals and no change in the overall morphology of the calyces (Fig 1C–E). Although we found no changes in gross morphology, it was possible that there were changes in presynaptic synapse number as the calyx of Held is a large nerve terminal that consists of hundreds of individual synapses in parallel22,31. Bassoon is an accepted marker of presynaptic AZs and is routinely used to count synapse numbers32, including at the calyx of Held33. Therefore, we performed bassoon labeling and volumetric reconstruction of Z-stack images33. Analysis revealed no difference in the total number of bassoon puncta or their density (Fig 1F–H). To determine if combined presynaptic loss of α2δ1–3 affected presynaptic ultrastructure we performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) on ultrathin sections and analyzed AZ length and synaptic vesicle (SV) distribution. Our analysis revealed no change in AZ length, SV distribution, or number of docked SVs (Fig 1I–K).To ascertain if presynaptic ablation of α2δ1–3 at an earlier developmental time point resulted in a similar phenotype, we bred our Cacna2d1,2,3 CKO to the Math5 Cre mouse line. Math5 expression comes on at ~E13.5 and is specific to bushy cells in the auditory brainstem34. Analysis of gross morphology revealed no differences (Fig S3). Taken together, in contrast to in vivo results from Drosophila20, our data indicates that presynaptic α2δ1–3 are not essential for synaptic formation, synapse numbers, or presynaptic ultrastructure in vivo.Presynaptic α2δ1, α2δ2, and α2δ3 are required for presynaptic CaV2.1 abundance, but not CaV2.1 clusteringPresynaptic CaV2 abundance and organization are key determinants of synaptic strength and plasticity35. While the α2δs regulate CaV2 abundance4, whether they are key regulators of presynaptic CaV2 organization was unresolved. We found presynaptic recordings showed a dramatic reduction in presynaptic CaV2 currents by P9–11 (Fig S3), however, they lack the resolution to directly determine CaV2 numbers. Therefore, we performed single molecule localization studies on presynaptic CaV2.1, as the calyx is CaV2.1 exclusive at this timepoint36, by carrying out SDS-digested freeze-fracture replica immunogold labeling (SDS-FFRIL) with TEM27 (Fig 2). Measurement of CaV2.1 particle density on the protoplasmic (P) face from the Cacna2d1,2,3−/− calyces revealed an ~80% reduction of presynaptic CaV2.1 channels compared to Cacna2d1,2,3+/+ calyces (Fig 2B; Cacna2d1,2,3+/+ Ipsi: 20.56 ± 3.19, Cacna2d1,2,3+/+ Contra: 22.65 ± 2.44, Cacna2d1,2,3−/−: 3.99 ± 0.62 particles/μm2). To determine if CaV2.1 clustering was impacted, we measured the number of gold particles per cluster using a 30 nm radius around individual particles27,37. This 30 nm radius reflects uncertainty from the length of the antibody and size of the gold particle. A cluster was defined as any two 12 nm diameter gold-labeled-CaV2.1s within a 30 nm radius. Cluster area was measured as the total area inside this 30 nm boundary for all CaV2.1s within the cluster, with overlapping area not counted twice27,28 (see Material and Methods). Our analysis revealed a reduction in number of gold particles per cluster (Fig 2D; Cacna2d1,2,3+/+ Ipsi: 4.25 ± 0.15, Cacna2d1,2,3+/+ Contra: 4.19 ± 0.24, Cacna2d1,2,3−/−: 3.13 ± 0.12 particles/cluster), however there was no change in the relative distribution of gold particles that were not clustered (Fig 2E). To determine CaV2.1 organization within the cluster we measured the mean clustered particle cohesion, the distance between one CaV2.1 gold particle and the next nearest neighboring CaV2.1 gold particle found in the cluster. We found no difference in mean cohesion between the Cacna2d1,2,3−/− vs Cacna2d1,2,3+/+ calyces (Fig 2F).Presynaptic α2δ1, α2δ2, and α2δ3 are positive regulators Munc13–1 levelsTo determine if α2δs controlled presynaptic AZ protein organization and CaV2.1 localization within the presynaptic AZ, we performed SDS-FFRIL with co-labeling of CaV2.1 and either RIM1/2 or Munc13–1, two core presynaptic AZ proteins38,39 (Fig 3). These two proteins regulate synaptic vesicle priming, the process of making synaptic vesicles fusion competent, and define release sites within the AZ, with RIM1/2 being closely correlated with AP-evoked release sites40,41. Analysis of RIM1/2 levels and clustering revealed no difference between the Cacna2d1,2,3−/− vs Cacna2d1,2,3+/+ calyces (Fig 3A–E). To determine CaV2.1 localization relative to RIM1/2, we performed Landmark Correlated Particle Index (LCPI) analysis to determine positional bias using Gold Rippler in the Gold In-and-Out (GIO) software package42. The LCPI is defined as the proportion of particles covered by the ripple divided by the proportion of P-face covered by the ripple (Fig 3F,G). Since we used 12 nm gold particles for CaV2.1 (30 nm uncertainty) and 6 nm gold particles for RIM1/2 (24 nm uncertainty), 54 nm reflects the combined uncertainty of CaV2.1 relative to RIM1/2. Results of the LCPI analysis revealed no difference in the relative distance between CaV2.1 and RIM1/2 (Fig 3H).Subsequently, we carried out SDS-FFRIL with Munc13–1 and CaV2.1 co-labeling (Fig 3I). In contrast to RIM1/2, we found that in Cacna2d1,2,3−/− calyces, Munc13–1 levels were reduced ~2 fold compared to Cacna2d1,2,3+/+ calyces (Fig 3J, Cacna2d1,2,3+/+: 16.35 ± 1.63, Cacna2d1,2,3−/−: 7.05 ± 0.63 particles/μm2). Analysis of Munc13–1 clustering revealed the number of Munc13–1 particles per cluster were slightly reduced (Fig 3K, Cacna2d1,2,3+/+: 3.70 ± 0.09, Cacna2d1,2,3−/−: 3.20 ± 0.13 particles) and there was an increase in unclustered Munc13–1 particles (Fig 3L, Cacna2d1,2,3+/+: 16.50% ± 1.11, Cacna2d1,2,3−/−: 24.99% ± 1.43), but with no change in Munc13–1 clustered particle cohesion (Fig 3M). However, despite the reduction in overall Munc13–1 levels, LCPI measurements revealed no difference in the relative distance between CaV2.1 and Munc13–1 (Fig 3N).Therefore, we conclude that presynaptic α2δs are not essential regulators of presynaptic CaV2.1 clustering and localization to the AZ, but they are essential regulators of presynaptic CaV2.1 abundance. Furthermore, presynaptic α2δs do not regulate CaV2.1 organization with respect to RIM1/2 and Munc13–1 in the presynaptic AZ. Finally, we reveal that presynaptic α2δ are specific regulators of Munc13–1 levels and organization in the presynaptic membrane, but not of RIM1/2.Presynaptic α2δ1, α2δ2, and α2δ3 are positive regulators of synaptic gain, but not synaptic vesicle release kineticsIn addition to CaV2 dependent mechanisms, synaptic strength is controlled by multiple CaV2 independent synaptic mechanisms. They are: 1) SV priming, the process of making SVs fusion competent, which controls the number SVs available for AP-evoked and AP-independent SV release43. 2) Coupling, the distance between the Ca2+ sensor of release and presynaptic CaV channels, which controls the number of SVs available and SV release kinetics for AP evoked release44. 3) Alignment of the presynaptic CaV and postsynaptic AMPARs, which controls EPSC kinetics45. 4) postsynaptic glutamate receptor abundance, which controls EPSC amplitude46. α2δs are proposed to control coupling, alignment, and glutamate receptor abundance30,47–49. Additionally, our finding of reduced Munc13–1 levels implies SV priming may be impacted. Therefore, we examined how AP-evoked release and AP-independent release were affected in the Cacna2d1,2,3−/− calyces.To probe AP-evoked release, we performed afferent fiber stimulation at three stimulation frequencies (100, 300, and 500 Hz) which represent a range of in vivo firing rates at the calyx, and recorded AMPAR EPSC currents from postsynaptic MNTB neurons innervated by either Cacna2d1/2/3+/+ or Cacna2d1/2/3−/− calyces in physiological [Ca2+] and temperature, 1.2 mM [Ca2+] and ~37 °C respectively. Analysis of the initial EPSC revealed a significant decrease in initial synaptic strength (Fig 4A,B, Cacna2d1,2,3+/+: 2.973 ± 0.5970 nA, Cacna2d1,2,3−/−: 0.1891 ± 0.0330 nA). Subsequently, we examined the EPSC waveform as SV release kinetics and release site alignment control EPSC waveform kinetics. We found no difference in the AP-evoked EPSC 10–90% rise time and full-width half-maximum (FWHM) between Cacna2d1/2/3−/− and Cacna2d1,2,3+/+ calyces (Fig 4C,D). By plotting EPSC amplitudes vs stimulus number at different frequencies we found that loss of α2δ1–3 led to changes in short-term plasticity. At all frequencies tested, we found frequency dependent facilitation throughout the stimulation train in Cacna2d1/2/3−/− calyces while Cacna2d1/2/3+/+ calyces exhibited facilitation early in the train and then depression (Fig 4F–K). Cacna2d1/2/3−/− calyces at 100 Hz and 300 Hz had similar steady state levels, while at 500 Hz lead to a 50% increase in relative steady state levels (Fig S4). The absolute steady state EPSC amplitudes of Cacna2d1/2/3−/− calyces at 100 and 300 Hz were (284.6 ± 67.0 pA and 352.3 ± 55.1 pA, respectively) while at 500 Hz they were increased (481.2 ± 117.0 pA).Since the α2δs are implied to be positive regulators of coupling47, we measured EPSC onset delay times and the FWHM. ESPC onset delay times, the time between the presynaptic AP and EPSC onset, are determined by coupling. They are indirect readouts of spatial coupling distances as short onset delays corresponds to tightly coupled SVs while loosely coupled SVs correspond to longer onset delay times50,51. FWHM can be used to indicate changes in SV release rates. Analysis of the initial EPSC onset times revealed no difference between the Cacna2d1/2/3−/− and Cacna2d1/2/3+/+ calyces (Fig 4E). Furthermore, EPSC onset delays did not increase as rapidly during train stimulation and the EPSC steady state onset delays were similar between Cacna2d1/2/3−/− and Cacna2d1/2/3+/+ calyces (Fig S4).Spontaneous release is largely considered CaV2 independent and is CaV2 independent at the calyx of Held52. Therefore, mEPSC frequency is interpreted as an indirect readout of SV priming. Since Munc13–1 regulates SV priming, reduced Munc13–1 levels could impact SV priming and contribute to a reduction in AP-evoked release in the Cacna2d1/2/3−/− calyces. To examine if reduced Munc13–1 levels impacted spontaneous release, we measured mEPSC frequencies. Our analysis revealed that mEPSC release rates increased in the absence of α2δ1–3 (Fig 4M, Cacna2d1,2,3+/+: 7.509 ± 2.211 Hz, Cacna2d1,2,3−/−: 15.02 ± 2.349 Hz). To rule out spontaneous release becoming CaV dependent in Cacna2d1/2/3−/− calyces, we analyzed mEPSC frequencies in the presence of Cd2+, a CaV1 and CaV2 blocker. We found that the addition of Cd2+ did not impact overall mEPSC frequences (Fig 4P, Cacna2d1,2,3−/− - Cd2+: 13.30 ± 5.080 Hz, + Cd2+: 10.02 ± 5.064 Hz). Although we cannot rule out other synaptic changes, we can conclude that lower Munc13–1 levels did not result in a reduction in the number of fusion competent SVs for AP-evoked release.In addition to presynaptic mechanisms regulating synaptic strength, postsynaptic changes in glutamate receptor abundance and localization regulate the quantal amplitude and postsynaptic current waveform. To explore if the loss of α2δ1–3 resulted in a change in postsynaptic glutamate receptor abundance, transsynaptic alignment between release sites, and glutamate receptor kinetics, we examined mEPSC amplitudes and kinetics. We found no difference in mEPSC amplitudes or 10–90% rise time between the Cacna2d1/2/3−/− and Cacna2d1/2/3+/+ calyces (Fig 4N,O). Therefore, we conclude that presynaptic α2δ1–3 do not alter glutamate receptor abundance, composition, and transsynaptic alignment between presynaptic CaV2 and postsynaptic glutamate receptors.

DiscussionOur findings are in contrast with current paradigms of α2δ function with respect to synapse development and maintenance10,11. Our phenotypes are not due to the timepoint of the presynaptic ablation or compensatory upregulation of Cacna2d4. Ablation of α2δ1–3 in GBCs at both ~E12.5/E13.5 and P1 resulted in a similar phenotype. In addition, presynaptic recordings as early as P9 after ablation at P1 resulted in a dramatic reduction in presynaptic CaV2 currents. Importantly, our manipulations all take place after the GBC axon has reached its target location, which allowed separation of axon outgrowth from synapse formation53,54. This is not the case in many of the previous studies18–20,55,56. Therefore, phenotypes previously attributed to presynaptic α2δs may be due to compensation for the loss of α2δ during axonal outgrowth. Cacna2d4 is not expressed in the auditory brainstem and was not detected in the absence of Cacna2d1,2,3. Presynaptic Cacna2d4 ablation in photoreceptors results in clear synaptic morphological deficits57,58 and are a result of the significant reduction of presynaptic CaV1. However, ablation of presynaptic CaV2 in CaV2 synapses does not impact synapse development, AZ ultrastructure, and synaptic AZ protein levels20,59. Studies from DRG neurons in a global Cacna2d1 KO animal resulted in a severe reduction in CaV2.2 with no effect on presynaptic ultrastructure60,61. Therefore, defects in CaV2 trafficking and function are not responsible for the phenotypes we observed.Presynaptic CaV2 abundance and coupling are key determinants of synaptic strength and plasticity35. Our data supports previous studies demonstrating that α2δs are positive regulators of CaV2 at the plasma membrane4,11. It is proposed that α2δs are positive regulators of presynaptic CaV2 coupling to SVs18,47. Based on this model, loss of α2δs would result in looser coupling and further distances between RIM1/2 and CaV2. Our findings are in contrast with current paradigms of α2δ function with respect to synapse development and maintenance10,11. Our phenotypes are not due to the timepoint of the presynaptic ablation or compensatory upregulation of Cacna2d4. Ablation of α2δ1–3 in GBCs at both ~E12.5/E13.5 and P1 resulted in a similar phenotype. In addition, presynaptic recordings as early as P9 after ablation at P1 resulted in a dramatic reduction in presynaptic CaV2 currents. Importantly, our manipulations all take place after the GBC axon has reached its target location, which allowed separation of axon outgrowth from synapse formation53,54. This is not the case in many of the previous studies18–20,55,56. Therefore, phenotypes previously attributed to presynaptic α2δs may be due to compensation for the loss of α2δ during axonal outgrowth. Cacna2d4 is not expressed in the auditory brainstem and was not detected in the absence of Cacna2d1,2,3. Presynaptic Cacna2d4 ablation in photoreceptors results in clear synaptic morphological deficits57,58 and are a result of the significant reduction of presynaptic CaV1. However, ablation of presynaptic CaV2 in CaV2 synapses does not impact synapse development, AZ ultrastructure, and synaptic AZ protein levels20,59. Studies from DRG neurons in a global Cacna2d1 KO animal resulted in a severe reduction in CaV2.2 with no effect on presynaptic ultrastructure60,61. Therefore, defects in CaV2 trafficking and function are not responsible for the phenotypes we observed.

Presynaptic CaV2 abundance and coupling are key determinants of synaptic strength and plasticity35. Our data supports previous studies demonstrating that α2δs are positive regulators of CaV2 at the plasma membrane4,11. It is proposed that α2δs are positive regulators of presynaptic CaV2 coupling to SVs18,47. DNA Construct and Recombinant Viral Vector ProductionHelper-dependent adenoviral vectors (HdAd) were produced as previously described26,31. HdAd vectors contained transgene cassettes that independently expressed a combination of Cre recombinase (Cre), EGFP, myristoylated EGFP (myrEGFP), or Mito-V5-APEX277 under the control of the 470 bp human synapsin promoter (syn). HdAd vectors expressing Cre, Mito-V5-APEX2, and EGFP were made from a previously constructed pdelta 28E4 plasmid that contained Mito-V5-APEX2 and EGFP31. This Mito-V5-APEX2 expression cassette was then cloned into a modified version of pdelta 28E4 using the AscI site, then further modified to contain separate syn EGFP and syn Cre expression cassettes to create a Mito-V5-APEX-Cre-EGFP construct26. HdAd vectors containing only Cre and EGFP or myrEGFP were created as above, but with the syn Cre expression cassette inserted into the AscI site. The final plasmids were then linearized using the PmeI enzyme and transfected (Profection Mammalian Transfection System, Promega) into 116 producer cells79. Helper virus (HV) was added the subsequent day. Forty-eight hours post infection, when cytopathic effects had occurred, cells went through three freeze/thaw cycles for lysis to release the viral particles. If needed, lysate could be amplified up to five times via serial coinfection with the HdAd and HV to increase HdAd titer. Stereotactic Viral Vector InjectionsDelivery of HdAd vectors was performed as previously described in established protocols80–82. Postnatal day 1 (P1) pups from Cacna2d1,2,3 CKO mice were anesthetized by a 5 min immersion into an ice bath. They were subsequently secured on a chilled aluminum block and checked throughout the surgery for anesthetic depth by tail pinch. Surgery was performed in an aseptic environment, using pulled pipettes with a 20 μm opening (Sutter Instruments, P1000), 2 μl of HdAd in storage buffer (containing in mM: 250 sucrose, 10 HEPES, 1 MgCl2 at a pH of 7.4) with 6.6% mannitol was injected into the Ventral Cochlear Nucleus (vCN) at a flow rate of 1 μl/min. The total amount of virus did not exceed 2*109 viral particles. The pipette was left in place for 1 min afterwards to release backpressure. Animals then recovered under a warming lamp (37 °C) until ambulatory and responsive to tail pinch and were then returned to their home cage. If animals did not recover they were euthanized, and if they presented as distressed at any later timepoint veterinary staff were consulted, and their directions followed.
