BACKGROUND: Metabolic adaptation is a crucial driver of melanoma progression and
therapy resistance. The pseudokinase Tribbles homolog 2 (TRIB2) is highly
expressed in melanoma and is associated with poor outcomes and treatment
resistance. However, its specific role in the metabolic profile of melanoma
cells remained unexplored. This study aimed to investigate the impact of TRIB2
on cellular metabolism, and its role in metabolic adaptation in melanoma.
MATERIALS AND METHODS: The study used UACC-62 human melanoma cells with high
endogenous TRIB2 expression and isogenic TRIB2-knockout (KO) clones generated
via CRISPR-Cas9. Cell proliferation was assessed using trypan blue exclusion
assays under treatment with metabolic drugs including 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG), and
dichloroacetate (DCA). Metabolic phenotypes were evaluated by measuring glucose
consumption, lactate production, and real-time analysis of glycolytic flux
(ECAR) and mitochondrial respiration (OCR) using a Seahorse bioanalyzer.
Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured by dihydroethidium (DHE)
fluorescence. Gene expression was analyzed by qPCR, and protein levels were
determined by western blot. Citrate synthase activity was measured
enzymatically.
RESULTS: TRIB2-KO cells exhibited significantly reduced basal proliferation
compared with wild-type (WT) cells. KO cells were also more sensitive to the
glycolytic inhibitor 2-DG. Conversely, WT cells were more susceptible to DCA
treatment. Metabolic analyses confirmed that TRIB2-KO cells had lower glucose
consumption, lactate production, and glycolytic capacity than WT cells.
Mitochondrial respiratory function and the protein levels of key respiratory
complexes were unchanged. Despite the reduction in glycolytic activity, TRIB2
depletion did not increase mitochondrial respiratory capacity and was associated
with elevated basal intracellular superoxide levels. Among the molecular
alterations identified, PDK4 was consistently reduced at both the mRNA and
protein levels, whereas citrate synthase activity was significantly decreased
despite preserved protein abundance, indicating selective alterations in
mitochondrial carbon metabolism.
CONCLUSIONS: TRIB2 expression promotes a glycolytic phenotype and contributes to
metabolic adaptation in melanoma. Loss of TRIB2 does not induce a metabolic
shift towards oxidative phosphorylation but instead results in broader metabolic
rewiring characterized by reduced glycolysis together with selective alterations
in mitochondrial carbon metabolism. These findings identify the TRIB2-PDK4 axis
as a previously unrecognized component of metabolic regulation in melanoma and
provide a rationale for further investigating its therapeutic potential.

We next evaluated citrate synthase, a key enzyme of the TCA cycle. CS mRNA levels were reduced in TRIB2-KO cells, whereas protein abundance remained comparable to WT cells (Fig 4D). However, citrate synthase enzymatic activity was significantly decreased in both TRIB2-KO clones (Fig 4E).

Additional proteins involved in glycolysis, including HK1, HK2, LDHA and PKM2, showed no detectable differences between WT and TRIB2-KO cells (S3 Fig).

Functionally, these metabolic changes were accompanied by increased sensitivity of TRIB2-deficient cells to glycolytic inhibition by 2-deoxy-D-glucose, whereas TRIB2-expressing cells were more sensitive to dichloroacetate. Together, these findings suggest that TRIB2 supports the metabolic flexibility required to sustain melanoma cell proliferation.

Melanoma exhibits remarkable metabolic plasticity, allowing tumour cells to dynamically adapt their bioenergetic state according to nutrient availability, oncogenic signalling and therapeutic pressure [2,26]. Rather than relying exclusively on aerobic glycolysis, melanoma cells can exploit both glycolytic and oxidative pathways, and alterations in this balance have been associated with tumour progression and therapeutic response [2,27]. In agreement with this concept, our data identify TRIB2 as a novel contributor to melanoma metabolic adaptation by promoting glucose utilization and lactate production.

To date, only one study has directly linked TRIB2 to glucose metabolism. Liu et al. reported that TRIB2 promotes aerobic glycolysis in lung cancer cells through interaction with PKM2, increasing both glucose uptake and lactate secretion [18]. Our results independently support a pro-glycolytic role for TRIB2, as TRIB2-expressing melanoma cells also exhibited higher glucose consumption and lactate production than TRIB2-KO cells. However, unlike Liu et al., we did not detect changes in PKM2 protein abundance, nor in LDHA, HK1 or HK2 protein levels. These differences most likely reflect tumour-specific metabolic regulation and suggest that, in melanoma, TRIB2 promotes glycolysis through mechanisms distinct from the PKM2-dependent pathway described in lung cancer.

Interestingly, despite the clear reduction in glycolytic activity, TRIB2 depletion did not result in a compensatory increase in mitochondrial respiration. Neither basal nor maximal oxygen consumption, ATP-linked respiration nor spare respiratory capacity differed between WT and TRIB2-KO cells, indicating that loss of TRIB2 does not simply redirect glucose-derived carbon towards oxidative phosphorylation. This observation is consistent with the current view that metabolic adaptation in melanoma is not governed by a binary switch between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, but rather by coordinated regulation of multiple interconnected metabolic pathways [2,27]. Indeed, previous studies have shown that glycolytic activity and mitochondrial respiration frequently coexist in melanoma cells and may be regulated independently according to the genetic and metabolic context [28,29].

Although the increase in proton leak did not reach statistical significance, both TRIB2-KO clones consistently displayed higher basal superoxide levels. Proton leak and mitochondrial ROS production are closely interconnected, and modest alterations in proton conductance can modify mitochondrial redox homeostasis depending on the metabolic state rather than following a simple linear relationship [30]. Therefore, our data suggest that TRIB2 depletion primarily affects basal cellular redox homeostasis without inducing major alterations in respiratory function.

To further investigate the molecular basis underlying the metabolic phenotype associated with TRIB2 depletion, we analysed the expression of several genes and proteins involved in glycolysis, pyruvate metabolism and the tricarboxylic acid cycle. Among all the molecules examined, the most consistent molecular alteration identified in TRIB2-deficient cells was the reduction of PDK4, observed at both the mRNA and protein levels. PDK4 is one of the four pyruvate dehydrogenase kinases that regulate the activity of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC), thereby controlling the entry of pyruvate into the tricarboxylic acid cycle. However, the contribution of individual PDK isoforms is highly context-dependent, and substantial functional redundancy exists between family members [31–33].

Despite the marked reduction in PDK4 expression, PDHA phosphorylation was modestly increased in both knockout clones. Since PDC activity is determined by the integrated action of all PDK isoforms together with pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatases, rather than by the abundance of a single kinase [31,32], this observation may reflect compensatory regulation by other PDK family members.

Our observations are also consistent with previous findings indicating that the metabolic consequences of PDK4 modulation cannot always be explained solely by changes in PDH phosphorylation. Tiersma et al. demonstrated that selective downregulation of individual PDK isoforms, including PDK4, did not produce proportional changes in PDH phosphorylation in melanoma cells, highlighting the robustness and functional redundancy of PDC regulation [34].

Beyond its canonical role in regulating PDH, increasing evidence indicates that PDK4 exerts additional biological functions. In KRAS-driven tumour models, PDK4 depletion impaired cell proliferation and altered KRAS signalling independently of PDH activity, supporting PDH-independent roles in tumour cell metabolism and proliferation [35]. Likewise, PDK4 has been shown to directly phosphorylate septin 2 (SEPT2), thereby regulating DRP1-dependent mitochondrial dynamics independently of PDH phosphorylation [36]. In addition, PDK4 has been implicated in the modulation of ERK, SRC and JNK signalling pathways in bladder cancer cells, further supporting functions unrelated to its classical control of pyruvate oxidation [37].

Although our study does not address these mechanisms directly, these observations provide a plausible framework to explain why reduced PDK4 expression is associated with a clear metabolic phenotype despite preserved regulation of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. Together, these findings suggest that TRIB2 regulates melanoma metabolism, at least in part, through modulation of PDK4. However, the downstream consequences of PDK4 depletion are likely to extend beyond the canonical regulation of PDH phosphorylation.

Interestingly, analysis of publicly available Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) datasets from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) [38] further supports the biological relevance of this relationship, as TRIB2 and PDK4 expression positively correlate across several tumour types, including melanoma, glioma, acute myeloid leukaemia, pancreatic adenocarcinoma and endometrial carcinoma (S4–S5 Figs). Although these correlations do not establish a causal relationship, they indicate that the association between TRIB2 and PDK4 extends beyond the UACC-62 melanoma model and supports the potential biological relevance of the TRIB2–PDK4 axis across multiple tumour types.

A second major molecular alteration was the reduction in citrate synthase activity observed in TRIB2-KO cells. Although CS transcript levels were decreased and protein abundance remained unchanged, enzymatic activity was significantly reduced. Citrate synthase catalyses the first committed step of the tricarboxylic acid cycle and its activity is widely used as a surrogate marker of mitochondrial content and oxidative capacity [39]. This dissociation between protein abundance and enzymatic activity suggests regulation beyond protein expression, potentially through post-translational or metabolic mechanisms that were not addressed in the present work. Accordingly, the reduced citrate synthase activity most likely reflects functional metabolic remodelling rather than decreased mitochondrial content, consistent with the preserved respiratory capacity and unchanged abundance of representative mitochondrial proteins observed in our study.

Taken together, the two most consistent molecular alterations identified in TRIB2-deficient cells—reduced PDK4 expression and decreased citrate synthase activity— both converge on altered regulation of mitochondrial carbon metabolism despite the preservation of mitochondrial respiratory function.

Our functional studies further indicate that TRIB2 expression influences the response of melanoma cells to metabolic therapies. TRIB2-deficient cells were more sensitive to glycolytic inhibition by 2-deoxy-D-glucose, whereas TRIB2-expressing cells displayed greater sensitivity to dichloroacetate. These findings reinforce the concept that metabolic dependencies differ according to the metabolic phenotype of tumour cells and suggest that TRIB2 expression may contribute to defining distinct metabolic vulnerabilities. Although these observations require validation in additional melanoma models and in vivo studies, they support the rationale for considering TRIB2 as a potential biomarker to guide metabolism-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Collectively, our findings indicate that TRIB2 depletion does not simply trigger a metabolic shift from glycolysis towards oxidative metabolism. Instead, loss of TRIB2 promotes a broader metabolic rewiring characterized by reduced glycolytic activity together with selective alterations in mitochondrial carbon metabolism, while preserving overall mitochondrial respiratory capacity. This metabolic phenotype is supported by the coordinated reduction in PDK4 expression and citrate synthase activity, identifying the TRIB2–PDK4 axis as a previously unrecognized component of metabolic regulation in melanoma. Although the molecular mechanisms linking TRIB2 to PDK4 remain to be elucidated, our findings provide a framework for future studies aimed at defining how TRIB2 controls metabolic adaptation in melanoma and whether this regulatory axis represents a therapeutic vulnerability in other tumor types.
