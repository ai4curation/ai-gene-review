## Full Text

Abstract

The expression of adhesion molecules on vascular endothelial cells determines the pattern of migration and extravasation of leucocytes in inflammation and immunity. Here we show that costimulation with CD40 ligand (CD40L) and interleukin (IL)-4 (or IL-13) gives rise to a unique pattern of adhesion molecule expression by human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC). CD40 ligation alone enhanced expression of vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), intracellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) and E-selectin whereas IL-4 and IL-13 increased expression of VCAM-1 and P-selectin but not ICAM-1 or E-selectin. When IL-4 and CD40L were combined there was an additional increase of both VCAM-1 and P-selectin, but ICAM-1 and E-selectin were both inhibited. The combined effects of IL-4 and CD40L signalling were not the result of altered response kinetics, enhanced sensitivity of the endothelium, or increased expression of CD40 or the IL-4 receptor. The rise in VCAM-1 expression induced by combined IL-4 and CD40L stimulation was slower and more sustained than with tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and occurred only on a subset (75–80%) of the endothelial cell population compared to 100% with TNF-α. Costimulation with IL-4 and CD40L increased adhesion of T cells and B cells above levels obtained with either signal alone, but decreased adhesion of neutrophils. Furthermore, CD40 and IL-4 synergistically increased IL-6 but decreased IL-8 production by HUVEC. These results show that interactions between IL-4 and CD40 on endothelial cells give rise to specific patterns of adhesion molecule expression and cytokine production that may have important implications for lymphocyte and neutrophil migration and function at sites of inflammation.

Introduction

Invasion with an infectious organism or foreign substance elicits a complex but co-ordinated set of defence mechanisms in the host that include inflammation together with innate and adaptive immunity. Vascular endothelial cells are central to both immune and inflammatory processes. In response to a variety of stimuli they undergo dramatic changes in their cytoskeletal structure, expression and activation of cell adhesion molecules, thrombotic and coagulant properties, and permeability to plasma proteins. 1 – 4 Adhesion of leucocytes to vascular endothelial cells is particularly important because it ultimately determines the number, phenotype and function of immune cells that arrive at sites of inflammation. The regulation of endothelial cell–leucocyte interactions is mediated by complex mechanisms involving both cytokines and surface adhesion and costimulatory molecules. Of these, there is now good evidence that CD40 has a critical role in activation of vascular endothelial cells and adhesion of leucocytes. 5

CD40 is a transmembrane signalling protein expressed on the surface of B cells, monocytes, dendritic cells and endothelial cells. 5 , 6 Cellular responses induced by ligation of CD40 are important in both inflammation and immunity. CD40-dependent T-cell activation of B cells is required for immunoglobulin switching, affinity maturation and the generation of B-cell memory. 6 In addition, CD40 activation of monocytes and dendritic cells results in release of interleukin (IL)-12, which is required for T helper 1 (Th1) activation and production of proinflammatory cytokines. 7 CD40 is expressed on vascular endothelium, both in vitro and in vivo, 8 , 9 and its expression is modulated by the proinflammatory cytokines tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), IL-1 and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and by bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS). 9 Ligation of CD40 on endothelial cells leads to production of proinflammatory cytokines 10 and enhanced expression of CD54 (intracellular adhesion molecule-1 [ICAM-1]), CD62E (E-selectin) and CD106 (vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 [VCAM-1]) with a consequent increase in leucocyte binding. 8 , 9 These effects identify CD40 as a proinflammatory signalling molecule on endothelial cells, consistent with a pivotal role in inflammation. CD40 is therefore an important regulatory molecule that links adaptive immunity and inflammation.

The cytokines IL-4 and IL-13 also have potent effects on endothelial cells, but their action is quite different to that of the proinflammatory cytokines and to CD40 ligand (CD40L). Both IL-4 and IL-13 cause endothelial cells to adopt a distinct cuboidal morphology and then to migrate into aggregates. 4 , 11 , 12 They also selectively increase expression of VCAM-1, but not ICAM-1 or E-selectin, and enhance lymphocyte and monocyte adhesion. 11 – 14 IL-4 and IL-13 are produced by T helper 2 (Th2) cells, required for humoral and mucosal immune responses, and both cytokines inhibit production of proinflammatory cytokines by monocytes. 15 , 16 They are also potent activators of B cells and induce immunoglobulin heavy chain switching to immunoglobulin E (IgE) production. 17 – 19 The complex biological interactions between the proinflammatory signal of CD40 and anti-inflammatory signals of IL-4 and IL-13 indicate an important relationship between them for regulating inflammation as well as adaptive immunity. In this study, we investigated the interactions between CD40 and IL-4 for endothelial cell activation. Our results show that interplay between these two signalling molecules uniquely regulates adhesion molecule expression and production of cytokines by endothelial cells responsible for selective leucocyte adhesion, migration and activation.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Purified recombinant IL-4 (specific activity 1·8 × 10 7 U/mg) was provided by the Sterling Drug Company Inc. (Malvern, PA). IL-4 at 100 U/ml (5·6 ng/ml) was defined as the concentration required for maximum proliferation of human B cells costimulated with anti-immunoglobulin M (IgM) sepharose beads or CD40L. IL-13 was the kind gift of Dr A. Minty (Sanofi Biorecherches, Labège, France). TNF-α (specific activity 40 IU/ng), IFN-γ (specific activity 20 IU/ng) and IL-1α (specific activity 50 IU/ng) were purchased from R & D Systems (Abingdon, Oxon, UK). Tissue culture media MCDB131 and RPMI-1640, Puck's saline A, fetal calf serum (FCS) and collagenase II were purchased from Life Technologies (Paisley, Strathclyde, UK). l -glutamine, endothelial cell attachment factor (ECAF) and endothelial cell growth factor (ECGF) were purchased from Sigma (Poole, Dorset, UK). Antibody to P-selectin (CD62P) clone G1, m immunoglobulin G1 (mIgG1), was obtained from the VI Leucocyte Differentiation Workshop. Soluble recombinant human CD40L trimer and CD124 (anti-IL-4 receptor α [anti-IL-4Rα]) monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), m56 and m57, were kindly supplied by Dr Richard Armitage (Immunex, Seattle, WA). Mouse anti-human VCAM-1 (1.G11B1, IgG1), ICAM-1 (84H10, IgG1), CD62E (1·2B6, IgG1), CD3-phycoerythrin (PE), CD15-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and irrelevant Fc-matched control mAbs for flow cytometry and tissue culture were purchased from Serotec (Kidlington, Oxon, UK). CD19-PE mAb was from Becton-Dickinson (Oxford, UK); FITC- and PE-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) F(ab′) 2 were from Dako (High Wycombe, Bucks., UK). Cytokine pair mAbs for enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10 and TNF-α) were from R & D Systems; streptavidin-conjugated horseradish peroxidase was from Amersham (Little Chalfont, Bucks., UK); 3,3′,5,5′ tetramethylbenzidine dihydrochloride (TMB) substrate and bovine serum albumin (BSA) were from Sigma; and High Bind immunoassay plates were from Costar (High Wycombe, Bucks., UK).

Cell culture and adhesion assays

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) were prepared as previously described. 4 Briefly, endothelial cells were removed from human umbilical cords by collagenase II digestion, resuspended in MCDB131 medium with 20% FCS and antibiotics, and grown in primary cultures for 24–48 hr until they reached 90–95% confluence. For flow cytometry and leucocyte adhesion assays, HUVEC monolayers from primary cultures were dispersed with trypsin/EDTA and recultured onto ECAF-coated tissue culture plates in MCDB131 medium with 20% FCS, ECGF and antibiotics. Cultures were grown for 24–48 hr until confluent, when ECGF-containing medium was removed and medium containing cytokines and/or antibodies or leucocytes was added for the times indicated. For antibody staining, HUVEC monolayers were incubated with modified Puck's saline A, resuspended by gentle mechanical action, washed immediately in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 5% FCS/0·02% NaN 3 and incubated with primary mAbs followed, after a further wash, with either FITC- or PE-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG, as required. The fluorescence data were acquired and analysed on a Becton-Dickinson fluorescence-activated cell sorter (FACScalibur) using cellquest software. In some experiments, the results were expressed as median fluorescence intensity (MFI). In other experiments, calibrated FITC-conjugated beads (Dako) were used to calculate the molecules of equivalent soluble fluorochrome (MESF), which allows direct comparison between experiments.

For leucocyte adhesion assays, peripheral blood leucocytes were prepared by collection of whole blood from normal healthy donors into heparin (10 IU/ml). The suspension was diluted twofold in RPMI-1640 medium and centrifuged at 200 g for 10 min. The red cell/leucocyte pellet was washed again in the same manner then resuspended in 10 volumes of red blood cell lysing medium (150 m m NH 4 Cl, 20 m m NaHCO 3 , 1 m m EDTA). Red cells were allowed to lyse for 10 min at room temperature, centrifuged at 200 g for 7 min and then washed twice in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FCS. The leucocyte pellet was resuspended in endothelial cell culture medium at a concentration of 2 × 10 6 cells/ml. To measure leucocyte adhesion to endothelial cells, 4 × 10 6 leucocytes (in 2 ml of endothelial cell culture medium) were added to endothelial cells in 24-well plates. Plates were placed on a rotary shaker to create flow and incubated for 1 hr at 37° in 5% CO 2 in air. Non-adherent cells were removed by aspiration of the supernatant and a further two washes. Endothelial cells and adherent leucocytes were dispersed by incubation in modified Puck's saline A, pH 7·2, supplemented with 0·2% EDTA and 10% FCS, for 15–20 min at room temperature, as described previously. 11 Both adherent and non-adherent cell fractions were washed and centrifuged at 200 g for 7 min then resuspended in equal volumes of PBS containing 5% FCS and 0·02% NaN 3 . Both fractions were stained for T cells (CD3), B cells (CD19) and neutrophils (CD15), and analysed on a FACScalibur.

Cytokine ELISA assays

Immunoassay plates were coated with anti-cytokine capture mAb (4 µg/ml) in PBS containing 0·05% Tween-20 and incubated at room temperature overnight. After washing, the plates were blocked with 1% BSA, 5% sucrose and 0·05% NaN 3 in PBS and incubated at room temperature for 1 hr. Samples and standards were added to the wells in a diluent buffer of Tris-buffered saline containing 0·1% BSA and 0·05% Tween-20, and incubated for 2 hr at room temperature. Bound cytokine was detected following incubation with cytokine-specific biotinlylated mAbs (200 ng/ml) in diluent buffer for 2 hr at room temperature followed by incubation with streptavidin-conjugated horseradish peroxidase (1 : 1000 in diluent buffer) for 20 min at room temperature and then addition of TMB substrate, in 0·05 m phosphate citrate buffer containing H 2 O 2 and 1 µg/ml of TMB. Colour development was stopped with 4N H 2 SO 4 and read at 450 nm on a Dynatech ELISA plate reader. Plates were washed three times between each step with PBS containing 0·05% Tween-20.

Results

Endothelial cell adhesion molecule expression in response to costimulation with IL-4 and CD40L

Adhesion molecule expression on HUVEC was determined following 24 hr of stimulation with 100 U/ml of IL-4 (or IL-13), 1 ng/ml of CD40L, or a combination of IL-4 (or IL-13) plus CD40L. The effects of IL-4 and CD40L were also compared with the classical proinflammatory cytokine, TNF-α. Concentrations of IL-4 and CD40L used were optimal for HUVEC activation, as determined in preliminary experiments. IL-4, IL-13 and CD40L alone all increased VCAM-1 expression by up to fivefold above background levels ( Fig. 1 ). When endothelial cells were stimulated with a combination of IL-4 (or IL-13) and CD40L, VCAM-1 expression was increased even further ( Fig. 1a , 1b ). In some experiments, VCAM-1 expression induced by costimulation with IL-4 (or IL-13) and CD40L was greater than that with TNF-α alone, but this was not a statistically significant finding ( Fig. 1b ).

Figure 1.

In time-response experiments, individual responses to IL-4 and CD40L, as measured by increased MFI, were very similar. Response was maximal at 24 hr and had decreased by 48 hr ( Fig. 2 ). Costimulation with IL-4 and CD40L typically resulted in substantially higher VCAM-1 expression at 24 hr, which was not diminished by 48 hr. Essentially the same result was obtained when the percentage of VCAM-1-positive cells, rather than MFI, was measured. However, it is noteworthy that the maximum percentage of positive cells was never greater than 70–80% in response to IL-4 and CD40L, even at the peak of the response, with a typical high and low staining distribution that was confirmed by immunogold staining (data not shown). This is different from the response to TNF-α, which typically induces maximum VCAM-1 expression after 6 hr of incubation ( Fig. 2 ) and on 100% of cultured cells.

Figure 2.

CD40 ligation did not increase the sensitivity of HUVEC to IL-4 or increase IL-4R expression, and IL-4 did not increase CD40 expression

The costimulatory effects of IL-4 and CD40L on HUVEC could have been caused by a reduction in the threshold response to IL-4 by, for example, increasing expression of the IL-4R. Alternatively, IL-4 activation of endothelial cells may have increased the expression of CD40 as it does on B cells. To investigate these possible mechanisms, VCAM-1 expression was measured in response to CD40L and IL-4 at concentrations from 0 to 180 U/ml ( Fig. 3 ). At concentrations of 0·2 U/ml and higher, IL-4 by itself increased VCAM-1 expression in a dose-dependent manner, and further increased expression induced by CD40L. In these experiments, 50% maximum expression was obtained with IL-4 at a concentration of 3–4 U/ml, whether or not CD40L was present. No effect was observed with IL-4 below concentrations of 0·2 U/ml, either with or without CD40L. This experiment shows that the threshold of activation by IL-4 is not lowered by CD40L. Furthermore, there was no increase in the constitutive expression of CD40 on endothelial cells in response to IL-4, whereas a two- to threefold increase was observed with TNF-α, IL-1 and IFN-γ ( Table 1 ). Similarly, expression of IL-4R α-chain (CD124) on cultured endothelial cells was not increased by activation with CD40L, as measured by flow cytometry ( Table 2 ) and by immunogold staining (results not shown).

Costimulation of HUVEC with IL-4 and CD40L enhances expression of P-selectin and VCAM-1 but inhibits expression of ICAM-1 and E-selectin

The combined action of IL-4 and CD40L on VCAM-1 expression suggested that other adhesion molecules might be regulated in a similar manner to enhance leucocyte adhesion and extravasation. The effect of costimulation on P-selectin (CD62P), E-selectin (CD62E) and ICAM-1 (CD54) was therefore examined as each of these have been reported previously to be increased on activation by either CD40L or IL-4. P-selectin expression on endothelial cells was increased during culture with IL-4 but not with CD40L ( Fig. 4a ). Costimulation with IL-4 and CD40L further increased P-selectin expression to a greater extent than that induced by IL-4 alone. The kinetics of this response were similar to those observed for VCAM-1 expression: very little P-selectin detected before 6 hr and maximal expression occurring from 24 to 48 hr. Although surface P-selectin expression was usually low in comparison to VCAM-1, the increase of expression in response to CD40L and IL-4 was significant and repeatable over six different experiments.

Figure 4.

Previous studies have shown that ICAM-1 expression on endothelial cells is enhanced by inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α. In our experiments, CD40L greatly increased ICAM-1 expression but IL-4 did not ( Fig. 4b ). Rather, IL-4 decreased the basal levels of ICAM-1 and profoundly inhibited ICAM-1 expression induced by CD40L. The kinetics of this response showed a steady increase over 48 hr in the amount of ICAM-1 induced by CD40L, which was inhibited by IL-4 at each time-point ( Fig. 4b ). E-selectin expression was also inhibited by IL-4 ( Fig. 4c ). In this case, constitutive levels were generally lower than those of either VCAM-1 or ICAM-1 and CD40L increased its expression at 6 hr but not at 24 or 48 hr. IL-4 inhibited E-selectin induced by CD40L and the constitutive (background) expression of E-selectin observed at 6 hr. At 24 and 48 hr, E-selectin expression was lost and the effect of IL-4 was no longer apparent. These effects of IL-4 on ICAM-1 and E-selectin expression were observed consistently over more than five experiments.

CD40L and IL-4 differentially regulate adhesion of neutrophils and lymphocytes to endothelial cells

The combined effects of IL-4 and CD40L on VCAM-1 and P-selectin expression, and the inhibitory effects of IL-4 on ICAM-1 and E-selectin expression, suggest that these signals may combine in different ways to control adhesion and migration of leucocyte populations. To investigate this aspect further, peripheral blood leucocytes were incubated on endothelial cell monolayers that had been activated for 18 hr with CD40L, IL-4 or a combination of CD40L and IL-4. After culture for 1 hr on a rotary shaker to induce a shear force, non-adherent cells were removed by gentle washing and the endothelial cells with adherent leucocytes attached were harvested for FACS analysis. Similar results were obtained in each of five such experiments ( Fig. 5 ). CD40L activation of endothelial cells increased adherence of T cells by ≈ 10-fold, B cells by up to 20-fold and neutrophils by 20–30 fold. IL-4 alone increased adhesion of T cells and B cells but had little or no effect on neutrophils. Costimulation with IL-4 and CD40L significantly increased adherence of T cells and B cells, and reduced the adhesion of neutrophils induced by activation with CD40L alone ( Fig. 5 ). These results provide a functional correlate to the differential regulation of VCAM-1, ICAM-1, E-selectin and P-selectin on endothelial cells by CD40L and IL-4. They also indicate an important distinction between CD40-mediated inflammatory signals and IL-4 anti-inflammatory signals on the nature of leucocyte adhesion to endothelial cells.

Figure 5.

IL-4 and CD40L combine to enhance IL-6 and inhibit IL-8 production by HUVEC

Endothelial-derived cytokines are important for leucocyte homing and transmigration. To investigate whether IL-4 and CD40 signalling influenced endothelial cytokine production, HUVEC were incubated for 24 hr with medium alone, or with IL-4, CD40L, or IL-4 plus CD40L. Supernatants were harvested and assayed, by ELISA, for IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10 and TNF-α. In these experiments, IL-4, but not CD40L, induced IL-6 production ( Fig. 6a ). Costimulation with IL-4 and CD40L, however, further increased IL-6 production, in some experiments by as much as 30 times ( P < 0·05). In contrast, CD40L, but not IL-4, induced IL-8 production ( Fig. 6b ). Moreover, IL-4 greatly inhibited IL-8 production induced by CD40L to levels below those induced by CD40L alone ( P < 0·05). All culture supernatants assayed were negative for IL-1, IL-10 and TNF-α.

Discussion

Leucocyte adhesion to endothelium and extravasation at sites of inflammation is a two-step process. Low-avidity binding by sialyted and fucosylated oligosaccharides and members of the selectin (CD62) family results initially in slowing down (rolling) of passing leucocytes. This is then followed by firm adhesion and transmigration mediated by activated β1 and β2 integrins and members of the immunoglobulin supergene family, particularly VCAM-1 and ICAM-1. 1 , 2 The migration of leucocytes, such as neutrophils, lymphocytes and monocytes, to sites of inflammation is therefore determined by the expression of adhesion molecules which, in turn, are regulated by signals mediated by surface costimulatory molecules and by cytokines.

CD40 is an important costimulatory molecule expressed on endothelial cells as well as B cells, monocytes and dendritic cells. Activation of endothelial cells by CD40 increases surface expression of VCAM-1, ICAM-1 and E-selectin (required for neutrophil and lymphocyte adhesion) and induces the production of cytokines involved in inflammation. 8 , 10 CD40L also induces production of the neutrophil chemoattractant IL-8 by endothelial cells ( Fig. 5 ), consistent with an important role for CD40 in inflammatory responses. TNF-α and IL-1 induce the same adhesion molecules as CD40L, and use similar signalling cascades for adhesion molecule expression. 20 IL-4, on the other hand, has quite different effects. VCAM-1 expression on endothelial cells is increased by IL-4, but ICAM-1 and E-selectin are not. 21 Endothelial cells also adopt a completely different morphology in response to IL-4 compared with TNF-α or IFN-γ. 4 In the present study we show that IL-4 modifies the endothelial cell response to CD40L by inhibiting neutrophil, and promoting lymphocyte, adhesion and migration.

In our experiments, costimulation with IL-4 (or IL-13) and CD40L was found to increase VCAM-1 and P-selectin expression to levels well above those induced by either IL-4 or CD40 alone and, in many experiments, a synergistic increase was observed ( Figs 1 and 4 ). The enhanced effect of costimulation with IL-4 and CD40L cannot be accounted for by a change in the kinetics of the response ( Fig. 2 ) or by increased expression of CD40 or the IL-4R ( Table 1 ). There is also no evidence for a decrease in the threshold of CD40 activation ( Fig. 3 ). IL-4 is known to increase VCAM-1 protein levels by stabilization of VCAM-1 mRNA 22 but whether this or some other mechanism is responsible for the synergistic increase in expression with CD40L is not known. It is noteworthy that the level of VCAM-1 expression (MFI) induced by CD40L and IL-4 was usually as high or even higher than that obtained with TNF-α, but the increase was only ever seen on ≈ 75–80% of cells compared to 100% with TNF-α (data not shown). Microscopic examination showed that this result was not caused by the presence of non-endothelial cells. It is not known whether this staining pattern is detecting a real subset of endothelial cells or whether it is related to some other feature, such as stage of cell cycle. It would be interesting to compare HUVEC with endothelial cells from other tissues for IL-4- and CD40L-induced VCAM-1 expression.

The additional increase in P-selectin expression obtained by costimulation with IL-4 and CD40L has not been previously described. P-selectin is normally stored in the α-granules of platelets and in the Weibel-Palade bodies of endothelial cells. 23 – 26 Agonists, such as thrombin, histamine, LPS and complement components, induce rapid (within 30 min) mobilization of P-selectin to the plasma membrane where it is important for initial tethering and rolling of neutrophils. 27 – 31 In contrast to this very rapid and short-lived response, stimulation with IL-4, or IL-4 and CD40, gave a sustained increase in P-selectin lasting for longer than 48 hr. A similar P-selectin response has previously been reported for IL-4 and for oncostatin M (OSM) where P-selectin mRNA and protein expression by endothelial cells persisted for up to 72 hr. 31 Rapid P-selectin expression and binding to its ligand, P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1; CD62L), is a major tethering mechanism for neutrophils, but PSGL-1 is also present on haematopoietic cells, myeloid lineages, dendritic cells, monocytes, stimulated T cells and in low numbers on B cells. 32 The sustained expression of P-selectin induced by IL-4 and CD40L may therefore be important for long-term recruitment of lymphocytes and other cells to sites of inflammation. Indeed, sustained expression of P-selectin is found in chronic inflammatory conditions, such as rheumatoid arthritis, and in atherosclerotic plaques where CD40 and CD40L are also expressed. 33 – 36

In contrast to its stimulatory effects on CD40L-induced VCAM-1 and P-selectin expression, IL-4 significantly inhibited ICAM-1 and E-selectin ( Fig. 4 ). This finding is consistent with a previous report describing IL-4 inhibition of ICAM-1 and E-selectin expression induced by TNF-α. 21 Furthermore, it has been shown that IL-4-induced STAT6 does not activate E-selectin transcription. Instead it suppresses TNF-α-induced expression of the E-selectin gene by competing for binding to a region in the gene promoter containing overlapping STAT6 and NF-κB binding sites. 37 CD40 is known to activate NF-κB 38 and is thought to stimulate endothelial cell adhesion molecule expression through similar signalling pathways as TNF-α. 20 It is therefore probable that IL-4 acts in a similar manner to inhibit CD40L-induced E-selectin and ICAM-1.

The alternative regulation of VCAM-1 and P-selectin on the one hand, and ICAM-1 and E-selectin on the other, in response to CD40L and IL-4, was reflected in the pattern of leucocyte adhesion ( Fig. 5 ). CD40L activation of endothelial cells enhanced both lymphocyte and neutrophil adhesion, as did TNF-α. IL-4 further increased T- and B-cell adhesion to CD40-activated endothelial cells but consistently inhibited adhesion of neutrophils. This finding is concordant with previous work showing that IL-4 alone promotes adhesion of mononuclear cells but not neutrophils. 13 , 39 , 40 The observed increase in VCAM-1 (which contributes to lymphocyte and monocyte, but not neutrophil, adhesion 1 ) and the inhibition of ICAM-1 and E-selectin expression (which contributes to neutrophil adhesion) in response to IL-4 and CD40L is also consistent with these findings.

In addition to its effect on leucocyte adhesion, IL-4 was also found to inhibit IL-8 and enhance IL-6 production by endothelial cells activated by CD40L. A similar effect of IL-4 on TNF-α- and LPS-activated endothelial cells has been reported previously. 41 The secretion of IL-8, a C-X-C chemokine and major attractant of neutrophils, 42 together with enhanced expression of E-selectin, VCAM-1 and ICAM-1, may account for the strong promotion of neutrophil adhesion induced by CD40L. Conversely, inhibition of E-selectin, ICAM-1 and IL-8 by IL-4 may be responsible for the reduced neutrophil adhesion and migration. These results show that the adhesion molecule and chemokine profile of endothelium regulated by CD40 and IL-4 can radically alter the leucocyte populations that bind. The role for IL-6 is less clear. It is a pleiotropic cytokine with many systemic effects, including B-cell immunoglobulin production and T-cell growth and differentiation. 43 IL-6 production by the endothelium stimulated with IL-4 and CD40L may be important for activation of trafficking B and T cells, and may modify the humoral immune response at sites of inflammation.
