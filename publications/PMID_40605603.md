CLCN3 and CLCN4 encode the endosomal 2Cl-/H+ exchangers ClC-3 and ClC-4, which
are highly expressed within the CNS, including the hippocampal formation.
Pathogenic variants recently found in these genes have given rise to the rare
CLCN3- and CLCN4-related neurodevelopmental conditions, characterized by a range
of neurological and neuropsychiatric complications, such as global developmental
delay, intellectual disability as a core feature, seizures, behavioural issues
and brain abnormalities. The mechanisms by which ClC-3 and ClC-4 regulate
neuronal function and viability, in addition to the molecular pathways affected
in CLCN3- and CLCN4-related neurodevelopmental conditions, remain unknown. In
neurodegenerative diseases, neuronal dendrites undergo pathological changes
often associated with aberrant electrical activity. To investigate how ClC-3 or
ClC-4 deficit alters neuronal excitability and morphology, we combined
patch-clamp recordings in acute hippocampal slice preparations with simultaneous
intracellular biocytin filling. We analysed the functional and structural
properties of Clcn3-/- and Clcn4-/- neurons. Two firing patterns are found in
the cornu ammonis 2 (CA2) region of the hippocampus: regular and burst firing.
At postnatal Day 13, 62% of the assessed CA2 wild-type neurons showed a rhythmic
bursting behaviour; this was reduced to 19% in Clcn4-/- and completely absent in
the Clcn3-/- condition. Changes in the firing patterns were accompanied by a
depolarizing shift in the action potential threshold and an increase in the
after-hyperpolarizing phase of the action potentials. Blockade of Kv7/KCNQ and,
to a lesser extent, Kv1, but not BK, SK or Kv2 channels, recapitulates the
wild-type firing pattern phenotype in the Clcn3-/- condition. Moreover, we
detected abnormalities in the complexity of the dendritic arborization.
Branching and lengths of apical and basal domains were significantly reduced in
the Clcn3-/- neurons and moderately altered in the Clcn4-/- neurons. At
postnatal Day 3, we found 25% of bursting neurons in Clcn3-/- with no
significant morphological abnormalities in the dendritic arborization in
comparison to the wild-type, suggesting that functional defects precede
structural changes in Cl-/H+ exchanger-deficient neurons. Likewise, dentate
granule cells exhibited defective action potential properties and reduced
burst-firing activity, which was substantially but not fully rescued by Kv7/KCNQ
blockage. We conclude that Cl-/H+ exchangers regulate the electrical
excitability and firing patterns of neurons primarily by fine-tuning Kv7/KCNQ
channel density, and that functional defects might contribute to alterations in
dendritic morphology. Our findings provide new insights into the underlying
molecular mechanisms of Cl-/H+ exchangers in neurons and pave the way for
potential therapeutic interventions for CLCN3- and CLCN4-related patients
associated with disruption of Cl-/H+ exchange function.

As a result, multidisciplinary support and occupational therapy are considered the most promising strategies for improving clinical outcomes in these patients.

ResultsCl−/H+ exchangers regulate the electrical properties and firing patterns of CA2 neuronsThe regulatory pathways by which ClC-3 and ClC-4 modulate neuronal function and viability, and how these pathways are affected in disease, remain unknown. To investigate the mechanisms underlying the dysfunction induced by Clcn3−/− and Clcn4−/−, we used patch-clamp recordings from hippocampal PCs and analysed their passive and active electrophysiological properties. We focused on the CA2 region, which is highly vulnerable to disruption of Cl−/H+ exchangers. To avoid any confounding effects of degeneration, we performed all experiments between P13 and P15, before any clear signs of neuronal degeneration were detected (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Immunohistochemical images from WT and mutant hippocampi at P15 showed comparable numbers of NeuN/RGS14-positive cells in the WT, Clcn3−/− and Clcn4−/− conditions, indicating that neuronal viability has not yet been compromised (Supplementary Fig. 1C). We assessed the firing pattern of CA2 PCs by eliciting trains of APs after injection of depolarizing current pulses (10 or 25 pA steps, 1 or 3 s duration) through the patch electrode (Fig. 1A). Hippocampal PCs can be classified as rhythmic bursting or regular spiking cells based on their firing patterns (Fig. 1C). In WT hippocampal slices, ∼62% of the analysed CA2 PCs showed a rhythmic bursting behaviour, whereas 38% showed a regular spiking pattern (Fig. 1D). The absence of ClC-4 or ClC-3 changed the firing behaviour of CA2 PCs (Fig. 1C and D). In Clcn4−/− animals, 19% of CA2 PCs showed rhythmic bursting, and the remaining 81% exhibited regular spiking behaviour, whereas in Clcn3−/− animals, all CA2 PCs showed a regular spiking pattern and did not develop AP bursts. These findings suggest that Cl−/H+ exchangers shape the intrinsic firing patterns of principal neurons in the hippocampus.Figure 1

CA2 = cornu ammonis 2.Analysis of passive membrane and AP properties (Supplementary Table 1) revealed decreased AP frequencies and longer ISIs in Clcn3−/− neurons (Fig. 2A–C). The AP threshold was significantly shifted to more depolarizing potentials in Clcn3−/−, but unchanged in Clcn4−/− PCs (Fig. 2D and E). The AHP, the hyperpolarizing phase that follows the AP(s), is a major determinant of neural excitability. It represents the principal mechanism for regulating ISI durations, spike frequency and, thus, neuronal firing patterns.21,22 Together with the spike ADP, the AHP controls the generation of AP bursts in hippocampal neurons.23 Activation of the persistent Na+ current (INap) primarily drives the ADP.24 A larger AHP amplitude will result in a reduction of the ADP component and the suppression of AP burst generation. Consistent with this idea, we observed that the ablation of ClC-3, but not of ClC-4, significantly enhanced the magnitude of the AHP and resulted in an almost undetectable ADP in the Clcn3−/− CA2 cells (Fig. 2D, F and G). The AP amplitude and half-width remained unchanged (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, the membrane time constant (τM) was significantly faster in the Clcn3−/− than in the WT or Clcn4−/− neurons (Fig. 2H). Cl−/H+ exchangers regulate the electrical properties and firing patterns of CA2 neurons

The regulatory pathways by which ClC-3 and ClC-4 modulate neuronal function and viability, and how these pathways are affected in disease, remain unknown. To investigate the mechanisms underlying the dysfunction induced by Clcn3−/− and Clcn4−/−, we used patch-clamp recordings from hippocampal PCs and analysed their passive and active electrophysiological properties. We focused on the CA2 region, which is highly vulnerable to disruption of Cl−/H+ exchangers. To avoid any confounding effects of degeneration, we performed all experiments between P13 and P15, before any clear signs of neuronal degeneration were detected (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Immunohistochemical images from WT and mutant hippocampi at P15 showed comparable numbers of NeuN/RGS14-positive cells in the WT, Clcn3−/− and Clcn4−/− conditions, indicating that neuronal viability has not yet been compromised (Supplementary Fig. 1C). We assessed the firing pattern of CA2 PCs by eliciting trains of APs after injection of depolarizing current pulses (10 or 25 pA steps, 1 or 3 s duration) through the patch electrode (Fig. 1A). Hippocampal PCs can be classified as rhythmic bursting or regular spiking cells based on their firing patterns (Fig. 1C). In WT hippocampal slices, ∼62% of the analysed CA2 PCs showed a rhythmic bursting behaviour, whereas 38% showed a regular spiking pattern (Fig. 1D). The absence of ClC-4 or ClC-3 changed the firing behaviour of CA2 PCs (Fig. 1C and D). In Clcn4−/− animals, 19% of CA2 PCs showed rhythmic bursting, and the remaining 81% exhibited regular spiking behaviour, whereas in Clcn3−/− animals, all CA2 PCs showed a regular spiking pattern and did not develop AP bursts. These findings suggest that Cl−/H+ exchangers shape the intrinsic firing patterns of principal neurons in the hippocampus.

Analysis of passive membrane and AP properties (Supplementary Table 1) revealed decreased AP frequencies and longer ISIs in Clcn3−/− neurons (Fig. 2A–C). The AP threshold was significantly shifted to more depolarizing potentials in Clcn3−/−, but unchanged in Clcn4−/− PCs (Fig. 2D and E). The AHP, the hyperpolarizing phase that follows the AP(s), is a major determinant of neural excitability. It represents the principal mechanism for regulating ISI durations, spike frequency and, thus, neuronal firing patterns.21,22 Together with the spike ADP, the AHP controls the generation of AP bursts in hippocampal neurons.23 Activation of the persistent Na+ current (INap) primarily drives the ADP.24 A larger AHP amplitude will result in a reduction of the ADP component and the suppression of AP burst generation. Consistent with this idea, we observed that the ablation of ClC-3, but not of ClC-4, significantly enhanced the magnitude of the AHP and resulted in an almost undetectable ADP in the Clcn3−/− CA2 cells (Fig. 2D, F and G). The AP amplitude and half-width remained unchanged (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, the membrane time constant (τM) was significantly faster in the Clcn3−/− than in the WT or Clcn4−/− neurons (Fig. 2H). Other passive properties, such as the resting membrane potential, rheobase current and input resistance (Rin), were unaffected (Supplementary Table 1). The alterations in AP features suggest that Cl−/H+ exchangers regulate the excitability and firing properties of CA2 PCs by increasing the AP threshold and shaping the descending phase of the AP waveform.

Cl−/H+ exchangers are crucial for the proper development of neuronal excitability
CLCN3 and CLCN4 variants, which have been shown to alter functional properties of the ClC-3 and ClC-4 proteins in in vitro cellular models, are associated with conditions primarily affecting neuronal development and function.8,11 To investigate whether the functional deficits observed in Clcn3−/− and Clcn4−/− PCs are progressive and to determine their onset, we examined the firing patterns of WT and mutant CA2 PCs at two different developmental stages, on the third and seventh postnatal days (P3 and P7). At P7, 63% of the WT CA2 cells showed rhythmic bursting and 37% a regular spiking pattern, similar to that observed at P15 [Fig. 5B(i and ii)]. In Clcn4−/− animals, we detected an age-dependent reduction in the population of CA2 PCs with rhythmic bursting firing patterns (from 50% at P7 to ∼20% at P15) and an increase in the number of regular spiking neurons (from 50% at P7 to ∼80% at P15) [Fig. 5B(i and ii)]. Bursts in P7 WT and Clcn4−/− PCs were complex, with three to five spikes per burst. Only one bursting neuron (1/18) was identified in the CA2 region of the P7 Clcn3−/− hippocampus [Fig. 5B(i and ii)]. The population of rhythmic bursting and regular spiking PCs remained relatively stable in the CA2 region of the WT hippocampus at P3, whereas the number of rhythmic bursting PCs increased in the Clcn3−/− condition. We found 6/25 Clcn3−/− neurons exhibiting a bursting discharge pattern [Fig. 5A(i and ii)]. In addition, spike parameters such as AHP were moderately shifted to more depolarizing potentials in the Clcn3−/− neurons at both ages [Fig. 5A(v) and B(v)]. Other passive properties and AP features, such as AP threshold, did not differ significantly between genotypes at both ages [Fig. 5A(iv) and B(iv) and Supplementary Table 1]. The membrane time constant (τM) was longer in Clcn3−/− neurons at both ages, with no changes in the Clcn4−/− mutant [Fig. 5A(iii) and B(iii)]. Cl−/H+ exchangers are crucial for the proper development of neuronal excitability

CLCN3 and CLCN4 variants, which have been shown to alter functional properties of the ClC-3 and ClC-4 proteins in in vitro cellular models, are associated with conditions primarily affecting neuronal development and function.8,11 To investigate whether the functional deficits observed in Clcn3−/− and Clcn4−/− PCs are progressive and to determine their onset, we examined the firing patterns of WT and mutant CA2 PCs at two different developmental stages, on the third and seventh postnatal days (P3 and P7). At P7, 63% of the WT CA2 cells showed rhythmic bursting and 37% a regular spiking pattern, similar to that observed at P15 [Fig. 5B(i and ii)]. In Clcn4−/− animals, we detected an age-dependent reduction in the population of CA2 PCs with rhythmic bursting firing patterns (from 50% at P7 to ∼20% at P15) and an increase in the number of regular spiking neurons (from 50% at P7 to ∼80% at P15) [Fig. 5B(i and ii)]. Bursts in P7 WT and Clcn4−/− PCs were complex, with three to five spikes per burst. Only one bursting neuron (1/18) was identified in the CA2 region of the P7 Clcn3−/− hippocampus [Fig. 5B(i and ii)]. The population of rhythmic bursting and regular spiking PCs remained relatively stable in the CA2 region of the WT hippocampus at P3, whereas the number of rhythmic bursting PCs increased in the Clcn3−/− condition. We found 6/25 Clcn3−/− neurons exhibiting a bursting discharge pattern [Fig. 5A(i and ii)]. In addition, spike parameters such as AHP were moderately shifted to more depolarizing potentials in the Clcn3−/− neurons at both ages [Fig. 5A(v) and B(v)]. Other passive properties and AP features, such as AP threshold, did not differ significantly between genotypes at both ages [Fig. 5A(iv) and B(iv) and Supplementary Table 1]. The membrane time constant (τM) was longer in Clcn3−/− neurons at both ages, with no changes in the Clcn4−/− mutant [Fig. 5A(iii) and B(iii)]. This dataset suggests that neuronal excitability declines progressively in the absence of CLC transporters, supporting the notion that Cl−/H+ exchangers are indispensable for the proper development of neuronal excitability.

Discussion
CLCN3- and CLCN4-related conditions are rare genetic conditions characterized by atypical neuronal development and function, leading to a spectrum of neurological manifestations, such as intellectual disability, global developmental delay, mental and behavioural conditions and epilepsy. For the CLCN4-related condition, the reported patients with epilepsy reach ∼37%, of whom ∼47% are refractory to pharmacological treatment.11-13,15 To date, only 13 patients have been reported for the CLCN3-related condition, with seizure occurring in 54% (7/13) of cases.8,14 Despite their clinical significance, the precise cellular functions of ClC-3 and ClC-4 transporters in neurons remain elusive. This lack of knowledge limits our understanding of the pathophysiology of CLCN3- and CLCN4-related conditions, hindering initiatives to develop targeted therapeutic strategies for patients. Multidisciplinary support, including speech and language therapy, occupational therapy, physiotherapy, and psychological and behavioural support, is vital and greatly enhances clinical outcomes for CLCN3/4 affected patients. At present, symptomatic and supportive drug therapies, however unspecific, remain the primary approaches for managing seizures and severe psychiatric complications associated with these conditions.The primary aim of this study was to investigate how Cl−/H+ exchangers regulate neuronal excitability and to identify the molecular link between this regulation and ClC-3/4.We observed a complete absence of rhythmic bursting firing patterns in Clcn3−/− CA2 PCs and a marked reduction in Clcn4−/− animals (Fig. 1). This phenotype cannot be attributed to the selective degeneration of rhythmic bursting PCs (Supplementary Fig. 1) but rather to a functional defect affecting the excitability properties of PCs, including a reduced number of APs per stimulus, a prolonged ISI, a depolarizing shift in the AP threshold and an increase in the AHP amplitude (Fig. 2). Notably, the currents governing the AHP are acknowledged to regulate neuronal excitability and play a role in determining the ISI duration and AP frequency.21 In addition, they can exert a dampening effect on repetitive or burst firing and contribute to the regulation of the AP threshold.40 Rescue experiments through pharmacological approaches suggest a role of Kv7/KCNQ and, to a lesser extent, Kv1, but not BK, SK or Kv2 channels (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs 2 and 3) in the phenotype of the Cl−/H+ exchanger-deficient CA2 PCs. The regulation of K+ channel densities appears to be a general function of CLC Cl−/H+ exchangers. We have recently shown a substantial increase in the slow-inactivating and delayed-rectifier voltage-gated K+ currents in sensory neurons of Clcn3−/− mice.3 Our data demonstrate unequivocally that the increased density of Kv7/KCNQ and probably Kv1 potassium channels is linked directly to the observed mutant phenotype in CA2 PCs.We confirmed the functional role of this K+ channel family in the mutant phenotype using retigabine, a Kv7/KCNQ activator (Fig. 4), which enhanced Ihold and IKCNQ more effectively in Clcn3−/− than in WT CA2 PCs (Fig. 4G). Retigabine does not increase the fraction of open KCNQ3 channels at −30 mV, the voltage used in this study, but instead increases the open probability of KCNQ2/KCNQ3 channels at −30 mV by shifting the voltage dependence of KCNQ2/KCNQ3 and KCNQ2 channels to more hyperpolarized potentials.31 This suggests that a substantial fraction of KCNQ2/KCNQ3 and/or KCNQ2, rather than KCNQ3 channels alone, contributes to the mutant phenotype. Given that the effect of retigabine depends on the KCNQ subunit composition, it raises the possibility that the difference observed between WT and Clcn3−/− with retigabine reflects an altered Kv7 subunit composition in Clcn3−/−. However, this might imply changes in KCNQ2 or KCNQ3 protein expression levels.32 Our western blot results (Supplementary Fig. 8) demonstrated comparable protein levels of KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 between WT and Clcn3−/−. In contrast to the differential effect of retigabine, XE-991 produced a similar fractional block of the IM deactivating current in both WT and Clcn3−/− mice (Fig. 4J and Supplementary Fig. 4). This apparent discrepancy might arise from the trafficking defect itself and the distinct biophysical properties of the KCNQ subunits. The surface expression of KCNQ3 in native systems is enhanced by its combination with KCNQ2.41 Thus, we speculate that ClC-3 disruption predominantly impairs the trafficking of KCNQ2/KCNQ3 and KCNQ2. Given that KCNQ2 homomers exhibit lower single-channel conductance and open probability compared with KCNQ2/KCNQ3 heteromers,42 an increased density of KCNQ2 homomers would attenuate the overall effect of XE-991 blockade on the total KCNQ current while enhancing the sensitivity to retigabine.31 Our TEA experiments support this interpretation, revealing a selective dysfunction in the KCNQ2 channels, suggesting that subunit composition of the M-current and trafficking have changed in the Clcn3−/− CA2 PCs, potentially reflecting an increased abundance of KCNQ2-containing channels. We conclude that the altered excitability properties of PCs arise from disrupted trafficking, resulting in increased surface expression of KCNQ2 and/or KCNQ2/KCNQ3 channels rather than homeostatic compensatory processes, such as changes in KCNQ2 or KCNQ3 protein levels.The phenotype observed in the Cl−/H+ exchanger-deficient PCs was not restricted to the CA2 region of the hippocampus but also extended to the dentate gyrus (Fig. 7). Mutant DGCs exhibited defective AP properties and a dampening of the burst-firing activity (Fig. 7). Pharmacological rescue with the Kv7/KCNQ antagonist linopiridine highlighted a significant role of Kv7/KCNQ in the phenotype of Clcn3−/− DGCs (Fig. 7H and I). The delayed onset of the first AP might indicate that K+ channels other than Kv7/KCNQ, probably Kv1.1,43 also contribute to the phenotype in mutant DGCs. Although voltage-gated Na+ channels play a crucial role in neuronal excitability, shaping the rising phase and peak amplitude of the AP,44 no difference in AP amplitude was found between genotypes in the CA2 or in the dentate gyrus (Supplementary Tables 1 and 3), ruling out a possible contribution of voltage-gated Na+ channels to the mutant phenotype. Our findings are consistent with previous observations in Clcn5−/− and Clcn4−/− mouse models where a trafficking defect has been reported.44-46 Disruption of ClC-5 and ClC-4, transporters highly homologous to ClC-3, results in a marked reduction in the internalization of crucial transporter and receptor molecules, such as Na(+)-P(i) cotransporter 2, Na(+)/H(+) exchanger 3, megalin and cubilin, in proximal tubules, in addition to transferrin receptors in fibroblasts.45-47 ClC-5, a renal transporter, has been implicated in Dent's disease.45 Although Clcn4−/− mouse models do not have a neurological phenotype,16 ClC-4 disruption in Clcn4−/− fibroblasts increased the cell surface expression of transferrin receptors, providing additional evidence for the role of Cl−/H+ exchangers in intracellular trafficking.47Over the past years, only 13 CLCN3 and >140 CLCN4 variants have been reported.8,10-15,17,48,49  In vitro studies have demonstrated various alterations in the transport properties of ClC-3 and ClC-4 linked to CLCN3 and CLCN4 variants, enabling their classification into loss- or gain-of-function categories.8,9,11,17 However, it remains unknown how a loss-of-function or gain-of-function variant impacts neuronal excitability and viability. Endosomal trafficking, the process of continual internalization and recycling of proteins from the plasma membrane or their degradation in lysosomes, is vital in maintaining the quality and quantity of functional levels of membrane proteins, such as KCNQ channels. All neuronal Cl−/H+ exchangers (ClC-3, ClC-4, ClC-6 and ClC-7) are endosomal proteins localized to distinct compartments within the endo/lysosomal system,64 where they have an undisputed function in maintaining the vesicular ion homeostasis and regulating endocytosis and lysosomal function.65 Our data are consistent with the notion that disruption of ClC-3 or ClC-4 leads to an increased density of Kv7/KCNQ channels, suggesting that ClC-3 and ClC-4 are involved in Kv7/KCNQ channel trafficking. This raises the possibility that Kv7/KCNQ channels are trafficked specifically through or regulated by ClC-3/ClC-4-enriched endosomes. ClC-3/ClC-4-positive endosomes might serve as critical hubs for the sorting, maturation or recycling of Kv7/KCNQ channels, potentially influencing their surface expression and functional availability. By using its clathrin-binding motif,66,68 ClC-3c recruits clathrin molecules to promote the endocytosis of ClC-3c/KCNQ-containing vesicles. These vesicles are directed to recycling endosomes via sorting signals located in the N-terminal region of ClC-3c.66 Dysfunction or disruption of the Cl−/H+ exchange machinery will primarily affect the endocytic pathway KCNQ-containing vesicles (Fig. 8). CLCN3- and CLCN4-related conditions are rare genetic conditions characterized by atypical neuronal development and function, leading to a spectrum of neurological manifestations, such as intellectual disability, global developmental delay, mental and behavioural conditions and epilepsy. For the CLCN4-related condition, the reported patients with epilepsy reach ∼37%, of whom ∼47% are refractory to pharmacological treatment.11-13,15 To date, only 13 patients have been reported for the CLCN3-related condition, with seizure occurring in 54% (7/13) of cases.8,14 Despite their clinical significance, the precise cellular functions of ClC-3 and ClC-4 transporters in neurons remain elusive. This lack of knowledge limits our understanding of the pathophysiology of CLCN3- and CLCN4-related conditions, hindering initiatives to develop targeted therapeutic strategies for patients. Multidisciplinary support, including speech and language therapy, occupational therapy, physiotherapy, and psychological and behavioural support, is vital and greatly enhances clinical outcomes for CLCN3/4 affected patients. At present, symptomatic and supportive drug therapies, however unspecific, remain the primary approaches for managing seizures and severe psychiatric complications associated with these conditions.

The primary aim of this study was to investigate how Cl−/H+ exchangers regulate neuronal excitability and to identify the molecular link between this regulation and ClC-3/4.

We observed a complete absence of rhythmic bursting firing patterns in Clcn3−/− CA2 PCs and a marked reduction in Clcn4−/− animals (Fig. 1). This phenotype cannot be attributed to the selective degeneration of rhythmic bursting PCs (Supplementary Fig. 1) but rather to a functional defect affecting the excitability properties of PCs, including a reduced number of APs per stimulus, a prolonged ISI, a depolarizing shift in the AP threshold and an increase in the AHP amplitude (Fig. 2). Notably, the currents governing the AHP are acknowledged to regulate neuronal excitability and play a role in determining the ISI duration and AP frequency.21 In addition, they can exert a dampening effect on repetitive or burst firing and contribute to the regulation of the AP threshold.40 Rescue experiments through pharmacological approaches suggest a role of Kv7/KCNQ and, to a lesser extent, Kv1, but not BK, SK or Kv2 channels (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs 2 and 3) in the phenotype of the Cl−/H+ exchanger-deficient CA2 PCs. The regulation of K+ channel densities appears to be a general function of CLC Cl−/H+ exchangers. We have recently shown a substantial increase in the slow-inactivating and delayed-rectifier voltage-gated K+ currents in sensory neurons of Clcn3−/− mice.3 Our data demonstrate unequivocally that the increased density of Kv7/KCNQ and probably Kv1 potassium channels is linked directly to the observed mutant phenotype in CA2 PCs.

We confirmed the functional role of this K+ channel family in the mutant phenotype using retigabine, a Kv7/KCNQ activator (Fig. 4), which enhanced Ihold and IKCNQ more effectively in Clcn3−/− than in WT CA2 PCs (Fig. 4G). Retigabine does not increase the fraction of open KCNQ3 channels at −30 mV, the voltage used in this study, but instead increases the open probability of KCNQ2/KCNQ3 channels at −30 mV by shifting the voltage dependence of KCNQ2/KCNQ3 and KCNQ2 channels to more hyperpolarized potentials.31 This suggests that a substantial fraction of KCNQ2/KCNQ3 and/or KCNQ2, rather than KCNQ3 channels alone, contributes to the mutant phenotype. Given that the effect of retigabine depends on the KCNQ subunit composition, it raises the possibility that the difference observed between WT and Clcn3−/− with retigabine reflects an altered Kv7 subunit composition in Clcn3−/−. However, this might imply changes in KCNQ2 or KCNQ3 protein expression levels.32 Our western blot results (Supplementary Fig. 8) demonstrated comparable protein levels of KCNQ2 and KCNQ3 between WT and Clcn3−/−. In contrast to the differential effect of retigabine, XE-991 produced a similar fractional block of the IM deactivating current in both WT and Clcn3−/− mice (Fig. 4J and Supplementary Fig. 4). This apparent discrepancy might arise from the trafficking defect itself and the distinct biophysical properties of the KCNQ subunits. The surface expression of KCNQ3 in native systems is enhanced by its combination with KCNQ2.41 Thus, we speculate that ClC-3 disruption predominantly impairs the trafficking of KCNQ2/KCNQ3 and KCNQ2. Given that KCNQ2 homomers exhibit lower single-channel conductance and open probability compared with KCNQ2/KCNQ3 heteromers,42 an increased density of KCNQ2 homomers would attenuate the overall effect of XE-991 blockade on the total KCNQ current while enhancing the sensitivity to retigabine.31 Our TEA experiments support this interpretation, revealing a selective dysfunction in the KCNQ2 channels, suggesting that subunit composition of the M-current and trafficking have changed in the Clcn3−/− CA2 PCs, potentially reflecting an increased abundance of KCNQ2-containing channels. We conclude that the altered excitability properties of PCs arise from disrupted trafficking, resulting in increased surface expression of KCNQ2 and/or KCNQ2/KCNQ3 channels rather than homeostatic compensatory processes, such as changes in KCNQ2 or KCNQ3 protein levels.

Mutant DGCs exhibited defective AP properties and a dampening of the burst-firing activity (Fig. 7). Pharmacological rescue with the Kv7/KCNQ antagonist linopiridine highlighted a significant role of Kv7/KCNQ in the phenotype of Clcn3−/− DGCs (Fig. 7H and I). The delayed onset of the first AP might indicate that K+ channels other than Kv7/KCNQ, probably Kv1.1,43 also contribute to the phenotype in mutant DGCs. ConclusionIn conclusion, our findings suggest that Cl−/H+ exchangers play a crucial role in regulating neuronal excitability, primarily by modulating the density of Kv7/KCNQ channels, with a modest effect on Kv1.1 voltage-gated K+ channels. This regulatory mechanism impacts the neuronal firing patterns and developmental processes of hippocampal neurons. Furthermore, our findings suggest a potential link between Kv7/KCNQ and Kv1.1 dysfunction and the pathophysiology of CLCN3- and CLCN4-related conditions, offering promising avenues for treating conditions associated with CLCN3 and CLCN4.
