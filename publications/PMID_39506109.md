Mitochondria serve a crucial role in cell growth and proliferation by supporting
both ATP synthesis and the production of macromolecular precursors. Whereas
oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) depends mainly on the oxidation of
intermediates from the tricarboxylic acid cycle, the mitochondrial production of
proline and ornithine relies on reductive synthesis1. How these competing
metabolic pathways take place in the same organelle is not clear. Here we show
that when cellular dependence on OXPHOS increases, pyrroline-5-carboxylate
synthase (P5CS)-the rate-limiting enzyme in the reductive synthesis of proline
and ornithine-becomes sequestered in a subset of mitochondria that lack cristae
and ATP synthase. This sequestration is driven by both the intrinsic ability of
P5CS to form filaments and the mitochondrial fusion and fission cycle.
Disruption of mitochondrial dynamics, by impeding mitofusin-mediated fusion or
dynamin-like-protein-1-mediated fission, impairs the separation of
P5CS-containing mitochondria from mitochondria that are enriched in cristae and
ATP synthase. Failure to segregate these metabolic pathways through
mitochondrial fusion and fission results in cells either sacrificing the
capacity for OXPHOS while sustaining the reductive synthesis of proline, or
foregoing proline synthesis while preserving adaptive OXPHOS. These findings
provide evidence of the key role of mitochondrial fission and fusion in
maintaining both oxidative and reductive biosyntheses in response to changing
nutrient availability and bioenergetic demand.

MAINMost mammalian cells contain between 50-1000 individual mitochondria. Although mitochondria act as discrete functional units, mitochondria engage in a continuous process of fusion and fission, which provides the mitochondria within a given cell with the ability to coordinate their functional activities while eliminating defective or excess components through mitophagy3. Mitochondria are well characterized for the ability to produce cellular ATP through OXPHOS but can also engage in various anabolic reactions to support the biosynthetic demands of proliferating cells1,2. When cells are supported in rich medium or are in a vascularized tissue, nutrient availability is in excess of that required to sustain ATP demand and mitochondria are able to utilize surplus substrates for the generation of macromolecular precursors that support cell growth and physiologic function4. However, when nutrients are limited, how mitochondria balance their oxidative synthesis of ATP with the reductive biosynthesis of macromolecular precursors required for cell maintenance is uncertain. The pathways that support OXPHOS and the pathways involved in amino acid synthesis and one carbon metabolism are each individually well studied1,2. In contrast, how the activities of these pathways are functionally coordinated under conditions of bioenergetic and nutrient stress is poorly defined.OXPHOS and biosynthesis are linkedTo take an unbiased approach to understanding how the enzymes engaged in supporting OXPHOS are functionally connected to enzymes engaged in biosynthesis, we used STRING protein-protein interaction analysis to profile enzymes involved in mitochondrial metabolism5 (Fig. 1a). This analysis grouped the genes encoding enzymes into distinct clusters that were consistent with the known roles of the proteins in mitochondrial metabolism. Proteins involved in the TCA cycle clustered together and shared a high degree of connectivity (Cluster 1). The proteins interconnected in Cluster 2 were enriched in enzymes engaged in amino acid biosynthesis. The majority of interconnected proteins in Cluster 3 were engaged in one-carbon metabolism. A single protein, the enzyme P5CS (encoded by the ALDH18A1 gene) linked the three major clusters. P5CS itself resides in Cluster 2 and it has previously been characterized as the rate limiting enzyme in the mitochondrial biosynthesis of proline and ornithine4,6–8. P5CS sits at the juncture of oxidative and reductive mitochondrial pathways that utilize glutamate as a precursor (Fig. 1b). P5CS reduces mitochondrial glutamate to glutamate semialdehyde (GSA) required for the synthesis of ornithine and proline (Fig. 1b). Alternatively, mitochondrial glutamate can be deaminated to α-ketoglutarate (αKG) to maintain the TCA cycle 4,7,9.Biosynthesis of proline and ornithineThe position of P5CS in the protein-to-protein interaction analysis suggested it played a role in regulating the balance between reductive and mitochondrial oxidative pathways. Since P5CS sits at the node between the TCA cycle cluster and the mitochondrial biosynthetic clusters, we tested whether growth conditions where cells are more dependent on OXPHOS for ATP production leads to compromised proline and ornithine-dependent polyamine synthesis. Culturing mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) in either glucose-deficient medium or medium where glucose is replaced with galactose (galactose medium) forces the cells to generate ATP exclusively through OXPHOS10 (Fig. 1c and Extended Data Fig. 1a). However, despite a significant decrease in the intracellular NADH/NAD+ ratio (Fig. 1d), MEFs were able to sustain their intracellular proline level in oxidative (galactose, or glucose-deficient) culture conditions (Fig. 1e). Mesenchymal progenitor cells (10T1/2), or human cancer cell lines (HCT116 and Panc1) were also able to maintain their proline level when cultured in galactose medium (Extended Data Fig. 1b), suggesting that the ability to maintain mitochondrial reductive biosynthesis under conditions where mitochondrial OXPHOS demand increases is not limited to MEFs.Glutamate metabolism can be monitored through metabolic tracing with [U-13C] glutamine (Fig. 1f). The entry of glutamate (M+5) into the TCA cycle was monitored by measuring the generation of α-ketoglutarate (αKG) (M+5) and malate (M+4), whereas the entry of glutamate (M+5) directly into mitochondrial reductive biosynthesis was monitored by measuring proline (M+5), ornithine (M+5), and putrescine (M+4)4,7,9. Oxidative culture conditions lead to a significant increase in glutamate oxidation and entry into the TCA cycle but did not compromise the cellular ability to engage in the reductive biosynthesis of proline, ornithine, and putrescine from glutamate (Fig. 1g, h and Extended Data Fig. 1c). As an alternative approach to increase mitochondrial ATP production, we used D-lactate to directly stimulate electron transport chain (ETC) activity and OXPHOS11. MEFs treated with D-lactate increased their ATP-coupled oxygen consumption rate without compromising intracellular proline production (Extended Data Fig. 1d, e). We also found that treating cells with the mitochondrial ETC uncoupler carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP) to increase ETC activity without producing ATP did not reduce the ability of the cells to produce proline (Extended Data Fig. 1f, g). Furthermore, decreasing intracellular NADH/NAD+ ratio by expressing the water-forming NADH oxidase from Lactobacillus brevis (LbNOX)12 in cells did not affect proline level (Extended Data Fig. 1h – j).The preservation of proline synthesis in the face of either increasing mitochondrial ATP demand or reducing the NADH/NAD+ ratio suggested that proliferating cells might be exploiting an alternative pathway other than P5CS to synthesize proline13 (Fig. 1). To rule out this possibility, we deleted P5CS in MEFs and U2OS cells using CRISPR-Cas9. Deletion of P5CS in both cell types leads to the loss of their ability to synthesize proline (Extended Data Fig. 2a – c) and results in proline auxotrophy (Extended Data Fig. 2d). Reintroducing CRISPR-Cas9-resistant P5CS rescued both intracellular proline levels and cell growth in medium lacking proline (Extended Data Fig. 2e – g), demonstrating that P5CS is required to sustain proline biosynthesis in sufficient quantities to support cell proliferation.P5CS forms reversible filamentsIt was previously reported that P5CS exhibits puncta-like structures inside mitochondria under nutrient stress14. Structural studies have demonstrated in Drosophila melanogaster that P5CS can increase its activity by forming filaments in the presence of glutamate15,16. To investigate whether changes in mitochondrial metabolism can trigger the molecular assembly of P5CS filaments, we cultured cells in the absence or presence of serum. In serum-starved fibroblasts, we found that P5CS exhibited a distribution pattern similar to that of the mitochondrial matrix protein pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH, Fig. 2a). However, when MEF cells were undergoing serum-dependent proliferation, P5CS formed filamentous structures that localized to only a small fraction of the mitochondria containing PDH. Co-staining with a mitochondrial outer membrane marker TOM20 (Fig. 2c) or performing Mito-IP17 (Extended Data Fig. 3b) suggested that these filaments formed inside mitochondria. Furthermore, P5CS filaments were also found to be contained within the mitochondrial inner membrane as imaged by TIM23 staining (Extended Data Fig. 3c) and P5CS-containing mitochondria also stained positive for intermembrane space proteins (AK2, Extended Data Fig. 3d) and matrix proteins (CS and GLS1, Extended Data Fig. 3e). Other interventions that increased ETC activity, such as stimulating OXPHOS with D-lactate, uncoupling ETC activity with FCCP, increasing bioenergetic demand by induction of oncogenic MYC21, or decreasing the cellular NADH/NAD+ ratio with LbNOX, also enhanced P5CS filament formation (Extended Data Fig. 3f – h). The distribution of P5CS-GFP became progressively filamentous and restricted to a small subset of mitochondria when quiescent MEFs were serum stimulated (Extended Data Fig. 3i).To assess whether P5CS clustering is required to maintain mitochondrial proline synthesis in proliferating cells in response to increasing oxidative demand, we expressed an enzymatically compromised P5CS mutant (P5CSR138W) reported to bind wildtype P5CS but unable to form higher order multimers22 in wild-type MEFs (Fig. 2f). Introduction of the P5CSR138W mutant, originally found in patients with autosomal dominant cutis laxa22, into cells expressing wildtype P5CS inhibited endogenous P5CS filament formation and significantly decreased intracellular proline levels (Fig. 2g, h, and Extended Data Fig. 3j). In contrast, overexpressing a catalytically-inactive mutant P5CS (P5CSC612A)14,16 that retains the ability to form oligomers with wildtype P5CS did not impact the ability of proliferating cells expressing endogenous P5CS from synthesizing proline. Together, these results suggest that clustering of P5CS enhances its activity. To test whether filament formation is regulated in part by demand for the end products of P5CS-dependent mitochondrial syntheses, we also examined whether the addition of ornithine and proline to the culture medium could prevent or reverse the clustering of P5CS. Addition of ornithine and proline prevented P5CS filament formation when MEFs were proliferating in galactose medium and also reversed existing P5CS filaments when added to cells where P5CS had already clustered (Fig. 2i – k and Extended Data Fig. 3k), resulting in the redistribution of P5CS throughout the mitochondrial compartment.P5CS clustering occurs in vivoTo examine whether P5CS clustering can also be observed in vivo, we obtained tissue sections of human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas (PDAC) that had been resected along with adjacent normal tissue. Cells in PDAC tumors have been shown to be limited in vivo by their ability to meet ATP demand in part through compromised vascularization and nutrient depletion as the tumors progressively grow in size23,24. Staining for endogenous P5CS in paraffin-embedded human PDAC sections revealed numerous areas where P5CS formed filamentous structures in the mitochondria of cytokeratin-positive tumor cells while cytokeratin-positive cells in uninvolved pancreatic tissue did not (Fig. 2l, m and Extended Data Fig. 4a). Separation of P5CS-containing mitochondria from ATP synthase-containing mitochondria was observed in both human and murine cell lines and using different antibodies against essential components of the ATP synthase, including ATP5B and ATP5A1 (Fig. 3). This separation was not observed in galactose-medium supplemented with ornithine and proline (Fig. 3b). High resolution microscopy was used to confirm that the segregation between P5CS and ATP synthase occurred within mitochondria containing both an intact outer membrane as visualized by TOM20 staining and an intact inner membrane as visualized by TIM23 staining (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 5b). OSCP localizes to the mitochondrial matrix and bridges the F0 and F1 subunits of the intact ATP synthase28. TurboID utilizes ATP to convert biotin into biotin–AMP, a reactive intermediate that covalently labels proteins localized in the same compartment29. Biotinylated proteins can subsequently be enriched with streptavidin beads to determine co-localization with the ATP synthase (Fig. 3e). In line with our observations using immunostaining, streptavidin pulldown revealed biotinylation of mitochondrial proteins NDUFV2 (Complex I), SDHA (Complex II), and P5CS but not cytoplasmic proteins such as vinculin when cell were cultured in the presence of ornithine and proline (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 5j). Consistent with this possibility, we found that ΔΨm, as assessed using the dye tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE), was significantly higher in P5CS-containing mitochondria compared to mitochondria lacking P5CS (Fig. 3g, h and Extended Data Fig. 5k). A corollary to this is that the subpopulation of ATP synthase-containing mitochondria engaged in OXPHOS should have a reduced ability to produce proline or polyamines (Extended Data Fig. 6a). MitoLbNOX distributed to all mitochondria, including those enriched in ATP synthase and those enriched in P5CS (Extended Data Fig. 6d – g).P5CS-mitochondria lack cristaeATP synthase contributes to the formation of mitochondrial cristae, which are the internal folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane crucial for efficient energy production. ATP synthase dimerization induces curvature in the inner membrane and promotes the development of tightly packed cristae structures31. These structures have been proposed to increase the surface area available for OXPHOS. As we observed that P5CS can segregate into a distinct subpopulation of mitochondria that lack ATP synthase, we investigated whether P5CS-containing mitochondria exhibit different ultrastructural features compared to mitochondria enriched for ATP synthase. Using correlative light and electron microscopy (CLEM) in cells expressing P5CS-GFP, we found that P5CS-containing mitochondria exhibit a near-complete loss of defined cristae structures (Fig. 4a, b and Extended Data Fig. 7a). Instead of cristae, stacks of protein filaments were observed within an intact double membrane in the P5CS-containing subpopulation. In contrast, the mitochondria devoid of P5CS displayed well-defined cristae. The ability of mitochondria to sustain their cristae also depends on the mitochondrial contact site and cristae organizing system (MICOS) complex, which localizes at cristae junctions and is crucial for maintaining cristae morphology32. The MIC60 component of MICOS complex is required for the formation and maintenance of cristae. The distribution of MIC60 in cells growing in galactose is restricted to mitochondria containing ATP5B. The P5CS-containing mitochondrial subpopulation was devoid of MIC60. (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 7b). Segregation of P5CS from the MICOS complex was also observed in tumor samples, demonstrating that this separation occurs in vivo (Fig. 4d). As suggested by the lack of cristae, P5CS-containing mitochondria also lacked the ATP synthase subunit e (ATP5I), which mediates the dimerization of ATP synthase and contributes to the formation of cristae33 (Extended Data Fig. 7c).The absence of the molecular machinery required to support cristae formation and the lack of cristae found in mitochondria subpopulation enriched for P5CS is likely to compromise OXPHOS and promote reductive synthesis from glutamate. Besides ATP synthase and the MICOS complex, mitochondrial cristae remodeling depends on optic atrophy 1 (OPA1). In addition to promoting mitochondrial fusion, OPA1 plays a crucial role in cristae formation by narrowing cristae junctions and promoting ATP synthase dimerization33. To investigate whether mitochondria that lack cristae structure can support proline biosynthesis, we utilized OPA1-knockout (Opa1−/−) MEFs (Extended Data Fig. 7d). Consistent with previous reports, Opa1−/− MEFs exhibit disrupted cristae structure and impaired OXPHOS33,34, which can be restored by reintroduction of wildtype OPA1 (Fig. 4e and f). Despite the inability to engage in OXPHOS, these Opa1−/− cells contain elevated levels of both total proline and ornithine, as well as enhanced synthesis of proline and ornithine from glutamate (Fig. 4g and h). In Opa1−/− cells, proline and ornithine synthesis was enhanced without P5CS being separated form ATP synthase or sequestered into a defined subpopulation of mitochondria. (Extended Data Fig. 7e). Mitochondrial fusion allows for the transfer of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), proteins, and metabolites between mitochondria to support optimal function, while fission enables both the biogenesis of new mitochondria and the removal of dysfunctional mitochondria. Mitochondrial fusion has been proposed to have a role in increasing OXPHOS35,36 by facilitating the formation of ETC supercomplexes34, while fission has been implicated in the ability to eliminate damaged material by releasing smaller mitochondria destined for mitophagy37. However, whether these fusion and fission processes also contribute to the formation of mitochondrial subpopulations that can support distinct metabolic processes is unclear. (Fig. 5b, Extended Data Fig. 8a and Supplementary Videos 1 and 2). Furthermore, cells lacking the ability to undergo mitochondrial fusion due to the deletion of Mitofusin 1 and 2 (Mfn1/2−/−) failed to separate P5CS from ATP synthase-containing mitochondria. The formation of mitochondria enriched in P5CS was abolished in Mfn1/2−/− cells, even when cells were cultured in galactose (Fig. 5c). MFN2 also serves as a tether between the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria, and ectopic expression of MFN2 can restore both mitochondrial fusion and ER-mitochondria tethering38,39. To further confirm that P5CS filament formation is mediated by mitochondrial fusion, Mfn1−/− MEFs were also studied. MFN1 exclusively contributes to fusion activity40. As shown in Extended Data Fig. 8c and d, loss of MFN1 was sufficient to abrogate the formation of P5CS filaments as well as the separation of P5CS-containing mitochondria from ATP synthase-containing mitochondria. Readdition of Flag-tagged MFN1 led to recovery of both processes.Mitochondrial fusion has been linked to the ability to engage in mitochondrial respiration34,35. Therefore, we tested whether the failure to separate P5CS from ATP synthase might correlate with a reduced ability to increase OXPHOS in response to cellular demand. We found that the Mfn1/2−/− cells exhibited reduced respiratory activity (Fig. 5d) and failed to proliferate when cultured in galactose medium (Fig. 5e). Reintroduction of MFN2-HA to Mfn1/2−/− cells was sufficient to restore respiratory capacity and allow cells to proliferate when cultured in galactose medium (Fig. 5d, e). The defect in OXPHOS exhibited by Mfn1/2−/− cells correlated with an increased NADH/NAD+ ratio and a reduced ability to maintain TCA cycle intermediates (Fig. 5f, g). In contrast, the ability of Mfn1/2−/− cells to produce proline was maintained (Fig. 5h). The separation of fused mitochondria is regulated by the recruitment and oligomerization of the large GTPase dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1), facilitated by several mitochondrial outer membrane proteins (MFF, Mid49, Mid51, and FIS1)3 (Fig. 5a). To examine the role of mitochondrial fission in regulating mitochondrial OXPHOS and reductive biosynthesis, we utilized Drp1−/− MEFs (Extended Data Fig. 8e), which are deficient in mitochondrial fission. In line with previous studies, Drp1−/− MEFs displayed elongated and bulb-like enlarged mitochondria41 (Fig. 5i). Reinstating the cell’s ability to undergo fission by introducing DRP1 cDNA was sufficient to reestablish the separation of P5CS-containing mitochondria from ATP synthase-containing mitochondria (Fig. 5j). Consistent with our observation in Drp1−/− MEFs, cells lacking the 4 known mitochondrial outer membrane fission proteins (Mff−/−, Fis1−/−, Mid49−/−, and Mid51−/−, referred to as QKO) also fail to separate P5CS from ATP synthase (Extended Data Fig. 8f). This reduction in proline synthesis in the absence of mitochondrial fission was also observed when Drp1 was deleted in the cancer cell line 143B or in QKO MEFs (Fig. 5m and Extended Data Fig. 8g). Although the reduction in proline synthesis was not sufficient to impede cell proliferation, it profoundly impaired the ability of MEFs to produce the extracellular matrix protein collagen (Fig. 5n). Proline accounts for 23% of the amino acids encoded by collagen mRNA42. Thus, although Drp1−/− MEFs can adaptively regulate OXPHOS, the cells are unable to synthesize sufficient proline to engage in extracellular matrix protein production. The proteins interconnected in Cluster 2 were enriched in enzymes engaged in amino acid biosynthesis. The majority of interconnected proteins in Cluster 3 were engaged in one-carbon metabolism. A single protein, the enzyme P5CS (encoded by the ALDH18A1 gene) linked the three major clusters. P5CS itself resides in Cluster 2 and it has previously been characterized as the rate limiting enzyme in the mitochondrial biosynthesis of proline and ornithine4,6–8. P5CS sits at the juncture of oxidative and reductive mitochondrial pathways that utilize glutamate as a precursor (Fig. 1b). P5CS reduces mitochondrial glutamate to glutamate semialdehyde (GSA) required for the synthesis of ornithine and proline (Fig. 1b). Alternatively, mitochondrial glutamate can be deaminated to α-ketoglutarate (αKG) to maintain the TCA cycle 4,7,9.

P5CS-mitochondria lack cristaeATP synthase contributes to the formation of mitochondrial cristae, which are the internal folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane crucial for efficient energy production. ATP synthase dimerization induces curvature in the inner membrane and promotes the development of tightly packed cristae structures31. These structures have been proposed to increase the surface area available for OXPHOS. As we observed that P5CS can segregate into a distinct subpopulation of mitochondria that lack ATP synthase, we investigated whether P5CS-containing mitochondria exhibit different ultrastructural features compared to mitochondria enriched for ATP synthase. Using correlative light and electron microscopy (CLEM) in cells expressing P5CS-GFP, we found that P5CS-containing mitochondria exhibit a near-complete loss of defined cristae structures (Fig. 4a, b and Extended Data Fig. 7a). Instead of cristae, stacks of protein filaments were observed within an intact double membrane in the P5CS-containing subpopulation. In contrast, the mitochondria devoid of P5CS displayed well-defined cristae. The ability of mitochondria to sustain their cristae also depends on the mitochondrial contact site and cristae organizing system (MICOS) complex, which localizes at cristae junctions and is crucial for maintaining cristae morphology32. The MIC60 component of MICOS complex is required for the formation and maintenance of cristae. The distribution of MIC60 in cells growing in galactose is restricted to mitochondria containing ATP5B. The P5CS-containing mitochondrial subpopulation was devoid of MIC60. (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 7b). Segregation of P5CS from the MICOS complex was also observed in tumor samples, demonstrating that this separation occurs in vivo (Fig. 4d). As suggested by the lack of cristae, P5CS-containing mitochondria also lacked the ATP synthase subunit e (ATP5I), which mediates the dimerization of ATP synthase and contributes to the formation of cristae33 (Extended Data Fig. 7c).The absence of the molecular machinery required to support cristae formation and the lack of cristae found in mitochondria subpopulation enriched for P5CS is likely to compromise OXPHOS and promote reductive synthesis from glutamate. Besides ATP synthase and the MICOS complex, mitochondrial cristae remodeling depends on optic atrophy 1 (OPA1). In addition to promoting mitochondrial fusion, OPA1 plays a crucial role in cristae formation by narrowing cristae junctions and promoting ATP synthase dimerization33. To investigate whether mitochondria that lack cristae structure can support proline biosynthesis, we utilized OPA1-knockout (Opa1−/−) MEFs (Extended Data Fig. 7d). Consistent with previous reports, Opa1−/− MEFs exhibit disrupted cristae structure and impaired OXPHOS33,34, which can be restored by reintroduction of wildtype OPA1 (Fig. 4e and f). Despite the inability to engage in OXPHOS, these Opa1−/− cells contain elevated levels of both total proline and ornithine, as well as enhanced synthesis of proline and ornithine from glutamate (Fig. 4g and h). In Opa1−/− cells, proline and ornithine synthesis was enhanced without P5CS being separated form ATP synthase or sequestered into a defined subpopulation of mitochondria. (Extended Data Fig. 7e). This suggests that the loss of cristae structure can promote reductive glutamate metabolism by P5CS.

The absence of the molecular machinery required to support cristae formation and the lack of cristae found in mitochondria subpopulation enriched for P5CS is likely to compromise OXPHOS and promote reductive synthesis from glutamate. Besides ATP synthase and the MICOS complex, mitochondrial cristae remodeling depends on optic atrophy 1 (OPA1). In addition to promoting mitochondrial fusion, OPA1 plays a crucial role in cristae formation by narrowing cristae junctions and promoting ATP synthase dimerization33. To investigate whether mitochondria that lack cristae structure can support proline biosynthesis, we utilized OPA1-knockout (Opa1−/−) MEFs (Extended Data Fig. 7d). Consistent with previous reports, Opa1−/− MEFs exhibit disrupted cristae structure and impaired OXPHOS33,34, which can be restored by reintroduction of wildtype OPA1 (Fig. 4e and f). Despite the inability to engage in OXPHOS, these Opa1−/− cells contain elevated levels of both total proline and ornithine, as well as enhanced synthesis of proline and ornithine from glutamate (Fig. 4g and h). In Opa1−/− cells, proline and ornithine synthesis was enhanced without P5CS being separated form ATP synthase or sequestered into a defined subpopulation of mitochondria. (Extended Data Fig. 7e). This suggests that the loss of cristae structure can promote reductive glutamate metabolism by P5CS.

The data presented above demonstrate that the ability of mitochondria to maintain both OXPHOS and the reductive biosynthesis of the macromolecular precursors, proline and ornithine, depends on ongoing mitochondrial fusion and fission and the biophysical and enzymatic properties of P5CS. In response to increasing bioenergetic demands, P5CS progressively forms filamentous clusters. Through reiterative rounds of mitochondrial fusion and fission, subpopulations of mitochondria are created that are enriched in either ATP synthase or in filamentous P5CS. The P5CS-containing mitochondria retain ETC activity as they display an increased mitochondrial membrane potential. The absence of ATP synthase in P5CS-enriched mitochondria will favor the dissipation of that potential through reductive biosynthesis in part by suppressing TCA cycle activity and reducing glutamate oxidation to αKG. In contrast, mitochondria enriched in ATP synthase and lacking P5CS are freed from competition for reducing equivalents and have highly ordered cristae and an increased capacity to engage in OXPHOS.

The separation of mitochondria into two functional subpopulations persisted as long as the demand for OXPHOS-dependent ATP production was maintained. This separation was also reversible in vitro upon a reduction in bioenergetic demand. In vivo, tumors are frequently nutrient-depleted and bioenergetically challenged23,24. The separation of mitochondria into two distinct and mutually exclusive subpopulations that was observed in PDAC cells in vivo may reflects the increased bioenergetic demand solid tumors experience in vivo. In vitro, cultured PDAC cells were found to be able to segregate P5CS into a subpopulation of mitochondria in response to shifts between glucose- and galactose-medium.

The functional segregation of mitochondria depends on the ability of P5CS to form multimeric assemblies. Mutations in Arg138 of P5CS have been shown to be a recurrent spontaneous cause of an autosomal-dominant form of cutis laxa with progeroid features22. All other well characterized autosomal dominant forms of cutis laxa are due to mutations encoding extracellular matrix proteins. The ability of wildtype P5CS to cluster in a subpopulation of mitochondria is prevented by expressing the disease-associated P5CSR138W and this results in a significant reduction in the ability of the cell to produce proline when cultured in galactose medium. In contrast, wildtype cells expressing a catalytically dead P5CS (P5CSC612A) retained the ability to form P5CS-enriched mitochondria and exhibited no significant impairment in proline synthesis when placed in galactose medium.

Although it is known that metabolic enzymes can form filaments to regulate their enzymatic activity, the above data demonstrate that filament formation can also contribute to cellular localization and the proximity to other proteins15,16,43–45. Which additional proteins are selectively recruited or excluded from mitochondria enriched in P5CS filaments remains to be fully determined. The mechanism by which proline and ornithine either inhibit or reverse P5CS clustering is also unclear. Neither metabolite is a direct end-product of P5CS as other proteins downstream of P5CS are involved in their synthesis. However, the results show that the process described here is dynamic. While mitochondrial separation into subpopulations can persist for prolonged periods of time, mitochondrial segregation is reversible when nutrient levels are restored or bioenergetic demand decreases.

The ability of P5CS filament clusters to separate into a discrete subpopulation of mitochondria devoid of the competing activity of the ATP synthase also depends on ongoing mitochondrial fusion/fission cycles. Previous studies have shown that mitochondrial fusion helps assemble ETC complexes more efficiently and increase OXPHOS capacity34,35. Consistent with these prior studies, we find Drp1−/− fused mitochondria have retained the ability to engage in adaptive ATP biosynthesis41, however they fail to produce proline effectively. In contrast, induction of DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission has been shown to promote interstitial fibrosis, mitochondrial redox stress, and a glycolytic shift in a murine model of chronic kidney disease46, a phenotype similar to what we observed in Mfn1/2−/− cells. Mfn1/2−/− cells have extensively fragmented mitochondria and exhibit defective OXPHOS but produce abundant proline.

While mitochondrial fusion and fission can maintain the homogeneity of composition between individual mitochondria of a given cell3, the data here demonstrate that mitochondrial fusion and fission can also contribute to the creation and maintenance of specialized subpopulations of mitochondria25–27,37, each dedicated to distinct mitochondrial synthetic activities. The ability to create stable mitochondrial subpopulations is dependent on the ability of P5CS to form filament clusters of enzymatically-active P5CS. Formation of P5CS filament clusters following a shift in ATP demand results from the asymmetric segregation of the growing P5CS filaments into a subpopulation of mitochondria that lack ATP synthase. Thus, mitochondrial dynamics support the ability of mitochondria to sustain both ATP synthesis and generation of macromolecular precursors over a wide range of bioenergetic demand and nutrient abundance.

NADH/NAD+ measurements using enzymatic assayThe NADH/NAD+ ratio was measured using an NAD+/NADH-Glo Assay kit (Promega, G9071) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with modifications. Briefly, 2.5 × 104 cells were seeded in 6-well plates in standard culture medium. The next day, cells were washed once with PBS, and the medium was replaced with experimental medium (glucose-deficient DMEM supplemented with 10 mM glucose and 0.5 μg/mL doxycycline) for an additional 24 hours. Cells were harvested using 1 mL of ice-cold 80% methanol, collected, and dried as described above. Dried metabolites were dissolved in buffer A (0.2 N NaOH diluted 1:1 with PBS). To measure NAD+, 20 μL of lysate was transferred to a PCR tube, diluted with 20 μL of buffer A and 20 μL of 0.4 N HCl, and subsequently incubated at 60°C for 20 minutes. For NADH measurement, 20 μL of freshly thawed lysate was transferred to PCR tubes and incubated at 75°C for 1 hour. The acidic conditions permit selective degradation of NADH, while the basic conditions degrade NAD+. Following the incubation, samples were spun in a bench-top centrifuge and quenched with 20 μL of neutralizing solution (0.5 M Tris base for NAD+ samples and 0.25 M Tris in 0.2 N HCl for NADH samples). Samples were then mixed 1:1 with NAD+/NADH-Glo detection reagent and incubated for 30 minutes. Luminescence was measured using a plate reader.
