Abstract

Purpose

The objective of this study was to elucidate the role of anaphase promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C)–related genes in cases of female infertility characterized by disturbances in oocyte maturation, failure of fertilization, and cessation of early embryonic growth among three distinct Chinese familial lineages.

Methods

We conducted whole-exome sequencing of patients with female infertility from 639 unrelated Chinese families and three probands with APC/C gene mutations were screened. Structure modeling and in vitro experiments were performed to analyze the effects of CDC23 and APC13 variants.

Results

We identified six rare missense variants in APC/C genes, including two compound heterozygous missense variants of CDC23 (c.A1277G, c.A833G, c.C182T and c.C301T) from case 1 and case 2 and one compound heterozygous variant of APC13 (c.C6A and c.116_126del) from case 3. These APC/C gene mutations all showed a recessive inheritance pattern. These mutations are conserved across different species. Mutation Taster, SIFT and PPH2 forecast that these variants are inclined towards exerting a deleterious effect. Structural analysis indicated that these mutations may result in changes in the chemical bonds between themselves and other APC/C subunits. In vitro experimental data suggested that mutations associated with CDC23 result in dysregulated protein expression, whereas missense mutation in APC13 is implicated in aberrant cellular localization patterns.

Conclusion

Our findings expand the genetic spectrum of APC/C genes, especially CDC23 and APC13 in female infertility, indicating that the significance of APC/C genes in female sterility should be emphasized in the future. And it provides a new diagnostic and therapeutic target for genetic counseling.

Introduction

Successful human reproduction starts with a mature metaphase II oocyte fusing with a sperm cell to form a fertilized egg. In human oocytes, the meiotic cell cycle pauses at germinal vesicle (GV) stage with resumption of the cycle at puberty, which leads to maturity of the oocyte from metaphase I (MI) to metaphase II (MII). Mature oocytes are then arrested at MII until fertilization [ 1 ]. Upon fertilization, the embryo initiates a sequence of cleavage divisions, compaction and polarization to form blastocyst [ 2 ]. Deficiencies at any stage of the reproductive process can lead to failure to conceive, and many aspects of the underlying causes have not yet been fully elucidated.

Recently, many genetic causes of female infertility have been discovered. For instance, variants in PATL2 and TBPL2 have been implicated in inducing oocyte arrest at the GV stage [ 3 – 7 ], while mutations in TUBB8 and TRIP13 are associated with substantial metaphase I (MI) arrest in oocytes [ 8 , 9 ]. WEE2 variants contribute to fertilization failure [ 10 , 11 ]. Variants in PADI6 , NLRP2 and NLRP5 have been documented to exert influences on the developmental trajectory of early embryos [ 12 – 15 ]. The ZP family, including ZP1 , ZP2 and ZP3 can give rise to empty follicle syndrome [ 16 – 18 ]. Several mutations from various genes can precipitate a spectrum of phenotypes, from oocyte maturation arrest at the GV stage to impediments in fertilization and subsequent embryonic development. Specifically, TUBB8 mutations have also been associated with embryonic arrest [ 19 ]. Similarly, PATL2 mutations have been documented to cause fertilization failure and arrest in embryonic development [ 20 ]. Nevertheless, the identified genetic variations are recognized as explaining only a portion of the underlying etiology.

The anaphase promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) is a multimeric cullin-RING ubiquitin ligase, consisting of the catalytic sub-complex (catalytic module), the substrate recognition sub-complex (TPR lobe) and the scaffolding sub-complex (platform) [ 21 ]. It functions to regulate both mitosis and meiosis accurately [ 22 ]. In mammals, APC/C is activated by its co-activators, Cdh1 or Cdc20, which subsequently orchestrate the targeted degradation of specific substrates, including cyclins. This intricate adjustment is essential for the accurate regulation of both mitotic and meiotic cell cycle progression [ 23 ].

Cell cycle abnormalities are associated with tumorigenesis. Most of the studies related to APC/C aberration have mainly focused on tumorigenesis [ 24 , 25 ]. While previous studies have identified that APC/C core subunits, cell division cycle 23 (CDC23, also called anaphase promoting complex subunit 8, ANAPC8 or APC8) and its co-activator cell division cycle 20 (CDC20) are responsible for female infertility. Two homozygous mutations of CDC23 were found in three families, and three patients mainly exhibited oocyte maturation arrest and early embryo development arrest [ 26 ]. Additionally, the loss of Cdc23 led to abnormal spindles, misaligned chromosomes, errors in homologous chromosome segregation, and the production of aneuploid oocytes in mice [ 27 ]. Females carrying CDC20 -related mutations, including three homozygous mutations and six compound heterozygous mutations, were found in 10 families, and these individuals mainly exhibited oocyte maturation arrest and early embryonic development arrest (Fig. S1 a) [ 28 – 31 ]. CDC26 (cell division cycle 26, also called anaphase promoting complex subunit 12, ANAPC12 or APC12 ) mRNA and protein levels in aged oocytes were severely decreased compared to young oocytes. Aged oocytes lacking Cdc26 were more prone to aneuploidy [ 32 ]. Recent studies have found a homozygous mutation from a consanguineous family (Fig. S1 b), shedding light on the role of CDC26 in meiotic metaphase I arrest in oocytes and its subsequent impact on female infertility [ 33 ]. Other subunits of APC/C like Apc2 (anaphase promoting complex subunit 2, also called Anapc2) and Apc15 (anaphase promoting complex subunit 15) are also related to cell cycle regulation [ 34 , 35 ]. APC/C is recognized for its pivotal role in regulating cell division. However, the implications of APC/C dysfunction in the context of human infertility warrant further investigation to elucidate its potential contributions to reproductive disorders.

In this study, we discovered six variants in CDC23 and APC13 coding the subunits of APC/C respectively, leading to various female infertility phenotypes, including oocyte maturation disorder, fertilization failure and early embryo arrest. The mutant amino acid sites are conserved in different species, and predicted to be harmful by at least one software. Three-dimensional modeling of proteins was used to predict structure changes caused by possible hydrogen bond changes after the mutation occurred. Experimental assays conducted in vitro have demonstrated that the specified mutations alter protein expression and localization, highlighting their significance in the pathogenesis of related conditions.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

In this study, 639 primary infertile individuals were recruited from the Center for Reproductive Medicine, Shandong University. They were conducted whole-exome sequencing and three probands with APC/C gene mutations were screened. These three probands had normal menstrual cycles and sex hormone levels. And the semen tests of their husbands were normal.

Genetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes of probands and their families using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Whole-exome capture and sequencing were performed using the Agilent SureSelect Whole Exome capture and Illumina sequencing technology by iGeneTech. The reads were aligned to the human genome reference assembly (hg38/Human) with the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner. The variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing with different primers (Table S1 ). The candidate mutations must met several criteria: (i) low allele frequency in the public human genome databases of the 1000 Genomes Project and the gnomAD, and (ii) no other candidate reported genes were screened in these individuals. The candidate mutations were validated by Sanger sequencing. PolyPhen-2, SIFT, MutationTaster and ACMG were used to predict pathogenicity.

Model building

The CDC23 and APC13 wild-type (WT) and mutation model structures were built by SWISS-MODEL according to homologous sequence from database to further display the electrostatic potential, and the structure of CDC23 related proteins was also used to evaluate changes in polar bonds. The APC13 mutations model structures were designed with PyMOL based on APC/C structure (PDB. 4UI9) to explore changes in polar bonds. Structural mapping of the evolutionary conservation of amino acid positions was performed with ConSurf based on APC/C structure (PDB. 4UI9). All protein structure-related presentations are based on PyMOL.

Oocyte collection, microinjection and culture

The GV oocytes were collected from 6–8-week-old female ICR mice. Female mice were intraperitoneally injected with 7.5 IU PMSG to promote follicular maturation, and 44–48 h later, mice were sacrificed for oocyte collection. The ovaries were dissected and then chopped with a razor blade. Tissue fragments were suspended with prewarmed (37 °C) oocyte retrieval buffer supplemented with 50 mM IBMX to prevent GVBD. The GV oocytes were picked out with a prefabricated glass tube. The GV oocytes were transferred into pre-warmed microdrops containing G-MOPS medium with 50 mM IBMX and were injected with 20 μM siRNA. Then the oocytes were transferred into pre-warmed microdrops, containing M16 medium with 50 mM IBMX in a condition of 37 °C, 5% CO 2 . 24 h later, oocytes were transferred into pre-warmed microdrops, containing M16 medium and cultured for 12–14 h in an atmosphere of 37 °C, 5% CO 2 .

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total oocyte RNA was extracted from oocytes using Dynabead mRNA DIRECT Kit (Invitrogen, 61011). The mRNA level of each gene was validated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis on LightCycler 480 (Roche, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers are shown in Table S2 .

Total cellular RNA was extracted from HEK293T cells post-transfection with both wild-type and mutant plasmids encoding CDC23 and APC13 , utilizing the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026CN) for isolation. The mRNA level of each variant of genes was validated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis on LightCycler 480 (Roche, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers are shown in Table S2 .

Expression vector construction

The vectors carried full-length coding sequence of wild-type human CDC23 (GenBank: NM_004661 ) with EGFP tag and APC13 (GenBank: NM_015391 ) with FLAG tag were provided by YouBio. Point mutagenesis was performed with Agilent QuickChange Lighting (Agilent, 200516) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T (MeilunBio, PWE-HU007) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37℃ with 5% CO 2 . Wild type and mutant plasmids of CDC23 and APC13 were transfected into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blotting (WB)

HEK293T cells were collected and lysed in RIPA Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail, and then all samples were added with loading buffer and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. Equal amounts of samples were electrophoresed on SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membrane. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in PBS with 0.1% Tween 20 and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies were anti-CDC23 (abcam, ab182003). Goat anti-rabbit IgG (ZSGB-BIO, ZB-2301) was used as the secondary antibody to detect the primary antibodies.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

HEK293T cells were transfected with WT or mutant CDC23 and APC13 expression plasmids, culturing for 48 h. Then, transfected cells were first treated with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed three times in PBS. CDC23 -transfected cells were prepared as specimens by employing Mounting Medium with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (abcam, ab104139). APC13 -transfected cells were then blocked in blocking buffer (1% BSA, 0.1% Triton X-100) for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were then incubated with anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma, F1804) overnight at 4°C. After being washed three times, the cells were incubated with Donkey anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody (Invitrogen, SA5-10166) for 1 h at 37 °C. Add Mounting Medium with DAPI for the specimens. Images were captured with confocal laser scanning microscope.

Statistical analysis

The optical density values were calculated with Image Lab (BIO-RAD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism. Differences between two groups were compared using the Student’s t -test. A value of P < 0.05 was considered to be significant.

Result

Clinical characteristics of affected individuals

Three affected individuals identified in this study had been diagnosed with primary infertility for several years without a definite cause.

The individual designated as II-3 within case 1 underwent two unsuccessful attempts at in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), resulting in the inability to secure a viable embryo for transfer. In the third cycle, with the utilization of donor oocytes, she achieved preterm delivery, culminating in the birth of a male infant. The affected individual II-I in case 2 had undergone four IVF/ICSI attempts. However, no transferable embryo was formed. These four attempted cycles exhibited distinct phenotypic outcomes. In the initial two cycles, a substantial number of oocytes were retrieved, with over half reaching the metaphase II (MII) stage, and a significant proportion of these demonstrated successful fertilization. In stark contrast, the last two cycles yielded a lower percentage of mature MII oocytes among those collected. Furthermore, these oocytes exhibited a notable incidence of fertilization failure. Nevertheless, during the fourth cycle with donor oocytes, she was able to obtain embryos suitable for transfer, which subsequently led to the successful delivery of twins post-transfer (Table 1 ).

The affected individual II-1 in case 3 had undergone a cumulative total of six attempts at IVF and ICSI. The initial two cycles were conducted at other medical institutions and neither achieved fertilization or further developmental progression. In the three subsequent cycles, no viable embryos were generated either. Ultimately, in their final attempt, the couple proceeded with ICSI utilized two oocytes donated by an external source, which led to the successful delivery, indicating that the etiology of the couple’s infertility was attributed to the female partner’s oocytes ( Table 1 ) .

These abnormal oocyte maturation and embryonic development phenotypes raised that there might be an underlying genetic etiology contributing to their infertility observed in these cases.

Pathogenic variants of APC/C genes observed in three families

Whole-exome sequencing was used to identify the variants from probands. Several variants related to APC/C genes were screened in these three families covering CDC23 and APC13 . Two novel compound heterozygous missense variants in CDC23 (GenBank: NM_004661 ) were found, including c.A1277G:p.H426R and c.A833G:p.D278G in case 1 and c.C182T:p.S61F and c.C301T:p.R101W in case 2. The genetic analysis of the proband in case 1 revealed the presence of a variant derived from the father, in contrast to her two elder sisters who lacked this variant and had both previously delivered offspring. In case 2, two heterozygous variants of the proband were inherited from her parents respectively. We also identified one compound heterozygous variants in APC13 (GenBank: NM_015391 ) in the proband from case 3 incorporating one missense variant c.C6A:p.D2E and one frameshift deletion variant c.116_126del:p.P39QfsTer25. All of these variants showed a recessive inheritance pattern and were conformed by Sanger sequencing ( Fig. 1 a ) .

Fig. 1.

The CDC23 protein consists of 13 tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) domains, highlighting its function in coordinating the higher-order assembly and protein recognition/binding [ 21 ]. All four variants c.A1277G:p.H426R, c.A833G:p.D278G, c.C182T:p.S61F and c.C301T:p.R101W are located in different TPR domains, unlike the sites previously found ( Fig. 1 b ) . All these altered amino acids in the patients are conserved in multiple species such as Pan troglodytes , Canis lupus familiaris , and Mus musculus ( Fig. 1 d ) , and the highly conserved residues are mainly located on the interaction surface with other proteins. Moreover, these genetic variants are predominantly predicted to be pathogenic based on the assessments conducted by SIFT, PPH2, and MutationTaster. At present, the pathogenicity predicted by ACMG is mainly VUS for most variants except a loss-of-function variant c.116_126del:p.P39QfsTer25 in APC13 , which is LP. Corresponding variants are rare in population databases such as 1000 g, GnomAD, ExAC ( Table 2 ) .

Hence, it is hypothesized that CDC23 and APC13 variants could potentially impact oocyte maturation and the developmental progression of the early embryo.

Disruption in APC/C subunits led to structure abnormality

APC/C is instrumental in the meiotic division of oocytes and the mitotic division of early embryos. To elucidate the potential impacts of mutations in CDC23 and APC13 on their respective protein architectures and interactions within the complex, we employed the SWISS-MODEL platform to construct both wild-type (WT) and mutant structures of CDC23, and used PyMOL to investigate the potential shifts in the protein interaction landscape within the APC/C (PDB.4UI9) following the mutations of APC13. Three-dimensional modeling of CDC23 revealed that the amino acid changes caused by different variants resulted in alterations of hydrogen bonding with its own residues. Residue 61S originally participated in three distinct hydrogen bonds with residues 57E, 58L and 259F. Upon mutation to phenylalanine, these hydrogen bonds were reconfigured to a single bond with 57E and another with 98E. Furthermore, the mutation of 101R to tryptophan resulted in a transformation of hydrogen bonding patterns, shifting from one bond with 104H, one with 105F, two with 93Y, and three with 98E to one bond with 104H and one with 105F. The mutation of residue 278D to glycine resulted in the preservation of two distinct hydrogen bonds with residues 273Y and 282A, while concurrently leading to a reduction in the hydrogen bonding interaction with residue 281K. Additionally, 426H was involved in three distinct hydrogen bonds with residues 422Y, 429R and 430P, which were converted to only one hydrogen bond left to 422Y when 426H was mutated to Arginine ( Fig. 2 a ) . Besides, 61S and 101R with high conservation predicted by ConSurf are located on the interaction surface ( Fig. 2 cd ) . Mutations at positions 101R and 426H induced significant alterations in the electrostatic potential of the protein structure, while the other two variants had no significant change in electrostatic potential ( Fig. 2 f–i , Fig. S2 ab). The observed alterations in electrostatic potential imply that these mutations may exert an influence on the affinity for neighboring proteins, potentially disrupting the binding interactions.

Fig. 2.

The missense variant 2D to 2E of APC13 mainly influenced one hydrogen bond to 3S within the protein itself without affecting the bond to 180R of CDC23 ( Fig. 2 b ) . And there was no significant change for electrostatic potential after the amino acid alteration (Fig. S2 cd). And the amino acid residue 2D in APC13 functioning to connect with other subunits is highly conserved ( Fig. 2 e ) .

The stabilizing function of Apc13 is highlighted by their interactions with the TPR subunits, particularly the TPR sub-complex, which includes subunits such as Apc3, Apc6, Apc9, and others, and presents in a supra-stoichiometric amount and participate in homo- and hetero-associations, reinforcing the scaffolding integrity of the APC/C complex [ 36 , 37 ]. Amino acid substitutions can lead to significant disruptions in the intricate network of hydrogen bonds that are essential for maintaining protein structure and function within the APC/C complex. These alterations may result in the destabilization of intra- and inter-subunit interactions, which are critical for the proper assembly and activity of the APC/C.

Loss of Apc13 results in abnormal oocyte maturation

Empirical evidence has indeed confirmed that aberrations in the CDC23 gene can result in impaired oocyte maturation, contributing to female infertility [ 27 ]. Building upon this foundation, we have conducted an experimental intervention by microinjecting Apc13 small interfering RNA (siRNA) into oocytes to inhibit the synthesis of Apc13 by knocking down mouse Apc13 . Scrambled siRNA was microinjected in the cytoplasm of GV stage oocytes. The targeted depletion of Apc13 via siRNA treatment has been experimentally demonstrated to induce meiotic failure in mouse oocytes, characterized by the arrest at metaphase I and the subsequent failure to extrude the first polar body (Fig. 3 ace).

Fig. 3.

In this study, we have discovered that the targeted knockout of Apc13 results in impaired mouse oocyte maturation. The majority of the oocytes were arrested at the metaphase I (MI) stage, with a subsequent failure in the extrusion of the first polar body, indicating a critical role for Apc13 in the meiotic process.

Variants in CDC23 and APC13 affected their functions

To evaluate the functional effects of the identified variants in vitro, we first performed Western blotting analysis in HEK293T cells transfected with EGFP-tagged WT or mutant CDC23 expression plasmids. Relative to the WT counterpart, the expression levels of the mutated proteins are markedly reduced ( Fig. 3 bd ) with normal intracellular localization (Fig. S3 c), potentially constituting the primary etiology underlying the observed functional anomalies. However, no significant variation in mRNA expression levels was observed within the cells (Fig. S3 a), implying that the mutations of CDC23 might render the protein more susceptible to degradation or induce instability. In the case of APC13, we conducted cellular transfection assays and immunofluorescence, revealing that the missense mutated APC13 variant predominantly localized to the nucleus, in contrast to the wild-type protein, which was primarily found in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3 f). Moreover, no significant difference was observed in mRNA levels between the missense mutation and the WT (Fig. S3 b). This discrepancy suggests that aberrant mutations in APC13 may lead to mislocalization within the cell, potentially disrupting its normal function.

Discussion

In this study, we identified three individuals suffering from primary infertility characterized by oocyte or embryonic defects with different compound-heterozygous variants in APC/C subunits coding genes, inherited in an autosomal recessive mode.

These individuals all experienced a recurrent pattern of failure in cycles of assisted reproductive technology (ART). WES was performed to analyze their genomic profiles, followed by targeted screening of the exome data for pathogenic variants informed by prior research findings.

The CDC23 protein is composed of 13 TPR domains. The TPR motif, which is characterized by its superhelical structure, stands as one of the most prevalent protein motifs within eukaryotic cells. It serves as a structural scaffold protein, facilitating the interaction between diverse proteins and orchestrating the assembly of multi-protein complexes. Protein domains within coactivators possess the specificity to discern the superhelical configurations of TPR motifs, thereby enabling subsequent binding interactions with the APC/C complex. This interaction is pivotal for the activation of the APC/C complex, thereby empowering it to execute its regulatory role in the orchestration of cell cycle progression [ 38 ]. Mutations within the protein sequence have the potential to induce alterations in the tertiary structure of the protein. These architectural alterations within the protein are not solely restricted to modulating the chemical bonds associated with other amino acid residues intrinsic to the protein itself (Fig. 2 a). They may also exert a significant impact on the overall protein conformation and its capacity to engage in interactions with the comprehensive suite of subunits that constitute the APC/C complex. We have conducted Western blotting analysis to evaluate the expressional alterations of the CDC23 variant in vitro. However, the physiological implications of these protein anomalies require further elucidation through in vivo studies in future research endeavors.

As reported before, different individuals with different variants showed varying reproductive phenotypes [ 26 ]. The individual in case 1 primarily presented with a dysfunction in oocyte maturation, while the individual in case 2 displayed an additional early embryo arrest. Concurrently, an anomaly in the fertilization process was observed in both cases. It is conceivable that different loci of genetic variation may correlate with a spectrum of phenotypic expression and severity.

APC13, despite its relatively low molecular weight, serves a critical role in the stabilization of the APC/C subunit architecture, especially their interaction with subunits containing TPR motif [ 36 ]. Mutations within the protein are hypothesized to interfere with neighboring protein interactions, leading to a destabilization of the complex architectural framework. This perturbation in structural stability can consequently result in the dysfunction of the APC/C's intended biological role, which is critical for the regulation of cell cycle progression.

Although in vitro studies have illustrated modifications in the functional attributes of CDC23 and APC13 proteins, including mutations that lead to reduced protein expression or irregular protein positioning, further in vitro experimentation and complementary in vivo studies are necessary to validate these observations.

Further investigation is also necessary to assess whether variants in APC13 are associated with premature ovarian insufficiency (POI). This inquiry is prompted by the clinical findings in case 3, where the individual exhibited an Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH) level of less than 0.5 ng/ml, a biomarker indicative of diminished ovarian reserve. Additionally, the individual has demonstrated a pattern of retrieving a diminished number of oocytes across multiple cycles, suggesting an underlying genetic predisposition. While the genes CDC23 and APC13 , which encode subunits of the APC/C, are known to play crucial roles in both mitotic and meiotic cell divisions [ 39 ], the cohort of three affected individuals in this study exclusively presented with female infertility, devoid of any observed abnormalities in somatic tissues or organs. This discrepancy may suggest that mitosis and meiosis have distinct threshold requirements for the levels of these proteins, or alternatively, that there may exist compensatory mechanisms within mitotic processes to counteract the deficiency of these proteins.

The recent discovery of novel variants in CDC23 and APC13 , along with previously identified variants in both CDC23 , CDC20 and CDC26 , has prompted an extensive investigation into the functional attributes of other APC/C subunits. This exploration has been conducted through a combination of in vitro assays and the application of relevant animal models, providing valuable insights into the complex’s role in cellular processes CDC20 may also be associated with male infertility by abnormally regulating securin and other cyclins [ 40 ]. In murine females, aberrant Cdc20 activity leads to dysregulated chromosome alignment and segregation, adversely impacting the first meiotic division. Concurrently, mice with a deficiency in Cdc20 exhibit early embryonic arrest, predominantly characterized by an arrest at the two-cell embryonic stage [ 41 ]. Reduced Cdc20 expression in bovine oocytes also showed abnormal first polar body extrusion (PB1E) [ 42 ]. Apc2 is a catalytic subunit. Several studies have indeed revealed the association between Apc2 and chromosome segregation, as well as polar body extrusion, which are critical processes in oocyte maturation. The depletion of Apc2 has been linked to the maturation of mouse oocytes, suggesting its essential role in these cellular events. Furthermore, disruptions in Apc2 function have been implicated in the pathogenesis of premature ovarian failure (POF), highlighting its significance in ovarian health and fertility [ 34 , 43 ]. In vitro experimental data indicate that the depletion of Apc3 (anaphase promoting complex subunit 3), Cdc16 (cell division cycle 16, also called anaphase promoting complex subunit 6, ANAPC6) or Apc15 would stabilize Cyclin B1, subsequently leading to a severe delay in mitosis [ 35 , 44 ]. It has also been shown in C. elegans and S. cerevisiae that abnormal APC/C subunits like Apc2 or Apc8 can lead to abnormal cell cycle [ 45 , 46 ]. However, previous findings showed that animal models of reduced Cdc20 expression just led to infertility instead of compromising the health or lifespan of the female mice, while knockout of Cdc20 and Apc1 (anaphase promoting complex subunit 1) results in embryonic lethality in mice. These observations further suggest that a higher threshold for Cdc20 activation or other APC/C subunits may be necessary in oocytes compared to somatic cells [ 47 – 49 ], and the severity of the manifestation may vary depending on the severity of the mutation.

In conclusion, we found that variants in APC/C related genes, including CDC23 and APC13 are responsible for human oocyte defects and early embryo abnormalities. We investigated the effects of CDC23 and APC13 variants in vitro. While our study did not provide additional genetic evidence for female infertility, it has nevertheless identified potential targets within APC/C-related genes that could be instrumental for genetic diagnosis of oocyte maturation dysfunction and early embryonic arrest.
