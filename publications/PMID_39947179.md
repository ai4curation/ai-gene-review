Stimulator of interferon genes (STING) transmits signals downstream of the
cytosolic DNA sensor cyclic guanosine monophosphate-AMP synthase (cGAS), leading
to transcriptional upregulation of cytokines. However, components of the STING
signaling pathway, such as IRF3 and IFNAR1, are not essential for
autoinflammatory disease in STING gain-of-function (STING-associated
vasculopathy with onset in infancy [SAVI]) mice. Recent discoveries revealed
that STING also functions as a proton channel that deacidifies the Golgi
apparatus. Because pH impacts Golgi enzyme activity, protein maturation, and
trafficking, we hypothesized that STING proton channel activity influences
multiple Golgi functions. Here, we show that STING-mediated proton efflux
non-transcriptionally regulates Golgi trafficking of protein cargos. This
process requires the Golgi-associated protein ArfGAP2, a cell-type-specific dual
regulator of STING-mediated proton efflux and signaling. Deletion of ArfGAP2 in
hematopoietic and endothelial cells markedly reduces STING-mediated cytokine and
chemokine secretion, immune cell activation, and autoinflammatory pathology in
SAVI mice. Thus, ArfGAP2 facilitates STING-mediated signaling and cytokine
release in hematopoietic cells, significantly contributing to autoinflammatory
disease pathogenesis.

INTRODUCTION

Stimulator of interferon (IFN) genes (STING) signaling induces production of type I IFN and IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs), which mediate antimicrobial and antitumoral immunity.1–6 However, STING also has other cellular and immunological functions, including regulation of processes as diverse as antigen presentation and insulin secretion.7,8 In addition to direct effects of STING on signal transduction, the many cellular and immunological functions of STING might also relate to its recently discovered Golgi proton channel activity.9,10 Unlike the well-established role of STING signaling during activation of the type I IFN response, the effects of STING proton channel activity at the Golgi are less completely understood, particularly under physiological conditions or in an animal model.

In response to cytosolic DNA, cyclic guanosine monophosphate (GMP)-AMP synthase (cGAMP synthase [cGAS]) produces the second messenger cGAMP. At the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane, cGAMP binds to STING, triggering large-scale conformational changes resulting in STING polymerization and translocation from the ER to the Golgi.11–13 ER export of STING initiates downstream signaling that induces inflammation as well as LC3 lipidation.14 The latter promotes noncanonical autophagy and degradation of activated STING, leading to termination of STING signaling.15–17 The fact that STING influences multiple immunological and cellular functions can make it particularly challenging to dissect mechanisms of STING-associated pathology under physiological conditions, including in patients and in animal models.

Disease-causing gain-of-function STING mutants constitutively localize within the Golgi, resulting in a devastating pediatric autoinflammatory disease known as STING-associated vasculopathy with onset in infancy (SAVI). We previously generated SAVI mice and were surprised to discover that pathology occurs independently of the type I IFN receptor (IFNAR1/2) and IFN-regulatory factors (IRFs) including IRF3 and IRF7, which are downstream of STING.18–20 Instead, SAVI pathogenesis in mice relies on a variety of cytokines and cell-type-specific STING activities.21,22 This body of work highlights the type I IFN-independent functions of STING in disease and suggests that Golgi-localized STING mutants may be useful in elucidating lesser-known activities of STING,13,23,24 including the potential role of Golgi proton efflux in autoimmune disease pathogenesis.

STING-mediated proton efflux causes deacidification of the Golgi,9,10 but the immunological and cellular implications of STING proton channel functions are less well understood. The luminal pH of the Golgi is tightly regulated, reflecting its crucial role in cellular functions such as protein glycosylation, lysosomal biogenesis, and membrane trafficking.25–28 For instance, inherited mutations that prevent acidification of the Golgi result in congenital disorders of protein glycosylation, processing, and sorting.29 Similarly, pathogen-encoded Golgi proton channels, such as the influenza M2 proton channel, can spuriously deacidify the Golgi, thereby impacting cargo sorting at the trans-Golgi.30,31 Additionally, Golgi pH can influence the activity of enzymes important for the maturation and secretion of cargos such as cytokines or viral proteins.30,32,33 This suggests that STING proton channel activity in the Golgi might similarly play a role in the transit, sorting, or secretion of immunological or viral protein cargos.

Here, we demonstrate that ArfGAP2 regulates STING and its proton channel activity in a cell-type- and compartment-specific manner. We generated ArfGAP2 knockout mice and discovered that STING signaling and downstream cytokine secretion are defective in macrophages from these animals. The effect of ArfGAP2 on cytokine secretion is not observed upon activation of other pattern recognition receptors. Additionally, we show that STING-mediated changes in Golgi pH, which require ArfGAP2, can influence the Golgi transit of protein cargos. Deletion of ArfGAP2 in the hematopoietic and endothelial cells of SAVI mice prevents STING-mediated inflammation and immune cell activation, further underscoring the important role of ArfGAP2 in autoinflammatory disease. Thus, ArfGAP2 dually regulates STING signaling and proton channel activity in specific cell types, revealing a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of STING-associated autoinflammation.

RESULTSSTING is regulated by the Golgi-associated protein ArfGAP2We performed a whole-genome CRISPR-Cas9 screen using a gain-of-function STING SAVI mutant (N154S), which constitutively localizes in the Golgi (Figures 1A and 1B). Our screening method leverages the anti-proliferative effects of STING gain-of-function in T cells,23 producing surviving cells that exhibit resistance to the deleterious effects of chronic STING activation. Given the recent discovery that STING modulates Golgi pH,9 we reasoned that our screen might uncover Golgi-associated factors that impact STING signaling and proton channel activity. Several Golgi-associated proteins were top hits in our screen, including ArfGAP2 (6 out of 6 single guide RNAs [sgRNAs]). ArfGAP2 was a strong hit, with similar fold-enhancement and p value to that of STING, TBK1, and IRF3, all critical components of the canonical STING signaling pathway (Figure 1B).1,34,35 We validated our results using ArfGAP2-deleted Jurkat T cells (Figures 1C and S1). Upon activation of STING, ArfGAP2 deletion prevented STING-induced ISG induction (Figure 1C). ArfGAPs can regulate membrane trafficking, vesicle transport, and cargo sorting, but ArfGAP2 is less well understood relative to ArfGAP1 and ArfGAP3 (28.9% and 55.7% identical to ArfGAP2, respectively).36–40 Deletion of ArfGAP1 had no effect on STING-induced ISGs, whereas deletion of ArfGAP3 had a mild effect on STING-mediated induction of the downstream ISG RSAD2 but not IFIT1 (Figures 1C, S1A, and S1B).To further examine the effect of ArfGAP2 deletion on STING-mediated gene expression, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of Jurkat T cells expressing either inactive wild-type (WT) STING or the STING gain-of-function mutant (STING N154S). RNA-seq analysis confirmed that deletion of ArfGAP2 downregulates many ISGs induced by the STING gain-of-function mutant (Figures 1D and S1C). STING-mediated ISG induction was restored upon complementation with ArfGAP2 or with a GAP-dead version of ArfGAP241,42 but not with over-expression of ArfGAP3 (Figures 1E and 1F). This suggests a GAP-independent function of ArfGAP2 in STING-mediated ISG induction within a hematopoietic cell culture system. Multiplex cytokine analysis of Jurkat T cell supernatants revealed that many STING-induced chemokines and cytokines were also downregulated upon deletion of ArfGAP2, whereas other cytokines and chemokines were either unaffected or only moderately affected (Figure 1G; Table S1). Based on SDS-PAGE and western blot analysis, ArfGAP2 deletion also disrupted STING signaling in Jurkat T cells expressing the constitutively active STING mutant (Figures S1D–S1F).Localization of STING regulates its signaling.2,13 Because ArfGAP proteins can impact sorting into, through, and out of the Golgi,37–40,43,44 we reasoned that ArfGAP2 might alter the localization of STING within the Golgi. As an alternative hypothesis, we considered whether ArfGAP2 might regulate the association between STING and clathrin adaptor protein 1 (AP1), a cargo adaptor protein that facilitates trafficking of STING to the lysosome.45 However, confocal microscopy studies demonstrated that ArfGAP2 deletion had no major effect on the colocalization of STING with AP1 nor on the localization of activated STING within the Golgi (Figures S2A–S2D). Similarly, ArfGAP2 did not affect STING palmitoylation, a post-translational modification known to regulate STING localization in the Golgi (Figures S2E and S2F).46To begin to test whether ArfGAP2 and STING might interact or localize within the same compartment, we conducted a second screen using an ascorbate peroxidase (APEX)-STING proximity labeling system (Figures 2A and S2G–S2J). By activating APEX-STING with diABZI, a cell-permeable STING agonist,47 we found that APEX-STING labeled itself, TBK1, and ArfGAP2 with similar kinetics (Figures 2A and S2G–S2J). Labeling of ArfGAP2 at early time points was far greater than labeling of many other previously reported STING-interacting proteins or STING regulators, including NPC1 and Arf1 (Figures 2A and S2G–S2J).48–50 Persistent labeling of ArfGAP2 probably reflects its comparatively long half-life, which we confirmed by cycloheximide chase (Figure S2K). Strong labeling of ArfGAP2 by APEX-STING suggests that ArfGAP2 and STING operate in the same subcellular compartment after STING activation. Consistent with this hypothesis, we found that STING and ArfGAP2 colocalize and remain colocalized upon STING activation (Figures 2B and 2C). However, ArfGAP2 colocalization with the cis-Golgi decreased after activation of STING, suggesting that STING mobilization into the Golgi can significantly alter the localization of ArfGAP2 (Figure 2C) or, alternatively, that STING and ArfGAP2 enter the same post-Golgi vesicles. Despite this, we did not find that STING and ArfGAP2 co-immunoprecipitate (Figure S2L). We therefore speculate that ArfGAP2 may transiently interact with or indirectly regulate STING within the same subdomains of the Golgi.Gain-of-function STING mutations are found in the connector loop (STING V147L, N154S, and V155M) and the polymeric interface (STING R281Q) (Figure 2D). However, we found that ArfGAP2 promotes both STING-dependent ISG expression and LC3B lipidation in cells expressing connector loop STING mutants but not in cells expressing STING R281Q (Figures 2E–2I). Thus, ArfGAP2 is dispensable for signaling downstream of the polymeric interface mutant, which may be a result of this mutant having slightly different subcellular localization than other gain-of-function STING mutants.51 In fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) studies, we observed that ArfGAP2 promotes clustering of WT STING (Figure 2J). Because the impact of ArfGAP2 on STING activation and autophagy depends on the type of STING mutant, ArfGAP2 is more likely to be a direct mediator of STING-induced cytokine secretion rather than an indirect regulator of autophagy and cytokine production.ArfGAP2 enhances STING signaling and IFN-stimulated gene induction in mouse macrophages expressing WT STINGTo determine whether ArfGAP2 promotes STING-mediated ISG induction in primary cells, we generated ArfGAP2f/f mice crossed to transgenic Cre animals (Figure 3A). RNA-seq analysis of gene expression after STING activation revealed that ArfGAP2 knockout bone-marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were deficient in type I IFN receptor (IFNAR1/2) responses (Figure 3B), potentially implicating ArfGAP2 in IFNAR1/2 signaling. However, IFN-β dose-response studies revealed that IFNAR1/2 signaling is completely functional in ArfGAP2 knockout cells (Figures 3C and S3A).Next, we tested responses to STING agonism and discovered that STING-mediated ISG induction, as well as downstream activation of TBK1 and IRF3, were nearly ablated in ArfGAP2 knockout BMDMs (Figures 4A and 4B). Deletion of ArfGAP2 resulted in a ~13-fold reduction in the IFN-β secretion in response to STING activation (Figure 4C), and RNA-seq analysis of BMDMs demonstrated almost complete loss of STING-induced ISGs in BMDMs lacking ArfGAP2 (Figure 4D). Activation of WT STING strongly suppressed West Nile virus (WNV) growth in ArfGAP2-sufficient BMDMs, but not in BMDMs lacking ArfGAP2 (Figure 4E), further confirming the functional implications of deficient STING responses. STING activation and IFNAR signaling also cause upregulation of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and class II molecules.4,52 In response to STING agonism, we found that ArfGAP2 deletion impairs STING-mediated induction of both MHC class I and II (Figures 4F and S3B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ArfGAP2 deletion in BMDMs prevents STING signaling and IFN-β secretion.We considered other possible effects of ArfGAP2, including whether deletion of ArfGAP2 might alter immune cell development in mice expressing unstimulated WT STING. However, ArfGAP2 deletion did not impact populations of splenocytes and thymocytes (Figures S3 and S4). Macrophage development and polarization were similarly unaffected by deletion of ArfGAP2 (Figures S3C–S3E), and there was also no effect on frequencies of thymocytes, splenic T cells, or splenic myeloid cells (Figures S4A–S4K). Furthermore, in the absence of STING activation, ArfGAP2 deletion did not affect T cell subsets in mice expressing unstimulated WT STING, including naive, effector, central memory, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) (Figures S4L–S4R).STING-mediated secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines is enhanced by ArfGAP2In addition to activating the type I IFN response, STING signaling also induces pro-inflammatory cytokine transcription downstream of nuclear factor κB (NF-κB).53,54 In cytokine multiplex assays of supernatants from THP1 monocytes, we found that ArfGAP2 promoted secretion of many cytokines and chemokines but only upon activation of STING (Figure S5; Table S2). We obtained similar results in an interleukin (IL)-1β ELISA in THP1 knockout macrophages (Figure S5D). Cytokine secretion downstream of STING was also blunted in primary ArfGAP2 knockout BMDMs (Figures 5A and 5B; Table S3). By contrast, ArfGAP2 deletion had no effect on cytokine secretion in BMDMs in response to LPS-mediated activation of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) (Figure 5C; Table S3),55 upon treatment with the RIG-I ligand 5ˊppp-double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) (Figures 5D and 5E),56 in response to the TLR3 ligand poly(I:C) (Figures 5F and S5E), or during infection with encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV), which activates the type I IFN response via the RNA sensor MDA5 (Figure 5G).57 Thus, ArfGAP2 promotes secretion of specific cytokines and chemokines downstream of STING but not downstream of several other pattern recognition receptors.STING and ArfGAP2 cooperatively regulate Golgi transit of protein cargosSTING activation leads to the formation of a proton channel that ultimately deacidifies the Golgi.9 Coincidentally, the luminal pH of the Golgi is well known to impact cargo sorting and secretion.25–27,58 We therefore reasoned that ArfGAP2 might impact Golgi functions by modifying Golgi pH via STING-mediated proton efflux. First, we confirmed the prior discovery that STING activation triggers Golgi deacidification using the cis- and trans-Golgi ratiometric pH sensors MGAT- and GALT-super ecliptic pHluorin (SEP)-mRuby3, respectively (Figure 6A).9,10 These sensors utilize the pH sensitivity of the SEP fluorescence to track changes in the luminal pH in the two Golgi compartments. By examining the increase in SEP fluorescence relative to mRuby3 fluorescence over time, we found that activation of STING by diABZI causes both the cis- and the trans-Golgi pH to rise (increase in the SEP/mRuby3 ratio) and that deletion of ArfGAP2 blunts STING proton channel activity in the trans-Golgi but not in the cis-Golgi (Figures 6A, S6A, and S6B).C53 is another small molecule agonist of STING signaling that can also inhibit STING proton channel activity, in part by blocking STING localization within the Golgi apparatus.9,10,59,60 Given the potential nonspecific effects of C53 as a small molecule, as well as its combined agonist function and localization effects, we reasoned that C53 is not an ideal tool for specifically defining functions of the STING proton channel. Nevertheless, we confirmed that C53 prevents Golgi deacidification as previously reported (Figure S6B).9 However, unlike C53, deletion of ArfGAP2 does not prevent STING localization in the trans-Golgi network (Figure S2).Because our earlier results suggested that ArfGAP2 could potentially modulate both STING signaling and cytokine secretion, depending on the cell type, we next decided to test whether STING and ArfGAP2 might affect protein trafficking and secretion independently of STING signaling. Unlike what occurs in primary murine macrophages, we found that STING-induced phosphorylation of TBK1 remains completely intact in STING-expressing 293T cells that lack ArfGAP2 (Figure 6B). Next, we performed cell surface-biotinylation studies followed by mass spectrometry using 293T cells expressing WT STING. When STING was not activated, deletion of ArfGAP2 had almost no detectable effect on the cell surface proteome. However, when STING was activated in ArfGAP2 knockout cells, there were nearly ~1,000 unique biotinylated proteins detectable by mass spectrometry, including many mislocalized proteins unrelated to STING signaling or IFN pathways (Figures 6C and S6C–S6F). These results suggest that ArfGAP2 maintains homeostasis of the plasma membrane proteome when STING is active, perhaps by regulating secretory trafficking.To test this hypothesis directly, we used the retention using selective hooks (RUSH) assay (Figures 6D–6I, S7A, and S7B), where mCherry-tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) was fused to a streptavidin-binding motif and constitutively expressed along with an ER-retention sequence (Lys-Asp-Glu-Leu; KDEL) fused to streptavidin (Figure 6D).61 Under basal conditions, mCherry-TNF-α is sequestered in the ER by the ER-retention hook, and, upon the addition of exogenous biotin, mCherry-TNF-α is released such that its journey through the secretory pathway can be tracked. Importantly, the RUSH assay examines mCherry-TNF-α under control of a constitutive promoter,61 and, thus, any changes in the rate of mCherry-TNF-α accumulation in the Golgi are due to alterations in secretory trafficking (Figure 6D).61 Consistent with our other results, ArfGAP2 deletion had no effect on transit of mCherry-TNF-α under conditions where STING was unstimulated (Figures 6E and S7A). The idea that ArfGAP2 does not broadly regulate protein cargo secretion is consistent with our observation that ArfGAP2 is dispensable for basal cytokine secretion, as well as for cytokine secretion induced by MDA5, RIG-I, TLR3, and TLR4 ligands (Figures 5 and S5). In addition, ArfGAP2 deletion had no effect on cell surface protein expression when STING was not active (Figures 6C and S6). Upon activation of STING, Golgi transit of mCherry-TNF-α was delayed (Figure 6F). This suggests that STING activity directly regulates Golgi transit, similarly to the M2 proton channel of influenza virus, which also deacidifies the Golgi.30 However, in ArfGAP2-deleted cells, activation of STING triggered very rapid dispersal of cytokine cargo from the Golgi (Figures 6G, 6H, and S7A). This was not due to changes in the ultrastructure of the Golgi because multiple imaging modalities, including electron microscopy, the RUSH assay, and confocal microscopy, further indicated that the Golgi apparatus remains intact in ArfGAP2-deleted cells, whether STING was activated or not (Figures 2B, 6E–6I, S2D, S7C, and S7D). Furthermore, transit or secretion of another cargo protein (E-cadherin) was unaffected by deletion of ArfGAP2 (Figures 6I and S7A), suggesting that some routes of Golgi trafficking are unperturbed in these cells. These results suggest that only specific cargo, or perhaps one specific route of Golgi trafficking, is perturbed in STING-activated, ArfGAP2-deleted cells. BafA1, a V-ATPase inhibitor that makes the Golgi less acidic,62 was sufficient to restore normal transit of mCherry-TNF-α in STING-activated, ArfGAP2-deleted cells (Figures 6G, S6B, and S7B). This suggests that Golgi pH is one determinant of STING-mediated trafficking and sorting. Indeed, deacidification of the Golgi is well known to alter enzyme functions, protein cargo modifications, sorting, and secretion.25–27 Importantly, clathrin-mediated endosomal pathways that are involved in cytokine secretion exhibit slower trafficking rates than default secretory pathways,63–65 and a fast rate of Golgi transit can negatively impact sorting and post-translational modifications.66 In pulse-chase studies using click chemistry, we observed that TNF-α processing and secretion were slightly delayed in STING-activated, ArfGAP2-deleted cells (Figures S7E and S7F). We also found that STING activation in ArfGAP2 knockout cells reduces localization of the clathrin-adaptor protein AP1 at the trans-Golgi network (Figure 6J). Thus, ArfGAP2 stabilizes Golgi transit and secretion upon STING activation (Figure 6K).Deletion of ArfGAP2 prevents autoinflammatory disease and immune cell activation in SAVI miceWe previously made the unexpected discovery that STING gain of function causes autoinflammatory disease independently of IRF3, IRF7, and IFNAR1 in mice.18,20 We and others also found that STING gain of function causes immune cell activation and hypersecretion of IFN-γ and chemokines that are associated with pathology, independent of the type I IFN receptor.18,20–23,67 Therefore, if ArfGAP2 is a proximal dual regulator of STING signaling and proton channel activity, we might expect reversal of a wider range of disease phenotypes in SAVI mice lacking ArfGAP2. Importantly, the RUSH assay examines mCherry-TNF-α under control of a constitutive promoter,61 and, thus, any changes in the rate of mCherry-TNF-α accumulation in the Golgi are due to alterations in secretory trafficking (Figure 6D).61 Consistent with our other results, ArfGAP2 deletion had no effect on transit of mCherry-TNF-α under conditions where STING was unstimulated (Figures 6E and S7A). The idea that ArfGAP2 does not broadly regulate protein cargo secretion is consistent with our observation that ArfGAP2 is dispensable for basal cytokine secretion, as well as for cytokine secretion induced by MDA5, RIG-I, TLR3, and TLR4 ligands (Figures 5 and S5). In addition, ArfGAP2 deletion had no effect on cell surface protein expression when STING was not active (Figures 6C and S6). Upon activation of STING, Golgi transit of mCherry-TNF-α was delayed (Figure 6F). This suggests that STING activity directly regulates Golgi transit, similarly to the M2 proton channel of influenza virus, which also deacidifies the Golgi.30 However, in ArfGAP2-deleted cells, activation of STING triggered very rapid dispersal of cytokine cargo from the Golgi (Figures 6G, 6H, and S7A). This was not due to changes in the ultrastructure of the Golgi because multiple imaging modalities, including electron microscopy, the RUSH assay, and confocal microscopy, further indicated that the Golgi apparatus remains intact in ArfGAP2-deleted cells, whether STING was activated or not (Figures 2B, 6E–6I, S2D, S7C, and S7D). Furthermore, transit or secretion of another cargo protein (E-cadherin) was unaffected by deletion of ArfGAP2 (Figures 6I and S7A), suggesting that some routes of Golgi trafficking are unperturbed in these cells. These results suggest that only specific cargo, or perhaps one specific route of Golgi trafficking, is perturbed in STING-activated, ArfGAP2-deleted cells. BafA1, a V-ATPase inhibitor that makes the Golgi less acidic,62 was sufficient to restore normal transit of mCherry-TNF-α in STING-activated, ArfGAP2-deleted cells (Figures 6G, S6B, and S7B). This suggests that Golgi pH is one determinant of STING-mediated trafficking and sorting. Indeed, deacidification of the Golgi is well known to alter enzyme functions, protein cargo modifications, sorting, and secretion.25–27 Importantly, clathrin-mediated endosomal pathways that are involved in cytokine secretion exhibit slower trafficking rates than default secretory pathways,63–65 and a fast rate of Golgi transit can negatively impact sorting and post-translational modifications.66 In pulse-chase studies using click chemistry, we observed that TNF-α processing and secretion were slightly delayed in STING-activated, ArfGAP2-deleted cells (Figures S7E and S7F). We also found that STING activation in ArfGAP2 knockout cells reduces localization of the clathrin-adaptor protein AP1 at the trans-Golgi network (Figure 6J). Thus, ArfGAP2 stabilizes Golgi transit and secretion upon STING activation (Figure 6K).

Deletion of ArfGAP2 prevents autoinflammatory disease and immune cell activation in SAVI miceWe previously made the unexpected discovery that STING gain of function causes autoinflammatory disease independently of IRF3, IRF7, and IFNAR1 in mice.18,20 We and others also found that STING gain of function causes immune cell activation and hypersecretion of IFN-γ and chemokines that are associated with pathology, independent of the type I IFN receptor.18,20–23,67 Therefore, if ArfGAP2 is a proximal dual regulator of STING signaling and proton channel activity, we might expect reversal of a wider range of disease phenotypes in SAVI mice lacking ArfGAP2. Indeed, we found that deletion of ArfGAP2 in hematopoietic and endothelial cells strongly reduces inflammation and lung disease in SAVI mice (Figure 7A). Amelioration of lung disease was associated with ~4- and ~6-fold reductions in levels of IL-1β and IFN-γ (Figures 7B and 7C; Table S4), as well as ~170-, ~30-, and ~12-fold reductions in levels of Cxcl9, Cxcl10, and Ccl5 transcript, respectively, with corresponding reductions in chemokine protein levels in the lungs of SAVI Vav-Cre ArfGAP2f/f animals compared with SAVI ArfGAP2f/f mice without Cre (Figures 7D and 7E; Table S4).STING gain-of-function mutants are associated with activated T cell phenotypes and hyperproduction of IFN-γ,21,23 and so it is not surprising that one hallmark of SAVI mouse models is T cell-mediated lung inflammation.20,22 SAVI mice also exhibit a high percentage of cytokine-producing effector T cells, fewer naive T cells, and diminished numbers of Tregs.21 Even though IRF3 has been implicated in some STING-associated T cell phenotypes,68 we previously demonstrated that genetic deletion of IRF3 and IRF7 could not correct the T cell phenotypes under physiological conditions in SAVI animals.18 Although deletion of IRF3/7 or IFNAR1 does not protect SAVI mice, we found that ArfGAP2 deletion in SAVI animals restored Tregs (Figures 7F–7I), increased the frequency of naive and memory CD4+ T cells (Figures 7J and 7K), and decreased the frequency of CD4+ effector T cells (Figure 7L). ArfGAP2 deletion also increased the CD4:CD8 ratio of T cells in SAVI mice (Figures 7M–7O). Importantly, deletion of ArfGAP2 had no effect on the frequencies of T cells in mice expressing only WT STING (Figure S4). We conclude that ArfGAP2 promotes STING-mediated T cell phenotypes and autoinflammation in SAVI mice. Because the protection afforded by genetically deleting ArfGAP2 exceeds that of other STING pathway components, our results suggest that ArfGAP2 may be a highly effective and proximal therapeutic target for STING activation syndromes.

Our results revealed that ArfGAP2 can regulate both STING-mediated processes in a cell-type-specific manner. Indeed, ArfGAP2 is critical for STING-dependent cytokine secretion in some cell types, although ArfGAP2 is dispensable for cytokine secretion triggered by other pattern recognition receptors. The cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen; L3000001) diluted in OptiMEM (Thermo Fisher; 31985062) containing either the following combination of plasmids: (1) 2 μg of Str-KDEL-SBP-mCherry-TNF-α (Addgene; 65279) and 1 μg of LifeAct-BFP (generated by Dr. David Kast) or (2) 2 μg of Str-KDEL-SBP-EGFP-E-cadherin (Addgene; 65286) and 1 μg of LifeAct-scarlet (generated by Dr. David Kast). After 4 hours of incubation the media was replaced with fresh complete DMEM media.

Twenty-four hours after transfection, we replaced the media with Fluorobrite DMEM (Thermo Fisher; A1896701) containing 5% FBS, and 2 mM GlutaMAX. The “rush” was initiated by adding a final concentration of 50 μM D-biotin (Sigma; B4501). Time-lapse acquisition began immediately after the addition of D-biotin, at 37°C in a thermostat-controlled CO2 incubation chamber using a Nikon Ti2 inverted microscope with a 100× (1.4 NA) Plan-Apo oil immersion objective and a Yokogawa CSU-W1 spinning disk confocal. Image Z-stacks, consisting of 7 image planes, were acquired at an interval of 2 minutes for a duration of 60–100 minutes using the Nikon Elements Software package. The resulting 4-D image stacks were analyzed using Fiji (https://imagej.net/Fiji) by first subtracting the background and measuring the intensity profile of the sum slices projection at each time point in a region of interest (ROI) encircling the Golgi complex. These values were then normalized to the maximum intensity value and plotted for each cell for each experimental condition using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). Additionally, these trajectories were further analyzed by separating the time to reach the maximum intensity value (fast phase) and the time to return from maximum intensity value to the base line (slow phase).
