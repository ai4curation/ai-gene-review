CRX (cone-rod homeobox) is a key regulator of retinal photoreceptor development,
yet its human-specific functions remain poorly understood due to scarce human
retinal tissues and significant species differences. Here, we established a
human CRX-mCherry fluorescent reporter retinal organoid (RO) model to dissect
CRX-mediated gene regulation. Using FACS, RNA sequencing, and Cleavage Under
Targets and Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) sequencing, we identified CRX target genes
and revealed its dual regulatory role: it activates photoreceptor-specific genes
(e.g., RP1L1, linked to inherited retinal degeneration) in a dose-dependent
manner, while suppressing non-photoreceptor genes (e.g., PCDH8 and PROX1).
Notably, we first generated the human CRX CUT&Tag dataset, providing direct
insights into CRX's genome-wide regulatory landscape in photoreceptor cell
development. These findings demonstrate that CRX functions as both a
transcriptional activator and repressor, ensuring photoreceptor-specific gene
expression and preventing aberrant cell fate transitions. Our study provides
critical insights into the role of human CRX in retinal development and
implications for retinal degenerative diseases.

Introduction

In the retinal photoreceptor cells of mammals, a set of highly expressed transcription factors (TFs) is integral to their development and functional maintenance, with particular emphasis on specific TFs. For instance, RXRγ and THRB are predominantly expressed in cone photoreceptors (Deeb, 2006; Ng et al., 2001), while NRL and NR2E3 are primarily associated with rod photoreceptors (Mears et al., 2001; Milam et al., 2002). Additionally, OTX2 and CRX are expressed across all photoreceptor types (Chen et al., 1997; Furukawa et al., 1997; Nishida et al., 2003). Dysfunction of these TFs can lead to retinal diseases (Corbo and Cepko, 2005; Daniele et al., 2005; Ng et al., 2001; Roberts et al., 2005; Sato et al., 2007; Tran et al., 2014; Yamamoto et al., 2020), as they regulate a multitude of target genes, many of which are linked to retinal degenerative diseases (Hsiau et al., 2007). This establishes a gene expression network that is unique to photoreceptor cells (Hennig et al., 2008). A comprehensive understanding of these regulatory networks is essential for elucidating the mechanisms underlying retinal diseases.

CRX is one of the most important TFs during retinal development (Hennig et al., 2008; Sun and Chen, 2023; Swaroop et al., 2010). The expression of CRX occurs after that of OTX2 (Nishida et al., 2003), playing a crucial regulatory role in the development of photoreceptor cells and in maintaining their maturing state (Furukawa et al., 1997; Pan et al., 2023; Ruzycki et al., 2018). CRX regulates the development of retinal photoreceptor cells and is a common causative gene for inherited retinal diseases (IRDs) (Freund et al., 1997). Variations in CRX can lead to IRDs with different clinical phenotypes, including Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA), cone-rod dystrophy (CoRD), and retinitis pigmentosa (RP) (Tran et al., 2014). These diverse clinical phenotypes may be attributed to the characteristics of the CRX protein structure. CRX contains two important functional domains: the DNA binding domain (DBD) and the transactivation domain (TAD) (Freund et al., 1997; Tran et al., 2014). The DBD comprises a highly conserved homeodomain (HD) that demonstrates the capability to bind to DNA, whereas the TAD domain can recruit other coactivators (Corbo et al., 2010; Freund et al., 1997; Hennig et al., 2013; Peng et al., 2005; Peng and Chen, 2007; Tran et al., 2014). These two domains function synergistically to facilitate transcriptional activation. Over the past 2 decades, research has encompassed comprehensive investigations into the regulatory functions of CRX. This includes an in-depth analysis of CRX-bound elements (CBEs) (Chen et al., 1997; Corbo et al., 2010; Friedman et al., 2021; Ruzycki et al., 2018) as well as the regulatory impacts of CRX on its target genes. Recent studies have demonstrated that CRX functions not only as a transcriptional activator but also as a repressor (Hsiau et al., 2007; Peng et al., 2005; Pittler et al., 2004; White et al., 2016). By repressing cone-specific gene expression and activating rod-specific gene expression, CRX plays a crucial role in regulating the differentiation fate of rod photoreceptor cells (Hsiau et al., 2007; Peng et al., 2005; Pittler et al., 2004).

Previous studies have been largely constrained to animal models due to the scarcity of human retinal target tissues. Notably, although CRX is highly conserved in mammals, its spatiotemporal expression patterns differ between animals and humans (Bibb et al., 2001). There are several amino acid differences in DBD or TAD of CRX protein between these animal models and humans (Chen et al., 1997), which may result in distinct regulatory mechanisms for target genes, according to the latest studies (Shepherdson et al., 2024a, 2024b; Zheng et al., 2023, 2025). Consequently, it is imperative to conduct further research into the functional roles of human CRX.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) is a powerful method for studying protein-DNA interactions and has been widely applied to research TF interactions (Johnson et al., 2007), including those involving CRX. However, ChIP-seq typically requires a substantial cell input, exhibits a high background signal, resulting in a low signal-to-noise ratio and reduced resolution, which makes it susceptible to false positives. In contrast, the recently developed CUT&Tag (Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation) technology offers several advantages, such as requiring fewer cells and providing a high signal-to-noise ratio, making it particularly suitable for studies on a limited input (Kaya-Okur et al., 2019). Over the past decade, retinal organoids (ROs) derived from human iPSCs (hiPSCs) have been extensively utilized to investigate retinal diseases (Chirco et al., 2021; Kruczek et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2023). In this study, we utilized CUT&Tag sequencing (CUT&Tag-seq) and employed human RO tissues to examine the regulatory function of human CRX in photoreceptor development, thereby elucidating its role in comparison to animal models.

Moreover, CRX can function as a transcriptional repressor as well, downregulating the expression of specific non-photoreceptor retinal cell-associated TFs to preserve the expression patterns of photoreceptor cells, such as the RPC-specific markers SIX3 and MEIS1 (Heine et al., 2008) (Figures 2E, 2H, 2I, and 3C), the AC-specific markers TFAP2C and EBF3 (Bassett et al., 2012; Watanabe et al., 2015) (Figures 2E, 2J, 2K, and 3C), the RGC-specific marker POU6F2 (Sapkota et al., 2014) (Figures 2E, 2L, and 3C), and the HC-specific marker PROX1 (Figures 2E, 2M, and 3C). To further investigate the function of CRX in the development of photoreceptor cells, we conducted a comprehensive analysis by integrating CUT&Tag-seq and RNA-seq data. Venn diagrams were utilized to depict the target genes associated with CRX as identified in the CUT&Tag-seq data, in conjunction with the DEGs that were found in the RNA-seq analysis. This approach revealed an intersection of 333 genes that exhibited upregulation and 386 genes that demonstrated downregulation (Figure 3B). Furthermore, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these genes indicated that the upregulated genes were primarily associated with cilia development, the formation of inner and outer segments, light stimulation, and phototransduction pathways related to photoreceptor cells (Figure 3D). Conversely, the downregulated genes were predominantly linked to functions specific to other retinal cell types, including cell junctions, synapses, adhesion, and ion binding (Figure 3E).

These findings suggest that human CRX plays a pivotal role in the development of photoreceptor cells by not only enhancing the expression of genes critical for the structure and metabolism of photoreceptor cells but also by acting as a transcriptional repressor to inhibit the expression of non-photoreceptor retinal genes, thereby facilitating the specialization and maturation of photoreceptor cells.

DiscussionTo dissect the transcriptional regulatory network of human CRX in photoreceptor development and generate high-quality profiles of both CRX-mediated chromatin-binding and gene expression, we established a CRISPR-Cas9-edited CRX-mCherry fluorescent reporter hiPSC line and differentiated it into ROs. This model was pivotal for acquiring paired RNA-seq and CUT&Tag-seq datasets: mCherry enabled real-time tracking of CRX expression dynamics across RO development and facilitated FACS-based isolation of pure photoreceptor populations, ensuring the cellular homogeneity critical for reliable transcriptional (RNA-seq) and chromatin-binding (CUT&Tag-seq) analyses. Leveraging this validated system, we generated the first CRX-bound CUT&Tag-seq dataset from human retinal tissues, and by integrating it with photoreceptor-specific RNA-seq data, established the first comprehensive repertoire of human CRX-regulated targets. These integrated datasets not only filled the gap in human-specific CRX chromatin-binding and transcriptional regulatory information but also provided a robust foundation for subsequent functional validation, linking CRX binding events to tangible gene expression outcomes.Through CUT&Tag-seq analysis, we identified two CRX-bound motifs in human retinal tissues that exhibit conservation with those observed in mice, contrasting with previous findings from animal model studies using ChIP-seq. This conservation likely stems from the complete identity of the HD domain (a main part of DBD) between human CRX and animal counterparts (Figure S9A), while observed differences may be attributed to divergence in the TAD and other regions of the DBD. Significantly, we established the first comprehensive profile of CRX-regulated target genes and associated pathways in human retinal tissues, providing a valuable reference for future investigations into CRX-related retinal diseases. Our dual-luciferase assay revealed RP1L1 and PCDH8 as novel direct targets of CRX, underscoring its dual role in transcriptional activation and repression (promoting RP1L1 expression while inhibiting PCDH8). Notably, RP1L1, which shares homology with RP1, has been implicated in IRDs. To dissect the transcriptional regulatory network of human CRX in photoreceptor development and generate high-quality profiles of both CRX-mediated chromatin-binding and gene expression, we established a CRISPR-Cas9-edited CRX-mCherry fluorescent reporter hiPSC line and differentiated it into ROs. This model was pivotal for acquiring paired RNA-seq and CUT&Tag-seq datasets: mCherry enabled real-time tracking of CRX expression dynamics across RO development and facilitated FACS-based isolation of pure photoreceptor populations, ensuring the cellular homogeneity critical for reliable transcriptional (RNA-seq) and chromatin-binding (CUT&Tag-seq) analyses. Leveraging this validated system, we generated the first CRX-bound CUT&Tag-seq dataset from human retinal tissues, and by integrating it with photoreceptor-specific RNA-seq data, established the first comprehensive repertoire of human CRX-regulated targets. These integrated datasets not only filled the gap in human-specific CRX chromatin-binding and transcriptional regulatory information but also provided a robust foundation for subsequent functional validation, linking CRX binding events to tangible gene expression outcomes.

Through CUT&Tag-seq analysis, we identified two CRX-bound motifs in human retinal tissues that exhibit conservation with those observed in mice, contrasting with previous findings from animal model studies using ChIP-seq. This conservation likely stems from the complete identity of the HD domain (a main part of DBD) between human CRX and animal counterparts (Figure S9A), while observed differences may be attributed to divergence in the TAD and other regions of the DBD. Significantly, we established the first comprehensive profile of CRX-regulated target genes and associated pathways in human retinal tissues, providing a valuable reference for future investigations into CRX-related retinal diseases. Our dual-luciferase assay revealed RP1L1 and PCDH8 as novel direct targets of CRX, underscoring its dual role in transcriptional activation and repression (promoting RP1L1 expression while inhibiting PCDH8). Notably, RP1L1, which shares homology with RP1, has been implicated in IRDs. These findings offer mechanistic insights into how CRX mutations can lead to a spectrum of overlapping yet distinct clinical phenotypes, including LCA, RP, and CoRD. The observed phenotypic variability likely reflects differential regulation of target genes by CRX, leading to diverse pathogenic outcomes.

Moreover, our investigation revealed that CRX exerts inhibitory effects on several cell-specific transcriptional factors associated with non-photoreceptor lineages. Through the generation of CRX-KO hiPSCs and ROs, we observed distinct regulatory patterns: in CRX-WT ROs, CRX effectively suppressed the expression of PROX1, a marker for HC, while this suppression was markedly attenuated in CRX-KO ROs. Intriguingly, Prox1 was found to be upregulated across various mouse photoreceptor injury models (Lee et al., 2025), implying its potential role as a stress-responsive factor in retina self-protection. These findings highlight the crucial role of human CRX in repressing retinal cell type-specific genes, a mechanism essential for proper photoreceptor cell development and maintenance. While previous studies have established the pivotal role of CRX in determining cone and rod photoreceptor cell fates through the activation and repression of key photoreceptor genes (Hsiau et al., 2007; Peng et al., 2005; Pittler et al., 2004; White et al., 2016), our study provides the first experimental evidence demonstrating that CRX plays a crucial role in maintaining photoreceptor cell identity through the transcriptional repression of non-photoreceptor lineage genes.

Our CUT&Tag-seq analysis identified striking differences in the genomic binding patterns between human CRX and mouse Crx. For instance, regarding core TFs, specifically, mouse Crx primarily targets the promoter regions of core photoreceptor TFs such as Nrl and Nr2e3 to drive transcriptional activation (Corbo et al., 2010), whereas human CRX exhibits numerous binding sites only in non-canonical regulatory regions, including core TFs such as PRDM1, RXRG, THRB, NRL, and NR2E3. The canonical role of TFs is to bind to the promoters of target genes, recruit RNA polymerase II, and directly determine whether transcription of the gene is initiated (Latchman, 1997; Roeder, 1996). While CRX-mediated regulation involves enhancer activation and chromatin remodeling (Ruzycki et al., 2018), we speculate that human CRX may recruit histone acetyltransferases (HATs) or chromatin remodelers to intronic or exonic regions, thereby modulating the accessibility of distal regulatory elements or exon-exon junctions to shape gene expression. This mechanism explains the correlation between CRX binding to non-promoter regions and the upregulation of target TFs observed in our RNA-seq data. Importantly, this regional specificity likely enables nuanced control of photoreceptor gene networks through enhancer activation, chromatin remodeling, and potential post-transcriptional regulation—a mechanism that has also been reported for other TFs such as TP53 and CMYC (Wojtyś and Oroń, 2023)—highlighting the importance of studying human models to understand human retinal development and disease.

While our study advances understanding of human CRX regulation, there exist several limitations. First, although our analysis suggested potential CRX regulation of GNB3, IGSF21, and SOX4, subsequent dual-luciferase assays failed to detect corresponding regulatory responses even with increasing CRX dosage, likely due to the use of non-physiological HEK293T cells, which lack the native co-factor pool and chromatin context required for CRX function, such as coactivators like CBP, P300, NRL, and NR2E3 (Corbo et al., 2010; Hennig et al., 2013; Peng et al., 2005; Peng and Chen, 2007; Sun and Chen, 2023). Second, we lack validation of the causal role of intronic or exonic CRX binding sites in gene regulation (e.g., via mutagenesis experiments). Therefore, further experiments are required to clarify and validate the distinct functions of CBRs in different genomic regions.

In summary, our study elucidates the dual regulatory role of human CRX in photoreceptor development through a combination of advanced genomic and cellular techniques. Notably, our work represents the first to generate CRX-bound CUT&Tag-seq profile from human retinal tissue, characterize the regulatory network of human CRX, and perform cross-species comparison of CRX target genes between humans and mice, uncovering several differences in CRX-mediated regulatory mechanisms across these species. These findings not only deepen our understanding of the transcriptional mechanisms of CRX but also provide critical insights into the molecular basis of CRX-associated retinal degenerative diseases. More validation will be needed to further verify the CRX target genes revealed in this study. Future studies should explore the functional significance of CRX binding in non-canonical regulatory regions and investigate its interactions with co-factors to fully unravel the complexities of its regulatory network. This work lays a foundation for developing targeted therapeutic strategies for retinal disorders linked to CRX dysfunction.

Peak calling analysis was performed across the entire genome using MACS2 analysis software (Zhang et al., 2008), with a threshold set at false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05. The ChIPseeker R package (Yu et al., 2015) was used to perform genome-wide element annotation on the peaks, the peak location information on the genome and the sequence information of the peak regions were analyzed to filter out peak-related genes. (Genes with peaks located within 2 kb upstream of the transcription start sites (TSSs), the gene body, and 2 kb downstream of the transcription termination sites (TTSs) were defined as peak-associated genes.) Subsequently, differential peak analysis was performed using the DiffBind software (Stark and Brown, 2011), with the screening criteria of |log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR ≤0.05 to identify differential peaks. Visualization of the peaks was accomplished using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) software (Robinson et al., 2011). Motif analysis was conducted using the MEME Suite (http://meme-suite.org/), employing MEME-ChIP to detect significant motif sequences within the peak sequences. In the GO enrichment analysis of peak-related genes, entries with FDR <0.05 were identified as significantly enriched.
