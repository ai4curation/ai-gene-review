## Full Text

Abstract

The multisubunit RNA polymerase (RNAP) in bacteria consists of a catalytically active core enzyme (α 2 ββ′ω) complexed with a σ factor that is required for promoter-specific transcription initiation. During early elongation the stability of interactions between σ and core decreases, in part because of the nascent RNA-mediated destabilization of an interaction between region 4 of σ and the flap domain of the β-subunit (β-flap). The nascent RNA-mediated destabilization of the σ region 4/β-flap interaction is required for the bacteriophage λ Q antiterminator protein (λQ) to engage the RNAP holoenzyme. Here, we provide an explanation for this requirement by showing that λQ establishes direct contact with the β-flap during the engagement process, thus competing with σ 70 region 4 for access to the β-flap. We also show that λQ's affinity for the β-flap is calibrated to ensure that λQ activity is restricted to the λ late promoter P R′ . Specifically, we find that strengthening the λQ/β-flap interaction allows λQ to bypass the requirement for specific cis -acting sequence elements, a λQ-DNA binding site and a RNAP pause-inducing element, that normally ensure λQ is recruited exclusively to transcription complexes associated with P R′ . Our findings demonstrate that the β-flap can serve as a direct target for regulators of elongation.

Keywords: sigma, transcription elongation

The bacterial RNA polymerase (RNAP) holoenzyme consists of a catalytically active core enzyme (α 2 ββ′ω) in complex with a σ factor, which confers on the core enzyme the ability to initiate promoter-specific transcription (reviewed in ref. 1 ). In the context of the RNAP holoenzyme, σ 70 (the primary σ factor in Escherichia coli ) contacts the conserved −10 and −35 promoter elements. All primary σ factors share four regions of conserved sequence (regions 1–4) ( 2 ). Regions 2 and 4 contain DNA-binding domains responsible for recognition of the promoter −10 element and −35 element, respectively. Holoenzyme formation depends on an interaction between σ 70 region 2 and a coiled-coil motif in the β′-subunit (the β′ coiled coil) ( 3 , 4 ); this interaction is also required for σ 70 to make functional contact with the promoter −10 element ( 5 ). Interaction between σ 70 region 4 and the flap domain of the β-subunit (the β-flap), although not required for holoenzyme formation, is required for contact with the promoter −35 element ( 6 ). In particular, the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction properly positions σ 70 region 4 with respect to σ 70 region 2, thus enabling σ 70 region 4 and σ 70 region 2 to simultaneously contact promoter elements separated by ≈17 bp ( 6 ).

During early elongation the stability of interactions between σ 70 and core RNAP decreases (reviewed in ref. 7 ), in part because of the nascent RNA-mediated destabilization of the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction ( 8 ). Specifically, σ 70 region 4, when bound to the β-flap, is positioned within the path of the nascent RNA as it first emerges from the RNA exit channel (the channel through which the nascent RNA is extruded during transcription elongation) ( 9 , 10 ) at a nascent RNA length of ≈16 nt ( 8 , 11 – 13 ). Thus, steric clash between the emerging nascent RNA and σ 70 region 4 destabilizes the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction ( 8 , 14 ). Prior work indicates that this nascent RNA-mediated destabilization of the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction facilitates the process by which the bacteriophage λ Q antiterminator protein (λQ) gains access to RNAP during early elongation ( 14 , 15 ).

An operon-specific elongation factor that controls the expression of the phage λ late genes, λQ has served as an important model for achieving a mechanistic understanding of the regulation of RNAP function during transcription elongation (reviewed in ref. 16 ). Through its association with RNAP, λQ renders RNAP resistant to transcription pausing and insensitive to downstream termination signals (reviewed in ref. 16 ). Although these effects occur in the context of a highly stable λQ-containing elongation complex ( 17 – 19 ), λQ requires two specialized cis -acting sequences associated with the λ late promoter λP R′ to gain access to the transcription complex: a Q binding element (QBE) and a pause-inducing element (reviewed in ref. 16 ) ( Fig. 1 A ). The pause-inducing element resembles a promoter −10 element and pausing of RNAP (which occurs at a nascent RNA length of 16 or 17 nt) is mediated by interaction between σ 70 region 2 and this −10-like element ( 20 ) ( Fig. 1 B ). Interactions that occur between λQ bound to the QBE and the paused transcription complex facilitate λQ's stable association with the elongation complex ( 21 ). Among these is an interaction between λQ and σ 70 region 4, which stabilizes a conformation of the RNAP holoenzyme in which σ 70 region 4 is displaced from the β-flap ( 15 , 22 ) ( Fig. 1 C ).

Fig. 1.

Here, we demonstrate that during its initial engagement with the RNAP holoenzyme λQ establishes direct contact with the β-flap. Thus, our findings provide an explanation for the mechanistic requirement that σ 70 region 4 be displaced from the β-flap during the λQ engagement process ( 14 , 15 , 22 ). In addition, we demonstrate that strengthening the λQ/β-flap interaction allows λQ to bypass the requirements for the QBE and the pause-inducing element in the engagement process. This finding indicates that λQ's affinity for the β-flap is calibrated to help ensure that λQ acts exclusively on transcription complexes associated with the λ late promoter P R′ .

Results

λQ Interacts with the β-Flap.

The finding that λQ's initial engagement with the holoenzyme requires the displacement of σ 70 region 4 from the β-flap raised the possibility that λQ competes with σ 70 region 4 for access to the β-flap. As an initial test of this hypothesis we used a bacterial two-hybrid assay ( 23 ) ( Fig. 2 A ) to determine whether λQ directly contacts the β-flap (residues 831-1057 of β). Using this assay, we detected a weak, but significant, interaction between λQ and the β-flap ( Fig. 2 B ). We next tested the Q proteins of several other lambdoid phage (21Q, 80Q and 82Q), all of which share a conserved mechanism of action with λQ (reviewed in ref. 16 ). Each of these Q proteins also interacted with the β-flap, and the magnitude of the interaction observed was higher in each case than that observed with λQ ( Fig. 2 B ). Taken together, these data suggest that λQ, 21Q, 80Q, and 82Q interact with the β-flap.

Fig. 2.

We next tested whether the Q proteins and σ 70 region 4 contact the same surface of the β-flap. To do this, we tested the effect of removing the flap-tip helix (β residues 900–909), the primary target of σ 70 region 4 ( 24 ). Removal of the flap-tip helix abolished the interaction between the β-flap and each of the Q proteins ( Fig. 2 B ) and, as expected, abolished the interaction between the β-flap and σ 70 region 4 [which can also be detected in the bacterial two-hybrid assay ( 6 , 8 )] [ supporting information (SI) Fig. S1 ]. These results imply that σ 70 region 4 and the Q proteins contact the same surface of the β-flap (i.e., participate in mutually exclusive interactions with the β-flap).

Isolation of Amino Acid Substitutions That Strengthen or Weaken the λQ/β-Flap Interaction.

We next sought to identify amino acid substitutions that alter the strength of the λQ/β-flap interaction. First, we performed a screen to identify substitutions in λQ that strengthen its interaction with the β-flap (see SI Text ). From this screen we identified three individual substitutions (T101I, A160V, and T165A) that modestly increased the strength of the λQ/β-flap interaction ( Fig. 2 C ). We next combined these three substitutions to generate a protein we call λQ***, which interacted significantly more strongly with the β-flap than did WT λQ ( Fig. 2 C ).

Next, we sought to identify substitutions in the β-flap that weaken its interaction with λQ. To facilitate the identification of such substitutions, we carried out the screen by using λQ*** instead of WT λQ. Because we found that removing the flap-tip helix eliminated the λQ/β-flap interaction ( Fig. 2 B ), we reasoned that we would be able to identify substitutions in the flap-tip helix that weaken the λQ/β-flap interaction.

We showed previously that disrupting the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction facilitates the ability of λQ to engage RNAP at λP R′ ( 14 ). In principle, therefore, interpreting the effects of substitutions that disrupt both the λQ/β-flap interaction and the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction might be complicated (i.e., any possible inhibitory effect of disrupting the λQ/β-flap interaction might be masked by a compensatory effect of disrupting the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction). For this reason, we sought to identify at least one substitution that disrupted the λQ/β-flap interaction without affecting the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction.

We introduced alanine substitutions at all positions between 900–909 (except 904, which is already an alanine) and assayed the effects of these substitutions on the λQ/β-flap interaction ( Fig. 2 D and data not shown) and the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction ( Fig. S1 and data not shown). From this analysis, we identified two substitutions, I905A and F906A, that strongly disrupted both the λQ/β-flap interaction ( Fig. 2 D ) and σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction ( Fig. S1 ) and one substitution G907A that weakened the λQ/β-flap interaction ( Fig. 2 D ) and did not affect the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction ( Fig. S1 ). We next randomized the codon for residue 907 and found that substitution G907K had the largest effect on the λQ/β-flap interaction without significantly affecting the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction ( Fig. 2 D and Fig. S1 ). For our subsequent analysis, we used the G907K substitution (for its preferential effect on the λQ/β-flap interaction) and the pair of disruptive substitutions I905A and F906A [which, when combined, essentially abolished both the λQ/β-flap and the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interactions, as detected in the two-hybrid assay ( Fig. 2 D and Fig. S1 )].

Effects of Weakening the λQ/β-Flap Interaction in Vitro .

We next tested the effect of weakening the λQ/β-flap interaction on λQ antitermination function in vitro . To do this, we purified wild-type RNAP and two mutants: β I905A/F906A RNAP and β G907K RNAP. Because the β-flap has been implicated in the process of intrinsic termination ( 25 ), we first tested whether the substitutions in the flap-tip helix inhibited the ability of RNAP to respond to an intrinsic terminator. Using these RNAPs and a λP R′ template bearing the natural λ late terminator t R′ , we performed single-round in vitro transcription assays. We found that for WT RNAP and both of the mutant RNAPs (β I905A/F906A RNAP and β G907K RNAP) essentially all of the RNAP molecules terminated at t R′ ( Fig. 3 and data not shown). Next, we performed reactions in the presence of a range of λQ concentrations and measured λQ antitermination function by calculating the percentage of full-length transcripts (percentage read-through) emanating from λP R′ . We observed no difference in the amount of λQ-dependent read-through in reactions performed with WT RNAP, β I905A/F906A RNAP, or β G907K RNAP (data not shown).

Fig. 3.

Several lines of evidence suggest that multiple interactions stabilize the initial complex that forms when λQ binds to the QBE and engages RNAP ( 15 , 21 ). Thus we reasoned that it might be necessary to perform the assays under conditions that destabilize the λQ-engaged transcription complex to reveal the effects of substitutions that weaken the λQ/β-flap interaction. Therefore, we performed assays using a λP R′ template that contained a single base pair substitution in the QBE (G–25C) that weakens the interaction between λQ and the QBE ( 26 ) ( Fig. 3 A ). When reactions were performed with this template, weakening the λQ/β-flap interaction resulted in a reduction in λQ-dependent antitermination ( Fig. 3 B ). In particular, when reactions were performed with β G907K RNAP, λQ-dependent read-through was slightly reduced compared with reactions performed with WT RNAP, and when reactions were performed with β I905A/F906A RNAP, λQ-dependent read-through was significantly reduced ( Fig. 3 B ).

Effects of Strengthening the λQ/β-Flap Interaction in Vivo .

We next tested the effect of strengthening the λQ/β-flap interaction on λQ-dependent antitermination in vivo . To do this, we used a reporter strain carrying a λP R′ -t R′ - lacZ fusion ( Fig. 4 A ) and assayed the ability of WT λQ or λQ*** to induce the expression of lacZ (which provides a measure of λQ's antitermination activity). We found that λQ*** exhibited slightly higher antitermination activity than WT λQ ( Fig. 4 A ). Because we had found that it was necessary to measure λQ-dependent antitermination under suboptimal conditions to reveal the effects of weakening the λQ/β-flap interaction, we tested what effect strengthening the λQ/β-flap interaction had under suboptimal conditions.

Fig. 4.

We first used the mutant λP R′ - lacZ reporter construct containing the G–25C base pair substitution in the QBE. On this mutant λP R′ - lacZ reporter, λQ*** was ≈2-fold more active than WT λQ in mediating terminator read-through (data not shown). Next, we used a λP R′ - lacZ reporter construct containing two base pair substitutions in the QBE (G–25C, T–22G) that, together, nearly eliminate the binding of λQ to the QBE ( 18 , 26 ). On this mutant λP R′ template (ΔQBE), λQ*** was ≈6-fold more active than WT λQ in mediating terminator read-through (i.e., WT λQ and λQ*** caused 2.5- and 14.6-fold increases in lacZ expression, respectively) ( Fig. 4 A ). Western blot analysis indicated that WT λQ and λQ*** were present at comparable levels in the cell (data not shown). Thus, strengthening the λQ/β-flap interaction suppresses the effect of substitutions in the QBE that disrupt λQ binding.

We next tested whether strengthening the λQ/β-flap interaction might also enable λQ to bypass the requirement for a pause-inducing element. To this end, we tested a λP R′ - lacZ reporter construct that contained base pair substitutions in the pause-inducing element (A+2G, T+6G) that eliminate σ 70 -dependent pausing ( 20 , 21 , 27 ). On this mutant λP R′ - lacZ template (Δpause) we found that λQ*** was ≈3-fold more active than WT λQ in mediating terminator read-through ( Fig. 4 A ). Finally, on a λP R′ - lacZ reporter construct containing base pair substitutions in both the QBE and pause-inducing element (ΔQBE Δpause), we found that λQ*** was ≈5-fold more active than WT λQ ( Fig. 4 A ). These results suggest that strengthening the λQ/β-flap interaction enables λQ to efficiently engage RNAP in a manner that requires no specific cis -acting sequence elements in vivo .

To confirm the ability of λQ*** to engage RNAP without the requirement for specific cis -acting sequence elements, we constructed a reporter in which a heterologous promoter, p lacUV5 , was placed upstream of tandem t R′ terminators with a downstream lacZ gene ( Fig. 4 B ). Once again, λQ*** was significantly more active (≈7-fold) than WT λQ in mediating terminator read-through ( Fig. 4 B ). [The use of tandem terminators significantly enhances the sensitivity of the assay, revealing that even WT λQ can functionally engage RNAP independently of the QBE and pause-inducing element, although not nearly as efficiently as λQ*** ( Fig. 4 B ).]

Effects of Strengthening the λQ/β-Flap Interaction in Vitro .

We next tested the effect of strengthening the λQ/β-flap interaction on λQ-dependent antitermination in vitro . When single-round in vitro transcription assays were performed with the WT λP R′ template at high concentrations of λQ or λQ***, we observed similar amounts of λQ-dependent readthrough (≈55%; Fig. 5 ). In contrast, when the assays were performed by using the λP R′ ΔQBE Δpause template, WT λQ did not increase terminator read-through compared with reactions performed in the absence of λQ (≈1% read-through was observed in each case; Fig. 5 ), whereas λQ*** resulted in ≈10% read-through ( Fig. 5 ). Thus, strengthening the λQ/β-flap interaction allows λQ to efficiently engage RNAP in a manner that requires no specific cis -acting sequence elements in vitro .

Discussion

Here, we demonstrate that λQ establishes direct contact with the β-flap as it engages the RNAP holoenzyme during early elongation at λP R′ . Our findings imply that λQ competes with σ 70 region 4 for access to the β-flap, thus explaining why the nascent RNA-mediated destabilization of the σ 70 region 4/β-flap interaction is required for λQ to engage the transcription complex. We also show that λQ's affinity for the β-flap is an important determinant that controls λQ's access to the transcription complex. Specifically, we find that strengthening the λQ/β-flap interaction allows λQ to bypass requirements for the QBE and the pause-inducing element in the engagement process.

The Role of the λQ/β-Flap Interaction.

Because we found that it was necessary to destabilize the binding of λQ to the QBE (by introduction of the G–25C mutation) to detect the destabilizing effect of weakening the λQ/β-flap interaction ( Fig. 3 B ), we infer that destabilizing the λQ/β-flap interaction compromises events that occur when λQ is still bound to the QBE, i.e., the process by which λQ engages RNAP at P R′ . Consistent with the idea that contact between λQ and the β-flap first occurs while λQ is bound to the QBE, we were able to detect an interaction between DNA-bound 21Q (the Q that interacts most strongly with the β-flap in the context of the two-hybrid assay; Fig. 2 B ) and the β-flap by using a one-hybrid assay ( Fig. S3 ).

Conversely, because we did not detect an effect of destabilizing the λQ/β-flap interaction when we used a WT λ P R′ template in vitro (data not shown), we infer that, under the conditions of our in vitro transcription assays, the substitutions tested did not have a disruptive effect on antitermination per se . Nevertheless, we suspect that interactions between λQ and the β-flap, once established during λQ's initial engagement with RNAP, are maintained during elongation. In support of this idea, experiments performed with static elongation complexes indicate that 82Q (along with the cofactor NusA) form a “protective barrier” around the nascent RNA transcript as the RNA emerges from the RNA exit channel ( 19 ). These findings suggest that 82Q is positioned near the exterior opening of the RNA exit channel in the context of the elongation complex. Because the β-flap largely defines the RNA exit channel and the nascent RNA actually emerges from underneath the β-flap ( 13 ), these results suggest that 82Q (and, by analogy, λQ) is positioned near the β-flap in the context of the elongation complex ( 19 ).

We propose that our inability to detect any effects of destabilizing the λQ/β-flap interaction on the efficiency of λQ-mediated antitermination per se may reflect the stability of λQ's association with the elongation complex ( 18 ). Thus, λQ may form an extended network of interactions with the β-flap and possibly with surrounding surfaces and/or the nascent RNA. Consequently, our in vitro transcription assays may not have been sensitive enough to reveal effects of the amino acid substitutions used in this work.

The β-flap is well positioned to influence transcription pausing and termination by interacting directly with the nascent RNA ( 12 , 13 ). Consistent with this idea, cross-linking experiments suggest that the hairpin structure that forms in the nascent RNA during hairpin-dependent pausing interacts with the β-flap ( 28 , 29 ). Furthermore, removal of the β-flap can impair the ability of RNAP to respond to intrinsic terminators ( 25 , 30 ). These considerations have led to the proposal that the β-flap may be targeted by regulatory factors, like Q, that affect the termination properties of RNAP ( 29 ). When bound to the β-flap, Q might inhibit termination by a passive mechanism, creating an obstruction or barrier that prevents either the nascent RNA or a domain of RNAP from adopting a termination competent conformation ( 19 ). Alternatively, Q might inhibit termination by actively restructuring the incipient termination complex. Because the β-flap is a conserved feature of all multisubunit RNAPs, it is likely to serve as a contact site for regulators that modify the elongation properties of RNAP in other organisms, as well.

Conversion of λQ to a Global Regulator.

We found that strengthening the λQ/β-flap interaction enables λQ to access the transcription complex in a manner that requires no specific cis -acting sequences (the QBE and the pause-inducing element) ( Figs. 4 and 5 ). The requirements for the QBE and the pause-inducing element ensure that λQ is recruited exclusively to transcription complexes associated with λP R′ . Thus strengthening the interaction between λQ and the β-flap allows λQ to efficiently associate with transcription complexes that have initiated transcription from promoters other than λP R′ ( Fig. 4 B ). These findings suggest that the strength of the λQ/β-flap interaction is calibrated to ensure that λQ activity is restricted to λP R′ .
