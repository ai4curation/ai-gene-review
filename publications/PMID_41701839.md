Pathological progression in sporadic Alzheimer's disease (sAD) initiates with an
early rise in soluble amyloid-β (Aβ), preceding plaque formation and
neurodegeneration. However, the molecular event triggering this initial
accumulation remains unknown. We report that phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase
(PHGDH), a consistent biomarker of prodromal sAD, drives Aβ production through a
previously unrecognized RNA-binding function. Specifically, PHGDH binds the
3'UTR of EIF2AK1 mRNA, enabling the physical interaction between PHGDH and the
EIF2AK1 protein. By facilitating the recruitment of EIF2AK1 to its substrate
EIF2α, this complex drives EIF2α phosphorylation, thereby selectively promoting
the translation of BACE1, the rate-limiting enzyme for Aβ generation. We
demonstrate that PHGDH overexpression elevates BACE1 protein and intracellular
Aβ in neurons and astrocytes across mouse models and human brain organoids,
independent of its canonical enzymatic or transcriptional roles.
Mechanistically, this process requires a specific RNA-binding surface within
PHGDH and the EIF2AK1 3'UTR. These findings define a PHGDH-EIF2AK1-EIF2α-BACE1
axis as a key driver of the earliest amyloid pathology in sAD.

## Full Text

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder worldwide. Sporadic Alzheimer’s disease (sAD), accounting for approximately 95% of AD cases, remains poorly understood (1). Patients with sAD typically lack mutations in the established disease-causing genes (e.g., APP, PSEN1, PSEN2, MAPT) and may not carry common risk alleles such as those in APOE and TREM2 genes, thus complicating efforts to identify consistent molecular mechanisms underlying the disease. Consequently, increasing attention has shifted toward the prodromal phase of sAD—before overt clinical symptoms appear—as this period may expose early pathological changes and offer important insights for developing effective therapeutic strategies.

A critical event in the prodromal phase of AD is the accumulation of intracellular amyloid-beta (iAβ), which disrupts cellular homeostasis prior to extracellular amyloid plaque formation and pathological tau propagation (2, 3). Elevated iAβ contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction (4), oxidative stress (5, 6), impairment of glutamatergic synapses (7), neuroinflammation (8), and ultimately extracellular amyloid deposition (9).

iAβ is produced through sequential cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP), initially by β-secretase (BACE1) and subsequently by γ-secretase (10, 11). BACE1 frequently acts as the rate-limiting enzyme in APP processing due to its cell-type specificity and dynamic expression regulated by the cellular state, in contrast to the ubiquitously expressed γ-secretase (12, 13). BACE1 expression is predominantly controlled at the translational level, a process critically governed by phosphorylated EIF2α (p-EIF2α). While p-EIF2α generally suppresses translation initiation, it selectively enhances the translation of specific mRNAs, including BACE1. EIF2α phosphorylation is catalyzed by a kinase family comprising EIF2AK1 (HRI), EIF2AK2 (PKR), EIF2AK3 (PERK), and EIF2AK4 (GCN2). However, the upstream regulators triggering these kinases during iAβ accumulation, especially within a genetically normal context, remain largely unknown—a critical knowledge gap in the understanding of sAD pathology.

Recent studies have identified phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) as a robust biomarker positively correlated with the prodromal stage of AD; it exhibits elevated expression prior to the onset of clinical symptom, and its progressively increasing levels are associated with disease severity (SI Appendix, Table S1) (14, 15). Single-cell analyses of human sAD brains revealed increased PHGDH expression across all major cell types—including astrocytes, excitatory neurons, and inhibitory neurons—compared to healthy controls (15, 16). Neurons have substantially lower baseline PHGDH levels relative to astrocytes (15, 17), directing previous research primarily toward astrocytic PHGDH. We previously demonstrated that astrocyte-specific PHGDH overexpression increases the total (intracellular and extracellular) amyloid-beta (Aβ) levels and other AD pathologies in both mouse models and human brain organoids, independent of familial AD mutations or APOE4 genotype (18, 19). Conversely, suppressing astrocytic PHGDH reduces total Aβ burden, as well as the associated synaptic loss and cell death, establishing a causal link between increased astrocytic PHGDH expression and amyloid pathology as well as its neurodegenerative consequences (19).

Intriguingly, this pathological role of PHGDH is independent of its canonical enzymatic activity in serine biosynthesis (18, 19). Instead, it partly involves a recently identified transcription factor role of PHGDH that promotes astrocytic expression of IKKa and HMGB1, thereby activating the mTOR and NF-κB signaling pathways and subsequently increasing total Aβ accumulation (19). However, this astrocytic mechanism alone does not fully explain the observed increase in total Aβ, as simultaneous suppression of astrocytic IKKa and HMGB1 only partially ameliorates the total Aβ induced by PHGDH overexpression (19). Additionally, this astrocytic pathway does not explain the initial neuronal production of intracellular Aβ (iAβ), an essential early step in amyloid pathology (20, 21).

The present study reveals a previously unrecognized RNA-binding function of PHGDH that increases iAβ production. We have shown that in both neurons and astrocytes, PHGDH binds directly to the 3′UTR of EIF2AK1 mRNA, enhancing EIF2AK1–EIF2α interaction and facilitating EIF2AK1-mediated phosphorylation of EIF2α. Increased EIF2α phosphorylation subsequently promotes BACE1 protein expression, ultimately elevating iAβ levels. These findings define a mechanistic pathway underlying the early accumulation of iAβ in sAD.

The final step to understanding PHGDH’s role is to elucidate how it facilitates the EIF2AK1–EIF2α interaction and why its RNA binding surface is required. To address this, we first examined whether PHGDH binding to EIF2AK1’s 3′UTR enhances PHGDH–EIF2AK1 protein interaction. Previous research indicates that mRNA 3′UTRs can mediate interactions between RNA-binding proteins and proteins newly translated from the same mRNA (39). Thus, we hypothesized that PHGDH binding to EIF2AK1’s 3′UTR enables direct protein–protein interaction between PHGDH and EIF2AK1.

Co-IP experiments demonstrated an interaction between PHGDH-WT and EIF2AK1 (Fig. 3F), supporting our hypothesis. However, co-IP did not detect interaction between PHGDH-dRBD and EIF2AK1 (Fig. 3F), underscoring the essential role of PHGDH’s RNA binding surface in facilitating the PHGDH–EIF2AK1 interaction.

To evaluate the necessity of EIF2AK1’s 3′UTR, we expressed EIF2AK1 either as a coding sequence alone (CDS) or in combination with its 3′UTR (CDS-3′UTR), along with PHGDH-WT in N2a and U87MG cells. Co-IP assays demonstrated that PHGDH interacted exclusively with EIF2AK1 expressed from the CDS-3′UTR construct, but not from the CDS alone (Fig. 3G). Collectively, these data underscore the necessity of both PHGDH’s RNA binding surface and EIF2AK1’s 3′UTR for mediating their protein–protein interaction.

Additionally, prior studies have identified interactions between PHGDH and the EIF2 complex (40), proposing a model in which PHGDH promotes EIF2AK1–EIF2α interaction by interacting with both proteins. Supporting this model, co-IP analyses in PHGDH-overexpressing N2a and U87MG cells confirmed that PHGDH coprecipitates with both EIF2AK1 and EIF2α (Fig. 3 F and H). Finally, given that PHGDH-dRBD retained interaction with EIF2α (Fig. 3F), we conclude that PHGDH’s RNA binding surface is dispensable for engaging EIF2α, but essential for mediating interaction with EIF2AK1.

Taken together, our data define a PHGDH–EIF2AK1–EIF2α–BACE1 axis in which PHGDH’s RNA-binding activity promotes iAβ accumulation (Fig. 3H), thus providing a molecular explanation for the earliest rise of Aβ in sporadic Alzheimer’s disease.

DiscussionA Common Mediator Model For Aβ Elevation in Sporadic AD.Diverse environmental factors have been linked to the multistage progression of AD, including its early-stage indication, elevated soluble Aβ, which precedes the formation of amyloid plaques, tau tangles, and neuronal loss. However, how these diverse stimuli converge to induce this early increase remains unclear.We hypothesize that a common mediator responsive to diverse AD-associated stimuli drives elevated Aβ production. Such a mediator should meet two criteria. First, it must function as a shared biomarker that consistently responds to varied external or microenvironmental triggers of sporadic AD. Evidence from multiple independent human studies shows that PHGDH expression is elevated during the prodromal and early stages of sporadic AD, suggesting its upregulation as a convergent response to external stimuli. Consistently, human brain organoids exposed to blood serum-a model of BBB leakage-exhibit increased PHGDH expression together with other hallmark AD pathologies (15, 23). Thus, PHGDH fulfills the first criterion as a candidate shared biomarker.While the specific molecular mechanisms governing PHGDH transcriptional upregulation in sAD remain to be fully characterized, evidence from oncological research demonstrates that PHGDH is a direct transcriptional target of stress-responsive factors such as HIF1α, ATF4, and c-Myc (4142, 43). Crucially, these transcription factors are robustly activated in the human AD brain by stressors inherent to the early stages of pathology. For instance, cerebral hypoperfusion, a vascular dysfunction often preceding clinical dementia, establishes a hypoxic microenvironment known to stabilize HIF1α (44). Concurrently, the nascent accumulation of Aβ oligomers precipitates endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and the Unfolded Protein Response (UPR), the canonical pathway for ATF4 induction (45). Furthermore, the abortive cell cycle reentry observed in vulnerable neuronal populations is marked by the aberrant upregulation of c-Myc (46). The convergence of these diverse upstream signals-hypoxia, proteotoxicity, and cell cycle dysregulation-on these specific transcription factors positions PHGDH as a plausible downstream integrator of the complex environmental and metabolic insults driving early sAD.The second required feature of this mediator is a direct mechanistic role in promoting Aβ production, setting it apart from factors that only exacerbate or accelerate the preexisting pathology. This mechanism represents a rare example of a 3′UTR-mediated protein–protein interaction directly linked to disease pathogenesis. Its enzymatic role, essential for normal development, is dispensable for Aβ production and other AD hallmarks in a brain organoid model of sporadic AD (15). Although PHGDH’s transcriptional regulatory role contributes to AD pathology (15), this function is restricted exclusively to glial cells, as PHGDH is not detected in neuronal nuclei; thus, it cannot fully explain the observed increases in neuronal Aβ levels. In contrast, PHGDH’s RNA-binding function drives increased Aβ levels in both neurons and glial cells, wherever PHGDH is elevated. These data suggest that PHGDH’s multifunctionality initiates and accelerates AD pathology.Canonical RBDs exhibit significant structural diversity, ranging from compact zinc finger motifs (~20 to 40 residues) to extensive modular scaffolds like PUF domains (>300 residues). Given its limited length, the RNA-binding segment identified in PHGDH (residues I22–K33) is unlikely to function as an autonomous RBD; rather, it likely represents a critical surface motif embedded within a larger structural architecture. Notably, this sequence is situated within a pair of consecutive Rossmann folds (spanning approximately K8–S280), a highly conserved supersecondary structure traditionally associated with nucleotide cofactor binding. Recent studies indicate that Rossmann folds can be evolutionarily co-opted for RNA recognition, suggesting that PHGDH’s RNA-binding capacity may have arisen through the adaptive “moonlighting” of a metabolic scaffold to engage regulatory RNA substrates (48). These include two nonsense mutations, L23Ter and Q24Ter, and the E32fs frameshift mutation, which are predicted to be pathogenic due to the loss of enzymatic activity. Additionally, several missense variants, I22N, L23F, L23W, and G27R, are predicted to be “probably/possibly damaging” by PolyPhen and “deleterious” by SIFT (49). Conversely, variants G26E, G26R, Q29H, and Q29R are currently predicted to be benign. Given their locations, all these mutations may alter the RNA binding of PHGDH, though their specific effect on RNA affinity remains to be experimentally determined.In conclusion, we identify an RNA-binding function of PHGDH that activates the EIF2AK1–EIF2α–BACE1 axis and triggers the earliest rise of Aβ in sporadic AD. A Common Mediator Model For Aβ Elevation in Sporadic AD.

Diverse environmental factors have been linked to the multistage progression of AD, including its early-stage indication, elevated soluble Aβ, which precedes the formation of amyloid plaques, tau tangles, and neuronal loss. However, how these diverse stimuli converge to induce this early increase remains unclear.

We hypothesize that a common mediator responsive to diverse AD-associated stimuli drives elevated Aβ production. Such a mediator should meet two criteria. First, it must function as a shared biomarker that consistently responds to varied external or microenvironmental triggers of sporadic AD. Evidence from multiple independent human studies shows that PHGDH expression is elevated during the prodromal and early stages of sporadic AD, suggesting its upregulation as a convergent response to external stimuli. Consistently, human brain organoids exposed to blood serum-a model of BBB leakage-exhibit increased PHGDH expression together with other hallmark AD pathologies (15, 23). Thus, PHGDH fulfills the first criterion as a candidate shared biomarker.

While the specific molecular mechanisms governing PHGDH transcriptional upregulation in sAD remain to be fully characterized, evidence from oncological research demonstrates that PHGDH is a direct transcriptional target of stress-responsive factors such as HIF1α, ATF4, and c-Myc (4142, 43). Crucially, these transcription factors are robustly activated in the human AD brain by stressors inherent to the early stages of pathology. For instance, cerebral hypoperfusion, a vascular dysfunction often preceding clinical dementia, establishes a hypoxic microenvironment known to stabilize HIF1α (44). Concurrently, the nascent accumulation of Aβ oligomers precipitates endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and the Unfolded Protein Response (UPR), the canonical pathway for ATF4 induction (45). The convergence of these diverse upstream signals-hypoxia, proteotoxicity, and cell cycle dysregulation-on these specific transcription factors positions PHGDH as a plausible downstream integrator of the complex environmental and metabolic insults driving early sAD.

Surprisingly, PHGDH promotes Aβ production via an unanticipated RNA-binding capability. PHGDH binds directly to the 3′UTR of EIF2AK1 mRNA, facilitating the interaction between PHGDH and newly synthesized EIF2AK1 protein. PHGDH has three characterized functions: an enzymatic role (47), a transcription regulatory function (19), and an RNA-binding capability. Its enzymatic role, essential for normal development, is dispensable for Aβ production and other AD hallmarks in a brain organoid model of sporadic AD (15). Although PHGDH’s transcriptional regulatory role contributes to AD pathology (15), this function is restricted exclusively to glial cells, as PHGDH is not detected in neuronal nuclei; thus, it cannot fully explain the observed increases in neuronal Aβ levels. In contrast, PHGDH’s RNA-binding function drives increased Aβ levels in both neurons and glial cells, wherever PHGDH is elevated. These data suggest that PHGDH’s multifunctionality initiates and accelerates AD pathology.

Canonical RBDs exhibit significant structural diversity, ranging from compact zinc finger motifs (~20 to 40 residues) to extensive modular scaffolds like PUF domains (>300 residues). Given its limited length, the RNA-binding segment identified in PHGDH (residues I22–K33) is unlikely to function as an autonomous RBD; rather, it likely represents a critical surface motif embedded within a larger structural architecture. Notably, this sequence is situated within a pair of consecutive Rossmann folds (spanning approximately K8–S280), a highly conserved supersecondary structure traditionally associated with nucleotide cofactor binding. Recent studies indicate that Rossmann folds can be evolutionarily co-opted for RNA recognition, suggesting that PHGDH’s RNA-binding capacity may have arisen through the adaptive “moonlighting” of a metabolic scaffold to engage regulatory RNA substrates (48). Although we identify the EIF2AK1 3′UTR and the PHGDH surface I22–K33 as the specific regulatory elements, defining the precise nucleotides and residues of the PHGDH–RNA interface remains a priority for future structural investigation.

A total of 11 human germline mutations have been identified within the I22-K33 region of PHGDH (49). These include two nonsense mutations, L23Ter and Q24Ter, and the E32fs frameshift mutation, which are predicted to be pathogenic due to the loss of enzymatic activity. Additionally, several missense variants, I22N, L23F, L23W, and G27R, are predicted to be “probably/possibly damaging” by PolyPhen and “deleterious” by SIFT (49). Conversely, variants G26E, G26R, Q29H, and Q29R are currently predicted to be benign. Given their locations, all these mutations may alter the RNA binding of PHGDH, though their specific effect on RNA affinity remains to be experimentally determined.

In conclusion, we identify an RNA-binding function of PHGDH that activates the EIF2AK1–EIF2α–BACE1 axis and triggers the earliest rise of Aβ in sporadic AD. This pathway suggests a common mediator of amyloid initiation and points to PHGDH’s RNA-binding pocket as a potential target for therapeutic intervention.

Materials and Methods

Human BOs were generated from H1 embryonic stem cells using a commercial cerebral organoid kit (STEMCELL Technologies). To model sAD, day-130 organoids were cultured in either standard medium or medium supplemented with 10% human serum until day 142, when they were harvested for analysis. For overexpression studies, BOs were transduced with AAV9 vectors expressing GFP or PHGDH under either a CMV, hSYN, or gfaABC1D (a truncated form of human GFAP) promoter.

For in vivo experiments, 6-mo-old 3xTg-AD and C57BL/6J mice received unilateral intrahippocampal injections of control or PHGDH-expressing AAV9. Brains were collected 4 wk later for immunohistochemistry and molecular analyses.

Cell culture, transfection, plasmid construction, immunostaining, qPCR, western blotting, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP-seq and RIP-qPCR), RNA pulldown, and coimmunoprecipitation assays were performed using standard protocols and are described in detail in SI Appendix.
