The receptor-like kinase FLAGELLIN-SENSITIVE 2 (FLS2) functions as a bacterial
flagellin receptor localized on the cell membrane of plants. In Arabidopsis, the
co-receptor BRI1-ASSOCIATED RECEPTOR KINASE 1 (BAK1) cooperates with FLS2 to
detect the flagellin epitope flg22, resulting in formation of a signaling
complex that triggers plant defense responses. However, the co-receptor
responsible for recognizing and signaling the flg22 epitope in rice remains to
be determined, and the precise structural mechanism underlying FLS2-mediated
signal activation and transduction has not been clarified. This study presents
the structural characterization of a kinase-dead mutant of the intracellular
kinase domain of OsFLS2 (OsFLS2-KDD1013A) in complex with ATP or ADP, resolved
at resolutions of 1.98 Å and 2.09 Å, respectively. Structural analysis revealed
that OsFLS2 can adopt an active conformation in the absence of phosphorylation,
although it exhibits only weak basal catalytic activity for autophosphorylation.
Subsequent investigations demonstrated that OsSERK2 effectively phosphorylates
OsFLS2, which reciprocally phosphorylates OsSERK2, leading to complete
activation of OsSERK2 and rapid phosphorylation of the downstream substrate
receptor-like cytoplasmic kinases OsRLCK176 and OsRLCK185. Through mass
spectrometry experiments, we successfully identified critical
autophosphorylation sites on OsSERK2, as well as sites transphosphorylated by
OsFLS2. Furthermore, we demonstrated the interaction between OsSERK2 and OsFLS2,
which is enhanced in the presence of flg22. Genetic evidence suggests that
OsRLCK176 and OsRLCK185 may function downstream of the OsFLS2-mediated signaling
pathway. Our study reveals the molecular mechanism by which OsFLS2 mediates
signal transduction pathways in rice and provides a valuable example for
understanding RLK-mediated signaling pathways in plants.

Introduction

Plants encounter a diverse array of bacterial pathogens in their natural environments, necessitating the activation of their ancient and fundamental innate immune system to safeguard against infections. By means of two distinct recognition-response mechanisms, the plant’s innate immune system demonstrates resistance against a wide range of pathogenic microorganisms. One such mechanism is the activation of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) located on the cell membrane. These PRRs detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and initiate PAMP-triggered immunity (PTI) (Boller and Felix, 2009; Macho and Zipfel, 2014). PTI triggers various defense responses in plants, including calcium influx, reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst, activation of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), stomatal closure, and upregulation of immune genes (Boller and Felix, 2009; Meng and Zhang, 2013; Monaghan et al., 2014). However, the effectiveness of PTI can be compromised by secreted bacterial effectors that invade host cells and disrupt their normal functions. In response, plants activate an alternative line of defense known as effector-triggered immunity (ETI) upon direct or indirect recognition of effectors released by pathogenic microorganisms through resistance proteins (R proteins) (Chisholm et al., 2006; Jones and Dangl, 2006; Hurley et al., 2014; Cui et al., 2015).

Plant receptor kinases (RKs) constitute a diverse class of protein kinases that play pivotal roles in multiple signaling pathways within plants. The Arabidopsis genome contains approximately 610 genes encoding RKs, with the largest subclass being the transmembrane receptor kinases known as LRR-RKs, distinguished by the presence of a leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain (Shiu and Bleecker, 2001). Among these RKs, Arabidopsis FLAGELLIN-SENSITIVE 2 (FLS2) was the first to be identified and characterized (Gómez-Gómez and Boller, 2000). Subsequent investigations have revealed the presence of FLS2 in numerous higher plant species and its important role in conferring resistance against bacterial pathogens through PTI (Robatzek et al., 2007; Takai et al., 2008; Mueller et al., 2012; Trdá et al., 2014). The signal perception mechanism of FLS2 in Arabidopsis has been extensively investigated. FLS2 consists of an LRR domain, a single transmembrane domain (TM), and an intracellular kinase domain (KD). The extracellular region of FLS2 contains LRRs, which are primarily responsible for recognizing the flagellar peptide flg22 (Sun et al., 2013). Upon flg22 detection, FLS2 rapidly forms a signaling complex with BRI1-ASSOCIATED RECEPTOR KINASE 1 (BAK1) (Gómez-Gómez and Boller, 2000; Chinchilla et al., 2007). Structural studies have provided insights into the detailed molecular interactions among flg22, the receptor FLS2, and the co-receptor BAK1 (Sun et al., 2013). However, despite these advances, the structural mechanism governing the activation and signaling of the intracellular KD remains unknown.

Arabidopsis BAK1, also known as SERK3, belongs to the SOMATIC EMBRYOGENESIS RECEPTOR KINASE (SERK) subfamily and is classified as an LRR-RK (Schmidt et al., 1997; Hecht et al., 2001). BAK1 functions as a multifunctional receptor and lacks independent receptor activity. It acts as a co-receptor involved in regulating plant growth and development while playing a pivotal role in plant immunity (Li et al., 2002; Chinchilla et al., 2007). Upon brassinosteroid (BR) induction, BAK1 forms a heterodimer with the LRR-RK BR INSENSITIVE 1 (BRI1), contributing to the regulation of plant growth and development (Nam and Li, 2002). In addition, BAK1 can form a heterodimer with various PRRs such as FLS2, EFR, and PEPR to initiate plant immune responses (Bentham et al., 2020; Margets et al., 2021; Song et al., 2021). Consequently, multiple effectors often target BAK1 to inhibit PTI (Zhou et al., 2014).

Another crucial player in FLS2-mediated signaling is BOTRYTIS-INDUCED KINASE 1 (BIK1), a receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase that directly interacts with FLS2 and BAK1. Upon stimulation with flg22, BIK1 undergoes rapid phosphorylation, resulting in its dissociation from FLS2 and BAK1, which activates the plant immune response (Lu et al., 2010). In contrast to RKs, Arabidopsis BIK1 possesses only a receptor-like cytoplasmic KD and lacks an extracellular domain and a TM domain (Zipfel, 2014; Lal et al., 2018). BIK1 functions downstream of various PRR complexes, including FLS2, EFR, and CERK1. Studies on bik1 mutants treated with flg22 have demonstrated reduced ROS production and weaker immune gene expression compared with the wild type, indicating that BIK1 positively regulates PTI (Li et al., 2014; Lal et al., 2018).

The rice ortholog of FLS2, known as OsFLS2, can recognize the flagellar peptide flg22 and initiate a plant immune response (Takai et al., 2008). The rice genome harbors four homologs of BAK1, namely OsSERK1–4, which exhibit several conserved features, including five LRRs, a TM domain, and a cytoplasmic KD (Li et al., 2009). Phylogenetic analysis suggests that OsSERK1 and OsSERK2 are most closely related to AtSERK1/AtSERK2 in Arabidopsis, rather than the immune-related AtSERK3 (AtBAK1) (Chinchilla et al., 2007; Li et al., 2009). OsSERK1, also referred to as OsBAK1, functions primarily in BR-mediated regulation of rice growth and development but does not activate an immune response against Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae or Magnaporthe oryzae (Zuo et al., 2014). Conversely, OsSERK2 plays a critical role in regulating growth, development, and RK-mediated immunity in rice (Li et al., 2009; Chen et al., 2014). Overexpression of OsSERK2 (referred to as OsSERK1 in the original publication) enhances resistance to the rice blast fungus Pyricularia oryzae (Hu et al., 2005). However, the specific roles of SERK family members in OsFLS2-mediated signaling remain to be fully determined and require further investigation. Although OsSERK2 has been shown to interact with OsFLS2 (Chen et al., 2014), its precise contribution to the signaling pathway and its functional significance in plant immune responses need to be clarified. Exploration of the potential involvement of other SERK family members, such as OsSERK1, OsSERK3, and OsSERK4, in OsFLS2-mediated signaling is also warranted.

OsRLCK176 (a direct homolog of BIK1) and OsRLCK185 interact directly with OsCERK1 and are phosphorylated by OsCERK1 in a ligand-dependent manner (Yamaguchi et al., 2013; Ao et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2017). Consequently, loss-of-function mutations in OsRLCK176 and OsRLCK185 lead to diminished expression of ligand-induced defense marker genes, reduced ROS generation, decreased MAPK activation, and enhanced susceptibility to pathogens (Yamaguchi et al., 2013; Ao et al., 2014). Although the chitin signaling pathway mediated by OsRLCK176 and OsRLCK185 has been comparatively well elucidated, how rice RLCK functions as a signaling component in flg22-induced PTI remains to be fully investigated.

In this study, we determined the crystal structure of OsFLS2-KD and investigated its signaling pathway in rice. We purified and characterized proteins involved in the pathway, including OsFLS2-KD, the cytoplasmic domain (CD) of OsSERK1/2/3/4, and OsRLCK176/185. Our results showed that OsSERK2, but not OsFLS2, phosphorylates the downstream receptor-like cytoplasmic kinases OsRLCK176/OsRLCK185. However, the kinase activity of OsFLS2 and its transphosphorylation of OsSERK2 are necessary for activation of downstream substrates. We identified critical phosphorylation sites for both OsSERK2 autophosphorylation and transphosphorylation of OsSERK2 by OsFLS2. We also demonstrated that OsSERK2 interacts with OsFLS2 and that this interaction is intensified by the presence of flg22. Moreover, our genetic evidence suggests that OsRLCK176 and OsRLCK185 may function downstream of the signaling pathway mediated by OsFLS2. Our work reveals distinct differences between the rice and Arabidopsis pathways mediated by FLS2. These findings advance our understanding of signal transduction through OsFLS2 and contribute to a deeper understanding of defense mechanisms in rice.

Consistent with the active conformation of OsFLS2-KDD1013A, we observed the formation of both the C-spine and the R-spine (Figure 2B). These spines play a crucial role in determining protein kinase activity by facilitating binding to ATP and peptide substrates (Kornev et al., 2006, 2008). The C-spine of OsFLS2-KDD1013A consists of eight residues (Leu1020, Leu1021, Val1019, Leu964, Leu1092, Leu1088, Ala906, and Val890) derived from both the N lobe and C lobe. Notably, the hydrophobic pocket formed by Ala906, Val890, and Leu1020 serves as an ATP adenine-ring binding site. The R-spine of OsFLS2-KDD1013A comprises four residues (Val941, Leu930 in the αC helix, Phe1032 in the DFG motif, and His1011 in the HRD motif), and their side chains adopt a similar conformation directed toward the catalytic center (Figure 2B). These structural characteristics provide further evidence supporting the formation of an active conformation of OsFLS2-KDD1013A.

Our investigation revealed that the ADP-bound OsFLS2-KDD1013A also adopted an active conformation characterized by αC-in and DFG-in/BLAminus features and formation of both the C-spine and the R-spine (Supplemental Figures 2, 4, and 5; Supplemental Tables 2 and 3).

Considering the essential role of ATP binding in facilitating phosphate transfer within the kinase, we comprehensively investigated the ATP-binding pocket of OsFLS2-KDD1013A. Interaction between the magnesium and the phosphate groups positioned at β- and γ-sites of ATP was observed. Eight residues were identified as playing a crucial role in stabilizing ATP binding in OsFLS2-KDD1013A (Figure 2C). Similarly, when examining the ADP-bound form of OsFLS2-KDD1013A, we found that magnesium was present, and the nucleotide exhibited a similar binding mode (Supplemental Figures 5 and 6).

Phosphorylation of the activation segment is widely recognized as a crucial mechanism for regulating the catalytic activity of kinases (Nolen et al., 2004). We therefore focused on characterizing phosphorylation sites within the activation segment of OsSERK2-CD. Our findings revealed that the T466A mutation, which affects an autophosphorylation site, significantly reduced substrate phosphorylation levels in the presence of OsFLS2-KD compared with those observed for wild-type OsSERK2-CD (Figure 6B and 6C). Furthermore, among the mutations that affected transphosphorylation sites, the T467A mutation was associated with a substantial decrease in substrate phosphorylation. We also observed significantly reduced levels of substrate phosphorylation with the S482A and T483A mutations (Figure 6B and 6C).

We next investigated whether these mutations had an effect on the ATP hydrolysis activity of the OsSERK2-CD kinase. Consistent with its relatively weak autophosphorylation, OsSERK2-CD exhibited lower ATP hydrolysis activity than did AtFER-KD (Figure 6D). Among the phosphorylation-site mutants, T466A and T467A displayed no detectable catalytic activity, but S482A and T483A showed higher catalytic activity than the wild-type kinase (Figure 6D). These results indicate that T466A and T467A affect substrate phosphorylation by influencing the catalytic ability of OsSERK2. Conversely, S482A and T483A affect substrate phosphorylation because they cannot be activated through OsFLS2-mediated phosphorylation.

In conclusion, the phosphorylation of OsSERK2 plays a crucial role in its substrate phosphorylation process, and OsFLS2 effectively activates the substrate phosphorylation capacity of OsSERK2.

Interestingly, the kinase assumes an active conformation even without phosphorylation, similar to the KD of AtFER (Kong et al., 2023). We speculate that this active conformation may facilitate the rapid phosphorylation of OsFLS2 by OsSERK2 within the ligand-induced complex (Figure 2). Furthermore, the KD exists as a monomer in solution (Supplemental Figure 11), similar to AtBRI1 (Bojar et al., 2014). This monomeric state with an active conformation likely promotes formation of the receptor-co-receptor complex in response to immune signals while remaining in a resting state in the absence of such signals to prevent unnecessary activation. In support of this hypothesis, previous studies have demonstrated that AvrPto can disrupt formation of the AtFLS2–AtBAK1 immune complex by interacting with AtFLS2, thereby inhibiting AtFLS2- and AtBAK1-mediated immune responses (Xiang et al., 2008, 2011).
