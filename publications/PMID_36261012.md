We identify ADIRF-AS1 circadian long non-coding RNA (lncRNA). Deletion of 
ADIRF-AS1 in U2OS cells alters rhythmicity of clock-controlled genes and 
expression of extracellular matrix genes. ADIRF-AS1 interacts with all 
components of the PBAF (PBRM1/BRG1) complex in U2OS cells. Because PBRM1 is a 
tumor suppressor mutated in over 40% of clear cell renal carcinoma (ccRCC) 
cases, we evaluate ADIRF-AS1 in ccRCC cells. Reducing ADIRF-AS1 expression in 
ccRCC cells decreases expression of some PBAF-suppressed genes. Expression of 
these genes is partially rescued by PBRM1 loss, consistent with ADIRF-AS1 acting 
in part to modulate PBAF. ADIRF-AS1 expression correlates with survival in human 
ccRCC, particularly in PBRM1 wild-type, but not mutant, tumors. Loss of 
ADIRF-AS1 eliminates in vivo tumorigenesis, partially rescued by concurrent loss 
of PBRM1 only when co-injected with Matrigel, suggesting a PBRM1-independent 
function of ADIRF-AS1. Our findings suggest that ADIRF-AS1 functions partly 
through PBAF to regulate specific genes as a BMAL1-CLOCK-regulated, oncogenic 
lncRNA.

INTRODUCTION

Circadian rhythms are driven by synchronization of peripheral tissue clocks with the central hypothalamic clock (Mohawk et al., 2012). Transcriptional clock outputs optimize metabolism by synchronizing cellular activities with the organismal sleep-wake, fasting-feeding cycle. The molecular clocks are composed of interlocking transcription-translation negative feedback loops. BMAL1 heterodimerizes with CLOCK (or NPAS2) to upregulate PERs and CRYs or REVERBs and RORs that downregulate BMAL1 activity or suppress BMAL1 mRNA expression, respectively. Thus, 24-h oscillatory activity of the BMAL1:CLOCK heterodimer leads to the circadian transcription of thousands of clock output noncoding and protein-coding genes (Bass, 2012; Mohawk et al., 2012; Panda et al., 2002) in which transcripts involved in metabolism are enriched (Mure et al., 2018; Walton et al., 2018a; Zhang et al., 2014a).

Noncoding transcripts with high circadian amplitudes are surmised to have important cellular functions (Coon et al., 2012). Among noncoding RNAs, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are defined as transcripts over 200 nucleotides (Hu et al., 2018), which can have tissue specific expression (Zhang et al., 2014a). LncRNAs can regulate the surrounding chromatin in cis, affecting transcriptional regulation of neighboring genes, but they are also capable of trans functions where they affect long-range transcription, often through protein interactions (Bester et al., 2018; Cajigas et al., 2015; Kawaguchi et al., 2015) or by regulating enhancer regions (Fan et al., 2017). Functioning in cis, the circadian antisense lncRNA Per2AS, encoded on the antisense strand of the core clock gene Per2, regulates clock amplitude by inhibiting Per2 transcription in addition to a direct interaction to regulate BMAL1 (Mosig et al., 2021). However, the functions of the majority of circadian lncRNA remain largely unknown. Given the central role circadian cycles play in essential cellular functions, these lncRNA are likely to have important biological activity.

The SWI/SNF complex is a chromatin modifying complex whose components are mutated in over 20% of all human cancers (Bailey et al., 2018). The mSWI/SNF complex is composed of three major multi-subunit complexes: canonical BRG1/BRM-associated factor (BAF), non-canonical BAF (ncBAF), and polybromo-associated BAF (PBAF) complexes (Mashtalir et al., 2018). The PBAF complex, which broadly affects chromatin accessibility for gene expression, contains protein components including PBRM1, ARlD2, PHF10, and BRD7 and either the ATPase BRM or BRG1 (Valencia and Kadoch, 2019). A global study of RNA-binding proteins revealed that PBAF-specific (BRD7, PBRM1) and shared (BRG1, SMARCE1, SMARCB1) proteins bound RNAs (He et al., 2016). Such RNA interactions can alter chromatin-modifying activity of SWI/SNF proteins (Cajigas et al., 2015; Hu et al., 2016; Jegu et al., 2019; Panatta et al., 2020; Tang et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2015). However, it is not known whether the PBAF complex activity is regulated by RNA binding in clear cell renal carcinoma (ccRCC) (Valencia and Kadoch, 2019; Varela et al., 2011), where PBRM1 is mutated in over 40% of cases.

PBRM1 inactivating mutations are common in ccRCC and cholangiocarcinoma (Gao et al., 2013, 2017; Jiao et al., 2013; Kadoch and Crabtree, 2015; Kadoch et al., 2013; Reisman et al., 2009; Wilson and Roberts, 2011). Likewise, there is frequent biallelic inactivation of VHL in ccRCC. The loss of VHL stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) (Gerlinger et al., 2014) that regulate genes involved in metabolism and angiogenesis (Xie and Simon, 2017). However, loss of VHL is not sufficient to induce ccRCC, which requires cooperating mutations. The most commonly co-mutated gene is PBRM1, whose loss is associated with angiogenesis in metastatic ccRCC (Hakimi et al., 2020). These observations are consistent with the ability of the PBAF complex to antagonize HIF-directed transcription of metabolic and angiogenic genes (Gao et al., 2017), including VEGFA, and that downregulating PBRM1 in ccRCC cell lines promotes tumor progression (Chowdhury et al., 2016; Gao et al., 2017; Varela et al., 2011).

In this report, we document that the ADIRF-AS1 is a high-amplitude lncRNA regulated by the molecular clock. We found that ADIRF-AS1 loss of function dampened the amplitude of many clock-controlled genes in synchronized osteosarcoma U2OS and entirely prevented ccRCC tumorigenesis in vivo. Our study uncovers that ADIRF-AS1 binds the PBAF chromatin-modifying complex and drives ccRCC tumorigenesis, revealing an unforeseen link between a clock-regulated lncRNA and tumorigenesis in ccRCC.

RESULTSIdentification of a functional circadian lncRNAWe identified high-amplitude circadian noncoding RNAs from our published RNA-seq data demonstrating that low pH can disrupt the circadian clock and transcriptome in U2OS cells (Walton et al., 2018b) (Figures 1A and S1A, Table S1). Among the group of oscillating noncoding RNAs, the lncRNA ADIRF-AS1 (ENSG00000272734) exhibited the highest amplitude expression. LncRNAs can function in cis to regulate neighboring genes or in trans to regulate distant genes or cellular components (Cui et al., 2015; Fan et al., 2017; Xiong et al., 2017). First, we evaluated the expression of ADIRF-AS1 neighboring genes. ADIRF-AS1 is transcribed from the antisense strand of the adipocyte regulatory factor gene (ADIRF), encoding a transcription factor regulator of lipid metabolism during early adipocyte differentiation (Ni et al., 2013). The median expression level of ADIRF-AS1 (FPKM = 12.8) over a 48-h time course is higher than the neighboring genes, including ADIRF (median FPKM: ADIRF = 0.5, AGAP11 = 0.2, MMRN2 = 2.5, SNCG = 1.7) (Figure S1B). Using qPCR primers targeting ADIRF-AS1, and not ADIRF, we validated the periodic expression pattern of ADIRF-AS1 (Figure S1C). The ADIRF-AS1 locus is closely spaced with AGAP11, with some overlap in the promoter region of AGAP11, encoded on the sense strand (Figure S1D). It is possible that ADIRF-AS1 expression can modulate that of AGAP11, and future work could explore this potential regulation. We evaluated neighboring genes by qRT-PCR and found that MMRN2 was below the limit of detection, and SNCG and AGAP11 exhibited low but detectable expression (Figure 1B). We used A431 cells as a positive control for the qRT-PCR primers as A431 cells exhibit detectable expression of ADIRF-AS1, MMRN2, SNCG, and AGAP11 (Figure 1B). Compared with A431 cells, which express detectable levels of ADIRF, U2OS cells have no detectable ADIRF expression (Figure 1C). Despite the absent or very low expression of flanking genes, it is possible that ADIRF-AS1 can function to modulate the expression of neighboring genes, but as shown below, loss of ADIRF-AS1 function altered rhythmicity of many clock-controlled non-neighboring genes.Because expression of ADIRF-AS1 oscillates diurnally, we sought to determine whether it is a direct target of BMAL1. Analysis of publicly available ChIP-seq data revealed that BMAL1 binds directly to the promoter of ADIRF-AS1 in U2OS cells (Hoffmann et al., 2014; Rey et al., 2016) (Figure S1E). To determine if ADIRF-AS1 is dependent on BMAL1, we collected protein and RNA every 4 h for 52 h from control and BMAL1 knockout (KO) U2OS cells, which lack a functional circadian clock (Lee et al., 2021). We verified loss of BMAL1 expression and decreased level of REVERBα, a clock-controlled protein, by immunoblot analysis (Figure 1D). Similar to REVERBα, ADIRF-AS1 expression peaks around CT8 and CT32 with a trough around CT24 (Figure 1E), antiphase of BMAL1 protein expression that peaks around CT24 (Figure 1D). As anticipated, 24-h periodic ADIRF-AS1 expression and amplitude were lost in BMAL1 KO cells (Figure 1E). To further explore the regulation of ADIRF-AS1, we constructed an ADIRF-AS1-promoter luciferase reporter, which contains a BMAL1 E-box consensus binding site. The robust diurnal oscillation of the ADIRF-AS1 luciferase reporter in control cells was lost in U2OS BMAL1 KO cells (Figure S1F). Taken together, we discovered a high-amplitude, circadian-expressed lncRNA that is regulated downstream of the circadian transcription factor BMAL1.Loss of ADIRF-AS1 disrupts clock output gene expressionBecause BMAL1 induces high amplitude of ADIRF-AS1 circadian expression as it does with negative regulators REV-ERBs, PERs, and CRYs, we asked whether ADIRF-AS1 could in turn regulate the core clock through a feedback loop. We knocked out ADIRF-AS1 using flanking CRISPR sgRNAs and isolated single-cell clones. We verified knockout of ADIRF-AS1 by genomic PCR (Figures S1G and S1H) and by qRT-PCR RNA expression analysis (Figure S1I). A limitation of this methodology is that any potential expression of ADIRF upon loss of ADIRF-AS1 would be lost due to deletion of the locus. However, there was no detectable expression of ADIRF in U2OS control cells or PC3 cells, which has a deletion of the locus, compared with A431 cells (Figure S1J).We tested if loss of ADIRF-AS1 could modulate the core clock. We used a Bmal1 luciferase reporter (Bmal1::dLuc), and we found no change in the diurnal oscillation of the reporter signal in dexamethasone-synchronized ADIRF-AS1 KO U2OS cells compared to the polyclonal sgSCR control cells (Figure S1K). We next sought to determine whether loss of ADIRF-AS1 disrupts clock-controlled genes. We collected protein and RNA every 4 h for 52 h after dexamethasone synchronization of control and ADIRF-AS1 KO U2OS cells. After confirming loss of ADIRF-AS1 expression in the knockout by qRT-PCR (Figure 1F) and sustained circadian BMAL1 protein levels by immunoblot (Figure S1L), we carried out Quant-seq 3′ mRNA-sequencing of control cells compared with two U2OS ADIRF-AS1 KO clonal cell lines. To identify differentially oscillating transcripts, we used the ECHO (Extended Circadian Harmonic Oscillator) application with a defined period range (20–28 h) (De Los Santos et al., 2020). We identified 1,020 significantly oscillating genes (ECHO p value < 0.01) in control cells. All genes lost significant oscillation in both ADIRF-AS1 KO clones (Table S2, Figure 1H) except 28 genes, which are associated with circadian rhythm and the molecular clock (Figures 1I and S1M). Thus, loss of ADIRF-AS1 did not affect the core circadian clock gene expression, but it diminished the amplitude of many rhythmic genes. Transcripts that lost circadian expression after ADIRF-AS1 loss (791 genes) (Figure 1H) are associated with cellular homeostasis (Figure 1I). Further, these 791 transcripts exhibit suppressed amplitude as suggested by a trend toward a higher amplitude change (AC) coefficient quantified by ECHO, increasing the number of genes with repressed amplitude (defined as having an AC greater than 0.15) (Figure S1N).Next, we evaluated the expression of ADIRF-AS1 neighboring transcripts in control and ADIRF-AS1 KO cells. Aside from decreased ADIRF expression, which is expected due to deletion of the locus, SNCG, MMRN2, and AGAP11 expression were not significantly changed in both ADIRF-AS1 clonal knockout cell lines (Table S2). We did note that in ADIRF-AS1 KO clone c1, there was significant increase of AGAP11, which was not consistent in clone c2 (Table S2). In addition, unlike our previous observation (Figrue S1B), MMRN2 expression did not exhibit a diurnal expression pattern in control sgSCR cells (Figure S2A), which could be attributed to noise due to low expression or technical limitations of Quant-seq compared with RNA-seq. While we found no consistent expression changes between the clonal KO cell lines, it remains possible that ADIRF-AS1 could modulate neighboring gene expression under conditions different from those we used.Beyond oscillating transcripts, we next looked at genes that were altered in expression between ADIRF-AS1 control and knockout to identify pathways impacted. Loss of ADIRF-AS1 resulted in differential expression of 351 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) when compared with control cells. Pathway analysis of DEGs in ADIRF-AS1 KO cells compared with control cells (Figure S2B) revealed enrichment in genes-associated cell adhesion and extracellular matrix (ECM) components (Figure S2C), including downregulated KIF11 and ITGB2 (Table S3). Collectively, these studies suggest that ADIRF-AS1 functions by regulating gene expression, broadly dampening the circadian transcriptome, and altering expression of ECM genes.ADIRF-AS1 localizes to the nucleus and binds the PBAF complexWe found that ADIRF-AS1 predominantly localizes to the nucleus (mean = 84%) in U2OS cells by cell fractionation (Figure 2A) and in all ADIRF-AS1-expressing cell lines documented by the GENCODE RNA-seq data (Figure S2D, https://lncatlas.crg.eu/). Further, RNA-scope analysis localized ADIRF-AS1 largely in the nucleus as puncta (Figure 2B). Evidence for the specificity of ADIRF-AS1 probes was documented by the lack of signals of a negative control targeting a bacterial gene (dapB) (Neg Ctrl, Figures 2B and S2E) and the lack of ADIRF-AS1 signals compared with NEAT1 in U2OS ADIRF-AS1 KO cells (Figure S2E). The number of nuclear puncta ranged from 6 to 45 in unsynchronized U2OS cells (Figures 2B and S2E). Cytoplasmic ADIRF-AS1 was reported to downregulate IRS1 expression (Xu et al., 2022); however, we could not validate this finding using U2OS ADIRF-AS1 KO cells in a time course study (Figure S2F). Since ADIRF-AS1 localized to the nucleus and its loss led to widespread alterations in the circadian transcriptome, we sought to further characterize possible functions of ADIRF-AS1 through putative interactions with proteins.We performed a biotinylated lncRNA pull-down assay using U2OS whole-cell lysate followed by proteomics analysis by mass spectrometry. For controls, we used the antisense RNA of ADIRF-AS1 as well as the antisense of an additional noncoding RNA (LOC105371288) (Feng and Zhang, 2016). Bound proteins were verified by silver stain (Figure S2G) prior to proteomics analysis. We analyzed the cellular components of the top 100 proteomic hits enriched in the ADIRF-AS1 pull-down compared with controls (Figure 2C). We found that all the SWI/SNF PBAF complex components were significantly enriched by both the iBAQ enrichment score as well as protein intensities in the ADIRF-AS1 pull-down, including the bromodomain containing protein PBRM1 and the ATPase BRG1 (Figures 2D, S2H, and S2I). Proteins specific to the BAF or ncBAF complexes were not enriched in the ADIRF-AS1 pull-down (Table S4).The nuclear localization of the PBAF complex (Gao et al., 2017) and ADIRF-AS1 is consistent with an interaction between the two in the nucleus. We observed that PBRM1 had the highest median iBAQ enrichment score by ADIRF-AS1 pull-down (Figure S2J, Table S4). Thus, we immunoprecipitated endogenous PBRM1 using IgG as a control and evaluated bound RNAs by qPCR. Ten percent of the PBRM1 IP lysate was used to validate PBRM1 protein immunoprecipitation (Figure 2E; immunoblot). Precipitation with an anti-PBRM1 antibody, compared with IgG control, enriched for ADIRF-AS1, compared to three known lncRNAs, HOTAIR, NEAT1 and MALAT1, as well as GAPDH (Figure 2E). As ADIRF-AS1 exists as nuclear puncta, we sought to determine the fraction of these puncta that co-localize with PBRM1. Consistent with RIP assays, we found that ADIRF-AS1 puncta contacted PBRM1 in the nucleus by confocal microscopy (Figure 2F), and we note that over 80% of the ADIRF-AS1 probe puncta overlaps with a fraction of the more pervasive nuclear PBRM1, using each slice to quantify all 3D information. Although microscopic co-localization does not necessarily reflect physical interaction, together with the biochemical assays, these observations suggest a role for ADIRF-AS1 through selective interaction with the PBAF complex to regulate gene expression. The RNA-scope data could also be used to estimate the stoichiometry of interaction between PBAF and ADIRF-AS1. While the number of ADIRF-AS1 nuclear puncta ranged from 6 to 45 in unsynchronized U2OS cells (Figures 2B and S2E), the reported copy number of PBAF-specific components is much larger: BRD7 (2,110), PHF10 (5,280), ARID2 (807), and PBRM1 (3,860) (Beck et al., 2011). Assuming that the PBRM1 copy number represents the copy number of PBAF complex, the stoichiometry is estimated by ratios of 6/3,860 to 45/3,860 for ADIRF-AS1 to PBAF complex. Thus, ADIRF-AS1 could bind within the range of 0.1%–1.2% of PBAF complexes (Table S4). This estimated stoichiometry suggests, by extrapolation, that ADIRF-AS1 may affect a small fraction of PBAF-responsive genes and thereby could affect its biological function on specific genes.ADIRF-AS1 expression in ccRCC is inversely correlated with survivalAs PBRM1, a key component of the PBAF complex, acts as a tumor suppressor and is mutated in ~40% of ccRCC (Gao et al., 2017), we hypothesized that ADIRF-AS1 directly interacts with and modulates PBAF complex in ccRCC, serving as an unforeseen oncogenic driver. We hypothesized that increased expression of ADIRF-AS1 would phenocopy PBRM1 mutations and hence only lead to decreased survival in patients with PBRM1 wild-type (WT) tumors, but not in those with PBRM1 mutant tumors. Without distinguishing PBRM1 mutation status, high expression of PBRM1 correlates with better clinical outcomes and improved survival in ccRCC patients (Gao et al., 2017; Hodges et al., 2016; Varela et al., 2011), and high expression of ADIRF-AS1 correlates with decreased survival (Figure S3A). Consistent with our hypothesis, the inverse correlation between ADIRF-AS1 expression and survival was only evident in patients with WT PBRM1 and not in those with mutant PBRM1 (Cerami et al., 2012; Gao et al., 2013) (Figures 3A and 3B). Unlike ADIRF-AS1, ADIRF expression is not correlated with survival in ccRCC TCGA data (Figure S3B). These data support our hypothesis that ADIRF-AS1 functions as an RNA inhibitory regulator of the PBAF complex that could be upregulated to dampen the tumor suppressive function of PBAF in ccRCC.We also found that ADIRF-AS1 was significantly elevated in ccRCC tumors compared with normal tissue (Figure S3C), but this analysis is unable to differentiate tumor cells from other cell types in bulk tumors. As such, we analyzed publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data from human ccRCC (Bi et al., 2021). Consistent with our TCGA data analysis, ADIRF-AS1 is elevated in tumor cells compared with non-tumor cells (Figure S3D). ADIRF-AS1 expression is also higher in a subset of tumor cells characterized by increased angiogenesis (Bi et al., 2021) (Figure S3E), noting that PBAF acts as a tumor suppressive complex by dampening the hypoxia response and angiogenesis pathways (Gao et al., 2017). With these observations, we hypothesized that loss of ADIRF-AS1 would affect some PBAF-mediated gene expression and tumor suppression in vivo in human ccRCC.ADIRF-AS1 is regulated by BMAL1 and binds PBAF in ccRCC cellsSimilar to U2OS cells, we also found that ADIRF-AS1 is a target of BMAL1 in ccRCC cells. We studied 786O and A498 ccRCC cell lines that express WT PBRM1 and ADIRF-AS1 (Figures S3F and S3G). Using siRNAs to silence BMAL1 in both 786O and A498 ccRCC cell lines, we found that BMAL1 knockdown decreased both NR1D1, a BMAL1 target gene, and ADIRF-AS1, and that silencing BMAL1 in synchronized cells suppressed oscillation of the ADIRF-AS1 luciferase reporter (Figures 3C–3F). These observations suggest that 786O and A498 ccRCC cell lines have at least partly functional BMAL1-dependent clocks. Further, we generated control and BMAL1 KO 786O cells and collected protein and RNA every 4 h from 24–72 h after cell synchronization with dexamethasone. We found that BMAL1 protein and its target, REVERBα, continuously oscillated in 786O cells (Figures 3G and 3H), and ADIRF-AS1 exhibited BMAL1-dependent oscillation in 786O cells (Figure 3I). ADIRF-AS1 expression peaked at CT32 and CT48, similar to BMAL1 target gene, PER2 (Figure S3H), whereas REVERBα was expressed highly at CT24 and peaked at CT48 (Figure 3D). Further, in the human ccRCC TCGA dataset, we observed a positive correlation between expression of ADIRF-AS1 and BMAL1:CLOCK targets, REVERBα (NR1D1), REVERBβ (NR1D2), PER1, and CRY2, whereas CLOCK and ARNTL2 correlated negatively (Figure S3I). These findings document that BMAL1 regulates ADIRF-AS1 in 786O and A498 ccRCC cells.As found with U2OS cells, ADIRF-AS1 localizes to the nucleus in 786O ccRCC cells by cell fractionation (Figure 4A) and by RNA-scope analysis (Figure S3J). We found that in 786O cells, RNA-scope estimates approximately 8–37 copies of ADIRF-AS1 per cell, similar to the range seen in U2OS cells. We also found that ADIRF-AS1 co-precipitated with an antibody against PBRM1 compared with control (Figure 4B) and conversely verified this interaction by biotinylated RNA pull-down assays in 786O cells (Figure 4C). For controls, we used the antisense RNA of ADIRF-AS1 as well as the antisense of an additional noncoding RNA (LOC105371288) (Feng and Zhang, 2016). Bound proteins were verified by silver stain (Figure S2G) prior to proteomics analysis. We analyzed the cellular components of the top 100 proteomic hits enriched in the ADIRF-AS1 pull-down compared with controls (Figure 2C). We found that all the SWI/SNF PBAF complex components were significantly enriched by both the iBAQ enrichment score as well as protein intensities in the ADIRF-AS1 pull-down, including the bromodomain containing protein PBRM1 and the ATPase BRG1 (Figures 2D, S2H, and S2I). Proteins specific to the BAF or ncBAF complexes were not enriched in the ADIRF-AS1 pull-down (Table S4).

The nuclear localization of the PBAF complex (Gao et al., 2017) and ADIRF-AS1 is consistent with an interaction between the two in the nucleus. We observed that PBRM1 had the highest median iBAQ enrichment score by ADIRF-AS1 pull-down (Figure S2J, Table S4). Thus, we immunoprecipitated endogenous PBRM1 using IgG as a control and evaluated bound RNAs by qPCR. Ten percent of the PBRM1 IP lysate was used to validate PBRM1 protein immunoprecipitation (Figure 2E; immunoblot). Precipitation with an anti-PBRM1 antibody, compared with IgG control, enriched for ADIRF-AS1, compared to three known lncRNAs, HOTAIR, NEAT1 and MALAT1, as well as GAPDH (Figure 2E). As ADIRF-AS1 exists as nuclear puncta, we sought to determine the fraction of these puncta that co-localize with PBRM1. Consistent with RIP assays, we found that ADIRF-AS1 puncta contacted PBRM1 in the nucleus by confocal microscopy (Figure 2F), and we note that over 80% of the ADIRF-AS1 probe puncta overlaps with a fraction of the more pervasive nuclear PBRM1, using each slice to quantify all 3D information. Although microscopic co-localization does not necessarily reflect physical interaction, together with the biochemical assays, these observations suggest a role for ADIRF-AS1 through selective interaction with the PBAF complex to regulate gene expression. The RNA-scope data could also be used to estimate the stoichiometry of interaction between PBAF and ADIRF-AS1. While the number of ADIRF-AS1 nuclear puncta ranged from 6 to 45 in unsynchronized U2OS cells (Figures 2B and S2E), the reported copy number of PBAF-specific components is much larger: BRD7 (2,110), PHF10 (5,280), ARID2 (807), and PBRM1 (3,860) (Beck et al., 2011). Assuming that the PBRM1 copy number represents the copy number of PBAF complex, the stoichiometry is estimated by ratios of 6/3,860 to 45/3,860 for ADIRF-AS1 to PBAF complex. Thus, ADIRF-AS1 could bind within the range of 0.1%–1.2% of PBAF complexes (Table S4). This estimated stoichiometry suggests, by extrapolation, that ADIRF-AS1 may affect a small fraction of PBAF-responsive genes and thereby could affect its biological function on specific genes.

Diminished tumorigenesis by ADIRF-AS1 loss is partially dependent on PBRM1 expressionGiven that PBRM1 loss rescued a subset of ADIRF-AS1 KO DEGs (Figures 4H and 4I; Table S6), we hypothesized that concurrent PBRM1 and ADIRF-AS1 loss could rescue tumorigenesis compared with ADIRF-AS1 loss alone. Intriguingly, among the top 200 downregulated DEGs with loss of ADIRF-AS1, genes involved in the ECM were largely not rescued by concurrent loss of PBRM1 in DKO cells (Figure 5D). ECM genes are also among DEGs in the CRISPRi ADIRF-AS1 KD 786O cells (Figures S4J and S4K) and ADIRF-AS1 KO U2OS cells (Figure S2C). The ECM-related gene NID1, also known as entactin, was among these PBRM1-independent downregulated genes. NID1 was suppressed in ADIRF-AS1 KO and DKO (sgP/sgA) 786O cells at both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Figure 5A). Thus, we hypothesized that both PBRM1-dependent and -independent tumorigenic roles of ADIRF-AS1 could be rescued in DKO cells only in the presence of Matrigel that contains entactin (Adelfio et al., 2020) to restore ECM components. Consistent with our hypothesis, we found that when co-injected with Matrigel, the DKO cells formed tumor xenografts, while the ADIRF-AS1 single KO did not form tumors. By day 35, the tumor weights of the DKO tumors from two different cell clones were significantly greater than the single ADIRF-AS1 KO tumors (Figures 5E and 5F), but they were less than those of single PBRM1 KO tumors. These observations demonstrate that ADIRF-AS1 is necessary for tumorigenesis in 786O ccRCC cells in a PBRM1-dependent and ECM-dependent manner.

Further, the PBAF complex was shown to regulate clock amplitude by re-organizing chromatin in mouse liver (Zhu et al., 2015). However, through time course transcriptomics analysis, we found that loss of ADIRF-AS1 disrupted many clock control genes but had no effect on oscillation of central components of the molecular clock in U2OS cells.

Loss-of-function PBRM1 mutations are found frequently in ccRCC cases, and our study suggests that high expression of ADIRF-AS1 in PBRM1 WT tumors could phenocopy mutant PBRM1 through the ability of ADIRF-AS1 to inhibit PBAF regulation of selective genes. Indeed, our analysis of TCGA data demonstrates that high expression of ADIRF-AS1 in WT but not mutant PBRM1 cases portends poor prognosis in ccRCC. While multiple studies demonstrate the RNA-binding capacity of SWI/SNF complexes, few RNA-protein interactions have been functionally characterized (Grossi et al., 2020), as we do here for ADIRF-AS1. Hence, understanding when and how functional SWI/SNF-RNA interactions occur could allow for targeted modulation of these extremely important complexes that are commonly mutated in cancers (Tang et al., 2017).

SWI/SNF complexes are phylogenetically conserved, but through combinatorial protein composition, they regulate diverse sets of genes across species and tissues (Zhang et al., 2014b). Whereas the PBAF complex is highly conserved, to date, ADIRF-AS1 is found selectively in primates, and there is no known ADIRF-AS1 mouse orthologue, limiting the ability to dissect its function in mice. The appearance of ADIRF-AS1 in primates could add a species-specific layer of regulation of specific genes that is reflected in the differences of PBAF target genes in murine and human ESCs (Zhang et al., 2014b). The interaction between ADIRF-AS1 and the PBAF complex could be an evolutionary gain in primates for fine-tuning PBAF activity on a subset of genes, particularly in regulating genes that have diurnal expression.

We demonstrated that ADIRF-AS1 can regulate expression of certain genes in a PBRM1-dependent manner, but ADIRF-AS1 likely has multiple functions. Loss of ADIRF-AS1 in 786O and U2OS cells led to DEGs associated with the cell adhesion and ECM organization. While many DEGs in ADIRF-AS1 KO cells were rescued by PBRM1 loss in ADIRF-AS1 DKO cells, few differentially expressed ECM genes were PBRM1 dependent. In this regard, concurrent loss of PBRM1 in ADIRF-AS1 DKO cells alone could not rescue the lack 786O tumorigenesis by loss of ADIRF-AS1. Remarkably and consistent with the ADIRF-AS1-dependent expression of ECM genes, ADIRF-AS1 DKO cells could only form tumors in the presence of Matrigel. Taken together, we found that ADIRF-AS1 is an oncogenic lncRNA that is required for tumorigenesis of 786O cells partly through regulation of PBAF-responsive gene expression and ECM gene expression.

PBRM1 mutation status and expression are used as therapeutic biomarkers in multiple tumor contexts, including ccRCC, and thus our work shows that the effect of ADIRF-AS1 on PBAF may have therapeutic implications. Notably, tumors harboring PBRM1 mutations are more susceptible to VEGF inhibitors (Motzer et al., 2020). Since tumors with high ADIRF-AS1 expression phenocopy PBRM1 mutant cells, they may be more responsive to anti-angiogenic therapies. Thus, re-examination of the clinical responses in patients with WT PBRM1 tumors with respect to high ADIRF-AS1 expression could uncover an additional group of ccRCC patients that might benefit from VEGF inhibitors. Future structural studies of the PBAF and ADIRF-AS1 complex could also yield therapeutic opportunities through approaches that block the inhibitory effect of this lncRNA on the tumor-suppressive function of PBAF as well as PBAF-independent tumorigenic activities. In aggregate, our studies reveal that the circadian lncRNA ADIRF-AS1 binds to the PBAF complex, affects expression of some PBAF-regulated genes, and profoundly alters 786-O tumorigenesis in vivo.

Limitations of the studyWe found that ADIRF-AS1 is a circadian-regulated lncRNA that can function through the PBAF complex to modulate gene expression and play a role in ccRCC tumorigenesis. However, we also point out that ADIRF-AS1 has an alternative function on ECM gene expression and may additionally regulate neighboring gene expression, a relationship not explored in our study. Our study demonstrated co-localization of ADIRF-AS1 with only a minority of nuclear PBRM1, but how ADIRF-AS1 selectively affects only a few percent of PBAF/PBRM1-responsive genes is not illuminated by our current study.

lncRNA pull-down followed by proteomicsIn order to pull down proteins associated with ADIRF-AS1, we used a previously reported protocol (Zhang et al., 2016). The full-length sequence of ADIRF-AS1 was retrieved from U2OS cDNA using sequence specific primers (For: CTTGGGATGAGAGGACTGGG, Rev: AGCATGCAAGGGGAAATCAG) using Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Kit (ThermoFisher F553L) with the GC Buffer. The cDNA sequence of ADIRF-AS1 was cloned into pBluescript II SK (+). Biotin-labeled RNAs were transcribed in vitro using a biotin labeling mix (Roche) and T7 polymerase (Roche) from linearized pBluescript II SK plasmid following the manufacturer’s instructions. Biotinylated RNAs were purified and 3 μg of biotinylated RNA in RNA structure buffer (10 mM Tris-HCL pH 7, 0.1 M KCl, 10 mM MgCl2). To allow proper secondary structure formation, RNA was heated to 95°C for 2 min, put on ice for 3 min, and left at room temperature (RT) for 30 min. Folded RNA and tRNA (0.1 μg/mL) were then mixed with U2OS whole cell lysate (containing 200ug proteins) in 500 μL RIP buffer (150 mM KCl, 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM DTT, 0.5% NP40, 100 U/mL RNase Out, protease inhibitor cocktail) and then incubated at RT for 1 h 50 μL washed Streptavidin agarose beads (Invitrogen) were added to each binding reaction and further incubated at RT for another 1 h. Beads were washed briefly with B&W (10mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1mM EDTA, 2M NaCl) three time and B&W+0.01% Tween 20 three times and boiled in SDS buffer. Then the retrieved proteins were analyzed on SDS gels followed by silver staining. Aliquots were then submitted for mass spectrometry analysis.Proteins pulled down by ADIRF-AS1 and negative controls were submitted to the Wistar Proteomics and Metabolomics Core Facility for LC-MS/MS analysis. In brief, samples were run on a 12% Bis-Tris NuPage gel for 0.5 cm, stained with Colloidal blue, and the entire stained region of each lane was digested with trypsin. The digested peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS on a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer. MS/MS spectra generated from the LC-MS/MS runs were searched using full tryptic specificity against the UniProt human database (www.uniprot.org; 10/01/2017) using the MaxQuant 1.6.0.16 program. False discovery rates for protein and peptide identifications were set at 1%. Protein quantification was performed using unique + razor peptides. Razor peptides are shared (non-unique) peptides assigned to the protein group containing the most unique peptides (Occam’s razor principle).
