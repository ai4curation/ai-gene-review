Chondroitin sulfate (CS) proteoglycans are extended (-GlcAβ1,3GalNAcβ1,4-)n
co-polymers attached to cell surface and extracellular matrix core proteins that
are further modified by extensive sulfation and epimerization. Four homologous
proteins contribute to CS backbone synthesis (CHPF1, CHPF2, CHSY1, and CHSY3)
and prior data suggests assembly of the proteins into heterocomplexes is
required for function. Here we show by sequence alignment and structural
modeling that all CHSYs and CHPFs contain an N-terminal CAZy GT31-like domain
and a C-terminal GT7-like domain separated by a cystatin-like linker domain.
Co-expression of one CHPF and one CHSY is required to form a soluble, functional
heterodimeric CS synthase and structural modeling indicates all four potential
CHSY-CHPF combinations can form equivalent heterodimeric complexes. Cryo-EM
studies on CHSY3-CHPF1 confirm the structure, interface, and active site
features predicted by the structural models. Enzymatic analyses of catalytic
mutants demonstrate that only the glycosyltransferase domains in the CHSYs are
responsible for polymer synthesis: the GT31 domain transfers β1,3-GlcA while the
GT7 domain transfers β1,4-GalNAc. The corresponding CHPF domains do not
contribute to polymer synthesis but stabilize the corresponding CHSY functional
domains. Additional mutagenesis and modeling suggest that the bridging
cystatin-like domains may contribute to efficient polymer synthesis.

Introduction

Chondroitin sulfate (CS) chains are extended carbohydrate polymers comprised of alternating disaccharide repeats of N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) and glucuronic acid (GlcA) residues further modified by sulfation and epimerization1–5. The CS chains are covalently attached to proteoglycan (PG) core proteins on cell surfaces and extracellular matrices and are ubiquitously expressed in higher metazoan organisms6. The glycan polymers participate in key developmental processes, including cytokinesis, morphogenesis, neural development, synaptic plasticity, post-injury axon regeneration, and numerous cell signaling pathways, as well as facilitating pathogen adhesion2,3,7–10. CS is also highly expressed within connective tissues, including cartilage, bone, skin, and ligaments6,11, where it performs multiple functional roles2,4,12.

CS and heparan sulfate (HS), are both glycosaminoglycan (GAG) chains attached to various PG core proteins through a common tetrasaccharide linker2,6. Most PG core proteins contain either CS or HS chains. By contrast, the committed step for CS synthesis is default and does not appear to be influenced by core protein features13. The CHPFs and CHSYs are homologous protein isoforms anchored in Golgi membranes through an N-terminal transmembrane domain with their catalytic regions facing the lumen of the secretory compartment14. The lumenal portion of each isoform contains an N-terminal Carbohydrate Active Enzyme (CAZy17) glycosyltransferase (GT) family 31 (GT31)-like domain and a C-terminal GT7-like domain (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, the CS initiating enzymes, CSGALNAC1/2, catalyze a single β1,4-GalNAc transfer and contain only a homologous GT7-like domain (Fig. 1b). Therefore, it is inferred that the corresponding CHSY/CHPF GT7-like domains catalyze β1,4-GalNAc transfer while the GT31-like domains synthesize the β1,3-GlcA linkages. No studies have demonstrated the roles of each of these domains, their overall structural features, or identified the roles of the additional sequences separating these two globular domains.

Here, we explore the roles of each CHSY and CHPF domain in CS synthesis using modeled and empirical structural approaches, mutagenesis, activity assays, and mass spectrometry analysis of the enzymatic products. Our data demonstrate that both CHSY1 and CHSY3 contain active N-terminal GT31 β1,3-GlcA transferase and C-terminal GT7 β1,4-GalNAc transferase domains. In contrast, the GT-like domains in the CHPFs do not contribute to CS synthesis but stabilize the structures of the catalytic CHSYs. We also show that co-expression of a CHSY with a CHPF is required for effective CS synthase folding and function. Modeling of the four relevant CS synthase heterocomplexes reveals the structural basis for heterodimerization, substrate binding, and catalysis, including the role of cystatin-like domains sandwiched between the two GT domains. The structural models were then validated through cryo-EM structure determination of the most active complex, CHSY3–CHPF1, with bound divalent cations and UDP. Additional mutational analysis of the bridging cystatin-like domains indicates they contribute to efficient polymer synthesis, possibly through interaction with the extending CS chain. Thus, our work demonstrates the structural basis for CS polymer backbone extension by a collection of heterodimeric CS synthase complexes essential for PG biosynthesis in animal cells.

ResultsDomain structure of CS biosynthetic proteinsThe alternating disaccharide repeat units of CS chains are extended on PG core proteins by a collection of four homologous gene products: CHSY1, CHSY3, CHPF1 and CHPF2 (Fig. 1a, b, Supplementary Fig. 1). Each of the four proteins has a similar domain architecture, with an N-terminal transmembrane segment, putative disordered stem region, a GT31-like domain, an extended helix linker, a cystatin-like domain, and a C-terminal GT7-like domain (Fig. 1b, e and Supplementary Fig. 1). By contrast, the two CS priming enzymes, CSGALNACT1/2, exhibit only β1,4-GalNAc transfer activity and are missing the equivalent GT31 domain (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2a). The cystatin-like linker domains show significant structural similarity to the secreted thiol proteinase inhibitors, Cystatin C and M, that have both been shown to bind sulfated HS chains both in vitro and in vivo18–20 through conserved basic patches18,21,22 (Fig. 1b, e).Both the GT31 and GT7 domains of the CS synthetic enzymes are comprised of GT-A Rossmann-like folds17,23 that commonly contain canonical motifs involved in divalent cation coordination (DxD and C-term His), sugar donor binding (G-loop), and catalysis (xED)24,25. While both the GT7 domains of CHSY1 and CHSY3, as well as the GT7 domains of CSGALNACT1/2 contain the respective conserved motif sequences (Fig. 1c), the corresponding GT7 domains of CHPF1 and CHPF2 are less conserved in sequence and lack the canonical motif elements (Fig. 1c). Similarly, the GT31 domains of CHSY1 and CHSY3 retain the conserved GT-A motif sequences, while the corresponding GT31 domains of CHPF1 and CHPF2 again lack the conserved motif elements (Fig. 1c) suggesting that both GT-like domains in the two CHPFs are likely inactive.Expression of soluble ectodomainsPrior studies suggested that expression of single CHSY or CHPF proteins leads to ineffective CS synthase activity, while co-expression can result in functional production of CS synthases2–6,13,14. We generated truncated CHSY and CHPF ectodomains by replacing their N-terminal transmembrane segments with an N-terminal signal sequence, 8xHis, and GFP fusion tag cassette26, and tested expression and secretion in mammalian cells. Expression of each single protein or co-expression of CHPF1-CHPF2 or CHSY1-CHSY3 led to no significant secretion (Fig. 1d). Only when one CHSY and one CHPF were co-expressed was appreciable secretion of the GFP fusion proteins achieved (Fig. 1d) that could also be imaged by GFP fluorescence detection following SDS-PAGE (Supplementary Fig. 2b). The fluorescence detection revealed equal abundance of CHSY3 with either CHPF1 or CHPF2 suggesting the secretion of a stable heterocomplex in the co-transfected cells. SDS-PAGE was unable to resolve CHSY1 from CHPF1 or CHPF2 on the gel, but the obligate co-expression of these proteins suggests a similar requirement of heterocomplex formation for secretion. CS co-polymerase activity was detected for each of the CHSY-CHPF heterocomplexes, but no secreted products or enzyme activities were observed for single protein expressions or the CHPF1-CHPF2 or CHSY1-CHSY3 co-transfections (Fig. 1d). Significantly higher secretion and activity were observed for heterocomplexes containing CHPF1 compared to those employing CHPF2 suggesting the former protein supported more efficient folding and/or secretion. Likewise, CHSY3 pairings appear to be more active than their CHSY1 counterparts (Fig. 1d) suggesting either higher activity or greater stability for the CHSY3-containing complexes13.Modeling CS synthase complexesWe also tested the modeling of various homodimeric and heterodimeric complexes of all CHSYs and CHPFs using AlphaFold 327 (AF3, Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. 3). Modeling of homodimeric complexes or CHPF1-CHPF2 or CHSY1-CHSY3 heterocomplexes led to structures with numerous low confidence regions, especially at the interface loops between subunits (Supplementary Fig. 16). By comparison, the four CHSY-CHPF heterodimeric models were strikingly similar (average pairwise RMSD: 0.93 Å, Supplementary Fig. 3c), contained more ordered, high-confidence loop regions, and parallel dimerization interfaces with pseudo-2-fold axes of symmetry. The interfaces were comprised of antiparallel coiled coils for the bridging helices and dimerization of the two cystatin domains28 (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Figs. 3a, d). The arrangement placed each GT31-like and GT7-like domain pair in adjacent positions in the heterocomplex, but each had minimal direct contributions to the dimer interface. The parallel heterodimeric assembly also placed the respective CHSY GT31 and GT7 active sites on the same face of the heterodimer ~63 Å apart and each active site extended toward the central CHSY cystatin-like domain.We compared structural confidence metrics for the models with a set of known homo- or heterodimeric proteins versus known monomeric proteins (incorrectly modeled in AF3 as homodimers) to assess the protein complexes. Expression of soluble ectodomainsPrior studies suggested that expression of single CHSY or CHPF proteins leads to ineffective CS synthase activity, while co-expression can result in functional production of CS synthases2–6,13,14. We generated truncated CHSY and CHPF ectodomains by replacing their N-terminal transmembrane segments with an N-terminal signal sequence, 8xHis, and GFP fusion tag cassette26, and tested expression and secretion in mammalian cells. DiscussionWhile the individual CHSY and CHPF genes involved in CS backbone synthesis were identified over two decades ago14,36–39, their individual and combined roles in GAG polymer synthesis have remained enigmatic. Expression of individual proteins resulted in no detectable activity, while co-expression data suggested a requirement for heterocomplex formation for CS synthase activity. By analogy to our prior work on the obligate heterodimeric assembly of EXT1-EXT2 as an HS synthase complex40, we systematically tested single and co-expression cultures of CHSY1, CHSY3, CHPF1, and CHPF2 and demonstrated that CS synthase activity requires the formation of heterodimeric complexes of a CHSY and CHPF. Comprehensive AF3 analysis also revealed that, among all permutations of homo- and heterodimeric complexes, only CHSY-CHPF heterocomplexes led to modeled structures with high confidence interface metrics. The four CHSY-CHPF models were highly similar in structure and in close agreement with our cryo-EM structure of the CHSY3–CHPF1:UDP:Mn2+ complex to provide strong support for the predicted models.The roles of individual domains were probed by mutagenesis and detailed enzymatic analysis. The combined data unequivocally demonstrated the GT31 domains of the CHSYs catalyze β1,3-GlcA transfer, while the GT7 domains transfer β1,4-GalNAc residues to the growing CS chain. The respective CHPF domains do not retain canonical GT-A fold features required for catalysis and do not contribute to catalysis. The extensive asymmetric and electrostatically complementary CHSY-CHPF interface is formed largely through coiled-coil interactions of the extended helixes on opposing monomers and dimerization of the cystatin-like domains analogous to dimeric interactions in other cystatins28,41. Thus, the lack of direct contribution of CHPFs to CS extension raises a question regarding their roles in the respective heterocomplexes.Analogous loss of function also occurs in the multi-domain HS synthase complex40. EXT1 and EXT2 each contain GT47-like and GT64-like domains and form a heterocomplex with four potential active sites. However, only the N-terminal EXT1 GT47 β1,4-GlcA transferase domain and the EXT2 GT64 α1,4-GlcNAc transferase domain are functional, while the corresponding EXT1 GT64 and EXT2 GT47 domains have evolved to be inactive. Similarly, EXTL3 catalyzes the committed step in HS synthesis and contains GT47-like and GT64 domains similar to EXT1 and EXT2. However, EXTL3 is a homodimeric enzyme13 and only the GT64 domain remains active while the GT47 domain has evolved to be non-functional. By comparison, the committed step in CS synthesis is catalyzed by CSGALNACT1/2, and these latter enzymes retain the conserved helix, cystatin domain, and functional GT7 domain analogous to the CHSYs/CHPFs, but the GT31-like domain of the CHSYs/CHPFs is not present. The bacterial CS synthase (e.g. E. coli K4CP42) is a more simplified monomeric enzyme with two closely adjoining functional domains that catalyze β1,3-GlcA and β1,4-GalNAc transfer, but without a discernible linker region. Thus, GAG synthases in higher metazoans appear to have evolved as multidomain heterocomplexes for disaccharide repeat synthesis through domain fusion, gene duplication, heterocomplex formation, and diversification of function. Presumably, this specialization provides enzyme stabilization and control of synthesis for diverse PG structures in different adult tissues or during development. In the case of CHSY-CHPF complexes, the CHPFs appear to play obligatory roles in folding and function of the CHSYs through their asymmetric and complementary interactions at the heterodimer interface. The inability to model homodimeric complexes with reasonable interface metrics and the lack of secretion for single CHSY or CHPF expression products in transfected cells strongly suggest that functional CHSY-CHSY or CHPF-CHPF homodimeric complexes are unlikely to be produced in vivo. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that additional cellular or regulatory factors, including association with other CS biosynthetic components, could contribute to the formation of functional single chains or homodimeric complexes for the full-length proteins in vivo. The extensive asymmetric and electrostatically complementary CHSY-CHPF interface is formed largely through coiled-coil interactions of the extended helixes on opposing monomers and dimerization of the cystatin-like domains analogous to dimeric interactions in other cystatins28,41. Thus, the lack of direct contribution of CHPFs to CS extension raises a question regarding their roles in the respective heterocomplexes.

Analogous loss of function also occurs in the multi-domain HS synthase complex40. EXT1 and EXT2 each contain GT47-like and GT64-like domains and form a heterocomplex with four potential active sites. However, only the N-terminal EXT1 GT47 β1,4-GlcA transferase domain and the EXT2 GT64 α1,4-GlcNAc transferase domain are functional, while the corresponding EXT1 GT64 and EXT2 GT47 domains have evolved to be inactive. Similarly, EXTL3 catalyzes the committed step in HS synthesis and contains GT47-like and GT64 domains similar to EXT1 and EXT2. However, EXTL3 is a homodimeric enzyme13 and only the GT64 domain remains active while the GT47 domain has evolved to be non-functional. By comparison, the committed step in CS synthesis is catalyzed by CSGALNACT1/2, and these latter enzymes retain the conserved helix, cystatin domain, and functional GT7 domain analogous to the CHSYs/CHPFs, but the GT31-like domain of the CHSYs/CHPFs is not present. The bacterial CS synthase (e.g. E. coli K4CP42) is a more simplified monomeric enzyme with two closely adjoining functional domains that catalyze β1,3-GlcA and β1,4-GalNAc transfer, but without a discernible linker region. Thus, GAG synthases in higher metazoans appear to have evolved as multidomain heterocomplexes for disaccharide repeat synthesis through domain fusion, gene duplication, heterocomplex formation, and diversification of function. Presumably, this specialization provides enzyme stabilization and control of synthesis for diverse PG structures in different adult tissues or during development. In the case of CHSY-CHPF complexes, the CHPFs appear to play obligatory roles in folding and function of the CHSYs through their asymmetric and complementary interactions at the heterodimer interface. The inability to model homodimeric complexes with reasonable interface metrics and the lack of secretion for single CHSY or CHPF expression products in transfected cells strongly suggest that functional CHSY-CHSY or CHPF-CHPF homodimeric complexes are unlikely to be produced in vivo. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that additional cellular or regulatory factors, including association with other CS biosynthetic components, could contribute to the formation of functional single chains or homodimeric complexes for the full-length proteins in vivo. While stabilization of the catalytic CHSYs is a plausible role for the CHPFs, additional contributions of the CHPFs to structural or allosteric dynamics within the heterocomplex cannot be ruled out and will require further study

To model details of substrate interactions in the CHSY-CHPF complexes, we leveraged the recent advancements in biomolecular modeling using open-source AF3 with expanded capabilities for modeling complex protein-ligand structures. The resulting structural models paired both donors, acceptors, and divalent cations in their respective active sites in positions for catalysis, despite providing no additional information for domain substrate specificity. Substrate interactions also resulted in plausible Michaelis complex models for the SN2 inverting catalytic mechanisms for both CHSY active sites. In agreement with our empirical CHSY3–CHPF1 structure, the models also support experimental data suggesting that the CHPFs do not bind substrates or contribute to catalysis. Proposed model for CS extension by CHSY-CHPF complexes.

CS synthase (CHSY–CHPF) complexes are membrane-anchored enzymes located in the Golgi apparatus, with their catalytic domains projecting into the Golgi lumen. These complexes are formed through the heterodimerization of CHSY and CHPF monomers. Each monomer contains a GT31-like domain, a GT7-like domain, and a bridging linker region composed of an extended helix and a cystatin-like domain, which facilitates subunit heterodimerization. Extension of CS chains on proteoglycan core proteins begins after CSGALNACT1/2 enzymes add a β1,4-GalNAc to the tetrasaccharide linker. This is followed by alternating additions of β1,3-GlcA and β1,4-GalNAc catalyzed by the CHSY-CHPF heterocomplexes. The GT31 domain of the CHSY subunit catalyzes GlcA transfer, while its GT7 domain mediates GalNAc transfer. The associated CHPF subunit does not contribute to catalysis but instead stabilizes the heterocomplex and the catalytic domains of the CHSY subunit. During chain elongation, the growing CS chain alternates between the GT31 and GT7 active sites through a dissociative mechanism. Meanwhile, the central cystatin-like domains of CHSY and CHPF may contribute to catalysis by interacting with the CS chain, potentially enhancing substrate positioning or improving processivity during chain extension.

The substrate-bound models also indicate that the β1,3-GlcA and β1,4-GalNAc transferase active sites are 63 Å apart. This suggests a dissociative enzymatic mechanism where the acceptor alternates between active sites during CS chain elongation. Interestingly, both active sites show acceptor interactions that extend toward the central cystatin-like domain between the respective catalytic domains. The structural similarity of these domains to secreted cystatin thiol proteinase inhibitors known to bind sulfated heparan sulfate chains18–20 suggests a potential role in substrate recognition or positioning. Cystatin domains have previously been shown to interact with HS chains through three characteristic basic patches19 and two of those equivalent patches are found in the CHSY and CHPF cystatin-like domains. Mutagenesis of these basic patches, and particularly cystatin-like cluster 3 of CHSY3 and CHPF1, led to significant loss of CS co-polymerase activity using large proteoglycan acceptors, but not short CS oligo acceptors. It is tempting to hypothesize that extended CS chain or core protein interactions with the CHSY3 cystatin cluster 3 region could anchor the polymer as it alternates between the two active sites to provide more efficient polymer synthesis. However, the cluster 3 cystatin-like domain of CHPF1 is on the opposite side of the heterocomplex and mutations in that cystatin cluster also lead to equivalent enzyme inactivation when large proteoglycan acceptors are employed. Cystatin-like domains are also found in other proteins involved in PG synthesis, including the CS committed step enzymes CSGALNACT1, CSGALNACT2 (Fig. 1), and PG linker initiation enzymes, XYLT143, and XYLT2. Thus, the roles of these domains in PG synthesis are more complex and will require further study to unravel their contributions in linker synthesis and CS chain elongation.

Human defects in CS synthase activity are relatively rare and the Human Gene Mutation Database44 notes only ten known missense/nonsense mutations in CHSY1, most leading to Temtamy preaxial brachydactyly syndrome (TPBS)45–48. Zebrafish models of antisense knockdown for chsy1 resulted in defects similar to human patients with TPBS. Since similar phenotypes in zebrafish were found after chsy1 inactivation or overexpression, the authors suggested that brachydactyly could be linked to either to loss or gain of BMP signaling45. Human mutations in CHSY3, CHPF1, or CHPF2 have not been described and this paucity of known genetic defects contrasts with over 100 human mutations in EXT1 or EXT2 that impact HS synthesis40. The potential for redundancy resulting from two functional CHSYs combined with two CHPFs in the formation of four putative CS synthase complexes could lead to masking of mutations that inactivate the individual enzyme subunits.

While this study provides a comprehensive structural and functional characterization of CS synthase complexes, several questions remain. Future research will need to explore how these complexes interact with other enzymes involved in CS modification, the precise mechanisms of substrate recognition and processivity during CS chain elongation, including the roles of the cystatin domains, and how the activity of these complexes is regulated in vivo. Additionally, the structural insights gained from this study may be leveraged to develop targeted therapies for diseases associated with CS dysregulation, including the roles of CS in inhibition of axon regeneration after injury10. Our findings provide a structural and mechanistic framework for understanding CS biosynthesis, opening additional avenues for investigating the roles of CS in development, disease, and potential therapeutic interventions.

PISA analysis of AlphaFold modelsAll protein complexes were analyzed in PISA56 to derive values for surface area, interface area, PISA ΔG, and PISA P-Values. A comparison of values for the positive and negative controls allowed us to set a cutoff of interface confidence at >80%, PISA P-value of <0.3, ratio of interface area to total protein surface area >4%, and PISA ΔG value <−20 kcal/mol. These values were then inverted or turned to relative values for plotting in Supplementary Fig. 3b.

Cryo-EM data processingCryo-EM data processing was carried out using cryoSPARC v4.5.160. The 5,811 micrographs were corrected for the beam-induced drift with patch motion correction and the contrast transfer function (CTF) of each aligned micrograph was determined using patch CTF estimation. The micrographs were analyzed and manually selected based on CTF fit resolution, ice thickness and total full-frame motion. The resulting 4,311 micrographs were used for iterative jobs of particle picking, first using blob picker and then template picker functions, resulting in 2,689,859 particles that were extracted with a box size of 512 pixels and Fourier-cropped to 192 pixels (rescaled pixel size of 1.442 Å). The particles were subjected to several rounds of 2D classification with 100 classes per round to discard bad particles. Particles were further sorted by a combination of ab-initio modeling and heterogeneous refinement. Non-uniform (NU) refinements were used to assess resolution and quality of the reconstruction maps. This resulted in a subset of 512,703 particles displaying a reconstruction map with an average resolution of 3.63 Å. This subset of particles was re-extracted and sorted through further heterogeneous refinements, in combination with 3D classification and NU-refinements. Finally, for the last 163,527 subset of particles, rounds of local refinements, local and Global-CTF refinements contributed to yield a final reconstruction map of 3.42 Å. The local resolution was estimated with cryoSPARC v4.5.160 and the directional resolution estimation was estimated using the 3DFSC server61, which calculated a sphericity of 0.931.
