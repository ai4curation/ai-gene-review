Gibberella stalk rot (GSR), caused by the fungal pathogen Fusarium graminearum,
severely threatens maize production. However, the molecular mechanisms
underlying maize resistance to GSR remain poorly understood. Here, we have
identified ZmWRKY29 as a transcriptional repressor induced by F. graminearum
infection, which negatively regulates maize resistance to GSR. Overexpression of
ZmWRKY29 significantly compromised GSR resistance with a marked reduction in
reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and callose deposition, while its
mutation enhanced resistance and elevated ROS accumulation. RNA-seq revealed
that the expression levels of two respiratory burst oxidase homologues, ZmRBOHA
and ZmRBOHC, were decreased in ZmWRKY29-OE lines following F. graminearum
challenge. ZmWRKY29 directly binds to the W-boxes in the promoters of ZmRBOHA
and ZmRBOHC to repress their transcription. Moreover, mutation of ZmRBOHC
similarly led to increased susceptibility and diminished ROS accumulation,
confirming that ZmWRKY29-targeted ZmRBOHC is required for ROS production and GSR
resistance. Thus, ZmWRKY29 acts as a susceptibility factor, repressing GSR
resistance possibly by inhibiting ZmRBOHC-mediated ROS production. This
highlights a pathogen strategy of exploiting host transcriptional machinery to
suppress ROS defences and facilitate infection.

Introduction

Stalk rot, a devastating stalk disease in maize (
Zea mays
 L.), primarily occurs during the tasselling‐silking phase of the reproductive stage (Kazan and Gardiner 2018). The infected maize plants display severely compromised water and nutrient uptake, elevated lodging susceptibility and ultimately result in substantial yield loss (Jackson et al. 2009). Among all the causal agents of stalk rot, Fusarium graminearum, responsible for Gibberella stalk rot (GSR), is dominant both in prevalence and pathogenicity (Broders et al. 2007; Sanna et al. 2023). At the initiation of infection in the stalk, F. graminearum hyphae grow intercellularly along the parenchyma cells and longitudinally expand to adjacent intercellular spaces. Later, the hyphae penetrate into the cells and break down the cell structures (Zhang et al. 2016). Beyond stalk infection, F. graminearum can reach maize ears through airborne transmission, causing Gibberella ear rot (Goswami and Kistler 2004). Or, it also colonises the seedling stems and roots, resulting in seedling blight and root rot (Goswami and Kistler 2004). Therefore, F. graminearum displays a multidimensional infection strategy, posing significant challenges for disease management in maize production.

Plants have evolved sophisticated defence mechanisms against pathogens, yet the molecular basis of maize resistance to GSR remains poorly understood. Known mechanisms include the receptor kinase ZmWAK17, which activates hypersensitive response‐mediated cell death (Sun et al. 2021), and terpenoid phytoalexins such as zealexins and kauralexins, whose absence reduces GSR resistance (Ding et al. 2019, 2020; Huffaker et al. 2011; Schmelz et al. 2011). Although resistance (R) genes are crucial for breeding, only three GSR‐resistant QTLs have been identified: qRfg1 (ZmCCT), which enhances cell wall lignification via phenylpropanoid metabolism (Liu et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2010); qRfg2 (ZmAuxRP1), balancing growth and defence through auxin and benzoxazinoid pathways (Ye et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2012) and qRfg3, which has not been cloned yet (Ma et al. 2017). Beyond R genes, susceptibility (S) genes offer promising resistance when disrupted or functionally impaired, as shown in rice where editing promoter elements of OsSWEET genes conferred broad‐spectrum resistance to all known
Xanthomonas oryzae
 pv. oryzae (Xoo) strains (Koseoglou et al. 2022; Oliva et al. 2019; Pavan et al. 2010). Yet the S genes of GSR remain poorly characterised.

The burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in apoplast is important for plant immunity, as it triggers the hypersensitive response (HR) and cell death to restrict pathogen infection (Qi et al. 2017). This process is mainly mediated by the membrane‐localised respiratory burst oxidase homologues (RBOHs), also known as NADPH oxidases (NOXs) (Wu et al. 2023). In Arabidopsis, AtRBOHD and AtRBOHF are two major enzymes involved in the ROS burst upon immunity (Torres et al. 2002). In maize, ZmRBOHD is phosphorylated by ZmBLK1 and executes the immune responses to maize grey leaf spot (Zhong et al. 2024). However, the involvement of RBOHs in maize GSR resistance remains elusive, and their regulatory mechanisms need further dissection.

Our study identifies ZmWRKY29 as a transcriptional repressor that is upregulated by F. graminearum infection and acts as a negative regulator for GSR. Overexpression of ZmWRKY29 increased susceptibility to GSR, while the Zmwrky29 mutant plants exhibited reduced disease symptoms. Further analysis showed that ZmWRKY29 inhibited ROS production after infection and repressed transcription of ZmRBOHA and ZmRBOHC by binding to specific W‐boxes in their promoters. Similarly, the Zmrbohc mutant displayed reduced resistance to GSR with diminished ROS production. Thus, our findings reveal ZmWRKY29 facilitates GSR susceptibility by attenuating ZmRBOHC‐mediated ROS production in maize.

The seeds were sown in March or April and harvested in July or August, 45–60 days after artificial self‐pollination. The planting was carried out using a wide‐narrow row system, with a row width of 3 m, wide row spacing of 0.8 m, narrow row spacing of 0.4 m, plant spacing of 0.3 m and 8 hills per row. Each genotype was planted in a wide‐narrow double‐row plot. A minimum of 6 replicate plots was used for the measurements. Based on pollination quality, 3–5 well‐pollinated ears were selected from each plot for the assessment of yield‐related traits.

Plant Materials and Growth Condition

Maize inbred lines used in this study included B73, Mo17 and KN5585. Each genotype was planted in a wide‐narrow double‐row plot. A minimum of 6 replicate plots was used for the measurements. Based on pollination quality, 3–5 well‐pollinated ears were selected from each plot for the assessment of yield‐related traits.

RNA Isolation and Expression Patterns AnalysisFresh samples from normal or infected leaves or stems were used for total RNA extraction. After grinding the samples into fine powder in liquid nitrogen, RNA extraction was carried out using the TRNzol reagent kit (Tiangen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The RNA samples were measured for absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer. The A260/A280 ratio was calculated to assess RNA purity. A total of 2 μL of RNA sample was loaded for electrophoresis analysis. The integrity of 28S and 18S rRNA bands was observed to evaluate RNA degradation. Using 1 μg of RNA as a template, subsequently DNA digestion and cDNA synthesis were carried out with the reverse transcriptase (Vazyme). The stems from WT and ZmWRKY29‐OE plants at 0 h and 24 h after infection (hai) were further used for RNA‐seq analysis. Gene expression levels were analysed using reads comparison IGV_2.17.0 software and a heatmap was drawn with the normalised FPKM by TBtools (Chen et al. 2020). Quantitative Real‐time PCR (qPCR) was performed on a Bio‐ CFX384 PCR system using the SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara) with gene specific primers listed in Table S1. The expression levels were normalised to the reference gene Ef1a with the 2−ΔΔCT method (Fu et al. 2016). At least three biological replicates were conducted for each experiment. The expression levels in different maize tissues were obtained from Hoopes et al. Atlas in Maize eFP Browser (https://bar.utoronto.ca/) (Hoopes et al. 2019; Winter et al. 2007). The expression levels from different maize temperate (TEM) and tropical/subtropical (TST) subgroups were obtained from ZEAMAP database (https://db.cngb.org/zeamap/) (Gui et al. 2020).

2.5Subcellular LocalizationThe CDS sequence of ZmWRKY29 was cloned into pCAMBIA‐GFP‐2300 vector to generate the ZmWRKY29‐GFP fusion protein. Subsequently, the vector was transformed into maize protoplasts prepared as described previously (Fu, Wang, et al. 2023). In brief, maize seedlings were grown in a weak light condition for about 2 weeks and the full‐expanded second leaves were cutting into strips. After 10 min Hyperosmotic treatment by soaking into 20% mannitol buffer, the leaf strips were subjected to for Enzymatic digestion in solution I [1.5% cellulase RS (w/v), 0.6% macerozyme R10 (w/v) (Yakult), 0.6 M mannitol, 10 mM MES (pH 5.7), 10 mM CaCl2 and 0.1% BSA (w/v)]. The protoplasts were released after 4–6 h incubation at 28°C and filtered with the nylon net to collected and reach a concentration of 2 × 106 cells mL−1. Then, the fluorescence was observed in a confocal microscopy (NiKon A1 i90, LSCM, Japan) after 16 h transformation. Empty pCAMBIA‐GFP‐2300 vector was used as the control.

2.6Transcriptional Activity AssayTo assess the transcriptional suppression activity of ZmWRKY29, experiments were conducted using both the yeast system and maize protoplast system according to previous studies (Yan et al. 2018; Zong et al. 2016). Firstly, in the yeast system, the CDS of ZmWRKY29 was inserted into the pGBKT7 vector to construct the recombinant plasmid pGBKT7‐ZmWRKY29, which was then transformed into the competent cells of yeast stain AH109. Simultaneously, an empty vector pGADT7 was co‐transformed as a control. The transformation products were spread on SD/−Trp/−Leu medium and incubated at 28°C for 3–5 days. Then, the clones were diluted 10×, 100× and 1000× sequentially and spotted on SD/−Trp/−Leu medium and SD/−Ade/‐His/−Trp/−Leu medium. Based on previous studies, to evaluate the transcriptional activity in protoplasts, a transcription activation domain VP16 was fused to the N‐terminus of ZmWRKY29 and then fused with GAL4‐DBD to construct the pBD‐VP16‐ZmWRKY29 vector as the effector (Aoyama and Chua 1997; Wang et al. 2021). GAL4‐DBD with or without VP16 (pBD and pBD‐VP16) were used as positive and negative control effectors, respectively. The effectors were then co‐transformed with the firefly luciferase (LUC) reporter vector and the renilla luciferase (REN) as the internal standard. The LUC/REN ratio was used to defined the transcriptional activity (Fu, Pei, et al. 2023). At least four biological replicates were conducted for this assay.

2.7Pathogen Inoculation and Symptom Evaluation2.7.1Seedlings Stem InoculationThe filter paper discs (6 mm) were evenly distributed on PDA medium for the cultivation of F. graminearum at 25°C for about 7 days until the mycelium completely covered the surface of the medium. A sterile incision was made 1.5 cm above the base of the stems of 2‐week‐old maize seedlings, and an inoculated filter paper disc was inserted and sealed with paraffin film to maintain humidity. The seedlings were then cultured under normal conditions for 3–5 days to observe disease symptoms and measure lesion lengths. Stems inoculated with sterile water‐moistened filter paper discs were used as control. DNA of infected stems was extracted using the CTAB method, and qRT‐PCR analysis was performed to determine the fungal biomass using specific primers for the FgTri6 gene. The relative amount of fungal DNA was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, normalised to ZmUBQ1 promoter as an internal reference (Fu, Wang, et al. 2023). Primers were listed in Table S1.2.7.2Seedling Root InoculationAfter germination on moist filter paper, the sterilised maize seeds were transferred to vertical germination bags and grown for 3–5 days. Seedlings with consistent growth were selected. The roots of these seedlings were immersed in a suspension of 1 × 106 spores/mL for half an hour, then returned to the bags for further cultivation for 3–5 days before observing disease symptoms. Seedlings immersed in water served as controls. Disease severity was categorised into five levels based on the colour intensity of lesions.2.7.3Field Stalk InoculationStem injection method was utilised at the third internode of maize stems during the booting stage, where a 45° angled insertion of an injector was made into the stem center followed by injecting 300 μL of a 1 × 106 spores/mL suspension. After 7 days, stems were longitudinally dissected to measure lesion lengths. At least ten biological replicates were conducted for these experiments.

Our work, along with prior studies, underscores the importance of WRKY transcription factors as key transcriptional regulators of RBOH genes across species, serving dual roles as activators or repressors. For instance, in tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana), multiple WRKY TFs directly bind to the NbRBOHB gene promoter and activate its expression (Adachi et al. 2015). Similarly, the CsAP2‐09‐CsWRKY25 module transcriptionally activates CsRBOH2 to maintain ROS homeostasis and citrus bacterial canker resistance in citrus (Li et al. 2024). Conversely, wheat TaWRKY19 represses TaNOX10 expression, attenuating stripe rust resistance (Wang et al. 2022). Our findings add a new dimension to this paradigm by demonstrating that maize ZmWRKY29 functions as a transcriptional repressor of ZmRBOHA and ZmRBOHC, thereby impairing ROS production and enhancing susceptibility to F. graminearum (Figure 8). This positions WRKY‐RBOH transcriptional modules as crucial, yet diversely manipulated, nodes in plant‐pathogen interactions.

Beyond the established role in defence, ROS play essential roles in growth, development, and abiotic stress responses, implying that manipulating these pathways can have pleiotropic effects (Morales and Munne‐Bosch 2016). This is exemplified by contrasting phenotypes in rboh mutants of different species. In rice, osrbohc mutants show severely reduced yield alongside compromised disease resistance (Zhu et al. 2024). Intriguingly, although ZmRBOHC is the maize ortholog of OsRBOHC and its mutation similarly reduces disease resistance, we observed a modest but significant yield increase in zmrbohc mutants under controlled conditions (Figure S10). This phenotypic divergence may be linked to distinct expression patterns: ZmRBOHC displays constitutively high expression during development and is slightly suppressed by pathogen infection (Figure S8), whereas OsRBOHC maintains low basal levels but is inducible by PAMPs (chitin, flg22) (Zhu et al. 2024). Given that ZmRBOHC‐mediated apoplast ROS production is also involved in stomatal closure to enhance drought resistance (Gao et al. 2022), its constitutive expression might influence developmental processes differently in maize. The observed ROS accumulation in stomata of inoculated WT plants, which was attenuated in ZmWRKY29‐OE lines (Figure 3A), further prompts the question of whether the ZmRBOHC‐mediated stomatal closure is involved in GSR resistance and whether such involvement is regulated by ZmWRKY29 to facilitate infection.

The utilisation of S genes like ZmWRKY29 in breeding is promising but challenging, as they often participate in essential physiological processes (Pavan et al. 2010). Thus, the enhanced disease resistance resulting from their mutations often comes at the cost of growth and yield penalties (Zaidi et al. 2018). As seen in wheat, TALEN‐mediated knockout of wheat MLO alleles confers powdery mildew resistance but induces deleterious phenotypes (Wang et al. 2014). To overcome such trade‐off, researchers identified the Tamlo‐R32 mutant that harbours a ~304‐kb deletion near TaMLO‐B1, overcoming the growth disadvantages while maintaining powdery mildew resistance (Li et al. 2022). In this study, ZmWRKY29 mutant plants exhibited significantly increased resistance to GSR (Figure 4). However, when we attempted to create ZmWRKY29‐edited plants in the KN5585 background, we found that the callus was difficult to regeneration, suggesting that ZmWRKY29 may affect maize growth. Although we obtained the Mu‐inserted mutant of ZmWRKY29, it should be noted that this mutant is in the B73 background, where unknown genetic mechanisms might compensate for the loss of ZmWRKY29 function, underscoring the influence of genetic context.

The utilisation of S genes like ZmWRKY29 in breeding is promising but challenging, as they often participate in essential physiological processes. Knockout may enhance resistance but at the cost of yield or growth penalties, as seen in wheat MLO mutants. We observed that ZmWRKY29 mutation significantly boosts GSR resistance. However, difficulties in regenerating edited calli in one genetic background hint at its potential role in growth regulation. It is noteworthy that our resistant mutant is in the B73 background, where unknown genetic mechanisms might compensate for the loss of ZmWRKY29 function, underscoring the influence of genetic context.

To potentially overcome the resistance‐growth trade‐off, future strategies could focus on fine‐tuning rather than eliminating ZmWRKY29 function. This could involve screening for natural haplotypes with weaker pathogen‐inducible expression or employing CRISPR/dCas9‐mediated transcriptional modulation to precisely disrupt its infection‐triggered upregulation. Such approaches may enable the development of maize lines with enhanced GSR resistance while minimising negative impacts on agronomic traits, leveraging the regulatory role of ZmWRKY29 on ZmRBOHs‐mediated ROS production.
