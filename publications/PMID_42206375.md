BACKGROUND: Bisphenol F (BPF) is a common substitute for bisphenol A and the
most prevalent bisphenol compound in diverse plastic manufacturing applications.
However, the potential toxicity of BPF remains largely unexplored. This study
investigates the effects of BPF on the cardiovascular system and intestinal
barrier.
METHODS: Germ-free mouse models and fecal microbiota transplantation techniques
were used to confirm the role of gut microbiota in BPF-induced cardiovascular
injury. Untargeted metabolomics and spatial metabolomics were used to identify
the in vivo metabolic products of BPF. Single-cell sequencing was used to
identify which cardiac cell types were damaged by BPF exposure.
RESULTS: BPF was detected in 90.5% of 285 human urine samples (median, 1.16
ng/μg creatinine). BPF exposure induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, cardiac
dysfunction, and intestinal barrier damage, effects contingent on the presence
of gut microbiota. Metabolomic analysis identified the microbial conversion of
BPF to N-acetylputrescine (NAP). Mechanistically, we found that BPF stimulated
intestinal epithelial cells to secrete spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase
1 (Sat1), which catalyzed this conversion. Furthermore, NAP impaired the
intestinal barrier by disrupting the Golgi-mitochondria axis and caused cardiac
hypertrophy by activating the p53 pathway and inhibiting glycolysis in
cardiomyocytes. Supplementation with Akkermansia muciniphila or its metabolite
tryptophol mitigated BPF-induced cardiac and intestinal injuries by
downregulating the Sat1-NAP axis. Clinical analysis further showed elevated
serum NAP levels in patients with inflammatory bowel disease, positively
correlating with cardiac injury markers.
CONCLUSIONS: BPF disrupts intestinal barrier function through microbial
metabolism involving the tryptophol/Sat1 pathway, leading to NAP production. NAP
damages intestinal organelles and enters circulation, inducing cardiac p53
activation and hypertrophy. This study delineates a novel gut
microbiota-Sat1-NAP pathway underlying BPF-induced cardiotoxicity, offering new
insights for risk assessment and therapeutic intervention.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

The identification of NAP, catalyzed by Sat1, as the key circulating metabolite linking intestinal injury to cardiac toxicity provides a potential biomarker (serum NAP) for monitoring cardiovascular risk in bisphenol F–exposed populations.

Supplementation with Akkermansia muciniphila or tryptophol offers a novel probiotic strategy for preventing and treating bisphenol F–induced cardiac injury.

The elucidation of this pathway provides a crucial mechanistic basis for assessing the cardiovascular toxicity risks of environmental pollutants such as chemicals associated with microplastics.

Bisphenol compounds are widely used as plasticizers and heat stabilizers in the production of bottled water containers, mobile phone cases, food packaging, and canned beverages.1 These substances are continuously released as plastics degrade.2 Bisphenol A (BPA) is a commonly used bisphenol compound; however, cumulative evidence suggests that BPA has reproductive toxicity, neurotoxicity, and cardiotoxicity.3–6 In a follow-up cohort of nearly 11 years, higher concentrations of BPA in human urine were associated with the occurrence of coronary heart disease (n=1919).6 This has caused the restriction of BPA use in many countries. Bisphenol F (BPF) is a popular alternative to BPA because it has a chemical structure similar to that of BPA. In addition, BPF is preferred in industrial applications as resin additives because of its superior solubility and lower viscosity.7 Clinical studies showed that BPF has a high detection rate in urine samples, that is, 51.7% in 8-year-old children,8 88% in adults,9 and as high as 98.3% in pregnant women,10 indicating broad and common exposure. Current research indicates that higher concentrations of bisphenols in maternal urine are negatively correlated with carotid intima-media thickness in children.11 In addition, epidemiological studies demonstrated a significant positive correlation between urinary BPF levels and the incidence of cardiac diseases, particularly congestive heart failure.12 Nevertheless, the underlying molecular mechanism of BPF cardiotoxicity remains elusive.

BPF enters the body predominantly through the digestive tract, with the majority accumulated in the digestive system.13 Therefore, the intestine serves as the primary target organ of BPF exposure. Despite the widespread BPF pollution, comprehensive safety assessments for BPF are lacking. Environmental compounds can compromise gut health by reducing mucosal barrier function, increasing intestinal permeability, disrupting homeostasis, or inducing gut injuries.14,15 Exposure to bisphenol compounds may also alter the diversity and composition of gut microbiota, exacerbating intestinal inflammation.16–18 However, current studies on BPF toxicity are limited primarily to zebrafish models, with minimal evidence from human population studies.19 Gut microbiome metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and tryptophan are key signaling molecules that regulate intestinal homeostasis20 and facilitate colorectal cancer progression.21 Recently, low doses of BPF (7 ng/mL) were shown to induce changes in mitochondrial function and ATP production in cardiomyocytes.22 Moreover, mitochondria may drive inflammatory responses and support host defense and immunity.23 Consistently, BPA exposure is associated with worsening symptoms in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).24 However, few studies have investigated whether BPF exacerbates symptoms in individuals with intestinal inflammation. A zebrafish study showed that BPF exposure exhibited various inflammatory changes, including increased intestinal mucus secretion, intestinal villus injury, and substantial upregulation of inflammatory factors.18 However, the mechanisms underlying the potential effects of BPF exposure remain largely unknown.

According to the tolerable daily intake of BPA for humans set by the US Environmental Protection Agency and the European Food Safety Authority at 50 μg·kg−1·d−1, accounting for the ≈9.1-fold difference in body surface area between humans and mice, we investigated the toxic effects of BPF exposure (500 μg·kg−1·d−1) in mice. We previously showed that this dose of BPF significantly induced pathological damage in the intestine.25 To further assess the toxicological impact of BPF exposure on the general population, we referred to our detection of BPF levels in human urine (1.16 ng/μg creatinine), which suggested 10 μg·kg−1·d−1 as an appropriate level for animal tests. Moreover, to examine the early ultrastructural changes in cells at doses that do not cause obvious pathological damage, we selected an even lower dose of 5 μg·kg−1·d−1 as the low-dose group and 50 μg·kg−1·d−1 as the high-dose group. Both levels were much lower than the tolerable daily intake for BPA (50 μg·kg−1·d−1) established by the US Environmental Protection Agency and European Food Safety Authority. Here, we investigated whether BPF exposure induces both cardiovascular injuries and intestinal injuries by focusing on metabolic alterations and changes in the gut microbial ecosystem.

ResultsLow-Dose BPF Exposure Leads to Cardiac Hypertrophy In VivoTo evaluate the impact of BPF exposure in humans, we first collected urine samples from 285 healthy individuals and analyzed them using high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Study population characteristics are shown in Figure S1A. The average BPF detection rate was 90.5% and increased with age. The median concentration of BPF was 1.16 ng/μg creatinine. These findings suggest that BPF contamination is widespread in the population. To assess the potential health risks of BPF exposure, we used 2 experimental dose groups (5 and 50 μg/kg) in mice that were substantially below the 50 μg·kg−1·d−1 tolerable daily intake threshold established for its structural analog BPA by the US Environmental Protection Agency and European Food Safety Authority in humans. To evaluate the potential impact of BPF exposure on cardiac function, a BPF-exposed mouse model was established with intragastric administration (Figure 1A). Animals were exposed daily for 28 consecutive days. We found that, compared with the control group, 28-day exposure to 50 μg/kg BPF significantly elevated blood pressure without overt changes in ratios of heart weight to body weight or heart weight to tibia length (Figure 1B–1E and Figure S1B). Cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area was increased, and ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide) expression, a marker of cardiac injury, was upregulated (Figure 1F and 1G and Figure S1C). Echocardiographic analysis showed no significant changes in ejection fraction or fractional shortening in BPF-exposed mice compared with control mice (Figure 1H and 1I). However, BPF exposure was associated with a significant increase in α-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (α-HBD), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) levels in mice, suggesting the onset of cardiomyocyte injury (Figure 1J and Figure S1D). Because BPF is used as an alternative to BPA, we compared the cardiotoxic effects of BPA and BPF exposure at the same concentration (50 μg/kg). The results showed that, compared with the control group, BPF and BPA both significantly increased the cross-sectional area of mouse cardiomyocytes. Although the BPA-exposed group exhibited a greater increasing trend than the BPF group, the difference was not statistically significant (Figure S1E and S1F). Furthermore, compared with the control group, BPF exposure elevated the level of the myocardial injury marker ANP, whereas BPA exposure significantly increased the levels of multiple myocardial injury markers, including ANP, BNP (b-type natriuretic peptide), and β-MHC (Figure S1G). In addition, BPF-induced cardiac injury showed no significant sex differences between male and female mice (Figure S1H–S1J). Gut Microbiome Mediates BPF-Induced Intestinal Barrier Damage

Given that the gut microbiota resides primarily in the colorectal tissue and our previous findings have demonstrated its crucial role in BPF-mediated cardiac injury (Figure 2), we further assessed the potential impact of BPF exposure on intestinal function by performing pathological analysis of colorectal tissues from the following established mouse models: wild-type mice with BPF exposure alone, Ang II+BPF groups, and their counterparts in germ-free mice. Furthermore, the intestinal barrier damage induced by BPF was not significantly different from that induced by BPA, with no significant differences observed between male and female mice (Figure S3C–S3E). Collectively, these findings suggest that BPF exposure may impair the intestinal barrier, a process that appears to be mediated by gut microbiota.

Considering that intestinal barrier dysfunction represents a primary manifestation of gut injury, we subsequently evaluated the impact of BPF exposure at different concentrations (5 and 50 μg/kg) on intestinal barrier–related parameters. NAP Promotes Mitochondrial Fusion by Reducing the Secretion of Golgi VesiclesKnockdown of triphosphatase ADP-ribosylation factor 1 (Arf1) or its effector phosphatidylinositol 4-kinase IIIβ (Pi4kb), which drives Drp1 (mitochondrial motion-related protein 1)-mediated mitochondrial fission, has been demonstrated to reduce Drp1, leading to increased mitochondrial fusion.33 Arf1, localized primarily to the Golgi apparatus, has been implicated in internal vesicle transport. We hypothesized that NAP suppresses Golgi vesicle formation by downregulating Arf1 or Pi4kb, thereby reducing Drp1 recruitment and triggering excessive mitochondrial fusion. Experimental results showed that, after NAP treatment, the expression levels of Arf1, Pi4kb, and Drp1 in colon organoids were significantly decreased, with the inhibition of Golgi function–associated Arf1 and Pi4kb preceding that of mitochondrial function-associated Drp1 (Figure 4J). Rasip1 possesses a Ras-association domain and functions as an effector of Rap and Ras.34 Arf1, a small GTP enzyme belonging to the Ras superfamily, is involved in COPI vesicle formation.35,36 The function of Arf1 requires a cycle of GTP binding and hydrolysis.35 Next, coimmunoprecipitation assays were used to test whether Rasip1 interacts with Arf1. Our results revealed that Rasip1 bound to Arf1, which was disrupted by NAP (Figure 4K). Consistently, overexpression of both Rasip1 and Arf1 significantly increased the production of Golgi vesicles and reversed the reduction in vesicles induced by NAP treatment (Figure S5J).Given that Arf1, Pi4kb, and Drp1 are all altered at the mRNA level by NAP treatment, we further explored their potential transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. Through prediction and ChIP analysis, we found that FoxQ1 transcriptionally regulates both Arf1 and Drp1, whereas Cebpβ transcriptionally regulates Pi4kb and Drp1 (Figure 4L and Figure S5K S5L). Subsequently, we treated colonic organoids with virus to overexpress Arf1, Pi4kb, or Drp1 (Figure S5M). As shown in Figure 4M, overexpression of Pi4kb or Pi4kb/Arf1 alleviated the NAP-induced damage to both the Golgi apparatus and mitochondria, whereas overexpression of Drp1 alleviated mitochondrial damage (damaged Golgi apparatus indicated in yellow; damaged mitochondria indicated in red). In summary, these results suggest that BPF-induced organelle damage in intestinal cells may result from the inhibition of the transcription factors Cebpβ and FoxQ1, which subsequently reduces Arf1 and Rasip1 levels and thereby suppresses the generation of Golgi vesicles. Knockdown of triphosphatase ADP-ribosylation factor 1 (Arf1) or its effector phosphatidylinositol 4-kinase IIIβ (Pi4kb), which drives Drp1 (mitochondrial motion-related protein 1)-mediated mitochondrial fission, has been demonstrated to reduce Drp1, leading to increased mitochondrial fusion.33 Arf1, localized primarily to the Golgi apparatus, has been implicated in internal vesicle transport. We hypothesized that NAP suppresses Golgi vesicle formation by downregulating Arf1 or Pi4kb, thereby reducing Drp1 recruitment and triggering excessive mitochondrial fusion. Experimental results showed that, after NAP treatment, the expression levels of Arf1, Pi4kb, and Drp1 in colon organoids were significantly decreased, with the inhibition of Golgi function–associated Arf1 and Pi4kb preceding that of mitochondrial function-associated Drp1 (Figure 4J). Rasip1 possesses a Ras-association domain and functions as an effector of Rap and Ras.34 Arf1, a small GTP enzyme belonging to the Ras superfamily, is involved in COPI vesicle formation.35,36 The function of Arf1 requires a cycle of GTP binding and hydrolysis.35 Next, coimmunoprecipitation assays were used to test whether Rasip1 interacts with Arf1. Our results revealed that Rasip1 bound to Arf1, which was disrupted by NAP (Figure 4K). Consistently, overexpression of both Rasip1 and Arf1 significantly increased the production of Golgi vesicles and reversed the reduction in vesicles induced by NAP treatment (Figure S5J).

Given that Arf1, Pi4kb, and Drp1 are all altered at the mRNA level by NAP treatment, we further explored their potential transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. Through prediction and ChIP analysis, we found that FoxQ1 transcriptionally regulates both Arf1 and Drp1, whereas Cebpβ transcriptionally regulates Pi4kb and Drp1 (Figure 4L and Figure S5K S5L). Subsequently, we treated colonic organoids with virus to overexpress Arf1, Pi4kb, or Drp1 (Figure S5M). As shown in Figure 4M, overexpression of Pi4kb or Pi4kb/Arf1 alleviated the NAP-induced damage to both the Golgi apparatus and mitochondria, whereas overexpression of Drp1 alleviated mitochondrial damage (damaged Golgi apparatus indicated in yellow; damaged mitochondria indicated in red). In summary, these results suggest that BPF-induced organelle damage in intestinal cells may result from the inhibition of the transcription factors Cebpβ and FoxQ1, which subsequently reduces Arf1 and Rasip1 levels and thereby suppresses the generation of Golgi vesicles. Because Pi4kb is a key functional protein within vesicles that targets mitochondria and promotes normal mitochondrial fission by recruiting Drp1,33 the decline in Pi4kb levels after NAP exposure consequently impairs Drp1‑driven mitochondrial fission (Figure S5N).
