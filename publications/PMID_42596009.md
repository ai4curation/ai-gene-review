BACKGROUND: Pathogenic sequence alterations in the SLC26A4 gene, which encodes
the solute carrier SLC26A4/pendrin, lead to Pendred syndrome and non-syndromic
autosomal recessive deafness type B4 (DFNB4), two of the most common forms of
hearing loss worldwide. Many pathogenic SLC26A4 protein variants exhibit reduced
cellular levels due to ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS)-mediated degradation,
and UPS inhibition rescues their plasma membrane expression and ion transport
function. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear and may
involve interactions with novel molecular partners.
METHODS: A candidate SLC26A4 protein partner was found by a yeast two-hybrid
screening. The biological significance of this interaction has been studied by
immunohistochemistry and co-localization in the mouse inner ear and kidney,
co-immunoprecipitation of endogenous and recombinant proteins, Liquid
Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry, and Fluorescence Resonance Energy
Transfer.
RESULTS: We identified the zinc finger and BTB domain-containing protein ZBTB16
as a novel SLC26A4-interacting partner. ZBTB16 co-localized with SLC26A4 in the
outer sulcus and spiral prominence epithelial cells of the mouse cochlea and in
the apical membrane of non-alpha non-beta intercalated cells of the distal
nephron. ZBTB16 was found to be part of a ubiquitin-ligase complex comprising
the scaffold protein Cullin 3 and the ubiquitin ligase RocI, and to bind with
its C-terminal zinc finger region a unique amino acid sequence within the
C-terminal Sulfate Transporter and Anti-Sigma factor Antagonist (STAS) domain of
SLC26A4. This direct molecular interaction leads to increased site- and
variant-specific ubiquitination and accelerated degradation of SLC26A4. Finally,
using AI-based structure prediction, we provide an atomistic model of the
complete SLC26A4/ZBTB16/Cullin 3/RocI complex in agreement with our experimental
results.
CONCLUSION: These findings describe a primary mechanism of SLC26A4 regulation in
the inner ear and kidney and of SLC26A4 loss of function in Pendred syndrome and
deafness DFNB4, and identify potential novel molecular targets for therapeutic
intervention.

BackgroundThe SLC26 family of solute carriers comprises ten protein members primarily functioning as anion exchangers moving multiple anions such as chloride, bicarbonate, sulfate, oxalate, formate, and iodide across cell membranes. Owing to their functional versatility and widespread expression or tissue specificity, SLC26 transporters play fundamental roles in several physiological processes, ranging from ion uptake and secretion across epithelia to the regulation of systemic and fluid compartment pH and volume [1, 2]. SLC26A4/pendrin is a chloride/iodide/bicarbonate exchanger expressed in some specific tissues, including distinct epithelial cells in the lateral wall of the cochlea, spindle-shaped cells within the stria vascularis, mitochondria-rich cells in the endolymphatic sac and duct, and transitional cells surrounding the sensory organs in the saccule, utricle, and ampullae of semicircular canals [3]. Other tissues with relevant SLC26A4 expression are the thyroid and the kidney; in the thyroid, SLC26A4 is expressed on the apical membrane of follicular cells and presumably permits iodide efflux from the thyrocyte to the follicular lumen [4]. In the kidney, SLC26A4 is expressed on the apical membrane of beta and non-alpha non-beta intercalated cells of the distal nephron and is essential for renal base excretion and chloride reabsorption, which are critical for renal acid–base and NaCl balance and the control of vascular volume and blood pressure [5].Studies in knockout mice and genetically modified mice with Slc26a4 expression restricted to the endolymphatic sac provided evidence that Slc26a4 is crucially involved in controlling the volume of the endolymph via chloride reabsorption in the endolymphatic sac and buffering the endolymphatic pH via bicarbonate secretion in the endolymph of all inner ear compartments [6–8]. Patients with pathogenic variants in the SLC26A4 gene develop autosomal recessive non-syndromic hearing loss (DFNB4) or Pendred syndrome. Both conditions are characterized by early-onset severe to profound or later-onset fluctuating and progressive hearing loss associated with inner ear malformations, i.e., an enlarged vestibular aqueduct with dilated endolymphatic sac and duct (EVA), with or without a cochlear incomplete partition type II. Vestibular dysfunction can also be present. In Pendred syndrome, patients also exhibit a partial iodide organification defect and may develop subclinical or overt hypothyroidism with or without goiter [9]. DFNB4 and Pendred syndrome are among the most common non-syndromic and syndromic forms of genetic hearing loss in humans, and EVA is the most common inner ear malformation [10].Reduced or absent ion-transport function in mammalian heterologous expression systems was observed for several pathogenic SLC26 protein variants, including SLC26A4 [11]. At the protein level, trapping within subcellular compartments and failure to reach the plasma membrane due to misfolding and lack of glycosylation have been reported for several SLC26 missense variants [12–15]. The loss of protein stability consequent to misfolding seems to represent a crucial pathophysiological mechanism in SLC26-related dysfunction [16]. A number of additional mechanisms involving amino acid substitutions affecting the SLC26 ion-binding sites, the anion exchange pathway, the dimerization interface, the interaction sites with membrane lipids or essential protein partners, or preventing the conformational changes necessary to ion transport have also been studied or proposed [16, 17]. In principle, however, any of these alterations can also lead to protein misfolding and consequent instability and degradation. Numerous SLC26A4 protein variants exhibit reduced cellular levels compared to the wild-type following heterologous expression and in the kidney of a knock-in mouse, regardless of being localized at the plasma membrane or in the intracellular compartments [18–21]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms involved in this process are only partially elucidated.We recently showed that many pathogenic SLC26A4 protein variants exhibit a reduction in protein expression that arises from accelerated protein degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS). We also demonstrated that total SLC26A4 protein expression and ion transport function are positively correlated. Accordingly, inhibition of the UPS with proteasome inhibitors (MG132, bortezomib, carfilzomib, and delanzomib) increased the total protein abundance of the SLC26A4 variants, paralleled by a significant increase in their plasma membrane expression, and led to a significant rescue of ion transport activity. The partially functional SLC26A4 variant p.R409H proved to be particularly responsive to proteasome inhibition, and its iodide transport was rescued to levels close to those of the wild-type [20]. Together, these findings showed that pathogenic SLC26A4 variants are degraded by the UPS, and preventing the UPS-mediated protein degradation can rescue their expression at the cell surface and ion transport function. Thus, the precise identification of the molecular machinery recruiting SLC26A4 to the UPS may offer specific targets for mechanistic interventions. Here, we show that the zinc finger and Bric-à-brac, Tramtrack, Broad-complex (BTB) domain-containing protein ZBTB16 is the receptor/adaptor protein that binds SLC26A4 within a specific sequence of its C-terminus for recruitment to UPS-mediated degradation by a molecular complex composed of the scaffold protein Cullin 3 (CUL3) and the ubiquitin ligase ROC1.

In order to obtain a plausible atomistic model of the entire SLC26A4/ZBTB16/CUL3/ROC1 complex, we utilized the AlphaFold 3 model [37] for deep learning-based structure prediction to calculate de novo structure models including two copies of each protein and phosphorylations in the SLC26A4 IVS region (see methods and Additional File 4). DiscussionSequence alterations in SLC26 genes are involved in determining human diseases, including various forms of chondrodysplasias (SLC26A2/DTDST), congenital chloride diarrhea (SLC26A3/DRA), kidney stones (SLC26A6), hearing loss (SLC26A4/pendrin and possibly SLC26A5/prestin), and spermatogenic failure (SLC26A8) [55–58]. Molecular genetics and functional studies helped disclose the mechanism of disease for some of these conditions. Aberrant mRNA splicing, copy number variation, complex gene rearrangements, insertions/deletions, and point mutations have all been described for SLC26 family members, with most gene variants leading to the production of an altered protein product. Reduced or absent ion transport function in mammalian heterologous expression systems was observed for several SLC26 pathogenic protein variants, including SLC26A2 [12], SLC26A3 [13], SLC26A4 [11], and SLC26A6 [59]. In parallel with reduced function, reduced protein abundance has been observed for some missense SLC variants, including SLC26A2 p.A386V expressed in Xenopus oocytes [60] and SLC26A2 p.R279W, p.Q454P, p.C653S, and p.A715V expressed in HEK cells [12]. The same was observed for pathogenic variants of SLC26A8 (p.R87Q, p.E812K, and p.R954C) expressed in CHO-K1 cells [58] and SLC26A6 (p.R621G, p.D674N, and p.R507W) in HEK293 and opossum kidney (OKP) cells [59, 61]. The ubiquitin–proteasome system has been implicated in the degradation of pathogenic variants of SLC26A8 and SLC26A4 [20, 58]. However, the molecular players that recruit these targets for degradation were not identified.In the quest for SLC26A4-interacting partners, our yeast two-hybrid screening detected Zbtb16 as a putative Slc26a4-binding protein (Fig. 1a, Figure S2, and Additional File 2). ZBTB16, formerly called promyelocytic leukemia zinc finger (PLZF), zinc finger protein 145 (ZFP145), or zinc finger 145 (ZNF145), is a multifunctional protein initially identified as the product of a gene translocated in acute promyelocytic leukemia [62]. Wild-type ZBTB16 works as a transcriptional regulator with multiple roles in fundamental biological functions [47], including repression of pro-inflammatory genes [63], spermatozoal maturation [64], expression of interferon-stimulated genes crucial for natural killer cell function and resistance to viral infection [65], and survival of mesenchymal cells involved in bone morphogenesis [66]. Consistent with this latter function, Zbtb16 knockout mice display patterning defects affecting multiple skeletal structures of the limb and axial skeleton [67].In the adult rat cochlea, Zbtb16 was shown to be strongly expressed in the organ of Corti and to interact with the outer cell motor protein prestin (SLC26A5) [68]. The functional significance of this interaction, however, remained unknown. Zbtb16 levels are up-regulated by corticosteroids in the adult mouse cochlea, and Zbtb16, as well as corticosteroids, protect the cochlea from acoustic trauma [69]. Although the molecular mechanism underlying this protective effect remained unknown, these findings point to important housekeeping functions of Zbtb16 in the inner ear.In addition to the role in transcriptional regulation, Zbtb16 has recently been shown to participate in protein ubiquitination. Polyubiquitination targets many cellular proteins for degradation by the 26S proteasome pathway via the sequential activity of three enzymes: ubiquitin-activating (E1), ubiquitin-conjugating (E2), and ubiquitin-ligases (E3). The largest family of E3 ligases takes part in macromolecular assemblies comprising receptor/adaptor proteins for target recognition and scaffold proteins represented by Cullins 1–7 [70, 71]. Zbtb16 establishes a direct molecular interaction with Cul3 and increases the polyubiquitination of its interaction partners CAND1, LMNB1, DNMT1, SATB1 and H1.2 [52, 72, 73]. Zbtb16 amino acids 1–118 form the BTB/POZ region, while the Ct portion contains 9 C2H2 zinc finger motifs ([74], Figure S2c and 8f). BTB domain-containing proteins serve as receptors/adaptors only for Cul3, and no other cullins [70, 71, 75], and Cul3 serves as a scaffolding protein for Roc1 (Rbx1) E3 ligase [73], pointing to the existence of a Zbtb16-Cul3-Roc1 complex. In fact, the Zbtb16-Cul3-Roc1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex was shown to determine the ubiquitination and degradation of Atg14L, a master regulator of autophagy that mediates the production of phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate to initiate the formation of autophagosomes [51]. To our knowledge, no other target of the Zbtb16-Cul3-Roc1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex has been found to date.In the adult mouse cochlea, abundant Zbtb16 expression was found by immunohistochemistry in the auditory nerve, organ of Corti, and stria vascularis [69]. We confirmed Zbtb16 expression in the neonatal stria vascularis and adult mouse cochlea, where it co-localized with Slc26a4 (Fig. 1b, d, and e). High expression of Cul3 and Roc1 transcript and/or protein was detected in the adult mouse cochlea and neonatal mouse stria vascularis, pointing to a fundamental function of these proteins in the inner ear (Fig. 1b and d). In addition, Cul3 co-localized with Slc26a4 in the adult mouse cochlea (Fig. 1f).In the mouse whole kidney, Slc26a4, Zbtb16, Cul3, and Roc1 transcripts and/or proteins were expressed (Fig. 1b and 2b), Zbtb16 co-localized with Slc26a4 on the apical membrane of a subpopulation of non-alpha non-beta-intercalated cells (Fig. 2a and S3c), and Slc26a4 and Zbtb16 proteins were co-immunoprecipitated from mouse whole kidney lysates, denoting an interaction of the endogenous proteins in mouse native tissue (Fig. 2e).In human tissues, we detected the expression of ZBTB16, CUL3, and ROC1 transcripts in thyroid and kidney, where SLC26A4 is known to be abundantly expressed (Fig. 1c). Based on the co-localization between Slc26a4 and Zbtb16 in the inner ear and kidney (Fig. 1e and 2a), the co-localization between Slc26a4 and Cul3 in the inner ear (Fig. 1f), and the co-immunoprecipitation of endogenous Slc26a4 and Zbtb16 from mouse kidney (Fig. 2e), we propose SLC26A4 as a novel target of the ZBTB16-CUL3-ROC1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex.The observation that ZBTB16 gave a significant FRET with intracellular SLC26A4 rather than SLC26A4 correctly trafficked to the plasma membrane (Fig. 4c) suggests that this molecular interaction can be relevant for misfolded pathogenic SLC26A4 protein variants. We formerly showed that protein levels of several pathogenic SLC26A4 protein variants, including p.R409H and p.L236P, are reduced compared to the wild-type due to degradation by the UPS [20]. Like many SLC family members, SLC26A4 is also known to exist as a dimer [17, 54, 93]. One hundred fifty-five predicted complex structures were produced using different seeds, with a wide diversity of tertiary and quaternary structures. Remarkably, the structure that had the highest pTM/ipTM scores also had one of the highest numbers of contacts between SLC26A4 and ZBTB16 (Fig. 10). Aberrant mRNA splicing, copy number variation, complex gene rearrangements, insertions/deletions, and point mutations have all been described for SLC26 family members, with most gene variants leading to the production of an altered protein product. Reduced or absent ion transport function in mammalian heterologous expression systems was observed for several SLC26 pathogenic protein variants, including SLC26A2 [12], SLC26A3 [13], SLC26A4 [11], and SLC26A6 [59]. In parallel with reduced function, reduced protein abundance has been observed for some missense SLC variants, including SLC26A2 p.A386V expressed in Xenopus oocytes [60] and SLC26A2 p.R279W, p.Q454P, p.C653S, and p.A715V expressed in HEK cells [12]. The same was observed for pathogenic variants of SLC26A8 (p.R87Q, p.E812K, and p.R954C) expressed in CHO-K1 cells [58] and SLC26A6 (p.R621G, p.D674N, and p.R507W) in HEK293 and opossum kidney (OKP) cells [59, 61]. The ubiquitin–proteasome system has been implicated in the degradation of pathogenic variants of SLC26A8 and SLC26A4 [20, 58]. However, the molecular players that recruit these targets for degradation were not identified.

In the quest for SLC26A4-interacting partners, our yeast two-hybrid screening detected Zbtb16 as a putative Slc26a4-binding protein (Fig. 1a, Figure S2, and Additional File 2). ZBTB16, formerly called promyelocytic leukemia zinc finger (PLZF), zinc finger protein 145 (ZFP145), or zinc finger 145 (ZNF145), is a multifunctional protein initially identified as the product of a gene translocated in acute promyelocytic leukemia [62]. Wild-type ZBTB16 works as a transcriptional regulator with multiple roles in fundamental biological functions [47], including repression of pro-inflammatory genes [63], spermatozoal maturation [64], expression of interferon-stimulated genes crucial for natural killer cell function and resistance to viral infection [65], and survival of mesenchymal cells involved in bone morphogenesis [66]. Consistent with this latter function, Zbtb16 knockout mice display patterning defects affecting multiple skeletal structures of the limb and axial skeleton [67].

In the adult rat cochlea, Zbtb16 was shown to be strongly expressed in the organ of Corti and to interact with the outer cell motor protein prestin (SLC26A5) [68]. The functional significance of this interaction, however, remained unknown. Zbtb16 levels are up-regulated by corticosteroids in the adult mouse cochlea, and Zbtb16, as well as corticosteroids, protect the cochlea from acoustic trauma [69]. Although the molecular mechanism underlying this protective effect remained unknown, these findings point to important housekeeping functions of Zbtb16 in the inner ear.

In addition to the role in transcriptional regulation, Zbtb16 has recently been shown to participate in protein ubiquitination. Polyubiquitination targets many cellular proteins for degradation by the 26S proteasome pathway via the sequential activity of three enzymes: ubiquitin-activating (E1), ubiquitin-conjugating (E2), and ubiquitin-ligases (E3). The largest family of E3 ligases takes part in macromolecular assemblies comprising receptor/adaptor proteins for target recognition and scaffold proteins represented by Cullins 1–7 [70, 71]. Zbtb16 establishes a direct molecular interaction with Cul3 and increases the polyubiquitination of its interaction partners CAND1, LMNB1, DNMT1, SATB1 and H1.2 [52, 72, 73]. Zbtb16 amino acids 1–118 form the BTB/POZ region, while the Ct portion contains 9 C2H2 zinc finger motifs ([74], Figure S2c and 8f). BTB domain-containing proteins serve as receptors/adaptors only for Cul3, and no other cullins [70, 71, 75], and Cul3 serves as a scaffolding protein for Roc1 (Rbx1) E3 ligase [73], pointing to the existence of a Zbtb16-Cul3-Roc1 complex. In fact, the Zbtb16-Cul3-Roc1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex was shown to determine the ubiquitination and degradation of Atg14L, a master regulator of autophagy that mediates the production of phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate to initiate the formation of autophagosomes [51]. To our knowledge, no other target of the Zbtb16-Cul3-Roc1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex has been found to date.

In the adult mouse cochlea, abundant Zbtb16 expression was found by immunohistochemistry in the auditory nerve, organ of Corti, and stria vascularis [69]. We confirmed Zbtb16 expression in the neonatal stria vascularis and adult mouse cochlea, where it co-localized with Slc26a4 (Fig. 1b, d, and e). High expression of Cul3 and Roc1 transcript and/or protein was detected in the adult mouse cochlea and neonatal mouse stria vascularis, pointing to a fundamental function of these proteins in the inner ear (Fig. 1b and d). In addition, Cul3 co-localized with Slc26a4 in the adult mouse cochlea (Fig. 1f).

In the mouse whole kidney, Slc26a4, Zbtb16, Cul3, and Roc1 transcripts and/or proteins were expressed (Fig. 1b and 2b), Zbtb16 co-localized with Slc26a4 on the apical membrane of a subpopulation of non-alpha non-beta-intercalated cells (Fig. 2a and S3c), and Slc26a4 and Zbtb16 proteins were co-immunoprecipitated from mouse whole kidney lysates, denoting an interaction of the endogenous proteins in mouse native tissue (Fig. 2e).

In human tissues, we detected the expression of ZBTB16, CUL3, and ROC1 transcripts in thyroid and kidney, where SLC26A4 is known to be abundantly expressed (Fig. 1c). In whole-cell lysates, human recombinant ZBTB16 co-immunoprecipitated with wild-type human SLC26A4, and vice versa (Fig. 3a, b and c). The strong FRET between wild-type SLC26A4 and ZBTB16 indicates a stable and direct interaction in intact cells, which would not be detected if the two proteins were localized in different subcellular compartments or if the interaction was indirect, i.e., mediated by other binding partners (Fig. 4a). Both co-immunoprecipitation and FRET experiments showed that ZBTB16 binds SLC26A4 with its zinc finger C-terminal domain (Fig. 3d and e and 4b), thus confirming the yeast-two-hybrid data (Additional File 2 and Figure S2) and supporting the concept that the zinc finger region of ZBTB16 recruits the target and the BTB domain binds CUL3 [72].

A strong FRET efficiency was also measured between ZBTB16 and CUL3, and between CUL3 and ROC1 (Fig. 5a and b). Strikingly, ZBTB16, CUL3, and ROC1 could all be simultaneously detected in immunoprecipitated SLC26A4 samples (Fig. 6c). These results and former findings [51, 52, 73] demonstrate a direct molecular interaction between these protein pairs and support the existence of a ZBTB16-CUL3-ROC1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. Based on the co-localization between Slc26a4 and Zbtb16 in the inner ear and kidney (Fig. 1e and 2a), the co-localization between Slc26a4 and Cul3 in the inner ear (Fig. 1f), and the co-immunoprecipitation of endogenous Slc26a4 and Zbtb16 from mouse kidney (Fig. 2e), we propose SLC26A4 as a novel target of the ZBTB16-CUL3-ROC1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex.

The observation that ZBTB16 gave a significant FRET with intracellular SLC26A4 rather than SLC26A4 correctly trafficked to the plasma membrane (Fig. 4c) suggests that this molecular interaction can be relevant for misfolded pathogenic SLC26A4 protein variants. Indeed, ZBTB16 interacted with all four SLC26A4 variants tested, including p.R409H and p.L236P, which are among the most common SLC26A4 pathogenic variants in Caucasians (Fig. 3f and g and 4a). We formerly showed that protein levels of several pathogenic SLC26A4 protein variants, including p.R409H and p.L236P, are reduced compared to the wild-type due to degradation by the UPS [20]. Notably, the ubiquitination levels of p.R409H and p.L236P variants were increased in the presence of ZBTB16 (Fig. 3h), supporting a role of ZBTB16 in their degradation.

Although expression of ZBTB16 alone did not lead to the expected acceleration of SLC26A4 degradation in protein half-life measurements (Figure S4), expression of all components of the degradation complex significantly shortened the half-life of the unglycosylated forms of p.R409H and p.L236P, while the effect on wild-type SLC26A4 did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 6a and b). In contrast, the abundance of the glycosylated forms of the proteins was not affected (Fig. 6a and b). These results suggest a role for the ZBTB16-CUL3-ROC1 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex in the degradation of immature SLC26A4 protein variants and are consistent with the FRET experiments revealing an interaction between SLC26A4 and ZBTB16 in the intracellular compartments (Fig. 4c). The ZBTB16-CUL3-ROC1 complex reduced the iodide influx driven by wild-type SLC26A4, suggesting functional repercussions of the ZBTB16-mediated protein degradation on the mature (glycosylated and plasma membrane-resident) SLC26A4 protein (Fig. 6e). Strikingly, the reduction in function of wild-type SLC26A4 (24%, Fig. 6e) exactly corresponded to the decrease in protein levels at 8 h (24%, Fig. 6b). The effect on p.R409H and p.L236P function could not be measured due to the minimal residual activity of these variants. Thus, one can envision the intracellular and plasma membrane compartments as two communicating protein reservoirs, in which degradation of the immature form of the protein ultimately affects the abundance of the plasma membrane-resident functional protein.

Immunoprecipitation of wild-type SLC26A4 and SLC26A4 variants p.R409H and p.L236P expressed in the presence and absence of the components of the degradation complex (ZBTB16-CUL3-ROC1), followed by nano LC–MS/MS, permitted the identification of specific ubiquitination sites and evaluation of their differential regulation (Fig. 10). First, wild-type SLC26A4 and both SLC26A4 variants appeared to be ubiquitinated at multiple sites (Fig. 10a); second, ubiquitination levels were significantly increased for SLC26A4 variants at sites shared with the wild-type, while variant-specific ubiquitination sites were detected (Fig. 10c); and third, the ZBTB16-CUL3-ROC1 complex increased ubiquitination levels of variant p.R409H at many sites, an effect that was particularly prominent at lysine 593, which is a variant-specific site (Fig. 10a). These results are consistent with those shown in Fig. 3, where the interaction strength and ZBTB16-dependent ubiquitination were particularly conspicuous for the SLC26A4 p.R409H variant.

Multiple plasma membrane ion and water transport proteins, including the chloride channel CFTR, are regulated by the UPS [76]. The Cullin/RING (really interesting new gene) E3 ubiquitin ligase complex Cul4-Rbx1-DDB1-Cereblon (CRL4CRBN) targets the large-conductance Ca2+ and voltage-activated K+ (BK) channels [77] and ClC1 chloride channels [78]. Cul7-Rbx1-Skp1-Fbxw8 (CRL7Fbxw8) mediates the proteasomal degradation of the Kv10.1 K+ channel [79], and inhibition of the chaperon-like small acidic protein 14-3-3 reduced Cul7-mediated degradation of Kv10.1 disease-associated protein variants [80]. A Cul5-containing E3 complex is involved in the degradation of the water channel aquaporin 2 (AQP2) [81]. The CRL3KLHL3 ubiquitin ligase complex, which comprises Cul3 and the Kelch-like protein 3 (KLHL3) substrate adaptor, is a master regulator of ion transport in the kidney and modulates the abundance of Ser-Thr kinases WNK1 and WNK4, which, in turn, regulate the activity of the Na+/Cl− co-transporter NCC, the epithelial Na+ channel ENaC, the K+ channel ROMK, and the Na+–K+–2Cl− co-transporter NKCC2 [82]. Because ZBTB16 and CUL3 are abundantly expressed in the mouse and human kidney (Fig. 1b and c and 2), Zbtb16 co-localized with Slc26a4 in the distal nephron (Fig. 2a and S3c) and co-immunoprecipitated with Slc26a4 in mouse whole kidney lysates (Fig. 2e), it is likely that CUL3 may use Zbtb16 as an alternative receptor to regulate Slc26a4, and possibly other targets, not only in the inner ear, but also in the kidney.

Owing to their structural complexity, membrane transporters and channels may be targeted for degradation by multiple systems, acting sequentially or in concert. It is conceivable that proteins with hydrophobic transmembrane domains and large intracellular portions might be the target of both ER membrane-associated and cytosolic E3 ligase complexes. For example, both the E3 ligases CRL5 and the C-terminus of Hsp70-interacting protein (CHIP) are involved in the degradation of AQP2 [81, 83]. The ER membrane-associated E3 ligase Rma1 recognizes folding defects in ΔF508 CFTR coincident with translation, while the cytosolic CHIP acts after translation [76, 84]. Rma1 is involved in SLC26A4 degradation, at least for the wild-type and variants with relatively mild folding defects, like p.H723R, while other variants are unaffected. It is worth noting that CHIP does not participate in this process [85]. Therefore, it is likely that an additional novel pathway may control the turnover of SLC26A4 and its variants, possibly in conjunction with Rma1.

We sought to identify the ZBTB16-binding region within the SLC26A4 C-terminus. Co-immunoprecipitation and FRET experiments involving sequential deletions of the SLC26A4 C-terminus led to the identification of a string of 90 amino acids (584–673) critically involved in the interaction with ZBTB16 (Fig. 7). This region comprises the intervening sequence (IVS) of the SLC26A4 Sulfate Transporter and Anti-Sigma factor antagonist (STAS) domain (Fig. 8a and g). This region is poorly conserved among SLC26 human paralogs (Figure S5), and its function is currently unknown. Interestingly, the IVS is particularly rich in acidic amino acids (E, glutamic acid, and D, aspartic acid, Fig. 8a), and can be responsible for electrostatic interactions with positively charged proteins. The zinc finger region of ZBTB16 is rich in basic amino acids (mainly K, lysine, and R, arginine; H, histidine, is also partially positively charged at physiological pH, Figure S2b). The IVS is flexible and therefore is missing in the two cryo-EM models of SLC26A4 available to date, and its structure is unresolved [17, 54]. The 584–674 amino-acid string isolated from the rest of the protein could establish a significant interaction with ZBTB16 (Fig. 8b and c). However, SLC26A4 C-terminal deletions p.A584X and p.Y530X still interacted with ZBTB16, although to a lower extent compared to p.R674X and the longer constructs (Fig. 7c and e), indicating that additional interaction sites must be present.

Ser/Thr phosphorylation is a prerequisite for targeting proteins to ubiquitin-mediated degradation [86, 87]. SLC26A4 is phosphorylated at multiple sites [88–90]; however, whether phosphorylation regulates SLC26A4 degradation remains unknown. The C-terminal SLC26A4 region that is crucial for the interaction with ZBTB16 (amino acids 584–673) contains six putative Ser/Thr phosphorylation sites for PKA, CKII, and GSK3 (Fig. 8a). In particular, the PKA site on Ser594 (phosphorylation score 0.549 on a scale from 0 to 1) lies within a region of high protein–protein interaction score (Figs. 7a and 8a), suggesting that phosphorylation at this site may control a protein–protein interaction. However, this site is not conserved between human and mouse (Figure S2a). The putative (606) SXXXS (610) CKII and GSK3 consensus sequence is conserved between human and mouse (Figure S2a) and is an additional phosphorylation site of particular interest. GSK3 is frequently involved in targeting proteins to UPS after a phosphorylation priming event at an SXXXS sequence by a distinct kinase has occurred [86]. Thus, two additional C-terminal deletions were designed to include or exclude these phosphorylation sites. Truncation of the SLC26A4 polypeptide at amino acid position 606 leads to a substantial loss of interaction with ZBTB16 compared to the longer construct p.S642X (Fig. 8e). This finding reduces the relevant interacting sequence to amino acids 606–641 (Fig. 8a and g) and further suggests that phosphorylation at sites within this amino acid string may (positively) modulate the interaction. Whether phosphorylation is involved in targeting SLC26A4 to ZBTB16-mediated degradation requires further investigation.

The Cullin 3-containing E3 ligases (CRL3 complex) act in a dimeric form comprising two separate CUL3-ROC1 subunits and two BTB domain-containing receptor/adaptor proteins to form a large, symmetric 2:2:2 complex containing two substrate-binding sites and two catalytic centers [91, 92]. Like many SLC family members, SLC26A4 is also known to exist as a dimer [17, 54, 93]. Thus, we have modeled an SLC26A4/ZBTB16/CUL3/ROC1 complex containing two units of each protomer by using AF3 (Fig. 9 and Additional File 4). One hundred fifty-five predicted complex structures were produced using different seeds, with a wide diversity of tertiary and quaternary structures. AF3 provides the predicted template modeling score (pTM) and its interfacial counterpart (ipTM) as internal confidence metrics in the overall complex, as well as confidence in the protomer interfaces. To narrow our selection and to identify the most plausible model consistent with our experimental data, we calculated the number of protein–protein contacts between a protomer of SLC26A4 and ZBTB16, using the residue ranges identified experimentally for SLC26A4 (residues 606–642) and ZBTB16 (406–673) as an experimental reference. Remarkably, the structure that had the highest pTM/ipTM scores also had one of the highest numbers of contacts between SLC26A4 and ZBTB16 (Fig. 10). Thus, our selected atomistic model of the multi-protein complex agrees well with our experimental data, assumes reasonable quality scores, and thus provides an excellent starting point for further investigations into the dynamic nature and functional significance of these interactions. Nevertheless, an important task of future studies will be to further refine and test our model using site-directed mutagenesis experiments or cryo-electron microscopy.

While remaining elusive for decades, molecular partners establishing a direct molecular interaction with SLC26A4 are starting to emerge. Ephrin receptor A2 (EphA2) binds to and controls the plasma membrane localization of Slc26a4 in the mouse inner ear, kidney, and thyroid. Some pathogenic variants of Slc26a4 do not interact with EphA2, and EPHA2 variants identified in Pendred syndrome patients may lead to an aberrant SLC26A4 turnover [94]. IQGAP1 colocalizes with Slc26a4 on the apical membrane of Slc26a4-expressing cells in the mouse kidney and significantly enhances its Cl−/HCO3− exchanger activity and plasma membrane localization following expression in HEK293 and MDCK cells, respectively [43]. Recently, the adaptor protein-2 (AP-2), an endocytic adaptor involved in cargo recognition and clathrin-mediated endocytosis, was shown to co-localize and interact with SLC26A4. Pharmacological inhibition of clathrin-mediated endocytosis led to an increased plasma membrane abundance of HA-tagged wild-type Slc26a4 in mitochondria-rich cells of the mouse endolymphatic sac, suggesting that this pathway can be targeted to rescue hypofunctional SLC26A4 variants with reduced expression at the plasma membrane [95].

The UPS and the Cullin-RING system are druggable targets. Several small-molecule compounds targeting the Cullin–RING E3 ubiquitin ligases that promote or prevent the degradation of specific substrates have been identified [71]. For example, several inhibitors of the interaction between Keap1, a substrate adaptor protein of the CUL3-based ubiquitin E3 ligase, and its target nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) are being tested in clinical trials or have been approved for clinical use [96]. On the other hand, UPS end-activity inhibitors, such as bortezomib, prevent the degradation of multiple cellular proteins. Disruption of protein turnover and consequent protein accumulation render these substances toxic and provide the rationale for their clinical use in cancer treatment. Our previous findings show that inhibition of proteasome-mediated protein degradation with end-activity inhibitors of the UPS rescues the function of pathogenic SLC26A4 protein variants [20]. Based on the findings presented here, we suggest that targeting the ZBTB16 interaction site in the C-terminus of SLC26A4 could provide a specific approach to prevent SLC26A4 degradation and may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for DFNB4 and Pendred syndrome.

Although several lines of evidence presented here point to ZBTB16 as a novel SLC26A4-interacting partner involved in CUL3-mediated SLC26A4 ubiquitination and degradation, our experimental approach has limitations. The evidence for the involvement of ZBTB16 in SLC26A4 ubiquitination and degradation mostly relies on ectopic expression of recombinant proteins; the lack of suitable inner ear cell models that express detectable levels of Zbtb16 and Slc26a4 precluded knock-in strategies. Mouse models may help validate the biological plausibility of our findings. However, because a single protein can be degraded via multiple pathways [97–99], compensatory mechanisms may occur in the global Zbtb16 knockout. A mouse model enabling tissue-specific, acute genetic ablation of Zbtb16 would be the ideal system for studying its impact on Slc26a4 turnover in vivo.

ConclusionsThe findings presented here identify ZBTB16 as a novel SLC26A4-interacting partner that directs immature/misfolded SLC26A4 variants for ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation. Targeting the ZBTB16 binding site might prevent UPS-mediated degradation of pathogenic SLC26A4 variants.

Conclusions

The findings presented here identify ZBTB16 as a novel SLC26A4-interacting partner that directs immature/misfolded SLC26A4 variants for ubiquitin-mediated proteasomal degradation. Targeting the ZBTB16 binding site might prevent UPS-mediated degradation of pathogenic SLC26A4 variants.
