BACKGROUND: The role of N1-methyladenosine (m1A) in cancer is poorly understood.
Here we explored the function of RNA methyltransferase TRNA methyltransferase
61A (TRMT61A) in colorectal cancer (CRC) and its potential as a therapeutic
target.
METHODS: RNA m1A levels were assessed through liquid chromatography-mass
spectrometry. The expression and clinical significance of TRMT61A were
investigated across five human CRC cohorts. The function of TRMT61A was
elucidated using CRC cell lines, patient-derived organoids, xenografts, and
transgenic mouse models. Integrated analyses of m1A-sequencing and
RNA-sequencing revealed the underlying mechanisms of TRMT61A. A
nanoparticle-based small interfering RNA (siRNA) delivery system and a specific
inhibitor were developed to target TRMT61A. The efficacy and safety of targeting
TRMT61A were assessed.
RESULTS: Our research revealed a consistent increase in TRMT61A expression and
total RNA m1A levels within primary CRCs. High TRMT61A expression was associated
with poor prognosis of CRC patients. Through CRISPR/Cas9 screenings, we
identified TRMT61A as the most essential gene among m1A regulators. Furthermore,
we established that TRMT61A promoted CRC tumorigenesis and progression by
enhancing the mRNA stability of critical targets in an m1A-dependent manner. In
particular, TRMT61A boosted the mRNA stability of one cut homeobox 2 (ONECUT2),
which in turn triggered son of sevenless homolog 1 (SOS1) transcription, leading
to the induction of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular
signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling in CRC. Notably, our study underscored
the safety and substantial anti-CRC effects achievable by inhibiting TRMT61A
using nanoparticle-encapsulated siTRMT61A or our newly discovered small molecule
compound, pentagalloylglucose.
CONCLUSIONS: Our study unveiled the tumor-promoting role of TRMT61A in CRC via
the m1A-ONECUT2-SOS1-MAPK/ERK pathway. Targeting TRMT61A showed promise as a
therapeutic strategy for treating CRC.

However, our current understanding of the precise location, regulation, and function of m1A in the human transcriptome remains limited, especially concerning its significance in CRC development.

In this study, we explored the change of RNA m1A levels in primary CRC specimens and non‐tumor normal tissues. To identify m1A regulatory enzymes that play a crucial role in CRC pathogenesis, we performed a CRISPR/Cas9 dropout screen of in‐house and publicly available genome‐wide CRISPR screen data for over 50 CRC cell lines. This study focused on characterizing the functional role and molecular mechanisms of TRMT61A, an m1A methyltransferase, in colorectal carcinogenesis using integrative m1A‐sequencing (m1A‐seq), RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐seq), and ribosome‐profiling (Ribo‐seq) analyses. Further, we explored the therapeutic potential of targeting TRMT61A through genetic and pharmacological strategies in preclinical models.

2MATERIALS AND METHODS2.1Human CRC samplesFive cohorts of CRC patients were included. The Beijing cohort from the Beijing University Cancer Hospital (Beijing, China) comprised 150 patients with surgically excised CRC tissues and surrounding adjacent normal tissues (colon or rectum). The adjacent normal tissues refer to the histologically normal tissues located adjacent to the tumors, typically at a distance greater than 5 cm. Specimens were immediately snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at ‐80°C until further processing (Supplementary Table S1). The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)‐CRC cohort (https://xenabrowser.net/heatmap/) consisted of TRMT61A mRNA expression data from 623 CRCs and 51 adjacent normal tissues, comprising 50 matched pairs. The Hong Kong cohort from the Chinese University of Hong Kong (Hong Kong, China) contained 188 patients, and overall survival (OS) data were available for 179 CRC patients. TRMT61A mRNA expression of 65 CRC tumors and the patient information were acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE29623, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc = GSE29623). Moreover, the mRNA expression data of TRMT61A, one cut homeobox 2 (ONECUT2), and son of sevenless homolog 1 (SOS1) in 156 CRC tumors were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE103479, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc = GSE103479). This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Chinese University of Hong Kong (CREC Ref No.: 2022.109).2.2Liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (LC‐MS) for RNA m1A quantificationRNA was extracted from primary CRCs and adjacent normal tissues of the Beijing cohort by TRIzol reagent (#9108, Takara, Shiga, Japan). Over 100 ng RNA were dissolved in 22 µL double‐distilled water (ddH2O), heated at 85°C for 3 min, and cooled on ice. To digest RNA, 2.5 µL nucleoside digestion mix buffer and 0.5 µL nucleoside digestion mix (#M0649S, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) were added to the RNA, and the mixture was incubated at 37°C for 18 h. The digestion mixture was quenched by heating at 80°C for 3 min, followed by the addition of 75 µL ddH2O. The samples were then injected into an LC‐MS (Dionex Ultimate 3000 UPLC, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). For tRNA m1A quantification, tRNA was purified using Magnetic Beads (tRNA Purification) (#40054S, Biodynami, Huntsville, AL, USA). Xcalibur (version 4.3.73.11, Thermo Fisher) was used to integrate chromatographic peaks and calculate peak areas of m1A. The RNA m1A level was determined by calculating the ratio of the m1A signal level to the adenosine signal level.2.3Tissue microarrays (TMA) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) stainingThe Hong Kong cohort comprised archival formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) CRC specimens from 188 patients collected between 2003 and 2005, including 28 with matched adjacent normal tissues. The median follow‐up time was 65.0 months (range: 0.2‐110 months). Clinicopathological data were obtained from hospital records, with incomplete TNM staging in two cases (one missing both T and M classifications, another missing M classification). OS data were available for 179 patients. Detailed patient clinicopathological characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table S2.For IHC analysis, TMAs were stained with TRMT61A polyclonal antibody (1:200 dilution, #PA5‐76553, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) using established protocols [23]. Two gastrointestinal pathologists blinded to patients’ clinical data independently evaluated all specimens to verify histological diagnosis and assess tumor cellularity. The IHC scoring criteria were as follows: score 0 (negative or weak staining in <10% of tumor cells), score 1 (weak staining in ≥10% of tumor cells), score 2 (moderate staining in ≥10% of tumor cells), and score 3 (strong staining in ≥10% of tumor cells) [24]. In the case of tissue array analysis, high TRMT61A protein expression corresponds to those with IHC scores of 2 and 3, while low TRMT61A expression pertains to scores of 0 and 1.2.4Human CRC cell linesThe normal colon epithelial cell line 2CT and patient‐derived colon cancer stem cell (CSC)‐enriched spheroid line POP92 were kindly provided by Dr. Catherine A. The complete list of single guide RNA (sgRNA) sequences and targeted genes is provided in Supplementary Table S3. In brief, HCT116 and POP92 cells were engineered to stably express Cas9. Fifty million Cas9‐stable cells were used for the library transduction with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of about 0.25. At 24 h after transduction, puromycin (#A1113803, Thermo Fisher) was added to the medium at 4 µg/mL. At 72 h after transduction, half of the cells were harvested for DNA extraction (day 0). Subsequently, the cells were maintained in complete medium with puromycin and collected after 8 days (HCT116) or 16 days (POP92) for DNA extraction to ensure a coverage of >500 cells per sgRNA. Purified DNA samples were sent for next‐generation sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted for replicate samples, and the sgRNA inserts were amplified by PCR for library preparation. Genomic DNA served as input, and the libraries were sequenced on Illumina HiSeq2500 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Initially, the sequencing data were aligned to the sgRNA library using bowtie (version 1.2.2) [27]. 3RESULTS3.1RNA m1A dysregulation was identified in CRCLC‐MS analysis demonstrated significantly elevated m1A levels in total RNA from 24 primary CRC specimens compared to their paired adjacent normal tissues (randomly selected from the Beijing cohort) (Figure 1A), suggesting a potential alteration in RNA m1A profile during CRC development. Using an expanded validation cohort (114 primary CRC tumors and 24 paired adjacent normal tissues with adequate RNA quantity in the Beijing cohort), we confirmed the consistent m1A elevation in total RNA, but not in tRNA fractions, from CRC samples (Supplementary Figure S1A). Using our previously established CRISPR/Cas9 dropout screening in POP92 and HCT116 cells [26], we next identified m1A regulators important for CRC. To address the role of YTHDC1 in m1A regulation, we performed m1A dot blotting analysis and found that knockdown of YTHDC1 did not alter global m1A levels (Supplementary Figure S1B‐C). Thus, we focused our investigation on TRMT61A as the key mediator of m1A modification in CRC. TRMT61A is recognized as the catalytic subunit of the m1A methyltransferase complex TRMT61A/TRMT6 [12]. Mutations within this domain (TRMT61A‐bMUT) diminished the interaction between TRMT61A and TRMT6 (Supplementary Figure S1E). Furthermore, analysis of genome‐scale CRISPR‐Cas9 screening data from 59 CRC cell lines verified that TRMT61A and TRMT6 were essential and functionally correlated in CRC (Supplementary Figure S1F). To confirm the role of TRMT61A as an m1A writer in CRC, we assessed the total RNA m1A levels in different CRC cells with TRMT61A knockout or overexpression. The results revealed that overexpression of TRMT61A augmented the m1A abundance, whereas the depletion of TRMT61A reduced the global m1A level (Figure 1C). Consistently, we observed a positive correlation between TRMT61A expression and global RNA m1A level in 24 primary CRCs (Figure 1D), suggesting that TRMT61A is responsible for m1A formation in CRC. For these 24 paired samples, the mRNA expression of TRMT61A and TRMT6 exhibited a significant increase in primary CRCs compared to adjacent normal tissues (Supplementary Figure S1G). It is noteworthy that TRMT61A and TRMT6 proteins can stabilize each other, as depletion of TRMT61A markedly decreased TRMT6 protein expression, and vice versa (Supplementary Figure S2A). Similarly, the knockdown of TRMT61A or TRMT6 significantly impeded CRC cell proliferation and colony formation (Supplementary Figure S2B‐C). Intriguingly, in TRMT61A‐depleted CRC cells, overexpression of TRMT6 failed to rescue the observed phenotypic defects (Supplementary Figure S2D‐E). This suggests that TRMT61A is the dominant functional driver in CRC progression. To identify signaling pathways altered by TRMT61A, we conducted GSEA on RNA‐seq and Ribo‐seq data, revealing multiple cancer‐related pathways (Supplementary Figure S13A‐B). Notably, MAPK/ERK signaling emerged as one of the consistently enriched pathways (Figure 5A). GSEA of RNA‐seq and Ribo‐seq data revealed that TRMT61A depletion was significantly associated with suppression of MAPK/ERK signaling activity (Figure 5B). In support of this, WB analysis revealed that knockout of TRMT61A reduced the phosphorylation of c‐raf, MEK1/2, ERK1/2, ribosomal S6 kinase (P90RSK), and mitogen‐ and stress‐activated kinase 1 (MSK1) in HCT116 and RKO (Figure 5C, Supplementary Figure S13C). Consistently, the depletion of TRMT61A effectively suppressed the proliferation of LOVO, LS180, HT29, and SW480 cells (Supplementary Figure S6B) and inhibited the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway (Supplementary Figure S13D). Conversely, overexpression of TRMT61A activated MAPK/ERK signaling (Supplementary Figure S13E). We went further to explore the potential role of ONECUT2 in mediating the activation of the MAPK/ERK pathway by TRMT61A. Our results demonstrated that the promoting effect of TRMT61A on MAPK/ERK signaling was abolished in ONECUT2‐deficient CRC cells (Figure 5D), implying that TRMT61A promotes MAPK/ERK signaling via ONECUT2. To determine whether TRMT61A's oncogenic role requires MAPK/ERK signaling activation, we treated CRC cells with or without TRMT61A overexpression with two distinct MAPK/ERK pathway inhibitors, AS‐IV [45] and FR180204 [46, 47]. Both inhibitors effectively reduced p‐ERK1/2 and p90RSK levels (Supplementary Figure S13F). Treatment with either AS‐IV or FR180204 abolished the promoting effect of TRMT61A on CRC cell proliferation (Figure 5E). In contrast, in TRMT61A‐depleted CRC cells, ERK activator TBHQ [48] restored ERK phosphorylation and rescued proliferation defects (Supplementary Figure S13G‐H), confirming MAPK/ERK signaling as a key mediator of TRMT61A's oncogenic role.

3.7ONECUT2 drove SOS1 transcription to induce MAPK/ERK signaling in CRCONECUT2 belongs to the ONECUT family of transcription factors [49]. To investigate molecular mechanisms underlying the MAPK/ERK pathway regulated by ONECUT2, RNA‐seq was conducted in HCT116 cells both in the presence and absence of siONECUT2 (Supplementary Figure S14A). Given that ONECUT2 is a downstream target of TMRT61A, we performed an integrative analysis by combining multiple datasets, comprising RNA‐seq data from HCT116 cells with or without sgTRMT61A, RNA‐seq data from HCT116 cells with or without siONECUT2, and genes involved in the MAPK/ERK pathway (Supplementary Table S8). Through this integrated approach, we identified 5 potential targets of ONECTUT2 that regulate MAPK/ERK (Figure 5F). Among them, putative ONECUT2‐binding motifs were identified in SOS1, NGFR, and ITGB1 promoters (Supplementary Figure S14B) using JASPAR [36]. qPCR analysis revealed that depletion of TRMT61A or ONECUT2 caused significant inhibition of ITGB1 and SOS1 mRNA expression in both HCT116 and RKO cells (Figure 5G), while NGFR mRNA was almost undetectable. WB confirmed that ONECUT2 knockdown inhibited both SOS1 and ITGB1 protein expressions (Figure 5H). Functional rescue experiments revealed that SOS1 knockdown, but not ITGB1 depletion, significantly attenuated TRMT61A‐mediated proliferation (Supplementary Figure S14C‐D), suggesting that SOS1 plays a critical role in this oncogenic pathway. Thus, SOS1 may mediate the activation of the MAPK/ERK pathway by TRMT61A‐ONECUT2. In support of this, overexpression of TRMT61A‐WT, but not TRMT61AD181A
, promoted SOS1 protein expression (Supplementary Figure S14E). To assess the direct binding of ONECUT2 at the SOS1 promoter, we employed ChIP assays. The results demonstrated that the IP of the promoter region of SOS1 by anti‐ONECUT2 was successfully achieved using three different primer sets specific to the SOS1 promoter region (Figure 5I). We next investigated the functional relevance of ONECUT2 binding within the SOS1 promoter region by inserting the wild‐type SOS1 promoter region or a mutant sequence lacking all three ONECUT2‐binding sites upstream of a luciferase gene (Figure 5J). The results revealed a significant reduction in luciferase activity of the reporter containing the wild‐type SOS1 promoter region upon knockdown of ONECUT2, whereas the mutant SOS1 promoter construct exhibited no significant change (Figure 5J). In addition, we found that primary CRCs expressed significantly higher SOS1 than adjacent normal tissues in the Beijing cohort (Supplementary Figure S15A). A positive correlation between TRMT61A and SOS1 expression was uncovered in primary CRCs from both the Beijing cohort and the GSE103479 dataset (Supplementary Figure S15B). We further validated these findings by utilizing colon tumors from ApcMin/+Trmt61acko
 mice and CRC xenografts treated with VNP‐siTRMT61A. Remarkably, both the silencing of Trmt61a and treatment with VNP‐siTRMT61A demonstrated the ability to inhibit ONECUT2 expression and SOS1‐MAPK/ERK signaling (Figure 5K, Supplementary Figure S15C). Collectively, these results strongly indicate that TRMT61A‐induced ONECUT2 binding to the SOS1 promoter, leading to the upregulation of SOS1 expression and subsequent activation of the MAPK/ERK pathway.

4DISCUSSIONHere, we present evidence demonstrating that the m1A writer TRMT61A expression was significantly increased in primary CRC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues, which was accompanied by an elevated level of RNA m1A modification. While total RNA m1A levels showed a statistically significant increase in CRC, the effect size, though reproducible, calls for cautious interpretation and further validation in future studies with larger sample size. Additionally, investigating m1A distribution across RNA fractions (tRNA, rRNA, mRNA) could refine our understanding of its role in CRC pathogenesis. High expression of TRMT61A enhanced the stability of ONECUT2 mRNA in an m1A‐dependent manner, resulting in increased expression of ONECUT2. The upregulated ONECUT2 protein then bounded to the SOS1 promoter, leading to the upregulation of SOS1 expression. This upregulation subsequently activated the MAPK/ERK pathway and promoted CRC tumorigenesis and progression. Inhibiting TRMT61A via genetic and pharmacological means significantly suppressed CRC growth in vitro and in vivo, suggesting its promise as a treatment for CRC (Figure 7J).Despite TRMT61A's role in driving CRC growth and metastasis, its expression lacked correlation with TNM stage. This discrepancy may arise from tumor heterogeneity, where TRMT61A's effects are subset‐specific, or from its influence on the tumor microenvironment (e.g., immune evasion) that TNM staging does not capture. Alternatively, its oncogenic function may depend on post‐transcriptional regulation (e.g., m1A methylation) rather than mRNA levels. Furthermore, high TRMT61A expression was associated with shortened OS only in patients with advanced‐stage CRC but not those with early‐stage disease. Several potential reasons may explain this observation: (1) Early‐stage CRC patients generally have longer OS, with fewer events of death occurring during follow‐up. This can make it more challenging to detect prognostic effects in this patient subset with early‐stage CRC. (2) High TRMT61A expression may confer resistance to chemotherapy, which is commonly administered to advanced‐stage patients. However, further studies are warranted to investigate whether TRMT61A expression influences patients’ responses to therapy.Extensive investigations employing in vitro CRC cell line models, in vivo CRC xenograft models, and Trmt61acko
 models consistently demonstrated the potent role of TMRT61A deletion in suppressing CRC cells. This suppression was primarily achieved through the profound inhibition of cell cycle progression and the induction of apoptosis. Furthermore, silencing of TMRT61A repressed CRC cell migration and invasion in vitro and in vivo. D181 of TMRT61A is responsible for S‐adenosyl‐l‐methionine (SAM) catalysis [37]. As such, D181A abrogated the m1A‐catalyzing and tumor‐promoting ability of TMRT61A, suggesting that TMRT61A promotes CRC through its m1A methyltransferase activity. Our findings are supported by other studies that have also reported oncogenic functions of TMRT61A in hepatocellular carcinoma [21] and bladder cancer [22]. Thus, TRMT61A plays an oncogenic role in gastrointestinal cancer.m1A has been reported in the mRNAs of human cells, which regulate mRNA stability and translation [12, 44]. In our study, we observed a significant increase in m1A levels in total RNA extracted from primary CRCs compared to adjacent normal tissues, a change not observed in tRNA. Importantly, the alteration in m1A levels in primary CRCs showed a positive correlation with TRMT61A expression. Furthermore, we have pinpointed a crucial regulatory axis of TRMT61A‐m1A‐ONECUT2 that fosters CRC progression. These findings imply that the dysregulated m1A modification induced by TRMT61A within mRNA plays a significant role in CRC development. Our study identifies ONECUT2, a member of the ONECUT family of transcription factors, as a critical downstream target of TRMT61A. We found that TRMT61A directly binds to ONECUT2 and catalyzes m1A modification, leading to stabilized ONECUT2 mRNA. Consistently, we identified a positive correlation between TRMT61A and ONECUT2 mRNA expression in CRC. Furthermore, ONECUT2 was upregulated in CRC and mediated the oncogenic role of TRMT61A. In line with our findings, ONECUT2 expression is upregulated in a broad range of cancers, including prostate cancer [50, 51], hepatocellular carcinoma [52], and lung cancer [53].Our investigation has uncovered a link between RNA m1A modification and the MAPK/ERK pathway in CRC, which plays a crucial role in tumor growth and metastasis [54]. SOS1 was identified as the critical mediator between TRMT61A and MAPK/ERK signaling. SOS proteins function as guanosine nucleotide exchange factors that catalyze the exchange of GDP for GTP on RAS, accelerating its activation and subsequently stimulating the MAPK/ERK pathway [55]. Additionally, SOS proteins can form a positive feedback loop with RAS, contributing to sustained MAPK/ERK signaling activation [56, 57]. Our study observed that TRMT61A‐induced SOS1 expression activated the MAPK/ERK pathway in CRC. Specifically, TRMT61A upregulated the expression of ONECUT2, which induced the transcription of SOS1.To explore the therapeutic potential of targeting TRMT61A in CRC, we generated a nanoparticle‐based delivery system to deliver TRMT61A‐siRNA in vivo. The administration of VNP‐siTRMT61A exerted a significant anti‐CRC effect. Furthermore, we have characterized PGG, a potent inhibitor of the m1A writer TRMT61A. PGG is a naturally occurring compound found in various plants and herbs [58, 59, 60, 61]. This polyphenolic molecule has demonstrated therapeutic potential against cancer as it could inhibit tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis. Moreover, PGG can enhance the efficacy of conventional cancer treatments, such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy [62, 63, 64, 65]. Several studies have examined the safety profile of PGG. One study found that oral administration of up to 200 mg/kg/day was safe in mice [66]. Additionally, PGG has exhibited low cytotoxicity in human cell lines based on in vitro studies [67, 68, 69]. These observations suggest that PGG may have a favorable safety profile. However, further research is still needed to fully establish the compound's pharmacokinetics and comprehensive safety profile in human subjects. In our study, we identified that PGG could directly bind to TRMT61A protein at the residues Q85, G111, S114, F136, D163, V164, and D181. As such, PGG treatment probably reduced RNA m1A levels in CRC cells by blocking the binding of methyl donor SAM [37]. Here, we present evidence demonstrating that the m1A writer TRMT61A expression was significantly increased in primary CRC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues, which was accompanied by an elevated level of RNA m1A modification. While total RNA m1A levels showed a statistically significant increase in CRC, the effect size, though reproducible, calls for cautious interpretation and further validation in future studies with larger sample size. Additionally, investigating m1A distribution across RNA fractions (tRNA, rRNA, mRNA) could refine our understanding of its role in CRC pathogenesis. High expression of TRMT61A enhanced the stability of ONECUT2 mRNA in an m1A‐dependent manner, resulting in increased expression of ONECUT2. The upregulated ONECUT2 protein then bounded to the SOS1 promoter, leading to the upregulation of SOS1 expression. This upregulation subsequently activated the MAPK/ERK pathway and promoted CRC tumorigenesis and progression. Inhibiting TRMT61A via genetic and pharmacological means significantly suppressed CRC growth in vitro and in vivo, suggesting its promise as a treatment for CRC (Figure 7J).

Despite TRMT61A's role in driving CRC growth and metastasis, its expression lacked correlation with TNM stage. This discrepancy may arise from tumor heterogeneity, where TRMT61A's effects are subset‐specific, or from its influence on the tumor microenvironment (e.g., immune evasion) that TNM staging does not capture. Alternatively, its oncogenic function may depend on post‐transcriptional regulation (e.g., m1A methylation) rather than mRNA levels. Furthermore, high TRMT61A expression was associated with shortened OS only in patients with advanced‐stage CRC but not those with early‐stage disease. Several potential reasons may explain this observation: (1) Early‐stage CRC patients generally have longer OS, with fewer events of death occurring during follow‐up. This can make it more challenging to detect prognostic effects in this patient subset with early‐stage CRC. (2) High TRMT61A expression may confer resistance to chemotherapy, which is commonly administered to advanced‐stage patients. However, further studies are warranted to investigate whether TRMT61A expression influences patients’ responses to therapy.

 models consistently demonstrated the potent role of TMRT61A deletion in suppressing CRC cells. This suppression was primarily achieved through the profound inhibition of cell cycle progression and the induction of apoptosis. Furthermore, silencing of TMRT61A repressed CRC cell migration and invasion in vitro and in vivo. D181 of TMRT61A is responsible for S‐adenosyl‐l‐methionine (SAM) catalysis [37]. As such, D181A abrogated the m1A‐catalyzing and tumor‐promoting ability of TMRT61A, suggesting that TMRT61A promotes CRC through its m1A methyltransferase activity. Our findings are supported by other studies that have also reported oncogenic functions of TMRT61A in hepatocellular carcinoma [21] and bladder cancer [22]. Thus, TRMT61A plays an oncogenic role in gastrointestinal cancer.

m1A has been reported in the mRNAs of human cells, which regulate mRNA stability and translation [12, 44]. In our study, we observed a significant increase in m1A levels in total RNA extracted from primary CRCs compared to adjacent normal tissues, a change not observed in tRNA. Importantly, the alteration in m1A levels in primary CRCs showed a positive correlation with TRMT61A expression. Furthermore, we have pinpointed a crucial regulatory axis of TRMT61A‐m1A‐ONECUT2 that fosters CRC progression. These findings imply that the dysregulated m1A modification induced by TRMT61A within mRNA plays a significant role in CRC development. Our study identifies ONECUT2, a member of the ONECUT family of transcription factors, as a critical downstream target of TRMT61A. We found that TRMT61A directly binds to ONECUT2 and catalyzes m1A modification, leading to stabilized ONECUT2 mRNA. Consistently, we identified a positive correlation between TRMT61A and ONECUT2 mRNA expression in CRC. Furthermore, ONECUT2 was upregulated in CRC and mediated the oncogenic role of TRMT61A. In line with our findings, ONECUT2 expression is upregulated in a broad range of cancers, including prostate cancer [50, 51], hepatocellular carcinoma [52], and lung cancer [53].

Our investigation has uncovered a link between RNA m1A modification and the MAPK/ERK pathway in CRC, which plays a crucial role in tumor growth and metastasis [54]. SOS1 was identified as the critical mediator between TRMT61A and MAPK/ERK signaling. SOS proteins function as guanosine nucleotide exchange factors that catalyze the exchange of GDP for GTP on RAS, accelerating its activation and subsequently stimulating the MAPK/ERK pathway [55]. Additionally, SOS proteins can form a positive feedback loop with RAS, contributing to sustained MAPK/ERK signaling activation [56, 57]. Our study observed that TRMT61A‐induced SOS1 expression activated the MAPK/ERK pathway in CRC. Specifically, TRMT61A upregulated the expression of ONECUT2, which induced the transcription of SOS1.

To explore the therapeutic potential of targeting TRMT61A in CRC, we generated a nanoparticle‐based delivery system to deliver TRMT61A‐siRNA in vivo. The administration of VNP‐siTRMT61A exerted a significant anti‐CRC effect. Furthermore, we have characterized PGG, a potent inhibitor of the m1A writer TRMT61A. PGG is a naturally occurring compound found in various plants and herbs [58, 59, 60, 61]. This polyphenolic molecule has demonstrated therapeutic potential against cancer as it could inhibit tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis. Moreover, PGG can enhance the efficacy of conventional cancer treatments, such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy [62, 63, 64, 65]. Several studies have examined the safety profile of PGG. One study found that oral administration of up to 200 mg/kg/day was safe in mice [66]. Additionally, PGG has exhibited low cytotoxicity in human cell lines based on in vitro studies [67, 68, 69]. These observations suggest that PGG may have a favorable safety profile. However, further research is still needed to fully establish the compound's pharmacokinetics and comprehensive safety profile in human subjects. In our study, we identified that PGG could directly bind to TRMT61A protein at the residues Q85, G111, S114, F136, D163, V164, and D181. As such, PGG treatment probably reduced RNA m1A levels in CRC cells by blocking the binding of methyl donor SAM [37]. Importantly, PGG treatment suppressed ONECUT2 expression and significantly reduced CRC growth both in vitro and in vivo. Collectively, our findings suggest that targeting TRMT61A represents a promising therapeutic strategy for CRC. Moreover, these results provide a strong rationale for exploring combination therapies involving TRMT61A inhibition alongside standard anti‐CRC treatments. Notably, this study provides in vivo evidence demonstrating the therapeutic efficacy of targeting an m1A RNA methyltransferase in CRC.

While our study demonstrated elevated total RNA m1A levels in primary CRCs, several limitations should be noted. First, the moderate effect size observed necessitates validation in larger, independent cohorts to establish clinical relevance. Second, our bulk analysis of total RNA m1A could be refined by characterizing m1A distribution across specific RNA species (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA) to better understand its functional implications in CRC pathogenesis. Third, the potential role of TRMT61A in modulating the tumor microenvironment remains unexplored, representing an important area for future investigation. Finally, prospective studies are needed to determine whether TRMT61A‐mediated m1A modification has predictive value for therapeutic response in CRC patients.

5CONCLUSIONSTaking together, our findings unveil the critical role of TRMT61A in CRC through the m1A‐ONECUT2‐SOS1‐MAPK/ERK axis, providing mechanistic insights into its tumorigenic function. Furthermore, our study highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting TRMT61A‐mediated m1A modifications as a promising strategy for CRC treatment.

CONCLUSIONS

Taking together, our findings unveil the critical role of TRMT61A in CRC through the m1A‐ONECUT2‐SOS1‐MAPK/ERK axis, providing mechanistic insights into its tumorigenic function. Furthermore, our study highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting TRMT61A‐mediated m1A modifications as a promising strategy for CRC treatment.
