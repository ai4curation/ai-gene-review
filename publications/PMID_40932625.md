Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have linked the locus encoding ankyrin
repeat domain 55 (ANKRD55) with numerous autoimmune diseases; however, its
biological function and role in inflammation are unclear. Here, we demonstrate
that Ankrd55-deficient mice are protected from T cell-mediated colitis but are
more susceptible to Citrobacter rodentium infection. Mechanistically, Ankrd55
deletion impairs CD4+ T cell proliferation and reduces effector cytokine
production in T helper 17 (TH17) cells in a cell-intrinsic manner. ANKRD55 is
associated with mitochondria, and its loss is associated with impaired
mitochondrial respiration and activation of the LKB1 pathway. Consistently,
IL-17 production can be rescued by the deletion of LKB1 in Ankrd55-deficient T
cells. Altogether, our study implicates the protein ANKRD55 as a functional
modulator of T cell metabolism that directly impacts TH17 responses,
highlighting it as a potential target across multiple autoimmune diseases.

For example, humans with STAT3 mutations suffer from hyper-immunoglobulin E syndrome and are susceptible to recurrent pulmonary infections due to the absence of IL-17-producing T cells (Milner et al., 2008; Minegishi et al., 2007).

ANKRD55 is a protein of unknown function that is highly expressed in CD4+ T cells (Lopez de Lapuente et al., 2016). It contains nine N-terminal ankyrin repeats, which are one of the most common protein–protein interaction motifs in nature (Li et al., 2006; Mosavi et al., 2002). GWAS indicate that common intronic variants in ANKRD55 have been conclusively linked to risk of autoimmune diseases across several conditions, including Crohn’s disease (Jostins et al., 2012; Li et al., 2015), rheumatoid arthritis (Eyre et al., 2012; Okada et al., 2014; Stahl et al., 2010), multiple sclerosis (Alloza et al., 2012; Lopez de Lapuente et al., 2016), and celiac disease (Zhernakova et al., 2011). Specifically, the maximum association in each case is found at the same common single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) (rs7731626), at which the minor allele (A) is associated with protection from all these diseases, and the major allele (G) confers risk (Tehranchi et al., 2019). Fine mapping studies conclusively showed this variant is causal in rheumatoid arthritis (posterior inclusion probability > 0.99) (Ishigaki et al., 2022). The same SNP demonstrates a strong cis-expression quantitative trait locus (cis-eQTL), in which the disease-protective allele reduces the expression of ANKRD55 in CD4+ T cells. However, it is also important to note that ANKRD55 is located in a syntenic block, conserved between humans and mice, directly adjacent to IL6ST, which encodes the IL-6 cytokine family signal transducer, and the same SNP is also an IL6ST cis-eQTL. This association and studies of chromatin structure in the locus (Nasser et al., 2021) have suggested the ANKRD55 intronic variant modulates disease through IL6ST. Therefore, despite these strong associations to autoimmune pathogenesis, the biological function of the ANKRD55 protein and its direct impact on disease remains unclear.

To address this, we generated Ankrd55-deficient mice and directly investigated the role of this gene in autoimmune disease susceptibility. Using a model of T cell-mediated colitis, we demonstrated that ANKRD55 is crucial for T cell expansion and that its deletion ameliorates colitis. Conversely, Ankrd55 deficiency results in impaired bacterial clearance during Citrobacter rodentium infection, owing to defects in TH17 cell development. We further confirmed that these effects are T cell-intrinsic and can be replicated in an in vitro TH17 differentiation model. Mechanistically, ANKRD55 regulates cell cycle transition to promote T cell proliferation and modulates mitochondrial function to regulate TH17 cell differentiation, as deficient cells exhibit lower mitochondrial transmembrane potential and respiratory capacity. Consistent with this metabolic phenotype, we showed that ANKRD55 is associated with mitochondria in T cells by both immunofluorescence and fractionation, and that the impact of Ankrd55-deficiency on IL-17 production can be rescued by the deletion of the metabolic sensor LKB1. Altogether, our work establishes ANKRD55 as a direct contributor to autoimmune susceptibility through its impact on TH17 differentiation and proliferation and highlights mechanistic targets to modulate this pathway.

ResultsLoss of Ankrd55 ameliorates T cell-mediated colitisAs noted above, while the Ankrd55 locus is strongly associated with multiple autoimmune diseases, the relative contribution of the Ankrd55 gene itself, compared to that of the neighboring Il6st gene, remains controversial. To directly investigate the function of ANKRD55, we generated Ankrd55−/− mice by homologous recombination in embryonic stem cells, using a LacZ-floxed neomycin construct to delete the end of the first exon and second exon of Ankrd55 (Fig. S1A). We confirmed the correct deletion and removal of the floxed neomycin cassette by genotyping the resulting animals (Fig. S1B). Ankrd55−/− animals were born at the expected Mendelian ratios (Fig. S1C) and showed no phenotypic abnormalities in our specific pathogen-free colony. We then immunophenotyped Ankrd55−/− mice and wild-type (WT) littermates and observed normal T cell development in the thymus, normal numbers of T cells, and normal IL-17A-producing TH17 and IFNγ-producing TH1 differentiation as well as Foxp3+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the spleens and mesenteric lymph nodes (mLNs) of Ankrd55−/− animals (Fig. S2, A–C). Moreover, there was no obvious difference in T cell activation status, as measured by CD44 and CD62L expression, in these compartments in naïve animals (Fig. S2D).Given the associations of ANKRD55 with multiple autoimmune diseases and its enrichment in CD4+ T cells in our study of human Crohn’s disease (Kong et al., 2023), we hypothesized that Ankrd55 may play a key role in T cell-driven autoimmunity. Therefore, we decided to investigate whether Ankrd55−/− cells are capable of inducing disease in a model of T cell transfer colitis (Eri et al., 2012; Ostanin et al., 2009). CD4+CD45RBhi T cells were isolated from either WT or Ankrd55−/− mice, and equal numbers were separately injected into Rag1−/− mice (Fig. 1A). As expected in this setting, mice that received WT T cells developed colitis and lost weight, while surprisingly mice that received Ankrd55−/− T cells demonstrated little to no body weight loss over an 8-week period (Fig. 1B). We also assessed intestinal inflammation by histology and confirmed tissue pathology after WT T cell transfer but observed only limited signs of inflammation and tissue damage in mice that received Ankrd55−/− T cells (Fig. 1C). Eight weeks after transfer, animals were sacrificed and the numbers and functional profiles of CD4+ T cells were examined. We recovered significantly lower numbers of CD4+ T cells in the blood, mLNs, and colonic lamina propria lymphocytes (cLPLs) of Rag1−/− mice that received Ankrd55−/− T cells compared to WT T cell recipients (Fig. 1, D and E). We next assessed tissue inflammation by performing qRT-PCR on colonic samples from both groups. This revealed that the expression of proinflammatory cytokine genes (particularly Ifng, Il17a, Il17f, and Tnf) was lower in animals that received Ankrd55−/− T cells than in those that received WT T cells (Fig. 1F). Accordingly, the expression of type 2 and anti-inflammatory cytokine genes such as Il4 and Il5 was higher in the Ankrd55−/− group. Next, to interrogate the functional profile of gut-associated T cells, we performed an ELISA analysis of the supernatant of cLPLs and mLNs reactivated with plate-bound anti-CD3/anti-CD28 ex vivo. This assay showed that the production of IFNγ, TNF, IL-17A, and IL-22 were all reduced in Ankrd55−/− T cells compared to their WT counterparts (Fig. 1G). We also validated these results by intracellular cytokine staining and observed a significant decrease in IL-17A+ and IL-17A+IFNγ+ T cells in Ankrd55−/− T cells recovered from Rag1−/− animals (Fig. 1H).Altogether, these data suggested that Ankrd55 is required for the development of colitis in a T cell transfer model of the disease. Importantly, in addition to decreased inflammation overall, Ankrd55-deficient cells showed a specific defect in IL-17A+ and IL-17A+IFNγ+ CD4+ cells, suggesting that ANKRD55 is directly involved in the regulation of this pathway. The direction of the effect is consistent with the results from human genetics linking lower expression of ANKRD55 to protection from inflammatory disease.Ankrd55 can modulate T cell function in a cell-intrinsic mannerTo confirm this direct association and separate the role of T cell-intrinsic Ankrd55 expression from broader inflammation-associated changes in T cell homeostasis (Li et al., 2018), we performed a co-transfer of equal numbers of congenically-marked CD4+CD45RBhi WT and Ankrd55−/− T cells to compare their ability to expand and differentiate toward a TH17 phenotype in the same Rag1−/− animal (Fig. 2A). Five weeks after transfer, cells were recovered from the mLN, colon, and spleen. WT cells were 2-fold more abundant among CD4+ T cells than Ankrd55−/− cells in mLNs, cLPLs, and spleens (Fig. 2, B and C), suggesting that the reduced proliferative capacity of Ankrd55-deficient T cells results at least in part from cell-intrinsic effects of Ankrd55 and not from differences in the inflammatory environments present in animals that received WT or Ankrd55−/− T cells.Naïve T cells acquire an activated or memory-like phenotype during homeostatic proliferation in lymphopenic animals (Jameson, 2002). CD69 expression was comparable in WT and Ankrd55−/− T cells from mLNs, cLPLs, and spleens, indicating that the absence of ANKRD55 did not inhibit T cell activation per se (Fig. S3A). Importantly, when we assessed cytokine production, the expression of IL-17A (either considered alone or in combination with IFNγ) was significantly lower in Ankrd55−/− CD4+ T cells compared to their WT counterparts in mLNs, cLPLs and spleens (Fig. 2D). We also observed a modest decrease in the frequency of cells expressing only IFNγ in the cLPLs and spleens of the same animals (Fig. 2D). This result indicates a cell-intrinsic impairment in TH17 cell differentiation in Ankrd55−/− cells, which occurs alongside the observed expansion defect. In parallel, we also observed a difference in the frequency of Foxp3+ Tregs in the mLN and spleen, where a higher percentage of Ankrd55-deficient cells showed a Treg phenotype, and there was a trend towards an increase in Foxp3+ CD4+ T cells in Ankrd55−/− cLPLs (Fig. 2D). Altogether, these results confirm that Ankrd55−/− T cells have a cell-intrinsic proliferation and TH17 differentiation defect in this model of colitis.Next, we sought to assess whether the differences in cell numbers were due to differences in cell proliferation or in cell survival. First, we sorted CD45.1/CD45.2 CD4+ T cells and analyzed the expression of cell cycle genes by qRT-PCR. We found that Ccne2 was significantly downregulated in Ankrd55−/− CD4+ T cells and that multiple other cell cycle genes showed a similar trend (Fig. 2E). To functionally validate changes in proliferation, we next performed a BrdU incorporation assay. This assay revealed a significantly decreased proportion of proliferating cells (BrdU+) in Ankrd55−/− CD4+ T cells of the mLN and colon (Fig. 2, F and G). Of note, Ankrd55−/− T cells in the mLN also presented a significant sub G1 peak (1.81%), characteristic of late apoptosis, compared to WT T cells (0.58%), which may indicate increased apoptosis, but it is unclear how this small percentage contributes to the expansion defect we observed. To understand if this difference in proliferation was linked to the colitis model we used, we also stimulated naïve CD4+ T cells with anti-CD3/anti-CD28 beads in vitro. Both a CFSE dilution assay and a BrdU assay showed that Ankrd55 deficiency is associated with slower cell division and decreased proliferation in this setting as well (Fig. S3, B and C). Finally, we analyzed the transcriptomes of these in vitro activated cells and found that genes involved in the cell cycle (Cyclin E, Ywhag, Bub1, Bub1b, Orc1) contributing to proper chromosome separation and cell cycle progression were down-regulated in Ankrd55−/− relative to WT cells (Fig. S3D, Table S1). Tgfb3 and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1B (Cdkn1b), which induce cell cycle arrest and thus control the cell cycle progression at G1, however, were up-regulated. These data suggest that Ankrd55 deletion compromises CD4+ T cell proliferation and cell cycle transition after activation.This co-transfer experiment allowed us to compare WT and Ankrd55-deficient T cells within the same animal. Our results clearly indicate that Ankrd55 can modulate T cell function in a cell-intrinsic manner, and that this gene modulates both TH17 differentiation as well as the proliferation of activated T cells.Ankrd55 loss impairs bacterial clearanceTo assess whether Ankrd55 also plays an important role in host defense against pathogenic bacteria, we used C. rodentium, a murine pathogen that results in colonic lesions similar to the clinical enteropathogenic Escherichia coli strains associated with Crohn’s disease. After infection, Ankrd55−/− mice had significantly increased levels of C. rodentium in the feces, especially at later time points (Fig. 3A), indicating that Ankrd55 may be critical in the late phase of infection or for disease resolution. Moreover, Ankrd55−/− mice showed drastically higher (up to 3 orders of magnitude) levels of C. rodentium in the colon after 15 days of infection (Fig. 3B). Despite the increased bacterial burden at this time point, the percentages of IL-17A+ and IL-17A+IFNγ+ T cells were dramatically decreased in the colon lamina propria of Ankrd55−/− mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 3C). We also found IL-17A and IL-22 were reduced at the protein levels in the anti-CD3/anti-CD28-treated colon lamina propria as well as mLNs from Ankrd55−/− mice (Fig. 3, D and E). To quantify antigen-specific T cell responses, we detected IL-17A production by mLN immune cells after stimulation with peptide antigen derived from C. rodentium. We found significantly fewer IL-17A-producing cells in Ankrd55−/− mice compared with WT mice (Fig. 3F). All of these findings suggest that TH17-associated immune responses in the colon are impaired by the loss of Ankrd55, resulting in increased susceptibility to C. rodentium infection. Interestingly, we did not observe an increase in histological inflammation (Fig. 3G), potentially because of opposing effects of increased pathogen burden and lower TH17 differentiation. Of note, the clearance of C. rodentium depends on both CD4+ T and B cell responses (Maaser et al., 2004), and our results do not rule out direct or indirect effects of Ankrd55 deletion on B cell function.To more directly assess the impact of Ankrd55 on T cell differentiation in an acute inflammation context, we also injected WT and Ankrd55−/− mice with anti-CD3 intraperitoneally, a protocol used to induce robust T cell activation in intestinal tissues (Musch et al., 2002). We analyzed the colonic tissue, where we observed a decreased frequency of both Rorɣt-expressing T cells and, within this population, of IL-17A- and TNF-producing T cells in Ankrd55−/− animals (Fig. 3H). Therefore, our findings demonstrate that loss of Ankrd55 limits TH17 differentiation in the context of acute T cell activation, likely resulting in delayed pathogen clearance.Ankrd55 deletion reduces experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis severityFinally, to assess the role of Ankrd55 outside of intestinal immunity, and because human ANKRD55 variants have been associated with multiple sclerosis, we used an experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) paradigm to model this disease. Animals were immunized with a myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) peptide and monitored daily for EAE symptoms (Fig. 3I). Male Ankrd55−/− mice showed both a delayed disease onset and reduced symptom severity compared to WT controls (p=0.0033), while female mice displayed a trend towards reduced severity in the later phase of the disease (p=0.0752).Ankrd55 is required for TH17 differentiation in vitroTo further investigate the function of Ankrd55 in regulating TH17 cell development, we isolated naïve CD4+ T cells from WT and Ankrd55−/− mice and performed differentiation assays in vitro. We evaluated standard conditions for TH1, TH2, and Treg differentiation as well as multiple cytokine conditions that lead to the development of TH17 cells. In line with our in vivo findings, Ankrd55 loss led to a dramatic deficiency in TH17 differentiation under the conditions we examined (Fig. 4A). However, we did not observe any effect of Ankrd55 loss on iTreg and TH2 development, and only a modest defect in TH1 cell differentiation, as evaluated by IFNγ production (Fig. 4A, Fig. S3E–G). Moreover, qRT-PCR revealed that Ankrd55 loss led to significantly reduced expression of Il17a in all of the TH17 conditions we examined, decreased Il17f and Rorc in both TH17 conditions with IL-6 + TGF-β and IL-6 + IL1β + IL-23, and downregulated Il21 or Il22 in these two TH17 conditions respectively (Fig. 4B). In addition, mRNA levels of Foxp3 were increased in Ankrd55−/− TH17 cells differentiated with IL-6 + TGF-β. As expected from the lack of impact on cytokine production, Ankrd55 loss did not affect TH1, Treg, or TH2 signature genes (Fig. S3E–G). As we observed an increase in Treg genes only in some TH17 conditions, but not canonical Treg conditions, our results support the hypothesis that Ankrd55 lowers TH17 differentiation and indirectly impacts the TH17/Treg balance (Lee et al., 2009), rather than directly increases Treg differentiation. Interestingly, we also noted that Ankrd55 itself is upregulated in TH17 conditions, particularly in the combined presence of IL-6 + TGF-β (Fig. 4B), supporting the notion that Ankrd55 is part of the TH17 differentiation program. We also confirmed these observations in supernatants and, as expected, observed lower cytokine levels for IL-17A, IL-17F, and IL-21 in supernatants of TH17 cells differentiated with IL-6 + TGF-β, as well as lower levels of IL-22 in supernatants of TH17 cells differentiated with IL-21 + TGF-β or IL-6 + IL-1β + IL-23 (Fig. 4C).Ankrd55 modulates TH17 biology independently of Il6stAs Il6st is located next to Ankrd55 in both mice and humans, one possibility is that effects of the variant in humans, or, in our system, of the deletion performed within the murine Ankrd55 locus affect IL6ST expression and downstream differentiation. To directly test the impact of the ANKRD55 protein, we exogenously rescued Ankrd55 expression in Ankrd55−/− naïve CD4+ T cells and induced TH17 differentiation. Ankrd55−/− cells successfully transduced to express ANKRD55 (identified as Thy1.1+ based on the co-expression of this marker in our vector) restored IL-17A expression to WT levels, while untransduced Ankrd55−/− cells in the same culture (Thy1.1−) maintained lower IL-17A levels than WT cells (Fig. 4D). This result shows that the overexpression of Ankrd55 from an independent locus in a null background rescues TH17 cell differentiation, validating that the ANKRD55 protein has an intrinsically important role in TH17 polarization.Epigenetic modifications have been reported as key contributors to TH17 lineage commitment and plasticity (Jiang et al., 2018; Mukasa et al., 2010; Nimmo et al., 2012; Xu et al., 2017). To investigate whether Ankrd55 deletion modulates chromatin accessibility both within the locus and at distant loci that are critical for TH17 biology, we assayed transposase-accessible chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (ATAC–seq) in WT and Ankrd55−/− cells activated in naïve (TH0), non-pathogenic TH17 (TH17n), or pathogenic TH17 (TH17p) conditions. Apart from one outlier, a global analysis showed that biological replicates clustered together well, and that cells were primarily clustered by differentiation condition and not by genotype (Fig. S4A). In agreement with this, the only significantly differential peaks were in the Ankrd55 locus itself and covered the region that is deleted in Ankrd55−/− cells, without affecting the main peaks of the Il6st locus (Fig. S4B, green arrows). Next, we closely examined the chromatin accessibility in regions with recognition motifs for TH17 differentiation. There was a comparable increase in chromatin accessibility after TH17 differentiation in WT and Ankrd55−/− cells at the Il17a, Il17f, and Rorc loci (Fig. S4B), suggesting that Ankrd55−/− cells are poised to differentiate into TH17 cells at the epigenetic level, and that their relative lack of IL-17 production is caused by downstream regulation of gene expression and cytokine production rather than by local or distal epigenetic effects.To more directly examine IL6ST signaling and because IL6–STAT3 signaling is a critical pathway for TH17 differentiation, we examined STAT3 activation in Ankrd55-deficient cells. We observed a modest decrease in STAT3 activity at 4 hours post-stimulation, in the context of identical peak levels of phospho-STAT3 at 30 minutes and no change in IL6ST protein levels in Ankrd55−/− TH17 cells (Fig. S4C). We next examined whether increased levels of IL-6 provided during differentiation could rescue the phenotype of Ankrd55−/− cells. However, the difference in IL-17A expression between WT and Ankrd55−/− TH17 cells was very consistent across ~100-fold range of IL-6 concentrations, and levels of IL-17A expression in Ankrd55−/− cells plateaued at roughly half of the amounts observed in WT cells at high levels of IL-6 (Fig. S4D). Altogether, these experiments suggest that ANKRD55 plays a critical role in TH17 differentiation and that epigenetic effects or changes in IL-6 signaling are unlikely to explain the differences observed in Ankrd55−/− animals.Finally, building on our observations that Ankrd55 is induced in TH17 conditions particularly in the presence of IL-6 and TGF-β, we reasoned that its expression may be regulated by core TH17 transcription factors (Ciofani et al., 2012) such as STAT3 and Smad3/Smad4 downstream of these cytokines. ANKRD55 modulates TH17 differentiation by an LKB1–mitochondria axisGiven the impact of ANKRD55 deletion and exogenous expression, and the lack of clear evidence for epigenetic or IL6ST-mediated changes in Ankrd55−/− cells, we next focused on understanding how the ANKRD55 protein modulates TH17 function. ANKRD55 has been reported to be a nuclear protein in some studies (Lopez de Lapuente et al., 2016; Mena et al., 2021; Ugidos et al., 2019) but does not possess a clear localization sequence. To assess its location in T cells, we tested two commercial antibodies to stain endogenous human ANKRD55, as no murine antibodies were available. We identified one specific antibody, as validated by the lack of signal in T cells edited with two independent sgRNAs (Fig. S5A), and performed immunofluorescence staining of ANKRD55 protein in in vitro differentiated human TH17 cells. ANKRD55 was primarily located around but not within the nucleus, and a counterstain with MitoTracker revealed a strong co-localization of ANKRD55 and mitochondria (Fig. S5B). We next separated cellular compartments in nuclear, mitochondrial, and cytoplasmic fractions and confirmed a strong enrichment of ANKRD55 in the mitochondrial fraction (Fig. 5A). Of note, we observed some ANKRD55 in nuclear fractions, but it is unclear whether this represents a true association or minor mitochondrial contamination, as we also observed low levels of the mitochondrial channel VDAC1 in nuclear extracts. Next, to test whether the association of ANKRD55 with mitochondria influences cellular bioenergetics, we quantified the mitochondrial membrane potential of murine T cells by flow cytometry analysis using tetramethylrhodamine, methyl ester (TMRM), as this dye is normally sequestered in healthy mitochondria and serves as a reporter of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm). We observed that Ankrd55−/− cells have a lower basal ΔΨm, and this reduction compared to WT was increased in TH17 conditions (Fig. 5B). We next checked whether the lower ΔΨm of Ankrd55−/− cells was due to a lower mitochondrial DNA copy number or to lower transcript levels of mitochondrial or nuclear-encoded electron transport chain subunits and found that both of these were unchanged, suggesting that mitochondrial abundance itself is not affected (Fig. S5, C and D). In line with changes in ΔΨm, we observed lower oxygen consumption rates (OCR) in Ankrd55−/− cells in a Seahorse assay, marked in particular by a decrease in maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity (SRC, calculated as maximal respiration - basal respiration) (Fig. 5C). Altogether, these data indicate that Ankrd55−/− cells exhibit disrupted mitochondrial function.To identify potential pathways associated with disordered mitochondria, we performed RNA-sequencing on in vitro differentiated WT and Ankrd55−/− TH17 cells. Our analysis revealed enriched pathways, including cell cycle, one carbon pool by folate, nucleotide metabolism, and steroid biosynthesis (Fig. S5E). We further validated differential regulation in steroid biosynthesis (Soat2) and one carbon and nucleotide metabolism (Atf4, Phgdh, Shmt2, Aldh1l1) by qRT-PCR (Fig. S5F). The importance of one carbon and nucleotide metabolism in regulating T cell function has been highlighted previously (Baixauli et al., 2022; Ron-Harel et al., 2016; Saveljeva et al., 2022). Prior reports uncovered LKB1 as a key regulator of IL-17A expression in a model of mitochondrial stress that results from optic atrophy 1 (OPA1) deficiency in TH17 cells (Baixauli et al., 2022). In this context, the deletion of LKB1 can rescue TH17 function in Opa1−/− CD4+ T cells. To investigate whether this regulatory mechanism also modulates IL-17A in the context of ANKRD55 deficiency, we first directly measured LKB1 activation in our system. LKB1 signaling itself, as measured by phospho-LKB1, was moderately increased in ANKRD55-deficient TH17 cells, and downstream enzymes PHGDH, PSPH, SHMT2, and MTHFD2 involved in serine-glycine-one-carbon (SGOC) metabolism were upregulated in ANKRD55-deficient TH17 cells (Fig. 5D), consistent with previous studies linking mitochondrial dysfunction to increases in the enzymes of the SGOC pathway (Bao et al., 2016). Next, we knocked-down LKB1 (Stk11) in ANKRD55-deficient T cells under TH17 differentiation conditions and observed a restoration of IL-17A production to levels comparable to WT cells (Fig. 5E). Interestingly, LKB1 knock-down reduced the frequency of Foxp3+ cells in both WT and Ankrd55−/− T cells. It also corrected the expression of the enzymes in the SGOC pathway to WT levels (Fig. 5F). Overall, our results suggest that the loss of ANKRD55 promotes mitochondrial dysfunction and limits IL-17A production through the LKB1 pathway, highlighting SGOC metabolism as a potential pathway involved.To further define alterations in cellular metabolism in Ankrd55−/− TH17 cells, we applied metabolomic profiling to in vitro differentiated TH17 cells. Nucleotides were elevated in Ankrd55−/− compared to WT TH17 cells, with one-carbon units’ provider folate (10-formyl-THF) being enriched (Fig. 5G, Table S2). This was in accordance with the upregulated expression of enzymes in the SGOC pathway. To test the effect of accumulated folate on IL-17A production, we cultured naïve CD4+ T cells under TH17 conditions with formate. IL-17A production was markedly reduced by formate (Fig. S5G). We then genetically targeted the upregulated genes in the SGOC pathway and observed that the deletion of mitochondrial proteins SHMT2 or MTHFD2 restored IL-17A production in Ankrd55−/− TH17 cells, with MTHFD2 deletion simultaneously reducing the frequency of Foxp3+ cells (Fig. 5H). Moreover, formate treatment neutralized the rescue effect of MTHFD2 deletion in Ankrd55−/− TH17 cells. In sum, our results indicate that ANKRD55 deletion leads to changes in mitochondrial function that activate the LKB1 pathway and negatively regulates TH17 function, at least in part through changes in SGOC metabolism.

DiscussionGWAS have now identified tens of thousands of non-coding loci that influence risk for human diseases and traits (Claussnitzer et al., 2020), each of which could provide insights into the biological mechanisms of disease. Given the fact that many of these variants are non-coding and part of complex loci, connecting GWAS results to molecular functions has been challenging. Despite common intronic variants in ANKRD55 associated with multiple autoimmune diseases, the direct role of the ANKRD55 protein, if any, has remained unclear. Here we found that ANKRD55 directly regulates T cell proliferation and TH17 differentiation and cytokine production – particularly IL-17A but also in multiple contexts IL-22 – in two models of autoimmunity and in a model of C. rodentium infection, with the loss of ANKRD55 resulting in decreased autoimmunity in colitis and EAE, but also decreased bacterial clearance during infection.Our in vitro differentiation assay and in vivo T cell activation studies validated the function of ANKRD55 in regulating TH17 cell development, although we note that we also observed a modest effect on TH1 cell differentiation, both in vitro and in vivo, which suggests the pathways discussed here may also play a role in TH1 function even though they primarily impact the TH17 program. Importantly, overexpressing ANKRD55 from an exogenous construct substantially rescued IL-17A production of Ankrd55−/− TH17 cells, confirming the important role of ANKRD55 protein in regulating TH17 differentiation. MethodsMouse strains and husbandryThe Ankrd55 mouse strain used for this research project was created from embryonic stem cell clone 19160A-E11, obtained from the Knockout Mouse Project Repository. Ankrd55 mutant mice, in which exon 1, intron 1, and exon 2 codon sequences were replaced by Velocigene cassette ZEN-Ub1, were crossed with Cre mice to remove the neomycin selection cassette, but the line retains a LacZ reporter. After removal of the selection cassette, descendants from this cross were crossed with WT C57BL/6 mice to remove the Cre transgene. The mice were backcrossed on a C57BL/6 background to more than 10 generations and validated by sequencing. Rag1−/− mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. All mouse experiments were performed using age- and sex-matched mice and carried out according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Massachusetts General Hospital. Experimental protocols were initiated when animals reached ~8–10 weeks of age, and WT littermates were used as controls in all experiments.Animal models of inflammationCD4+CD45RBhi T cell adoptive transfer colitis modelCD4+ T cells were enriched from the spleens of healthy WT or Ankrd55−/− mice using EasySep Mouse CD4+ T Cell Enrichment Kit (Stemcell Technologies, catalog 19852). After removal of the selection cassette, descendants from this cross were crossed with WT C57BL/6 mice to remove the Cre transgene. The mice were backcrossed on a C57BL/6 background to more than 10 generations and validated by sequencing. Rag1−/− mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. All mouse experiments were performed using age- and sex-matched mice and carried out according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Massachusetts General Hospital. Metabolomic analysis

Three biological replicates were generated for each WT and Ankrd55−/− cell lines. A polar extract was prepared using 1,800,000 cells extracted in 80% methanol, while a lipid extract was obtained from 600,000 cells using 100% isopropanol. The cleared extracts were analyzed with a combination of four previously described liquid chromatography high-resolution mass spectrometry methods (Li et al., 2024). The polar methanol extract was analyzed using the HILIC-pos (10 μL), HILIC-neg (30 μL), and C18-neg (30 μL) methods, which target polar metabolites and those of intermediate polarity.
