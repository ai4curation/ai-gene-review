## Full Text

Abstract

Xylan, a pivotal polymer with diversified structures, is indispensable for cell wall integrity and contributes to plant growth and biomass recalcitrance. Xylan is synthesized by multienzyme complexes named xylan synthase complexes (XSCs). However, the biochemical mechanism of XSCs and the functions of core components within XSC remain unclear. Here, we report that rice ( Oryza sativa ) XYLAN O -ACETYLTRANSFERASE 6 (XOAT6) and the xylan synthase IRREGULAR XYLEM10 (IRX10) represent core components of the XSC, acting together to biosynthesize acetyl-xylans. Co-fractionation mass spectrometry and protein–protein interaction analyses revealed that IRX10 and XOAT6 physically interact within XSC, corroborated by similar xylan defects in xoat6 and irx10 mutants. Biochemical assays showed that XOAT6 is an O -acetyltransferase of the xylan backbone and facilitates chain polymerization catalyzed by IRX10. Fluorescence correlation spectroscopy further visualized the xylooligomer polymerization process at a single-molecule level. Solid-state NMR analysis, electron microscopy observations, and nanoindentation examinations identified the altered xylan conformation, disorganized cellulosic structure, and increased wall rigidity and cellulose accessibility in the mutants, leading to brittleness and improved saccharification efficiency. Our findings provide insights into the assembly of XSCs and xylan biosynthesis and offer a framework for tailoring xylans to improve crop traits and biomass.

Xylan O -acetyltransferase 6 interacts with xylosyltransferase IRX10 to form a xylan synthase complex, governing xylan structures and influencing cell wall strength and applications.

Introduction

Xylan, composed of β-(1,4)-linked xylosyl residues, is a major noncellulosic polysaccharide in vascular plants. It is predominantly present in secondary cell walls (SCWs) and accounts for a large portion of plant biomass in angiosperm and gymnosperm plants ( Smith et al. 2017 ; Ye and Zhong 2022 ). Xylans made in land plants usually contribute to the integrity of cell walls to sustain erect growth habits, physiological robustness, and viability in the terrestrial environment. Hence, xylan has long been thought to play a crucial role in SCW assembly and plant growth. Deficiency in xylan synthesis usually results in vulnerable SCW and collapsed xylem vessels, ultimately impairing plant growth ( Brown et al. 2007 ; Wu et al. 2009 ; Wang et al. 2022a ). Meanwhile, xylan serves as a significant factor in controlling biomass recalcitrance, hindering forage digestibility and biomass biorefinery ( Marriott et al. 2016 ; Hatfield et al. 2017 ). Therefore, xylan is a pivotal polymer for understanding cell wall assembly and a key factor affecting biomass utilization.

Xylan is a cell wall polymer with great structural diversity in different plant species and developmental stages, which is mainly manifested in the degree of polymerization (DP) and substituents ( Pena et al. 2016 ; Tryfona et al. 2023 ). In dicots, xylan is substituted with glucuronic acid (GlcA) and/or 4- O -methylglucuronic acid (Me-GlcA) at O -2 position of xylosyl residues, called glucuronoxylan (GX). In grasses, the substitutions on xylan mainly include arabinofuranose (Ara f ) via α-(1,3) or/and α-(1,2)-linkage to the xylan backbone, called arabinoxylan (AX), and AX occasionally decorated with (methyl)glucuronyl side chain, termed as glucuronoarabinoxylan (GAX) ( Busse-Wicher et al. 2014 ; Rennie and Scheller 2014 ; Zhang et al. 2024 ). The α-(1,3)-arabinosyl residues can be further substituted with xylosyl, coumaric acid, or ferulic acid moieties ( Chiniquy et al. 2012 ; Fejiao et al. 2022 ). In addition, plant xylans are often highly O -acetylated. The degree of acetylation on xylan ranges from 20% to 60% depending upon plant species and developmental stages, representing a major source of cell wall-bound acetates ( Xiong et al. 2013 ; Busse-Wicher et al. 2016 ). All these confer this polysaccharide with structural flexibility, enabling xylan to fold into 2-fold screw conformers that bind to cellulose microfibrils, or 3-fold screw conformers that preferentially interact with lignin or amorphous cellulose, as revealed by solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (ssNMR) spectroscopy studies ( Simmons et al. 2016 ; Kang et al. 2019 ; Gao et al. 2020 ). There is growing evidence that xylan is a crucial heterogeneous polymer with tissue-specific properties that control cell wall assembly and cellular functions ( Mortimer et al. 2015 ; Pena et al. 2016 ; Tryfona et al. 2023 ; Xu et al. 2024 ).

The structural diversity on xylans suggests that plants have evolved a sophisticated mechanism to make this polymer with fine structures. Plenty of genetic and biochemical studies have identified several families of genes involved in xylan biosynthesis, including those responsible for substrate supply ( Ebert et al. 2015 ; Kuang et al. 2016 ; Zhao et al. 2018 ), backbone synthesis ( Brown et al. 2007 , 2009 ; Pena et al. 2007 ; Wu et al. 2009 ; Lee et al. 2010 ; Chen et al. 2013 ; Zhang et al. 2014 ; Wang et al. 2022a ), and side chain addition ( Mortimer et al. 2010 ; Anders et al. 2012 ; Chiniquy et al. 2012 ; Rennie et al. 2012 ; Zhong et al. 2021 ). Consistent with the abundant acetyl esters on xylans, at least 3 groups of proteins were required for this modification. REDUCED WALL ACETYLATION (RWA) and ALTERED XYLOGLUCAN 9 (AXY9) nonspecifically modulate the acetylation degree on xylans and other cell wall polymers ( Manabe et al. 2011 ; Schultink et al. 2015 ). TRICHOME BIREFRINGENCE-LIKE (TBL) proteins containing the TBL and DUF231 domains have been demonstrated to transfer acetyl groups onto specific cell wall polymers ( Bischoff et al. 2010 ; Gille et al. 2011 ; Xiong et al. 2013 ; Yuan et al. 2016 ), among which those functioning on xylans are therefore termed as xylan O -acetyltransferases (XOATs) ( Urbanowicz et al. 2014 ; Gao et al. 2017 ; Zhong et al. 2018 ; Lunin et al. 2020 ). Together with xylan esterases ( Zhang et al. 2017 , 2019 ; Rastogi et al. 2022 ), XOATs are thought to be essential for maintaining a certain acetylation profile on xylans ( Zhang et al. 2024 ). Despite these advances in protein characterization, the biochemical mechanism of heteroxylan synthesis is still not fully understood.

The formation of multienzyme complexes is considered a key mechanism of xylan biosynthesis. Despite the variations in heteroxylans, the main chain consisting of 4-Xyl residues is the consistent core structure. In Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ), 3 pairs of proteins, including IRX9/IRX9L and IRX14/IRX14L from glycosyltransferase family 43 (GT43) and IRX10/IRX10L from GT47, have been genetically and/or biochemically identified to be required for xylan backbone synthesis ( Brown et al. 2009 ; Wu et al. 2009 , 2010 ), indicating the existence of xylan synthase complexes (XSCs). Furthermore, IRX9 does not reciprocally complement the mutant phenotypes of irx14 ( Lee et al. 2010 ; Wu et al. 2010 ), corroborating the complexity of xylan biosynthesis. The homologues of these Arabidopsis GTs have been subsequently characterized in moss ( Physcomitrium patens ) ( Jensen et al. 2014 ), wheat ( Triticum aestivum ) ( Zeng et al. 2010 ), rice ( Oryza sativa ) ( Chen et al. 2013 ; Wang et al. 2022a ), Brachypodium distachyon ( Petrik et al. 2020 ), etc., suggesting that the biosynthetic mechanism of xylan backbone is likely conserved in different plant species.

As these GTs are likely the central components of XSCs, identification of their protein–protein interacting sets is crucial for clarifying the assemblies of XSCs. Based on the evidence from co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays in etiolated wheat seedlings and protein–protein interactions in asparagus and wheat, IRX10/IRX10L, IRX9/9L, and IRX14/14L can form different interacting pairs, placing them as core components of XSCs ( Zeng et al. 2010 , 2016 ; Rennie and Scheller 2014 ; Jiang et al. 2016 ; Smith et al. 2017 ). Due to the existence of multiple GT43 and GT47 members, various GT43/GT47 combinations are proposed. This hypothesis has been verified by assessing the specific protein interactions between GT43 and GT47 members in rice, in which 4 GT43 members and 4 GT47 proteins interact to form central cores of different XSCs ( Javaid et al. 2024 ). Hence, the combination of GT43/GT47 within XSCs would be diverse and specific, in agreement with the tissue specificity of xylans.

Although many studies have suggested that multiple XSCs may exist in plants ( Zeng et al. 2016 ; Anders et al. 2023 ), biochemical characterization of XSCs and the role of individual core components in XSCs is still challenging. IRX10 or IRX10L proteins from Arabidopsis, rice, moss, psyllium ( Plantago ovata ), and algae have been demonstrated to transfer xylosyl residues onto xylooligomers in vitro ( Urbanowicz et al. 2014 ; Jensen et al. 2018 ; Wang et al. 2022a ). IRX10/IRX10L proteins act as the central cores of XSC; however, their concomitants/partners in the XSCs and the specific functions are unclear. Given that chain polymerization catalyzed by IRX10/IRX10L is the core process in xylan biosynthesis, acetylation would be another essential process. Xylan in the Golgi apparatus displays similar acetylation patterns as that in apoplast ( Busse-Wicher et al. 2014 ), suggesting that xylan acetylation is incorporated in xylan synthesis. Arabidopsis IRX10L and TBL29/XOAT1 indeed can synthesize acetyl-xylan in vitro. Incubating the two proteins sequentially or simultaneously generated different acetyl-xylans ( Urbanowicz et al. 2014 ), but it is not enough to clarify how the two steps proceed. Discovery of the concomitant partners and/or auxiliary proteins of IRX10/IRX10L in XSCs will expand and strengthen our understanding of xylan biosynthesis.

Here, we report the identification of the interaction between IRX10 and XOAT6 that forms a central core of XSC and promotes xylan polymerization based on the evidence from multiscale experiments. XOAT6 is an acetyltransferase of xylan backbone, which is essential for SCW formation. The rice mutants deficient in XOAT6 and IRX10 displayed similar defects in acetyl ester and xylose content, resulting in low DP of xylan. We further demonstrated that the IRX10–XOAT6 module in XSC synergistically catalyzes the synthesis of acetyl-xylans, thereby affecting mechanical support and straw saccharification. Hence, this study identifies the central core members within XSC that facilitate acetyl-xylan synthesis in rice, offering insights into the mechanism underlying xylan biosynthesis and providing a strategy for precisely tailoring this important polymer for multiple uses.

Results

XOAT6 is an IRX10-interacting component within XSC

Co-fractionation mass spectrometry (CF-MS) is a widely used approach to identify the assemblies of protein complexes ( McWhite et al. 2020 ). As rice IRX10 (Os01g70200), one of the 6 homologues of Arabidopsis IRX10, was recently demonstrated to catalyze xylan backbone synthesis ( Wang et al. 2022a ), it should be an essential core member of XSCs. To identify the associated proteins of IRX10 in XSC, we carried out CF-MS analysis using the developing rice internodes that are undergoing vigorous SCW formation. We used a detergent n -Dodecyl β-D-maltoside (DDM) to solubilize total microsome proteins extracted from Nipponbare internodes and fractionated them using ion exchange chromatography (IEX). We probed the resulting fractions with the specific anti-IRX10 antibodies ( Wang et al. 2022a ; Supplementary Fig. S1, A to C ), and combined the fractions displaying IRX10 signal (IEX fractions 16 to 19) and subjected to size exclusion chromatography (SEC) for further fractionation using a Superdex 200 column ( Supplementary Fig. S1, D and E ). Through probing the resulting SEC fractions (1 to 15) with the anti-IRX10 antibodies, we detected the obvious IRX10 signal in SEC fractions 7 and 8 ( Fig. 1A ), indicating the successful enrichment of XSC containing IRX10. By subjecting the SEC fractions separately to liquid chromatography-coupled mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis, we discerned a total of 1,270 proteins with peptide spectrum match (PSM) score above 7 in at least 1 fraction ( Supplementary Data Set 1 ).

Figure 1.

Since the components in this XSC should be associated with IRX10 during the fractionation, we conducted profiling analysis of the 1,270 detected proteins using the PSM values in the SEC fractions and generated 7 clusters with significance verified by Fisher's exact test and multiple comparisons ( Fig. 1B ). IRX10 was grouped to C14, which contained 11 proteins related to cell wall polysaccharide biosynthesis, including glycosyltransferases, XOATs, and xylose isomerase ( Supplementary Fig. S1F ). Os03g18140 (XOAT6), a protein homologous to Arabidopsis XOAT1/TBL29 ( Supplementary Fig. S2, A and B ), displayed the most similar PSM profile (as indicated by the most significant P value) to IRX10 ( Fig. 1C ; Supplementary Fig. S1F ). Hence, XOAT6 is likely one of the IRX10 companions/partners in rice internodes.

To confirm this hypothesis, we probed the above-obtained fractions using the specific anti-XOAT6 antibodies ( Supplementary Fig. S2C ). The signal pattern of XOAT6 was highly consistent with that of IRX10 ( Fig. 1A ; Supplementary Fig. S1B ), confirming that both proteins were cofractionated. We further performed split-luciferase complementation assay to validate the interaction and observed a clear interaction between XOAT6 and IRX10 ( Fig. 1D ), whereas no interaction was observed with BC10, another Golgi-localized protein with similar topology ( Supplementary Fig. S2D ; Zhou et al. 2009 ). We further performed Co-IP assays using the stable transgenic complementary plants that express mNeonGreen (mNG)-fused IRX10 in the irx10 background. A co-precipitation signal was detected between IRX10 and XOAT6, but not between IRX10 and CESA9 or BiP (A luminal binding protein) ( Fig. 1E ), corroborating the interaction between IRX10 and XOAT6. We further analyzed the DDM solubilized total microsomal proteins through blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN-PAGE). As revealed by protein blotting, we observed both XOAT6 and IRX10 signals at ∼200 kDa ( Fig. 1F ; Supplementary Fig. S1G ), consistent with the molecular weight range analyzed by SEC ( Supplementary Fig. S1D ). XOAT6 is an interacting protein of IRX10 in XSC.

To determine the spatio-temporal profile of XOAT6–IRX10 interaction, we fused green fluorescent protein (GFP) at the carboxyl (C) terminus of XOAT6 and transfected Nicotiana benthamiana leaves together with a red fluorescent protein (mCherry)-fused Golgi marker Man49. The XOAT6-GFP signal was nicely matched with the fluorescent signal of the Golgi marker ( Fig. 1G ). The colocalization was further verified using the Pearson correlation coefficient and Mander's coefficient ( Fig. 1H ), indicating that XOAT6 is localized in the Golgi apparatus similarly as IRX10. We further analyzed the expression profiles of both genes in wild-type rice plants. As revealed by reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis, XOAT6 was mainly expressed in the developing internodes, exhibiting an expression profile similar to that of IRX10 ( Fig. 1I ). We next examined their expression at the cellular level. We collected sclerenchyma cells (SCs) that harbor SCW and parenchyma cells (PCs) that contain primary cell wall from young internode sections of Nipponbare using laser microdissection ( Fig. 1J ). By performing RT-qPCR analysis using these collected cells, we found that both IRX10 and XOAT6 were predominantly expressed in SCs ( Fig. 1K ). Therefore, IRX10 and XOAT6 have comparable expression patterns, supporting the interaction between IRX10 and XOAT6.

Loss of XOAT6 causes defects in acetyl-xylan, similar to IRX10 mutation

The mutant deficient in XOAT6 has not yet been reported in rice. By screening rice brittle culm ( bc ) mutants, we identified bc17 , a spontaneous mutant derived from an indica variety Minhui63 (MH63). Through map-based cloning in 1,987 F 2 mutants generated by crossing bc17 with a japonica variety Xiushui09, BC17 was mapped within a 47-kb region on chromosome 3 that comprises 4 open reading frames (ORFs) ( Supplementary Fig. S3 and Table S1 ). Sequencing analysis revealed a G to A substitution at the 738 base pair (bp) site of ORF1 ( Os03g18140/XOAT6 ), which introduces a stop codon terminating translation at the 246th amino acid (aa) ( bc17-1 ; Fig. 2A ; Supplementary Fig. S3A ). Because genetic transformation is difficult to carry out in indica varieties, we employed CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing to generate an additional mutant allele of Os03g18140/XOAT6 in the japonica variety Nipponbare. The bc17-2 allele has a 1-bp deletion at the 344-bp site, which causes a frame shift and terminates translation at 115th aa ( Fig. 2A ; Supplementary Fig. S3B ). Although both alleles had an overall appearance similar to wild-type plants ( Supplementary Fig. S3C ), they displayed brittle internodes and significantly reduced breaking force ( Fig. 2B ), suggesting that Os03g18140/XOAT6 corresponds to BC17 .

Figure 2.

Cell wall composition analyses revealed that the wall-bound acetate content in the internodes of the two alleles of bc17 was reduced by 20% to 30% compared to wild-type plants ( Fig. 2C ), suggesting that XOAT6 indeed affects cell wall acetylation. Interestingly, the xylose content unexpectedly decreased in both alleles of bc17 mutants ( Fig. 2D ), while most other sugars were not significantly reduced ( Supplementary Table S2 ). Hence, XOAT6 may also affect xylan polymerization. To dissect whether the alterations were attributed to xylan defects, we extracted acetyl-xylans from the internodes of wild-type, bc17 and the previously reported irx10 mutant bc18 , and subjected to heteronuclear single-quantum coherence (HSQC) analysis. By measuring the anomeric signal of acetylated xylosyl residues, we found that the acetylation degree was obviously decreased in bc17 mutants, as well as in bc18 ( Fig. 2, E and F ). In addition, we observed a comparable increase in xylosyl residues at the reducing end (Re-Xyl) compared to the wild type, leading to a decrease in xylan DP in the bc17 and bc18 mutants ( Fig. 2G ). Furthermore, we determined molecular size of acetyl-xylans extracted from the wild type and mutant plants by SEC on an ACQUITY UPLC system (Waters) equipped with a fluorescence detector. Based on the elution volume of the major peak, we found that the molecular size of xylans is significantly reduced in the bc17 and bc18 mutants ( Fig. 2H ). All those suggested that the chain length of xylan in bc17 and bc18 was significantly decreased. Hence, the mutations in BC17 and BC18 compromise xylan acetylation and polymerization.

To verify the genetic relationship between BC17/XOAT6 and BC18/IRX10, we generated a double mutant by crossing bc18 with bc17-2 and conducted the above examinations in the internodes of the double mutant. In addition to the brittleness ( Fig. 2B ), the double mutant plants displayed comparable morphological appearance as bc18 ( Supplementary Fig. S3C ). The wall-bound acetate level was reduced like in the bc17 single mutant ( Fig. 2, C and E ). Consistent with the reduction in xylose content ( Fig. 2D ), the DP and molecular size of xylan in the double mutant were also significantly decreased ( Fig. 2, G and H ). These similar defects in xylans support the existence of XOAT6–IRX10 interaction in XSC in rice.

XOAT6 catalyzes xylan backbone acetylation and promotes IRX10 activity

Rice XOAT6 is a close homolog of Arabidopsis xylan acetyltransferase XOAT1 ( Xiong et al. 2013 ; Zhong et al. 2018 ; Lunin et al. 2020 ). To verify its enzymatic activity, we expressed rice XOAT6 in Pichia pastoris ( Supplementary Fig. S4A ). Through incubating the purified XOAT6 with acetyl-CoA (donor substrate) and xylopentaose (X5, acceptor substrate), we detected the transfer of 1 or 2 acetyl groups onto X5, as revealed by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) assay ( Fig. 3A ). By adding a gradient concentration of X6 into the reactions, we found that XOAT6 acetyltransferase activity fits a saturable Michaelis–Menten kinetics with a K m value of approximately 2.66 m m ( Fig. 3B ). We further examined the activity of XOAT6 on xylohexaose (X6) for varied time. The acetylated epitopes were obviously detected on X6 at 1 h, and reached high abundance at 5 h ( Fig. 3C ; Supplementary Fig. S4B ). We also supplied xylooligosaccharides ranging from DP3 (X3) to DP8 (X8) in the reactions. With the exception of X3, we detected the addition of 1 to 3 acetyl groups onto X4 to X8 ( Supplementary Fig. S4C ). Taken together, XOAT6 is an acetyltransferase of xylan backbone.

Figure 3.

To determine the effect of XOAT6–IRX10 interaction on enzyme activity, we carried out time-course experiments by supplying XOAT6 to the reactions containing IRX10, X5, and the essential donor substrates. With increasing reaction time, we observed the formation of xylooligosaccharides with increased DP, as demonstrated by carbohydrate electrophoresis ( Fig. 3D ). Notably, XOAT6 obviously promoted xylooligosaccharide polymerization compared to the reactions containing IRX10 alone ( Fig. 3D ). To eliminate the interference of acetylation to the detection, we deacetylated the products with 1 m of sodium hydroxide prior to carbohydrate electrophoresis. The unchanged ladder pattern suggested that XOAT6-mediated acetylation does not cause interference with the detection ( Supplementary Fig. S4D ), so the changes in DP is attributed to the alterations in IRX10 polymerization activity. The xylooligosaccharides generated at various reaction time points were further analyzed by MALDI-TOF MS. Over the examining time, we detected longer and/or more abundant elongated xylooligomers in the reactions containing IRX10 and XOAT6 than the parallel reactions containing IRX10 alone ( Fig. 3, E and H ; Supplementary Fig. S4E ). Upon extending the reaction time to 300 min, significant acetylated xylooligomers were detected in the reaction containing both IRX10 and XOAT6 ( Fig. 3E ; Supplementary Table S3 ). Therefore, XOAT6 facilitates chain polymerization mediated by IRX10.

Next, we investigated whether the enhancement is dependent on the acetyltransferase activity of XOAT6. We performed site-directed mutagenesis on the three conserved amino acids (S278A, D528A, and H531A) in the predicted catalytic triad of XOAT6. As a result, the three XOAT6 variants exhibited abolished acetyltransferase activity on X5 substrate ( Supplementary Fig. S5, A and B ). We next determined the impact of these XOAT6 variants on xylan polymerization individually. By supplying XOAT6 or its variants to the reactions containing IRX10 and the substrates, we observed that the wild-type XOAT6 and its variants promoted the polymerization of X5 into similar DP, as revealed by carbohydrate electrophoresis and MALDI-TOF assays ( Fig. 3, F, G, and I ; Supplementary Fig. S5D ). XOAT13/TBL1, another cofractionated acetyltransferase of xylan backbone ( Gao et al. 2017 ; Supplementary Fig. S1F ), was used as a control ( Supplementary Fig. S5C ). We found that the supplied XOAT13 in the parallel reactions can transfer the acetyl group onto xylooligomers, but failed to promote xylooligomer polymerization like XOAT6 and its variants ( Fig. 3, F and I ). Hence, the promoting effect of XOAT6 on IRX10 activity does not necessarily depend on its acetyltransferase activity, at least in short-chain polymerization.

Single-molecule examination of XOAT6–IRX10 interaction

Next, we employed microscale thermophoresis (MST) to quantify the interacting affinity between XOAT6 and IRX10. By examining the thermophoretic movement of the fluorescence dye-labeled IRX10 in response to a gradient of XOAT6 or XOAT6 variants examined above ( Supplementary Fig. S5 ), we obtained typical binding curves ( Fig. 4A ), verifying the interaction of IRX10 with XOAT6 and its variants. The K D value for wild-type XOAT6 was comparable to that for the two XOAT6 variants ( Fig. 4A ). Therefore, the interaction between XOAT6 and IRX10 is not affected by catalytic inactivation mutations.

Figure 4.

Fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS) is an advanced technique for in vitro measuring dynamics of biomolecules at single-molecule level ( Wang et al. 2022b ). We then conducted an FCS assay to visualize the polymerization process of xylooligosaccharides. We labeled the acceptor substrate xylobiose (X2) with a fluorophore tag Alexa Fluor 488 (AF488) and measured the hydrodynamic radius (R H ) during the reaction ( Fig. 4B ). As a result, we observed the R H changes when incubating IRX10 with the donor and AF488-labeled X2; and the changes in R H became more obvious upon further addition of XOAT6 ( Fig. 4C ). We did not observe the changes of R H when the reactions only contained the donors and the AF488-labeled X2 or contained the proteins and labeled X2 but in the absence of the donor substrate UDP-Xyl ( Fig. 4C ), suggesting that the changes in R H are mainly due to the polymerization on the AF488-labeled X2. Therefore, the supply of XOAT6 can promote the polymerization mediated by IRX10. To visualize the dynamics of R H changes, we performed a time-course examination. By measuring the changes in R H within 320 min at 40-min intervals, we observed a gradual increase in R H ( Fig. 4D ). The increment of R H was significantly enhanced when both IRX10 and XOAT6 were present ( Fig. 4D ). Hence, as revealed by visualizing the polymerization process, the IRX10–XOAT6 interaction promotes elongation activity of IRX10 in vitro.

XOAT6 governs cell wall structure, affecting mechanical strength

Acetylation is a key determinant of xylan folding. To investigate whether XOAT6 can alter xylan conformation, we cultivated the wild type and bc17 seedlings in a chamber supplying with 13 CO 2 and obtained 13 C-enriched leaf sheathes ( Supplementary Fig. S6A ). We then conducted ssNMR analysis to examine the native status of xylan in the resulting wild type and bc17 samples. By performing 2D double-quantum and single-quantum 13 C– 13 C correlation experiments using the refocused INADEQUATE sequence with cross polarization and direct polarization, we detected abundant 2-fold (Xn4, 82.1 ppm; Xn5, 64.3 ppm) and 3-fold (Xn4, 77.8 ppm; Xn5, 64.1 ppm) xylan conformers in the cross polarization spectra; but the 2-fold conformers were obviously reduced in bc17 ( Fig. 5, A and B ; Supplementary Fig. S6, B to D ). Hence, the loss of XOAT6 causes alterations in xylan conformation and significantly lessens the rigid xylan molecules bound onto cellulose.

Figure 5.

To determine the cause of the reduced mechanical strength, we measured the wall thickness of fiber cells using scanning electron microscopy. The bc17 , bc18 , and the double mutant displayed decreased cell wall thickness in fiber cells ( Supplementary Fig. S7, A and B ), in agreement with the slightly reduced cellulose content in the internodes of these mutants ( Supplementary Table S2 ). Hence, lesions in bc17 and bc18 compromise SCW thickening. Furthermore, we longitudinally dissected the internodes to expose the cellulosic nanofibers embedded in the cell wall of fiber cells for field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) analysis ( Supplementary Fig. S7C ). We found that the cellulosic nanofibers in bc17 exhibited disordered arrangement and decreased diameter compared to the wild type ( Fig. 5, C to E ). We further examined the cellulosic nanofiber organization in bc18 and the double mutant. A similar disordered orientation of nanofibers was observed in bc18 and the double mutant ( Fig. 5, C to E ). Therefore, both BC17 and BC18 regulate cellulose organization and cell wall structure.

Next, we measured the elastic modulus of fiber cells isolated from the internodes of the wild type and the single and double mutants using an atomic force microscope equipped with a triangular pyramid tip. Based on the force curves during indentation and retraction ( Supplementary Fig. S8A ), we calculated the Young's moduli of the examined fiber cells. The Young's moduli of the bc17 walls varied from 20 to 200 MPa, whereas those of the wild-type walls ranged from 20 to 120 MPa ( Fig. 5F ). The Young's moduli of bc18 and the double mutant further increased, ranging from 20 to 260 MPa ( Fig. 5F ). Consequently, the average Young's modulus of the bc17 fiber cell wall exhibited a 30% increase compared to the wild type, while that of bc18 and the double mutant was one time higher than the wild type ( Fig. 5G ). Hence, both XOAT6 and IRX10 regulate mechanical force.

Lesions in XOAT6 reduce wall recalcitrance and increase saccharification efficiency

The changes in cell wall structure may affect saccharification efficiency. Next, we subjected the internode cell wall residues of wild type and the mutants with comparable particle size to saccharification assay. More glucose and xylose were released from the cell wall residues of bc17 , bc18 , and the double mutant compared to the wild type ( Supplementary Fig. S8, B to D ). The mutant internodes displayed approximately 20% to 46% increases in saccharification efficiency ( Fig. 6A ). To determine the cause of the improvement, cellulose accessibility was evaluated by Simon's staining and cellulase binding assays. By using filtrated Direct Orange 15 (DO) dye, we observed increased dye adsorption curve and distribution coefficient in the mutant cell wall samples compared to the wild-type samples ( Fig. 6, B and C ), suggesting that cellulose accessibility is improved in the mutants. To confirm this result, we further examined the in situ binding capacity of cellulases to internode sections. By incubating the rice internode sections with Alexa Fluor 488-labeled cellulases, we observed increased fluorescent signal intensity in the sections of bc17 , bc18 , and the double mutant plants compared to the wild type ( Fig. 6, D and E ). Hence, the loss of BC17 and BC18 decreases xylan synthesis and acetyl modification, leading to improved cellulose accessibility and saccharification.

Discussion

Xylan, the second most abundant cell wall polymer after cellulose, is indispensable for the assembly of cohesive cell wall structure through interlocking wall polymers ( Carpita and Gibeaut 1993 ; Simmons et al. 2016 ; Ye and Zhong 2022 ; Zhang et al. 2024 ). Xylan usually displays wide structural heterogeneity across different species, tissues, cell types, and different developmental stages ( Pena et al. 2016 ), which contributes to the formation of different xylan conformers and thereby various cell wall networks. Land plants thus evolved dozens of proteins to make xylans, probably by forming multienzyme complexes. However, what the core components of the complexes are, how these central enzymes and/or auxiliary proteins dictate heteroxylan synthesis, and whether the proteins involved in backbone elongation and acetylation are incorporated in a XSC, remain unanswered ( Ye and Zhong 2022 ; Anders et al. 2023 ; Curry et al. 2023 ). Here, we provided a series of evidence that XOAT6, an acetyltransferase of xylan backbone, interacts with IRX10 to form a central core of XSC, thereby promoting xylan backbone polymerization. Xylans made by the XSC containing the IRX10–XOAT6 module contribute to the organization of cellulose microfibrils and cell wall structure, ultimately influencing SCW properties, mechanical strength, and saccharification efficiency.

As the core framework structure of cell wall polysaccharides, the synthesis of their backbone has received extensive attention. Interestingly, unlike cellulose and other β-(1,4)-linked hemicellulose backbones that are catalyzed by cellulose synthases or cellulose synthase-like proteins ( Arioli et al. 1998 ; Dhugga et al. 2004 ; Burton et al. 2006 ; Cocuron et al. 2007 ), the xylan backbone is synthesized by type II membrane proteins, such as the GT47 members, in the Golgi apparatus ( Jensen et al. 2014 ; Urbanowicz et al. 2014 ; Zeng et al. 2016 ; Jensen et al. 2018 ; Wang et al. 2022a ). Based on the in-depth studies on protein complexes, protein–protein interactions, and co-expression networks, proteins from GT47 and GT43 families are considered to be crucial components of XSCs ( Zeng et al. 2010 , 2016 ; Jiang et al. 2016 ; Javaid et al. 2024 ). Since plants generally possess multiple GT43 and GT47 homologues, the synthesis of xylan backbone seems to share a conserved mechanism, but the combination of GT43/GT47 may be varied to accommodate the different requirements of xylan levels and structures in tissues or cell types ( Zeng et al. 2016 ; Anders et al. 2023 ; Javaid et al. 2024 ). However, the biochemical function of these GTs within individual XSC has not been largely elucidated.

The activities of IRX10 and IRX10L proteins from GT47 family in Arabidopsis, rice, psyllium, Physcomitrium patens , and Klebsormidium flaccidum have been demonstrated to catalyze xylan backbone elongation ( Jensen et al. 2014 , 2018 ; Urbanowicz et al. 2014 ; Wang et al. 2022a ). IRX10 is thus a likely core component of XSCs, representing a key point for dissecting the assembly and biochemical mechanism of XSCs. In this study, we used IRX10 as an indicator to track the co-fractionating proteins in CF-MS analysis, a powerful technique that allows to identify the intact and native complexes ( McWhite et al. 2020 ; Fossati et al. 2021 ). A total of 11 candidates, including 6 GTs, 4 XOATs, and 1 xylose isomerase, were found within the cluster containing IRX10, based on PSM profiling analysis. The high profile similarity and PSM abundance suggested XOAT6 as the most likely component of the IRX10-containing XSC. Interestingly, 2 XAX1-like proteins (GT61 members) and another candidate GT outside the GT43 or GT47 family were also grouped in this cluster, indicating a potential diversity of XSC composition. Notably, the CF-MS analysis also identified 4 GT43 members (IRX9/IRX9L and IRX14/IRX14L) ( Supplementary Data Set 1 ), however, the PSM profiling did not group them with IRX10 into the same cluster. In addition, the molecular size of IRX10-containing XSC is ∼200 kDa, which is greater than the sum of IRX10 (47 kDa) and XOAT6 (47 kDa). Therefore, we do not rule out the possibility that GT43 members also exist in XSCs. Our study hence provides an unprecedented view for the intact assemblies of XSCs in rice, although authenticity of these candidates other than XOAT6 remains to be confirmed.

Native xylan often harbors high levels of acetyl esters on its backbone ( Xiong et al. 2013 ; Pena et al. 2016 ; Gao et al. 2017 ; Tryfona et al. 2023 ). Since O -acetylation enhances the solubility of xylan chains, the nascent xylan chains are considered to be acetylated in the Golgi apparatus ( Busse-Wicher et al. 2014 ; Urbanowicz et al. 2014 ). Whether xylan chain elongation and acetylation occur through synergistic or nonsynergistic mechanism has been debated for a long time. In this study, based on a series of genetic and biochemical evidence, we found that four XOATs were cofractionated with IRX10, and XOAT6 directly interacts with IRX10, providing molecular proofs for that the two fundamental processes are synergistic during xylan synthesis. Additionally, single mutations in either XOAT6 or IRX10 led to simultaneous reductions in both xylose and acetyl ester levels, providing genetic supports for their interaction. The in vitro enzymatic assays and FCS analysis further revealed that XOAT6 can promote xylan polymerization catalyzed by IRX10. Such promotion is likely attributed to allosteric changes in the proteins caused by the formation of a multimeric XSC. More interestingly, the promotion seemed not fully dependent on the XOAT6 acetyltransferase activity, as the catalytic inactivation variants of XOAT6 can promote IRX10 polymerization, which is supported by the MST results that these catalytic inactivation mutations did not interfere with the interaction with IRX10. This unexpected finding also hinted that the interaction between IRX10 and XOAT6 may contribute to stabilizing the complex. In contrast, XOAT13, another XOAT cofractionated with IRX10, did not exhibit a boost to IRX10 activity. XOAT13 seems to play a different role in XSC than XOAT6 and is not necessary for xylan backbone elongation, in agreement with the unchanged xylose content in xoat13 mutant ( Gao et al. 2017 ). Since the development of rice internodes involves massive xylan synthesis, efficient xylan backbone polymerization relies on the IRX10–XOAT6 interaction in the XSC, offering valuable insights into the acetyl-xylan biosynthesis.

In addition to the proofs from biochemical and single molecule analyses, the genetic evidence from the double mutant also supported our conclusion that xylan polymerization is coordinated with XOAT6-mediated acetylation. Some additive effects observed in the double mutant may be due to the presence of XSCs containing different combinations of IRX10 and XOAT6 homologous proteins. Rice genome contains 5 IRX10-like proteins and at least 2 or 6 close XOAT homologs ( Gao et al. 2017 ; Wang et al. 2022a ), offering an opportunity to form XSCs with different IRX10/IRX10L-XOAT combinations. A recent study reported that rice may have at least 3 different XSCs containing various combinations of GT43 and GT47 members, including at least 1 member of the IRX10/IRX10L clade ( Javaid et al. 2024 ). Regardless, other possibilities should not be ruled out, in particular that the assemblies of XSCs may vary in different tissues and cell types ( Faik 2010 ; Rennie and Scheller 2014 ; Anders et al. 2023 ). Further studies, such as determining XSC atomic structures using cryoelectron microscopy and global allostery mapping, will advance the understanding of xylan biosynthesis mechanisms ( Wilson et al. 2022 ; Faure et al. 2022 ).

Acetylation often alters the physicochemical properties of xylan. Acetylation pattern on xylan allows it folding into diverse conformers to interlock different cell wall polymers ( Grantham et al. 2017 ; Zhang et al. 2024 ). Therefore, xylan plays an important role in cellulose microfibril organization and wall integrity. Deficiency in xylan usually results in abnormal bundling of cellulose microfibrils and plant growth ( Zhang et al. 2019 ; Wang et al. 2022a ). Here, mutation of XOAT6 lessens 2-fold xylan conformer. The fiber cell walls in bc17 and bc18 , as well as in the double mutant, exhibited nanofibril misalignment and loosely arranged network of cellulose microfibrils, leading to altered cellular mechanical performances. Hence, xylan biosynthesis mediated by IRX10 and XOAT6 is critical for the assembly of the competent load-bearing network of SCW.

The presence of O -acetyl groups also contributes to biomass recalcitrance, affecting saccharification ( Smith et al. 2017 ). The acetylation degree on xylan is a crucial factor to affect biorefinery ( Xiong et al. 2013 ; Gao et al. 2017 ; Zhang et al. 2017 ). In our study, the loss of IRX10 or XOAT6 decreases both xylan and acetyl ester levels, leading to improved saccharification efficiency without acid pretreatment. Although it is difficult to assess the exact effects, the changes in cellulose organization and cellulose accessibility caused by xylan alterations may be the main causes. Interestingly, mutation in the Arabidopsis homologous gene ( TBL29 ) compromised saccharification efficiency ( Xiong et al. 2013 ). However, the esk1-5 mutant (another allele of tbl29 ) exhibited reduced 2-fold xylan conformer and increased saccharification ( Bensussan et al. 2015 ; Grantham et al. 2017 ), similar to the rice bc17 mutants in this study. Understanding the genetic networks and the mechanisms that govern the formation of cell wall structures will help resolve this puzzle.

In summary, our study reports the biochemical characterization of XSC containing an IRX10–XOAT6-interacting module, which promotes the concomitant synthesis of acetyl-xylans. These findings suggest an undescribed mechanism of xylan biosynthesis and provide a tool for manipulating xylan for diverse uses.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The rice plants ( Oryza sativa L.) used in this study were cultivated in experimental fields at the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology in Beijing (China), in Yangzhou University (Jiangsu Province, China), and in Lingshui (Hainan Province, China) in the growing seasons. N. benthamiana plants were grown in a controlled environment room (22 °C) under a 16-h light and 8-h dark photoperiod with ∼8,000 Lux light intensity.

Gene cloning and editing

To clone the BC17 gene, ∼8,000 F 2 plants were generated by crossing bc17-1 with the japonica wild-type cultivar Xiushui09. A total of 1,987 mutant plants were isolated and applied to locate the causal gene using molecular markers ( Supplementary Table S1 ). The mapped region was narrowed down within a 46.96-kb fragment on chromosome 3. The 4 ORFs within this region were amplified from both mutant and wild-type genomic DNA and sequenced using a 3,730 sequencer (ABI). For genetic validation, a target sequence (ACGGCGATGGCGGCCGCCGCCGG) was cloned into a binary plasmid (pYLCRISPR/Cas9 Pubi-MH) to generate an additional mutant allele of Os03g18140/XOAT6 ( bc17-2 ) in the wild-type variety Nipponbare using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing ( Ma et al. 2016 ). A double mutant was generated by crossing bc17-2 and bc18 and followed by genetic screening. To generate a construct for green fluorescence protein (GFP)-fused OsXOAT6, the coding sequence of GFP was inserted into the carboxyl of XOAT6 via in-frame fusion in the pCAMBIA1300 between the Cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter and the NOS terminator.

CF-MS analysis

For CF-MS analysis, young internodes were harvested and ground in liquid nitrogen. The tissues were homogenized in a buffer (20 m m Tris-HCl at pH 7.5, 250 m m sucrose, 1× protease inhibitor, 10% glycerol and 2 m m EDTA). After centrifuging at 10,000 × g for 15 min, the supernatants were further ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 2 h at 4 °C to collect microsomes. The pellets were resuspended in a buffer (20 m m Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 1 m m EDTA, 1× protease inhibitor) and solubilized using 1% DDM on ice for 30 min. After centrifuging at 10,000 × g for 15 min, the solubilized membrane protein samples were filtrated through 0.22- μ m filters. Approximately 2 mg of protein was loaded onto a HiTrap Q HP column (GE) equilibrated with Q column buffer (20 m m Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 1% DDM), and fractionated with a linear gradient of 0 to 1 m NaCl in the Q column buffer using an FPLC system (ÄKTA pure, GE). The eluted protein samples were collected in 40 fractions, and 100 µ L of each fraction was subjected to SDS-PAGE. The fractions were blotted onto membranes and probed with the antibodies against IRX10 ( Wang et al. 2022a ) and XOAT6 at a 1:1,000 dilution. The antigens of IRX10 and XOAT6 were generated in rabbit against specific polypeptides of rice IRX10 (385 to 417 aa) and XOAT6 (100 to 200 aa), respectively. Horseradish-peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Dingguo) was applied as secondary antibodies at a 1:5,000 dilution. The fractions containing IRX10 were combined and purified using a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) at 0.5 mL/min in column buffer (150 m m NaCl, 50 m m Tris-HCl at pH 7.5, 1 m m EDTA, 1% DDM). Thyroglobulin (Mr 669,000), ferritin (Mr 440,000), aldolase (Mr 158,000), conalbumin (Mr 75,000), and ovalbumin (Mr 44,000) were used as molecular mass markers. Eighteen fractions were collected, and fractions 2 to 15 were analyzed by western blotting using anti-IRX10 and anti-XOAT6 antibodies. These examined fractions were concentrated and subjected to SDS-PAGE. The gel strips containing proteins ranging from 15 to 130 kDa were excised and digested with trypsin. The resulting peptide mixtures were separated on a reverse-phase C18 column and detected with a linear ion trap mass spectrometer (nanoLC-Q EXACTIVE, Thermo). The mass spectra data were analyzed using the SEQUEST HT search engine in Thermo Proteome Discoverer (v1.4.0.288), using protein sequences from the IRGSP 1.0 genome assembly retrieved from the rice annotation project database (RAP-DB, https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/ ). We replicated this experiment for 3 times.

Protein interaction and localization analyses

The coding region of XOAT6 (Os03g18140), IRX10 (Os01g70200), and BC10 (Os05g07790) were amplified and in-frame fused with the modified pCAMBIA1300-NLuc and pCAMBIA1300-CLuc vectors containing split firefly luciferase ( Supplementary Table S4 ). The resulting constructs in Agrobaterium tumefaciens (strain EHA105) cells (OD 600 = 0.5) were pairwise infected the leaves of 4-wk-old N. benthamiana along with the RNAi inhibitor P19 (OD 600 = 1.0). After cultivating the infected plants in a greenhouse for 40 to 48 h, 1 m m D-luciferin was sprayed onto the infiltrated leaves and then kept them in the dark to decay chlorophyll luminescence. The interaction was determined by measuring the bioluminescence signal intensity using IndiGO software (Berthold). The experiment was repeated for 3 times.

For Co-IP assay, the young internodes of the previously reported transgenic plants that express mNeonGreen (mNG) fused IRX10 in irx10-1 background ( Wang et al. 2022a ) were collected and subjected to isolation of the total membrane proteins in the extraction buffer (50 m m Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 m m NaCl, 2 m m EDTA, 0.1% Triton X-100, 0.2% NP-40, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail). After solubilized using 1% DDM, the protein samples were incubated with 30 μ L of mNeonGreen-Trap Agarose at 4 °C for 2 h. The co-precipitated proteins were eluted and separated by SDS-PAGE. Immunoblotting analysis was performed using anti-mNG primary antibodies (Abcam) and goat anti-rabbit HRP-linked secondary antibodies (Dingguo). Rice CESA9 and BiP were used as negative controls. The experiment was repeated for 3 times.

For blue native gel electrophoresis and blotting, 3.5 to 15% gradient gels containing 250 m m 6-aminohexanoic acid and 0.1% (w/v) DDM were freshly prepared as previously described ( Atanassov et al. 2009 ). Total microsomal membrane proteins were extracted as described above and solubilized using 1% DDM. The solubilized membrane proteins were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min. NativePAGE 5% G-250 Sample Additive (Invitrogen) was added to obtain a final G-250 concentration of 0.1%. Approximately 80 μ g of solubilized membrane proteins were loaded and separated at low voltage (150 V) for 1 h, followed by high voltage (250 V) until the dye front ran off the bottom of the gel. The separated proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane and subjected to immunoblotting analysis as described above. Additionally, anti-CESA8 antibodies generated in rabbit against a specific polypeptide of rice CESA8 (Os07g10770, 76 to 161 aa) were used as a control. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using a cold CCD device after developing with SuperSignal West substrate mixture (Thermo). The experiment was repeated for at least 3 times.

The similarity of sequences was aligned and displayed using ClustalW ( https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalw2 ) and ESPript3.0 ( https://espript.ibcp.fr/ ). AlphaFold model of XOAT6 (AF- Q10MX2 ) retrieved from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database ( https://alphafold.com/ ) and ESK1 (6CCI) obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) were aligned using the PDB pairwise alignment tool ( https://www.rcsb.org/alignment ). The Z -scores were calculated using DALI-Server ( http://ekhidna.biocenter.helsinki.fi/dali/ ). The superimposed models were visualized using UCSF ChimeraX ( https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimerax ).

For the profiling analysis, the PSM of 1,270 proteins (PSM ≥ 7 in at least 1 fraction) detected in individual fractions by mass spectrometry were subjected to cluster analysis using the STEM (Short Time-series Expression Miner) program (version 1.3.13) with the parameters of 30 maximum model profiles and 3 maximum unit changes. Profile significance was evaluated using Fisher's exact test and Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. AgriGO ( http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/agriGOv2/ ) was applied for GO analysis.

Gene expression

The leaf sheathes and leaves were harvested from 2-wk-old wild-type seedlings. The 9-cm internodes were collected at the booting stage and were evenly cut into 9 segments. The plant samples were subjected to extraction of total RNA using Plant RNA Reagent (Invitrogen). Then, approximately 2 µ g of the total RNA were treated with DNase I (TaKaRa) and used to generate cDNA using PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa). RT-qPCR was performed on a cycler apparatus (Bio-Rad CFX96) using FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (Roche). Rice TP1 (Os01g05490) encoding triosephosphate isomerase was used as an internal control.

For examining the cellular expression pattern, the young internodes were embedded in a mixture of polyethylene glycol 400 and 1-hexadecanol (9:1, w/v) and sectioned in 15- μ m thickness. Laser capture microdissection was performed on a laser microdissection system (Leica, LMD 7000). The samples were subjected to total RNA isolation using RNeasy micro kit (Qiagen). RT-qPCR analysis was performed as described above. The primers for gene expression analysis are listed in Supplementary Table S4 . Three biological replicates were included.

Cell wall composition analyses

Approximately 20 mature second internodes were harvested from the mutant and wild-type plants and ground into powder. The cell wall residues were sieved through a 0.15-mm mesh and sequentially extracted with 70% (v/v) ethanol, chloroform:methanol (1:1), and acetone to obtain alcohol-insoluble cell wall residues (AIRs). The AIRs were further treated with α-amylase (Sigma) in 0.1 m sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0) overnight to remove starch. To determine the content of acetyl esters, 1 mg of destarched AIRs were saponified by incubating with 100 μ L of 1 m sodium hydroxide at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by neutralization with 100 μ L of 1 m hydrogen chloride. The released acetic acids were quantified immediately using an acetic acid assay kit (Megazyme). For sugar composition analysis, 2 mg of destarched AIRs were hydrolyzed by 250 μ L of 2 m trifluoroacetic acid at 121 °C for 90 min. Approximately 200 μ L of supernatants were transferred to new tubes and acetylated to alditol acetate derivates after reduction reaction as described previously ( Zhang and Zhou 2017 ). The resulting derivates were analyzed by gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GC-MS, Agilent) to determine monosaccharide content. The remaining were hydrolyzed using Updegraff reagent (acetic acid:nitric acid:water, 8:1:2 v/v/v) at 100 °C for 30 min. The crystalline cellulose content was determined by the anthrone method.

To isolate acetyl-xylans, approximately 50 mg of destarched AIRs from wild type and mutants were incubated in 1% ammonium oxalate and then in 11% peracetic acid solution to remove pectin and lignin. The resulting pellets were subjected to DMSO extraction at 70 °C overnight twice. The extracted acetyl-xylans were precipitated with 5 volumes of ethanol/methanol/water solution (7:2:1, pH 3.0) for 3 d. For HSQC analysis, 20 mg of acetyl-xylans of wild type and mutants were dissolved in 0.6 mL deuterated DMSO- d6 (99.9%, Sigma). The standard pulse sequence gHSQCAD was used to determine one-bond 13 C– 1 H correlations in samples. 1 H– 13 C HSQC spectra were collected using a spectrum width of 10 ppm in F2 ( 1 H) dimension and 200 ppm in F1 ( 13 C) dimension. The 2,048 × 512 (F2 × F1) complex data points were collected with a receiver gain set to 30; 64 scans per FID were accumulated with an interscan delay (d1) of 1 s. The DP of xylan in each sample was determined by dividing the sum of abundances of total xylosyl residues by that of xylosyl residues at reducing ends.

To determine the molecular size of acetyl-xylans, the extracted acetyl-xylans from wild type and the mutants were analyzed using UPLC-FLR (Waters ACQUITY) after labeling with procainamide in a 1:20 Molar ratio at 37 °C overnight. After dissolving in ammonium formate buffer (50 m m in 20% ACN), labeled xylans were separated on a column ACQUITY APC AQ 200 Å (Waters) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and detected at excitation and emission wavelengths of 310 and 370 nm. The system was calibrated using dextran standards (Sigma-Aldrich) with molecular weights of 5, 12, 50, 80, 150, and 401 kDa. This experiment was repeated for 3 times.

Purification of recombinant proteins

The coding sequence of XOAT6 without the transmembrane domain (55 to 559 aa) and that of IRX10 without the transmembrane domain (73 to 1,251 aa) were fused with 6 × histidine tag and cloned into the pPICZα-C vector (Invitrogen) ( Supplementary Table S4 ). In addition, we performed site-directed mutagenesis on XOAT6 at the 3 conserved amino acids (S278A, D528A, and H531A). The resulting constructs were introduced into Pichia pastoris X-33 cells by electroporation following the manufacturer's instructions. The high-yield clones were screened in the culture medium (1% yeast extract [w/v], 2% peptone [w/v], 100 m m potassium phosphate, pH 6.0, 1.34% yeast nitrogen base without amino acids [w/v], 0.4 µ g/mL biotin, and 0.5% methanol [v/v]). Methanol was added to a final concentration of 0.5% (v/v) every 24 h to induce the proteins. After 4 d of induction, the recombinant proteins were purified using a HisTrap crude FF column pre-equilibrated with the binding buffer (20 m m sodium phosphate, pH 7.4, 0.5 m NaCl, and 20 m m imidazole) and eluted with a gradient of imidazole (0 to 500 m m ) using an ÄKTA FPLC system (GE Healthcare). The recombinant proteins were confirmed by probing with the anti-His antibody (Sigma-Aldrich), and then desalted using a PD-10 column (GE Healthcare) into sodium phosphate buffer (50 m m , pH 6.8). The purified recombinant proteins were concentrated using 10 kDa MWCO Amicon Ultra filter (Millipore) before use.

Enzymatic activity assays

The purified recombinant proteins of XOAT6 (∼4.8 μ m ) were incubated with 0.1 m m of xylooligosaccharides acceptor substrates (X3 to X8) in sodium phosphate buffer (50 m m , pH 6.8) containing 200 μ m acetyl-CoA (Sigma) at 25 °C for 16 h or varied time. For the MALDI-TOF MS assay, the reaction products were mixed with a matrix solution (25 mg/mL 2,5-dihydroxbenzoic acid) and spotted on the target plate with 0.5 μ L of 5-chloro-2-mercaptobenothiazole (10 mg/mL). The mass spectra were scanned within m/z range of 200 to 5,000 in the reflection positive ion mode (laser intensity 95%, ion source 1 voltage 20.00 kV, ion source 2 voltage 17.75 kV, lens voltage 7.6 kV, detector voltage 2,127 V, and pulsed ion extraction 180 ns). Each spectrum corresponded to an ion accumulation of 2000 to 5,000 laser shots randomly distributed on the spot. The spectra were processed using FlexAnalysis v.3.4 software (Bruker Daltonics) to quantify oligomer abundance according to the peak heights. Cellulohexaose was included as an internal standard for quantification. To determine the kinetics of XOAT6, 3 µ g purified proteins were incubated with a gradient amount of X6 in the presence of 1 m m acetyl-CoA for 16 h. The reaction was stopped and applied to a MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer to quantify the acetyl residues on X6. The steady-state parameters K m were determined by Michaelis–Menten plots using nonlinear curve-fitting in Origin 9.1 (OriginLab). Three biological replicates were included in this experiment.

To determine the interaction effect between IRX10 and XOAT6, approximately 6 μ m recombinant proteins of IRX10 alone or combined with XOAT6 (∼1.5 μ m ) were incubated with 0.5 m m of UDP-xylose (CarboSource Services), 1 m m of acetyl-CoA (Sigma), and 0.1 m m of X5 (Megazyme) in 15 µ L reaction buffer (50 m m of sodium phosphate, pH 6.8, 1 m m of MgCl 2 ) at 25 °C for varied time intervals. The reactions were stopped by flash-freezing in liquid nitrogen. Meanwhile, aliquots of the reaction products were deacetylated using 1 m of NaOH containing 0.2 m of NaCNBH 3 at 37 °C for 2 h and then neutralized with 1 m of HCl. For polysaccharide analysis by carbohydrate gel electrophoresis (PACE), the reaction products and the deacetylated aliquotes were derivatized by the freshly prepared 0.1 m of 8-aminonaphthalene-1,3,6-trisulfonic acid (ANTS) in 7.5% acetic acid containing 0.5 m of sodium cyanoborohydride at 25 °C overnight. The resulting derivatives were suspended in 6 m urea and separated on 20% polyacrylamide gel at 200 V for 0.5 h and at 1,000 V for 1.5 h. Images were taken using a transilluminator with short wavelength UV by Image Lab software (Version 4.1 build 16), employing UV and short-pass emission filters. MALDI-TOF MS analysis of the reaction products was performed as described above. The data were analyzed using FlexAnalysis v.3.4 software (Bruker Daltonics). These experiments were repeated for at least 3 times.

To examine the acetyltransferase activity of XOAT6 variants and XOAT13, approximately 4.8 μ m of wild-type XOAT6/XOAT6 variants or XOAT13 were incubated with 0.1 m m of X5 in sodium phosphate buffer (50 m m , pH 6.8) containing 200 μ m of acetyl-CoA (Sigma) at 25 °C for 24 h or the indicated time, respectively. The reaction products were analyzed by MALDI-TOF MS as described above. To investigate the promotion effects of XOAT6 variants and XOAT13, 6 μ m of IRX10 recombinant proteins alone or combined with 1.5 μ m of wild-type XOAT6 or XOAT6 variants were incubated with 0.5 m m of UDP-xylose, 1 m m of acetyl-CoA (Sigma), and 0.1 m m of X5 (Megazyme) in 15 µ L reaction buffer (50 m m of sodium phosphate, pH 6.8, 1 m m MgCl 2 ) at 25 °C for 5 h. The reaction products were detected by MALDI-TOF MS as described above. The relative elongation activity was defined as the average ratio of extended xylosyl residues to the total original X5 substrate supplied, based on the peak heights measured from the MALDI-TOF spectra. We repeated this experiment for at least 3 times.

MST and FCS assays

To perform MST assays, the purified recombinant IRX10 proteins (10 μ m ) were labeled using a dye NT-647-NHS according to the manufacturer's operation manual (NanoTemper) and suspended in the assay buffer (50 m m sodium phosphate, pH 6.8, 150 m m NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20). Ten microliters of IRX10 were mixed with 10 μ L of XOAT6 or XOAT6 variants with gradient concentrations in the assay buffer. The binding affinity was measured using an MST instrument MO Control Software (V1.6.1) (NanoTemper) at 80% MST power and 20% LED power. The dissociation constant (K D ) value was calculated using Mo Affinity Analysis (V2.3) based on the fit curve. This experiment included 3 biological replicates.

To determine the hydrodynamic radius, xylobiose (X2) was labeled by Alexa Fluor 488 Hydrazide (AF488) using sodium borocyanate as a catalyst and purified by Hypersep Tip Hypercarb (Thermo-Scientific). IRX10 and/or XOAT6 proteins (2 μ m ) were added in 50 μ L PBS buffer (50 m m , pH 6.8) including 20 n m of AF488-xylobiose and 5 μ m of acetyl-CoA with or without 2 μ m of UDP-xylose. After reacting for 15 min, the reactions were placed onto a microscopic high-precision glass cover slip (Menzel-Gläser) and were observed using a CorTector SX100 fluorescence correlation spectrometer (LightEdge) equipped with Olympus water immersion lens (C-Apochromat 40X/1.2NA). Fluorescence auto-correlation data were collected every 5-s interval for 100 times. AF488 fluorescence was excited using a 488 nm laser and detected using a T647lpxr dichroic mirror and an ET525/50 m filter (Chroma). A 10-nM AF488 solution was used as the standard fluorophore to calibrate the confocal optical pathway that produces a typical confocal fluorescence excitation with a detection volume (<1 femtoliter). Hydrodynamic radius was analyzed using the Correlation Analysis software (LightEdge) based on a mathematic model incorporating a single diffusion component and a fluorophore's singlet–triplet photophysical dynamics ( Wang et al. 2023 ).

Microscopy

To investigate the subcellular localization of XOAT6, agrobacteria harboring the XOAT6-GFP construct were infiltrated into 4-wk-old N. benthamiana leaves along with a mCherry-tagged Golgi marker (Man49-mCherry). Fluorescence signals were observed using an Airyscan2 confocal microscope (LSM 980, Carl Zeiss). GFP fluorescence was detected with 488 nm excitation and 505 to 550 nm for emission collection, while mCherry fluorescence was detected with 561 nm excitation and 600 to 650 nm for emission collection. The degree of colocalization was quantified in 10 leaf cells using the Pearson correlation coefficient and Mander's coefficient.

To observe the secondary wall thickness of fiber cells, the mature 2nd internodes of wild type, the xoat6 and irx10 single mutants, and the double mutant were cross-sectioned with razor blades. After dehydrating in a gradient of ethanol and critical point drying, the samples were sprayed with gold particles. The images of fiber cells were taken using a scanning electron microscope (S-3000N, Hitachi). Wall thickness was measured based on the images of 100 fiber cells from 3 individual plants.

To display the deposition pattern of cellulosic microfibrils, the matured 2nd internodes of wild type, the xoat6 and irx10 single mutants, and the double mutant were longitudinally sectioned and treated in 2.5% peracetic acid solution at 80 °C for 12 h. The samples were sprayed with gold and then examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (Crossbeam 340, Zeiss). The cellulosic microfibrils at the incisions of the fiber cells were measured using the software ImageJ ( https://imagej.net/ ) with the plugin shape index map and then converted to “mask”. The cellulosic nanofiber segments were defined as “snakes” using 1 pixel of snake point space and 20 pixels of minimum snake length using SOAX software. The orientation histogram was calculated based on that of snakes after cutting at the junctions. More than 18,000 snakes from the images of 3 fiber cells of 3 individual plants were analyzed, showing as frequency percentage in a histogram. The width of cellulosic nanofibers in fiber cell walls was measured using at least 100 nanofibers obtained from 3 individual fiber cells.

ssNMR analysis

The wild type and bc17-2 seedlings were hydroponically cultivated with Kimura B nutrition solution in a customized plant growth chamber, and supplied with 13 CO 2 starting from before the emergence of the fourth leaf and continuing until the fifth leaf reached full development. The fourth leaf sheaths were collected by cutting into 2 to 4 cm pieces and stored at −80 °C. To determine the 13 C incorporation rate, around 1 mg of AIR prepared from the harvested leaf sheaths was digested with xylanase M6 in 50 m m ammonium acetate at 37 °C for 16 h. The released oligosaccharides were further derivatized by reductive amination with Biotin-Gly-Arg (BA), and then subjected to MALDI-TOF MS analysis as described above. The 13 C incorporation of the wild type and bc17-2 was 74% and 72%, respectively.

For magic angle spinning ssNMR examination, the wild type and bc17-2 sheath tissues were analyzed as reported previously ( Kang et al. 2019 ). The never-dried 13 C-enriched sheaths (∼30 mg) were sliced into small pieces and packed into a 3.2-mm NMR rotor. Solid-state experiments were conducted on a Bruker Neo 800 MHz spectrometer with a 1 H/ 13 C/ 15 N E-free probe under 16 kHz MAS frequency at 295 K. 13 C chemical shifts were externally referenced to adamantane CH 2 signal at 38.48 ppm on the tetramethylsilane (TMS) scale. The refocused INADEQUATE sequence was performed with cross polarization and direct polarization to distinguish components with different rigidity. Typical radiofrequency field strengths were 50 to 83.3 kHz for 1 H decoupling and hard pulse and 62.5 kHz for 13 C. 2D INADEQUATE experiments were measured with a recycle delay of 2 s and a total of 96 transients. The acquisition time for indirect dimension (F1) was set as 4.6 and 5.3 ms for cross and direct polarization spectra, respectively.

Biomechanics analysis

Breaking force was examined using the mature 2nd internodes of the wild type, bc17 and bc18 single mutants, and the double mutant. The internode segments with equal length were applied for breaking force tests using a digital dynamometer (NK-200). For nanoindentation analysis, the internode segments were treated in 2.5% peracetic acid solution to isolate the individual fiber cells by micromanipulation. After drying by lyophilization, the fiber cells were placed under a Nanowizard 3 AFM with PeakForce Quantitative Nanomechanical Mapping (JPK). RTESPA-525 probe with triangular pyramid shape (Bruker) was used to carry out indentation examination. The tip can bear a resonant frequency of ∼2,048 Hz and a spring constant of ∼200 N m −1 . AFM tip was calibrated by engaging the tip onto a clean glass slide in single ramp mode before each indentation experiment. Deflection sensitivity was automatically recorded and calculated using JPK Data Processing software (version 7.097, Bruker). Average deflection sensitivity was used from at least 120 ramp curves. At least 5 fiber cells were measured.

Saccharification assay

The AIRs with similar particle size ( Supplementary Fig. S8D ) were collected after filtering through a 100-mesh sieve and subjected to destarching treatment as described above. One milligram of the destarched AIRs was suspended in water and boiled for 1 h to inactive endogenous enzymes. After cooling, the digestion was carried out with an enzyme mixture containing 5 μ L of Ctec2 enzyme (Novozymes) in 50 m m of citrate buffer (pH 6.2) at 37 °C for 48 h. The released sugars were then reduced and acetylated, followed by analyzed using GC-MS as described above. Three biological replicates were included in this experiment.

To evaluate the cellulose accessibility using modified Simon's staining, 2 mg destarched AIRs were incubated with increasing amounts of DO 15 (Pontamine Fast Orange 6RN) dyes (0.1 to 1.00 mg/mL) and Direct Blue 1 in phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) at 70 °C for 6 h ( Biswal et al. 2018 ). The supernatants were measured at 599 nm using a Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer EnSpire). The maximum concentration of adsorbed dye (Γmax, mg/g substrate) and Langmuir constant ( K , mL/mg) were calculated by nonlinear fitting to the Langmuir adsorption model. Distribution coefficient R (mL/g) was calculated as a product of Γmax and K . These experiments included 3 biological replicates.

For in situ examination of cellulose accessibility, the internode cross sections were prepared from at least 3 individual plants of the wild type and mutants using Vibratome (VT1000S, Leica). Cellulase RS (Yakult) was labeled with the Alexa Fluor 488 protein labeling kit (A10235, Invitrogen) accordingly ( Guo et al. 2023 ). The labeled cellulase was incubated with the internode sections for 30 min. After adequately rinsing, the samples were imaged by high-resolution fluorescence confocal microscopy (LSM 980, ZEISS). Cellulase-Alexa Fluor 488 was excited by a 488 nm laser and collected with a 500 to 550 nm filter. The fluorescence images were analyzed using ZEN 2.0.

Statistical analysis

Average phenotypes and measurements were compared between groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc tests at a significance level of 0.05 performed in GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. All two-tailed Welch's unpaired t -tests were conducted in Excel. The results of all ANOVAs are provided in Supplementary Data Set 2 .
