CHAMP1 (chromosome alignment-maintaining phosphoprotein 1) plays a role in the
repair of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) by homologous recombination (HR). The
CHAMP1 gene is one of the genes mutated in individuals with intellectual
disability. The majority of the mutations are premature termination codon (PTC)
mutations, while missense mutations have also been reported. How these mutations
affect the functions of CHAMP1 has not been clarified yet. Here we investigated
the effects of the CHAMP1 mutations on HR. In Epstein-Barr virus-induced
lymphoblastoid cells and fibroblasts derived from individuals with CHAMP1 PTC
mutations, truncated CHAMP1 proteins of the expected sizes were detected. When
DSBs were induced in fibroblasts with PTC mutations, a defect in HR was
detected. U2OS cells expressing the CHAMP1 mutants did not show an HR defect in
the presence of endogenous wild-type (WT) CHAMP1, whereas they were unable to
restore HR activity when CHAMP1 WT was depleted, suggesting that the PTC
mutations are loss-of-function mutations. On the other hand, the CHAMP1 mutants
with missense mutations restored HR activity when CHAMP1 WT was depleted. In
DLD-1 cells, heterozygous depletion of CHAMP1 resulted in an HR defect,
indicating haploinsufficiency. These results suggest that CHAMP1 PTC mutations
cause an HR defect through a haploinsufficient mechanism, while CHAMP1 missense
mutations do not affect the HR function of CHAMP1.

Introduction

DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) are among the most severe forms of DNA damage that can result in cell death or genomic alterations. There are two main pathways for repairing DSBs: non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) and homologous recombination (HR)1,2. The NHEJ pathway directly ligates broken DNA ends without sequence homology, generating insertions and deletions. On the other hand, HR uses a sister chromatid with a homologous sequence as a template to repair the broken DNA, achieving high-fidelity repair. The choice between these two DSB repair pathways is tightly regulated. Since HR requires a sister chromatid to be used as a template, it can only occur in the S/G2 phases of the cell cycle, while NHEJ can function throughout the cell cycle. Previous research has revealed that the pathway choice to HR is determined by a process called 5’ DNA end resection3,4. The CtIP (CtBP-interacting protein) protein initiates this process to produce 3’ protruding single stranded DNA (ssDNA)5–7. This ssDNA is then bound by replication protein A (RPA) for stabilization. RAD51 then replaces phosphorylated RPA, facilitating the invasion of sister chromatids with sequence homology and enabling accurate DNA repair through HR5. The pathway choice to NHEJ repair is facilitated by a protein called 53BP18,9. 53BP1 antagonizes the HR repair pathway by inhibiting DNA end resection, allowing for NHEJ to proceed. On the other hand, the BRCA1 protein antagonizes the accumulation of 53BP1 at DSB sites, thereby promoting HR repair10,11.

CHAMP1 (chromosome alignment-maintaining phosphoprotein 1; CAMP, C13orf8, Znf828) is a protein involved in the HR repair of DSBs. It was originally identified as a binding partner of REV7 (Mad2L2)12,13, and was found to play a role in the maintenance of kinetochore-microtubule attachment14, which is dependent on mitotic phosphorylation12. CHAMP1 was also reported to play a role in cell survival during mitotic arrest through the regulation of anti-apoptotic proteins15. Recent reports revealed an additional role for CHAMP1 during interphase, where it promotes the HR repair pathway16,17. We reported that CHAMP1 promotes HR repair by forming a complex with POGZ (POGO transposable element with ZNF domain) and HP1 (Heterochromatin protein 1)16. POGZ and HP1 interact with each other to retain BRCA1 at DSB sites, allowing for the initiation of DNA end resection and the promotion of HR repair18,19. CHAMP1 promotes HR together with POGZ, and the C-terminus of CHAMP1 is necessary and sufficient for this HR-promoting activity16. In another report, it was proposed that CHAMP1 plays a role in HR by sequestering REV7 from the Shieldin complex17, which functions in suppressing DNA end resection downstream of 53BP1, thereby inhibiting HR and promoting NHEJ20–24. The interaction of CHAMP1 with REV7 through the WK motif of CHAMP1 is supposed to hinder the formation of the Shieldin complex and its ability to inhibit DNA end resection17.

Both CHAMP1 and POGZ are genes that are mutated in individuals with intellectual disability (ID)25–31. Disorders associated with CHAMP1 and POGZ mutations also include phenotypes of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), collectively called neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs)30,32,33. In the Mendelian Inheritance in Man (MIM) database, disorders associated with CHAMP1 mutations are described as “Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia, impaired language, and dysmorphic features (NEDHILD; MIM number 616579)”, while disorders associated with POGZ mutations are described as “White-Sutton syndrome (WHSUS; MIM number 616364)”. Studies using mouse models in which CHAMP1 and POGZ were modified showed that these genes are involved in neuronal development34–36. Pathogenic variants in CHAMP1 and POGZ are predominantly categorized as heterozygous premature termination codon (PTC) mutations including nonsense and frameshift mutations, resulting in the production of truncated proteins lacking the C-terminal region25–31. Individuals harboring these mutations commonly exhibit severe ID and significant developmental delay. Although the number of reported cases is limited, individuals with heterozygous missense mutations in the N-terminal C2H2 zinc-finger domains of CHAMP1 have also been reported37,38. These individuals also exhibit severe ID and developmental delays. In addition, they exhibit an epilepsy phenotype. Interestingly, individuals with heterozygous CHAMP1 whole gene deletions exhibit a milder developmental and adaptive deficiency compared to those with the PTC and missense mutations32,38–40. These data suggest that CHAMP1 PTC mutations may cause a more severe phenotype than CHAMP1 whole gene deletions by affecting the functions of CHAMP1 WT through a dominant negative mechanism32,38. CHAMP1 missense mutations may give rise to an additional phenotype by acting as gain-of-function mutations38.

Malfunctions in the DNA repair machinery are linked to various disorders, including neurodegeneration41,42. Notably, deficiencies in DNA DSB repair pathways have been suggested to cause adverse effects on neural development and neurogenesis43,44. The depletion of crucial proteins involved in HR repair has been demonstrated to lead to neural disorders such as microcephaly45. This implies that defective DSB repair through HR may cause ID. However, the relationship between the etiology of ID in individuals with CHAMP1 mutations and the effect of these mutations on HR function has not yet been explored. Here, we uncovered HR deficiency associated with various heterozygous CHAMP1 mutations that were identified in individuals with ID. Notably, the cells derived from individuals with CHAMP1 PTC mutations showed an HR defect. However, when the CHAMP1 PTC mutants were exogenously expressed in U2OS cells, they did not disrupt the functions of CHAMP1 WT in HR, while they could not restore HR activity when CHAMP1 WT was depleted, suggesting that they are loss-of-function mutants. On the other hand, the CHAMP1 missense mutants restored HR activity in cells depleted of CHAMP1 WT. In DLD-1 cells, heterozygous depletion of CHAMP1 resulted in an HR defect, indicating haploinsufficiency. These data suggest that CHAMP1 PTC mutations are loss-of-function mutations and cause an HR defect through haploinsufficiency, while CHAMP1 missense mutations are functional in HR.

We next investigated whether the CHAMP1 mutants were expressed in cells derived from individuals with the CHAMP1 PTC mutations. Cells derived from individuals with the CHAMP1 PTC mutations exhibit HR deficiencyWe then investigated whether these patient-derived cells with the heterozygous CHAMP1 PTC mutations are defective in HR repair. We recently reported that CHAMP1 depletion causes an HR defect, as evidenced by an increase in sensitivity to genotoxic drugs that induce DSBs, including camptothecin (CPT), a DNA topoisomerase I inhibitor that produces single-strand breaks (SSBs) that result in DSBs when encountering the DNA replication machinery16. We also found that CHAMP1 depletion causes a reduction in the foci formation of phosphorylated RPA2 (pRPA2), the second subunit of RPA, which localizes to ssDNA produced by DNA end resection. In the present study, we observed the formation of pRPA2 foci after CPT treatment as a measure of HR activity. The number of pRPA2 foci was quantified in patient-derived fibroblasts using immunofluorescence staining after DSB induction by CPT treatment. Cyclin A was also co-stained to limit our analysis to cells in the S/G2 phase of the cell cycle, when HR repair occurs. In fibroblasts derived from individuals with heterozygous Ser181Cysfs*5 and Arg497* mutations of CHAMP1, there was a significant decrease in the number of pRPA2 foci (Fig. 3A, B). We also investigated whether the foci formation of 53BP1, which antagonizes HR repair by inhibiting DNA end resection, was affected in these cells. There was a significant increase in 53BP1 foci in cells with the CHAMP1 mutations compared to those in cells from healthy individuals (Fig. 3C, D), presumably reflecting inefficient HR repair. These data suggest that cells with the heterozygous CHAMP1 PTC mutations have reduced HR repair activity.

Establishment of U2OS cell lines expressing the CHAMP1 mutants with PTC or missense mutationsIn studies using fibroblasts derived from healthy individuals or individuals with CHAMP1 mutations, we noticed that the number of pRPA2 and 53BP1 foci varied considerably even among cells from healthy individuals (Fig. 3B, D), which likely stems from the inherent heterogeneity between individuals. Various factors, including age and genetic background, may influence the DSB repair independently of CHAMP1 mutations. To specifically investigate the effect of CHAMP1 mutations under a condition without these heterogeneities, we established cell lines with stable expression of the pathogenic CHAMP1 mutants using U2OS cells, an osteosarcoma-derived cell line commonly used for DNA repair studies including those on CHAMP1 16,17. Since these U2OS cell lines simultaneously express endogenous CHAMP1 WT as well as the CHAMP1 mutants, they closely mimic the conditions found in cells from individuals with heterozygous CHAMP1 mutations. Three constructs encoding the CHAMP1 PTC mutants (Ser181Cysfs*5, Arg497*, and Gln649*)25–27 as N-terminal EGFP-fused proteins were retrovirally transfected into U2OS cells, and stable transformants were selected. In addition to these CHAMP1 PTC mutants, U2OS cell lines stably expressing two reported pathogenic missense mutations at the N-terminal zinc-finger domain (Gly23Ser, Cys16Arg)37,38 as well as CHAMP1 WT as a control were also established. To observe the HR activity of these CHAMP1 mutants in the absence of endogenous CHAMP1 WT, these constructs were designed to be resistant to RNAi for endogenous CHAMP1.The expression of each EGFP-CHAMP1 construct was confirmed by western blotting analysis using an antibody that recognizes the N-terminal region of CHAMP1 (Fig. 4A). The expression levels varied between the mutants but were comparable to or greater than the expression level of endogenous CHAMP1 WT. The subcellular localization of each EGFP-CHAMP1 construct was observed by fluorescence microscopy. CHAMP1 WT was found to localize to the nucleus, as previously reported26 (Fig. 4B). The N-terminal missense mutants of CHAMP1 (Gly23Ser, Cys16Arg) also localized to the nucleus, similar to CHAMP1 WT. For the PTC mutants of CHAMP1, the longer mutants (Gln649*) localized to the nucleus, whereas the shorter mutants (Ser181Cysfs*5 and Arg497*) did not. This is probably due to the loss of a putative nuclear localization signal (KKLKK) located at aa 617–621 in the shorter mutants.

DiscussionIn this study, we investigated the effects of CHAMP1 mutations found in individuals with ID on the HR activity of CHAMP1. We first observed cells originating from individuals with heterozygous CHAMP1 PTC mutations. We found that the truncated CHAMP1 mutants were present in the cell lysate while the CHAMP1 WT levels were approximately halved. Fibroblasts with the PTC mutations showed reduced pRPA2 and increased 53BP1 foci upon DSB induction, suggesting that HR repair was impaired. We then addressed the possibility that the CHAMP1 PTC mutants disrupt the HR function of the remaining CHAMP1 WT in a dominant negative manner. U2OS cells stably expressing the CHAMP1 PTC mutants did not exhibit defective HR repair, ruling out the possibility of dominant negative effects. When endogenous CHAMP1 WT was depleted in these cells, the PTC mutants failed to rescue the HR function of CHAMP1 WT, demonstrating that they are loss-of-function mutations. Using DLD-1 cells in which CHAMP1 expression is controlled by the auxin-inducible degron system, we found that heterozygous CHAMP1 depletion disrupted HR activity, indicating haploinsufficiency. Taken together, these data suggest that haploinsufficiency is the cause of defective HR repair in cells derived from individuals with the heterozygous CHAMP1 PTC mutations. In contrast, the reported pathogenic heterozygous CHAMP1 N-terminal missense mutations were capable of HR repair in U2OS cells, although cells derived from individuals with these mutations were not available.We have shown that CHAMP1 PTC mutations are loss-of-function mutations with respect to HR repair. In this study, we investigated the effects of CHAMP1 mutations found in individuals with ID on the HR activity of CHAMP1. We first observed cells originating from individuals with heterozygous CHAMP1 PTC mutations. We found that the truncated CHAMP1 mutants were present in the cell lysate while the CHAMP1 WT levels were approximately halved. Fibroblasts with the PTC mutations showed reduced pRPA2 and increased 53BP1 foci upon DSB induction, suggesting that HR repair was impaired. We then addressed the possibility that the CHAMP1 PTC mutants disrupt the HR function of the remaining CHAMP1 WT in a dominant negative manner. U2OS cells stably expressing the CHAMP1 PTC mutants did not exhibit defective HR repair, ruling out the possibility of dominant negative effects. When endogenous CHAMP1 WT was depleted in these cells, the PTC mutants failed to rescue the HR function of CHAMP1 WT, demonstrating that they are loss-of-function mutations. Using DLD-1 cells in which CHAMP1 expression is controlled by the auxin-inducible degron system, we found that heterozygous CHAMP1 depletion disrupted HR activity, indicating haploinsufficiency. Taken together, these data suggest that haploinsufficiency is the cause of defective HR repair in cells derived from individuals with the heterozygous CHAMP1 PTC mutations. In contrast, the reported pathogenic heterozygous CHAMP1 N-terminal missense mutations were capable of HR repair in U2OS cells, although cells derived from individuals with these mutations were not available.

We have shown that CHAMP1 PTC mutations are loss-of-function mutations with respect to HR repair. Haploinsufficiency for HR repair was also reported in heterozygous Pogz knockout mice19 as well as in cells harboring heterozygous BRCA1 mutations49,50, suggesting that the expression level of wild-type proteins is critical for HR activity.

Interestingly, POGZ, a CHAMP1-binding protein, has been reported to bind to promoter and enhancer regions in the genome and to be involved in the transcriptional regulation of genes involved in neuronal development53, suggesting that CHAMP1 may share a similar function with POGZ. Statistical analysisFor comparisons between all groups showing a normal distribution, one-way ANOVA was used with the Tukey–Kramer post hoc test. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to verify the normality of the data distribution for each group before the one-way ANOVA. If the results of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test showed a nonnominal distribution, the significant differences between all groups were validated by the Kruskal–Wallis test, which was used with the Steel–Dwass post hoc test. For comparisons between the single group and multiple groups with a normal distribution, Dunnett’s post hoc test was used after one-way ANOVA. If the results of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test showed a nonnominal distribution, the significant differences between a single group and multiple groups were validated by a Steel post hoc test after the Kruskal–Wallis test. For comparisons between groups with time series, Bonferroni post hoc test was used after repeated measures ANOVA. All the statistical analyses were performed with EZR59, which is a graphical user interface for R (R Core Team, 2018). More precisely, it is a modified version of the R commander designed to add statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics. Samples for analysis in each dataset were acquired in the same experiment, and all samples were calculated at the same time for each dataset.
