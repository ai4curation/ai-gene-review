# Cytochrome b(5) enhances androgen synthesis by rapidly reducing the CYP17A1 oxy-complex in the lyase step.

Author information:

Cytochrome P450 17A1 (CYP17A1) catalyzes the synthesis of androgens from the
steroid precursors pregnenolone and progesterone in a two-step reaction process:
allylic hydroxylation and carbo-carbon bond scission. Cytochrome b5 (Cyt-b5 ) is
a stimulator of the second lyase reaction, but the chemical mechanism is
unclear. We have shown previously that this stimulatory effect requires redox
active Cyt-b5 . To investigate the origin of the lyase reaction enhancement by
electron transfer from Cyt-b5 , we measured the reduction rates of oxy-ferrous
substrate-bound CYP17A1 by Cyt-b5 and by cytochrome P450 reductase (CPR)
coincorporated in Nanodiscs using stopped flow spectroscopy. We observed that
Cyt-b5 reduces oxy-ferrous CYP17A1 10-fold faster than CPR, with the rate
similar to that observed in a ternary complex of all three proteins.

## Full Text

Cytochrome b5 (Cyt b5) is a small heme protein known to be involved in many reactions mediated by cytochromes P450 [1], and its effect can be stimulatory or inhibitory [2]. Despite decades of work, the role of Cyt b5 in steroid hormone synthesis is still not well understood. Human cytochrome P450 17A1 (CYP17A1) is a microsomal steroidogenic P450, produced in the gonads and adrenal cortical tissue. CYP17A1 catalyzes two sequential reactions, the hydroxylation of pregnenolone (PREG) or progesterone (PROG) at C-17, followed by scission of the C17–C20 bond of the 17a-hydroxy-PREG or 17a-hydroxy-PROG produced in the first step [3]. This latter lyase reaction leads to the formation of androgens, dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) and androstenedione (AD), respectively [3]. The hydroxylation and lyase reactions are fundamentally different with respect to chemical mechanism as well as the regulation of activity [4]. Cytochrome b5 is coexpressed in androgen producing tissues with its expression correlating with the need for androgens in the body [5,6]. Cytochrome b5 is known to enhance the CYP17A1 catalyzed lyase turnover 5- to 10-fold, but has little or no effect on the preceding hydroxylation reaction [3,7]. Whether this effect of Cyt b5 is due to conformational changes induced in CYP17A1, or because of accelerated electron transfer from Cyt b5 to the oxygenated CYP17A1, has been a subject of intense debate for many years [8–11]. We have shown previously that a redox inactive form of Cyt b5, where the heme has been replaced with a manganese protoporphyrin ring (Mn b5), forms a complex with CYP17A1, but does not enhance the rate of the lyase reaction [12]. This indicates that for Cyt b5-mediated lyase enhancement, an electron transfer interaction is crucial. The redox potentials of ferric form of P450 and Cyt b5 have been determined [13] and the relatively high (~0 mV vs. NHE) redox potential of Cyt b5 does not permit reduction of the ferric P450 protein (~−300 mV) [14]. Hence, the only feasible redox reaction is electron transfer from Cyt b5 to the ferrous-dioxygen species, which has a redox potential closer to 0 mV [2,15].

The canonical P450 reaction cycle, as shown in Fig. 1, begins at the resting ferric low spin heme, which upon binding its specific substrate shifts to the high spin state due to displacement of axial aqueous ligand of the heme iron. This complex is reduced by the redox partner cytochrome P450 reductase (CPR) to the ferrous state, which then binds molecular oxygen resulting in the formation of ferrous-dioxygen species (oxy-complex). The oxy-complex is a branch point between productive and unproductive routes in that it can either unproductively lose a superoxide ion and autoxidize back to the high spin ferric heme, or it can undergo another reduction by an associated redox partner protein to form the peroxo-ferric intermediate. It has been shown that CYP17A1-mediated carbon–carbon bond scission of 17-hydroxy-pregnenolone (OH-PREG), as well as 17-OH PROG [14,15], proceeds through a peroxoanion-mediated nucleophilic attack on the carbonyl group of the substrate, forming a peroxo-hemiketal intermediate [16]. This intermediate can then undergo homolytic or heterolytic cleavage of the O-O bond and rearrange to release the dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) product and a molecule of acetic acid. A recent NMR study revealed that CPR and Cyt b5 occupy partially overlapping binding sites on the CYP17A1 near the proximal heme site, and hence may compete for the redox partner role [17]. Thus, depending on relative concentrations CPR and b5 as well as the redox potentials of the ferric/ferrous couple and that of the oxycomplex, Cyt b5 can participate as an electron donor or modulator of the CYP17A1 structure.

Given our previous observation that the stimulatory effect on lyase activity by Cyt b5 requires a redox transfer event [12], and recent reports showing that Cyt b5 improves coupling efficiency in CYP17A1 lyase chemistry [18,19], we set out to compare the rates of reduction of ferrous-dioxygen species by Cyt b5 and by CPR. We also determined the effects of these redox partners on the autoxidation rate of CYP17A1. Because CYP17A1, as well as CPR and Cyt b5, are incorporated in the membrane, which plays an important role in defining their mutual orientation and interactions [7], we employed Nanodisc technology to reconstitute active complexes of these full-length proteins in a native-like membrane environment. In order to compare the rates of oxy-complex reduction with Cyt b5 and with CPR, we performed rapid mixing experiments of oxygen-saturated buffer with OH-PREG-bound ferrous CYP17A1 reconstituted in Nanodiscs together with reduced redox partners. Immediately after mixing, the visible spectrum was monitored using a photodiode array (PDA) detector over the course of 50–200 s. To investigate effects of b5 and CPR on the autoxidation rate of CYP17A1, we also measured the autoxidation rates of all the proteins separately. We observed that Cyt b5 reduced oxy-ferrous CYP17A1 ~50 times faster than the autoxidation rate of CYP17A1 and ~100 times faster than oxidation rate of Cyt b5. We also observed that the reduction rate of oxy-complex by Cyt b5 was ~10 times faster than by CPR, and comparable to that observed in a ternary complex of all three proteins in Nanodiscs. These results suggest that, in the presence of only CPR, owing to its slower rate of second electron transfer, the oxy-ferrous CYP17A1 autoxidizes to a greater extent as compared to a Cyt b5-CYP17A1 complex, where the faster reduction results in enhanced activity. This view of the Cyt b5 effect on CYP17A1 catalysis is consistent with earlier results obtained using rabbit P450 CYP2B4 by Waskell and coworkers, where they observed that Cyt b5-mediated reduction rate was ~100-fold faster than the rate of reduction by CPR [20].

This suggests that conformation and/or dynamic properties of CYP17A1 may be modulated by interactions with Cyt b5, which in turn can affect the proton transfer steps or orientation of the substrate with respect to catalytic intermediate.

Results and Discussion

In order to evaluate the effect of presence of Cyt b5 on CYP17A1 oxy-complex decay, we first determined the rate of unproductive autoxidation of OH-PREG-bound CYP17A1 in the absence of any redox partners. We identified a fast process (~20 s−1 at 25 °C) corresponding to formation of the ferrous-dioxygen complex, which was followed by a much slower process comprising the autoxidative decay of this intermediate back to ferric state with release of superoxide. The rate constant for CYP17A1 autoxidation was found to be 0.02 s−1 (Fig. 2A). The ferrous-to-ferric oxidation of reduced Cyt b5 was observed to be even slower, with a rate of 0.01 s−1 (Fig. 2B). However, when CYP17A1 was in complex with Cyt b5, an intermediate kinetic phase appeared between 0.5 to 5 s, which was fit to one exponential decay with the rate 0.9 s−1 (Fig. 3) followed by a slower phase with a decay constant of 0.044 s−1. About 20% of the mixture decayed in this intermediate phase, which constitutes disappearance of the CYP17A1 oxy-complex and formation of the ferric form of CYP17A1 and the simultaneous oxidation of reduced Cyt b5. The spectral signal observed for this process is well represented as a 1 : 1 combination of difference spectra corresponding to autoxidation of the oxy-complex in CYP17A1 and oxidation of Cyt b5. Both the spectral signal and the stark difference between the decay rate of oxy-complex by autoxidation at 0.02 s−1, and the decay to ferric form after reduction by Cyt b5 at a 45-fold faster rate, clearly indicate the direct electron transfer from ferrous b5 to the oxy-complex of CYP17A1.

The oxidation of fully reduced CPR alone in the Nanodisc membrane is a complex multistate process, with four kinetic processes corresponding to two-electron oxidations of FAD and FMN. A fast phase in the initial 500 ms with a decay constant of 6 s−1 was followed by an intermediate phase (1.5 s−1), a slow phase shown in Fig. 2C (0.08 s−1), and a very slow phase with a decay constant of less than 0.001 s−1. On the other hand, in the sample with a binary complex of CYP17A1 and CPR, three phases were identified (Fig. 4). The fast phase, attributed to the formation of the oxy-complex of CYP17A1, proceeded with a rate of 18 s−1 (not shown). An intermediate phase, with a decay constant of 0.09 s−1, and a slow phase with a decay constant of 0.01 s−1 are also observed. The fast phase of the first step in CPR oxidation is not resolved as a separate process due to a smaller spectral signal strongly overlapped with the Soret band of CYP17A1. Approximately 85% of the decay was observed to proceed in the slow phase. Comparing the binary complex of CYP17A1 with CPR to that with Cyt b5, it is evident that reduction of oxy-complex by Cyt b5 is at least ~10-fold faster than that by CPR.

Taking into account the recent observation that Cyt b5 and CPR occupy partially overlapping binding sites on CYP17A1 surface [17], we decided to investigate the reduction rate of the oxy-complex when both the redox partners are available to the P450. In this case, four kinetic processes were identified. The formation of the oxy-complex was complete in the first 500 ms, with the rate of 21 s−1, similar to that observed in the absence of redox partners. From 500 ms to 5 s, two intermediate phases were observed: with decay constants of 1.3 s−1 and 0.2 s−1 (Fig. 5). A final slow phase, with a decay constant of 0.025 s−1, was seen at longer times (5 s to 200 s). Comparing the kinetics in the binary and ternary complexes, the rates of disappearance of CYP17A1 oxy-complex in a ternary complex with Cyt b5-CPR are more comparable to that in CYP17A1-Cyt b5 complex (1.3 s−1 and 0.9 s−1). On the other hand, the decay rate in CYP17A1-CPR complex is at least 10-fold slower. Taken together, this indicates that Cyt b5 reduces the CYP17A1 oxy-complex approximately 10-fold faster than CPR, and, in a ternary complex of all three proteins at an equivalent molar ratio, this second reduction is carried out mostly by Cyt b5.

Our results demonstrate that oxygen binding by CYP17A1 saturated with 17OH-PREG is fast and does not depend on the presence of CPR or Cyt b5. Comparing the decay kinetics of the CYP17A1 oxy-complex reveals the presence of an intermediate phase when Cyt b5 is reconstituted with CYP17A1 in the Nanodisc membrane bilayer. This kinetic process, with a rate ~ 1 s−1 at 298 K, is assigned to the electron transfer from reduced Cyt b5 (i.e., the oxidation of Cyt b5) to the oxy-complex in CYP17A1 (the transition of the oxy-complex to the ferric form of CYP17A1) based on a clean spectral signal, corresponding to the linear combination of two processes. This process is significantly faster than the second electron transfer from CPR to the oxy-complex in CYP17A1. Interestingly, when both redox partners are reconstituted in Nanodiscs with CYP17A1, the faster kinetic process with a Cyt b5 spectral contribution is clearly observed, indicating more favorable interaction of the latter with CYP17A1, even in the presence of CPR. This preference of Cyt b5 as a redox partner in the second electron transfer step suggests a mechanism of NADPH supported catalytic cycling, where CPR separates from the CYP17A1 enzyme after the first electron transfer step, and is replaced by reduced Cyt b5, which serves as a second electron donor. Of course, Cyt b5 may also play a role of allosteric effector, modulating further catalytically important events, such as the protonation of peroxo- and hydroperoxo-ferric intermediates, or conformational alignment of the substrate to the active iron-oxygen intermediates, as has been suggested [17,26]. These effects depend on the particular substrate, and the overall effect of Cyt b5 should be considered with respect to the specific substrate and reaction catalyzed by CYP17A1.

Acceleration of the second electron transfer in the presence of Cyt b5 can explain the improvement in overall coupling of the CYP17A1 lyase reaction [18]. Slow reduction of the CYP17A1 oxy-complex by CPR competes with unproductive autooxidative decomposition and, in the absence of Cyt b5, results in lower efficiency of the steady-state turnover. Faster reduction of oxy-complex by Cyt b5 significantly changes the partitioning of productive and nonproductive reaction fluxes at this step in favor of the former (Fig. 1). The significant increase in coupling of CYP17A1 lyase reaction in the presence of Cyt b5 in steady-state turnover experiments strongly suggests that the second electron transfer is at least partially the rate-limiting step when 17OH-PREG is used as a substrate. On the other hand, in PREG hydroxylation catalyzed by CYP17A1, the rate-limiting step is subsequent proton-dependent formation of Cpd I [19,21], and the presence of Cyt b5 does not affect this reaction.

In conclusion, we measured oxygen binding and oxidation rates of reduced CYP17A1, Cyt b5 and CPR in Nanodiscs using stopped flow spectroscopy. We compared these rates with the rates of the same reactions for CYP17A1 reconstituted with both redox partners, separately or together, and identified the second electron transfer event from Cyt b5 and CPR to the oxy-complex in CYP17A1. Cyt b5 reduces the oxy-complex in CYP17A1 much faster than CPR, and dominates as a redox partner in this ternary complex when both Cyt b5 and CPR are reconstituted with CYP17A1. The major contribution of Cyt b5 as the second electron donor in CYP17A1 lyase catalysis explains the better coupling observed and also indicates a potential role of Cyt b5 as allosteric effector at the further steps of catalytic cycle. As these subsequent steps are fast, they likely happen before Cyt b5 dissociates from CYP17A1 after the second electron transfer step. This suggests that conformation and/or dynamic properties of CYP17A1 may be modulated by interactions with Cyt b5, which in turn can affect the proton transfer steps or orientation of the substrate with respect to catalytic intermediate.
