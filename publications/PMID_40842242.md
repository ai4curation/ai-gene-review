# Orchestrated long-distance gene activation by a ParB-like BisD-CTP DNA clamp in low-frequency transfer competence development in Pseudomonas putida.
**Authors:** Antar H, Carraro N, Budny H, Gruber S, van der Meer JR

## Abstract

1. Nucleic Acids Res. 2025 Aug 11;53(15):gkaf802. doi: 10.1093/nar/gkaf802.

Orchestrated long-distance gene activation by a ParB-like BisD-CTP DNA clamp in
low-frequency transfer competence development in Pseudomonas putida.

Antar H(1), Carraro N(1), Budny H(1), Gruber S(1), van der Meer JR(1).

Author information:
(1)Department of Fundamental Microbiology, Faculty of Biology and Medicine,
University of Lausanne, CH-1015 Lausanne, Switzerland.

Integrative conjugative elements (ICEs) are mobile DNA that remain integrated
within the host bacterial genome until they activate their excision and
transfer. ICE transfer is initiated in a small subpopulation of cells that leads
to transfer competence. Here we study the role of the ParB-like protein BisD for
ICEclc transfer competence formation in Pseudomonas putida. Genetic dissection
and single cell microscopy showed that mCherry-BisD fusion proteins form
fluorescent foci on parS-like DNA sequences on ICEclc. Chromatin
immunoprecipitation followed by DNA sequencing showed that BisD accumulates at
two bisS sites, and further enriches around 11 ICEclc transfer competence
promoters. BisD enrichments suggest its sliding over ca. 50 kb of ICE-DNA in an
asymmetric manner, following the direction of inwards ICE gene transcription.
BisD has a unique domain absent from ParB, which is essential for ICE promoter
activation in conjunction with the ICEclc encoded factor BisC, and for ICE
transfer. Similar to ParB, purified BisD preferentially binds and hydrolyses
cytidine triphosphate (CTP). Mutation of a conserved Arg- at position 95 to an
Ala-residue abolished nucleotide binding by BisD, and also inhibited ICE
promoter activation and transfer. BisD action might thus serve two purposes,
orchestrating ICE gene activation and ICE-DNA clamping.

Introduction

ParABS systems are widely distributed on bacterial chromosomes, plasmids, and extrachromosomal prophages [1] and ensure the faithful segregation of genetic material during cell division by partitioning copies of the DNA within the bacterial cell (reviewed in [2]). The tripartite system consists of the ParB protein, acting as an adaptor connecting the parS DNA sequences to the ParA-ATPase motor proteins. ParB accumulates near parS sites at high concentrations generating distinct clusters within the cell. This accumulation involves ParB proteins binding CTP nucleotides and forming ParB DNA clamps, a reaction which is catalyzed by parS DNA [3–5]. Subsequently, ParB DNA clamps are released from parS DNA and slide along the DNA double helix onto neighboring regions. Cytidine triphosphate (CTP) hydrolysis destabilizes the ParB clamp allowing for ParB-DNA dissociation, which then enables renewed ParB DNA clamping at parS DNA [6–8]. Additional interactions between ParB and nonspecific DNA sequences, as well as to other ParB dimers, are likely to contribute to the stabilization and further enlargement of the ParB cluster [9–11]. Typically, ParB covers up to 10–15 kb of DNA flanking a parS site [6, 12, 13]. Variants of the ParABS systems have also been identified in mobile genetic elements such as integrative and conjugative elements (ICEs) [14, 15], but their precise functions remain unclear. Here, we investigate a variant of ParABS encoded by the ICEclc element in Pseudomonas putida that contains a hybrid ParB protein, denoted as BisD, acting in conjunction with an activator protein BisC to initiate the expression of factors involved in ICE conjugative self-transfer [14].

ICEs comprise groups of chromosomally integrated genetic elements with different evolutionary origins that transfer at low frequencies among related species [16–18]. Transfer of the ICE is initiated by its excision from the chromosome through site-specific recombination, formation of a conjugative system, transfer of a single-stranded ICE-DNA molecule through the conjugative pore, replication in the new recipient cell and integration into the new host’s chromosome (summarized in [17]). ICEs have attracted attention because they encode a large variety of functions affecting bacterial lifestyles, which as a result of their self-transfer capacity can lead to adaptation of new recipient strains under selective conditions. Examples of such cargo genes include resistances to heavy metals or to antimicrobial compounds, and catabolic pathways [14, 19–26].

A prominent example of adaptation is demonstrated in the ICEclc element discovered originally in Pseudomonas knackmussii B13 [27]. The element was identified for its role in metabolizing 3-chlorobenzoate (3-CBA) by the clcRABDE operon genes that it carries [28]. These genes encode enzymes crucial for the degradation of chlorocatechols (clc), formed as intermediates in 3-CBA metabolism. Conjugative transfer initiation of ICEclc occurs in a small percentage of cells (3%–5%) in stationary phase, particularly after growth on 3-CBA [29, 30]. These cells activate a bistable switch, which starts a cascade of ICEclc gene expression, named the transfer competence program [31], leading to a subpopulation of cells able to transfer the ICE. In contrast, the majority of cells keeps an inactive chromosomally integrated ICEclc. Cells with initiated transfer competence do not revert to the ICE-integrated state, and their cell division is eventually arrested, as a consequence of the ICE-encoded Shi and ParA proteins [15, 32].

Activation of ICEclc transfer competence involves a regulatory hierarchy, passing step-by-step from the transcriptional factors MfsR, TciR [33], BisR, AlpA to BisDC [34]. BisDC was thought to exist as a complex formed of BisD and BisC subunits, based on analogy to the Escherichia coli flagellar synthesis transcription factor FlhDC, even though only BisC has very low similarity to FlhC [34]. Both components of BisDC are necessary for activation of its own expression, leading to a positive feedback loop that maintains bistable expression of the ICE transfer competence program [34]. The transfer competence regulon involves at least eight further ICEclc-specific polycistronic units that are controlled by BisDC [31], among which the formation of a type IV conjugation system [35]. Only part of BisD is homologous to ParB proteins and the bisD gene is located downstream of a parA-like gene on the ICEclc [15], suggesting that it is a hybrid protein with dual function in the ICE-transfer process: gene activation and ICEclc DNA clamping.

The major aim of this study was thus to elucidate the role of the BisD protein in ICEclc transfer and uncover its potential analogies to typical ParB functionalities. By gene deletions, transfer experiments, expression of fluorescent-fusion proteins, and epifluorescence microscopy promoter reporter assays, we characterize the importance of BisD domains for ICE gene activation and transfer. We show that BisD shares structural features with ParB proteins, loads, and accumulates around specific parS-like DNA sequences on ICEclc, herein referred to as the bistability regulatory sites bisS. We purify the BisD protein and study its binding to ribonucleotides as well as their hydrolysis in the presence of bisS DNA. We propose a CTP-dependent mechanism of BisD accumulation near bisS DNA. Additionally, we conducted chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) to determine BisC and BisD distribution on chromosomal DNA, demonstrating close colocalization at known ICE-transfer competence-activated promoters in the core region of the ICEclc [31], from which we propose a consensus BisC DNA recognition motif. We produce a BisD mutant no longer capable of binding nucleotides and show that this abolishes ICE activation and transfer. Overall, our results show the unique dual character of the BisD protein having both ParB-analogous CTP-dependent DNA loading and sliding activity, and enabling temporal activation of key ICEclc promoters in conjunction with BisC. This provides new insights and starting points for further investigations into the mechanistic details of ICEclc transfer in single transfer competent cells.

Construction of mCherry-BisD and BisC-mCherry fusionsTranslational fusions were constructed between mCherry and BisD, and between BisC and mCherry, which then replaced the wild-type gene on a single ICEclc copy integrated in the chromosome of P. putida UWC1. First we amplified the mcherry gene without stop codon and with a 45-bp extension for a linker peptide, as described in [41], which was fused downstream to a ca. 700-bp amplified 5′-gene fragment of bisD and upstream to a ca. 700-bp fragment covering the shi and part of parA-gene, such that the start codon of mcherry becomes the native start of bisD. Similarly, a DNA fragment was constructed carrying bisC without its stop codon, followed by the linker and mcherry open reading frame, and a ca. 700-bp recombination region directly downstream of bisC. Further bisD derivatives were produced in which the interdomain linker between the bisD-parB and bisD-act domain regions was removed but with in-frame translational fusion of the domains (i.e. bisDΔlink), or with introduced stop codons and renewed ribosome binding site and start upstream of bisD-act (i.e. bisD-split; Supplementary Fig. S1). Finally, a bisD mutant was produced by overlapping PCR in which nucleotides 283–284 were changed from CG to GC, leading to an Ala- instead of Arg-residue at position 95 (BisD-R95A), which abolishes nucleotide binding (see below). Assembled fragments were cloned individually into plasmid pJP5903, introduced into P. putida UWC1-ICEclc, and selected for single recombination events. Purified single recombinants were then induced by transformation with plasmid pSW and 3-methylbenzoate to force unique chromosome breaks and facilitate recovery of seamless in-frame double recombinants, as described by Martinez et al. [37]. Double recombinants were purified, cured from pSW and verified by PCR and sequencing for the correctness of the intended construction. The same fusion fragments were then also cloned into pME6032 or into mini-Tn5 or mini-Tn7 vectors for ectopic expression.

Epifluorescence microscopyExpression and localization of fluorescent reporter signals in P. putida cultures were quantified by epifluorescence microscopy on individual cells. Cells (150 μl aliquots) were sampled from IPTG-induced cultures or from stationary phase cultures on 3-CBA after 48 or 72 h, which were briefly vortexed (30 s at max speed) to disrupt potential cell aggregates. Aliquots of 5 μl were then spread on agarose-precoated glass slides (ca. 1 mm thick, using 1% agarose in 21C MM). Glass slides were covered with a regular 24 × 50 × 0.15 mm cover slip (Menzel-Gläser), and imaged in upright position with a Zeiss Axioplan II or in inverted position with a Nikon Eclipse Ti-E microscope, as previously described [34, 35]. Cells were imaged in phase contrast (PhC, 40 ms exposure), mCherry (500 ms), and eGFP fluorescence wavelengths (500 ms), and images were saved as 16-bit TIF-files. Cells were segmented on PhC images using Omnipose [42], used to build a logic-gate mask and measure fluorescence values of individual cells. Quantile-quantile plotting of fluorescence values with a conservative cut-off of the 99.95 confidence interval was then applied to estimate the proportion of the subpopulation of transfer competent cells expressing the ICE-promoter fluorescent reporters, as described previously [31]. For better foci resolution, we also used a ZEISS LSM 980 Airyscan 2 microscope, imaging cells in 14 subsequent Z-layers of 0.17 μm each in either GFP or e/mCherry channels (314 ms exposure), which were deconvoluted using the Zeiss system software with default settings. Fluorescence images for display were scaled to the same brightness in ImageJ [43] as indicated, saved as 8-bit gray TIF-files, cropped to the display area and saved as 300 dpi resolution in Adobe Photoshop (Adobe versus 2023).

Measurement of NTP hydrolysis by Malachite Green colorimetric detectionThe method for measuring NTP hydrolysis was adapted from the procedure outlined by [45]. In brief, mixtures of NTP (2× conc.) with or without 40 nucleotide double-stranded DNA (“DNA40”) (2× conc.) and mixture of protein solutions (2× conc.) were prepared in reaction buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2) on ice. Equal volumes of each solution were mixed, achieving a protein to ligand ratio of 1:1, and thoroughly mixed via pipetting. The resulting samples (containing 1 mM NTP, 1 μM DNA40, and 10 μM protein) were incubated at 25°C for 1 h, and a phosphate standard curve were prepared in parallel. Post-incubation, the samples were diluted four-fold by the addition of 60 μl of water to stop the reaction. Twenty microliter of working reagent (Sigma) were then added before transferring to a flat-bottom 96-well plate. The plate was left to incubate for 30 min at 25°C to allow for color development, and then the absorbance was measured at 620 nm. A standard curve of samples with increasing phosphate concentration was stained similarly and measured at 620-nm absorbance. Raw values were converted to rate values using the standard curve, and absolute rates were calculated by adjusting for the protein concentration. Finally, the mean values and standard deviations were calculated and graphically represented using GraphPad Prism software.

ResultsStructure prediction suggests BisD dimers to form a ParB-like DNA clampThe BisD protein of ICEclc has a length of 550 amino acids making it significantly larger than ParB proteins. Sequence comparison by BLAST had previously suggested that BisD might be composed of two distinct parts: an N-terminal sequence (here designated as BisDpar) with similarity to N-terminal sequences of ParB proteins, and a C-terminal domain with unclear function and no obvious sequence similarity (designated as BisDact for activation domain) [34]. AF3 structure prediction of full-length BisD proteins reveals a tightly interacting clamp-like homodimer, with dimerization interfaces located at both the N- and the C-terminal parts of the protein (Fig. 1A). These parts are connected by an about 70-aa long apparently unstructured linker (Fig. 1A). The potential for dimerization can also be seen in AF3 predictions of isolated BisDpar and BisDact (Fig. 1B). The folding of BisDpar strongly resembles the corresponding parts of canonical ParB proteins including a putative CTP-binding domain, with noticeable conservation of residues responsible for forming the CTP-binding pocket in ParB (GxxR and ENxxR; G92NTR95, E136N137ELR140 in BisD; Fig. 1C) and a helix–turn–helix domain (Fig. 1B), implying a capability of recognizing parS-like DNA sequences. A domain swap characteristic for ParB dimers (Fig. 1B) is also observed in the predicted BisDpar dimer structure, underscoring its similarities with ParB proteins [6]. The unstructured flexible linker region in BisD is significantly longer than in ParB and may have importance for contacts to BisC (see below). BisDact forms a tight homodimer in AF3 predictions comprising exclusively alpha-helical elements but without noticeable homology to other known protein structures (Fig. 1D). The predicted BisC structure has low but detectable structural similarities of its C-terminal domain to FlhC, although it lacks the zinc-binding site implicated in DNA binding by FlhC and has an additional N-terminal dimerization domain that more distantly positions the putative DNA binding regions in the dimer (Supplementary Fig. S2). In contrast, expression of bisC-mCherry under the same conditions did not lead to visible fluorescent foci in regular epifluorescence microscopy, although very weak and diffuse foci were observed in confocal scanning high resolution microscopy (Fig. 2B; Airyscan). The latter may be the consequence of binding of BisC-mCherry or BisD–BisC-mCherry complexes to their respective promoters on chromosomal or excised copies of ICEclc in the cell. To study the requirements for BisD focus formation, we (over-) expressed the BisD translational fusion construct in various configurations in P. putida with or without ICEclc (Fig. 3D). As expected, inducing mCherry-bisD together with bisC from a plasmid in P. putida with otherwise wild-type ICE, again yielded a small number of distinct fluorescent foci in individual cells in stationary phase (Fig. 2D). In absence of ICEclc, however, ectopic expression of mCherry-bisD and bisC led to homogenously fluorescent cells (Fig. 2D), indicating that foci formation is specific for ICEclc DNA. Induction of a single copy ectopically integrated mCherry-bisD in presence of the ICE, but without co-expression of bisC, resulted in individual cells displaying mostly a single focus—and again homogenous fluorescent cells in absence of the ICE (Fig. 2E). The reason for a single instead of multiple fluorescent foci as in Fig. 2D is due to the fact that BisD alone is insufficient to activate ICE excision and replication. Hence, only mCherry-BisD binding to the integrated ICEclc can be detected. Under conditions of its natural activation in stationary phase cells grown on 3-CBA, the proportion of cells displaying fluorescent foci is much lower, since limited to the 3%–5% of formed transfer competent cells [31], but those again displayed between 1–3 foci (Supplementary Fig. S3). This proportion is similar to estimates of the number of excised ICE copies in individual cells [49] (see below) and suggests that each BisD focus represents an individual copy of chromosomal or excised ICEclc.

BisD recognizes two parS-like bisS sequences on ICEclcAs the epifluorescence microscopy experiments suggested specific recognition of ICEclc DNA by BisD, we attempted to map the cis-regulatory sequences on ICEclc required for BisD focus formation. We assumed that BisD may recognize specific DNA sequences similar to ParB binding to the cognate 16-bp palindromic parS sequences. Pseudomonas strains typically have four chromosomal parS sites with a consensus sequence of TGTTCCACGTGGAACN, at which the chromosomally encoded ParB accumulates (Fig. 3A). Screening ICEclc for the occurrence of (near)-palindromic parS-like sequences in the main ICEclc regulatory region, from attL to ssb (Fig. 3A), resulted in two candidate bisS sequences: CTTCGGAtTCCGAAG and GAGCTATCCCCnGGGGATAGCTC. The former (candidate bisS-3) comprising a 7-bp inverted repeat is located in a gene annotated as orf96323 (Fig. 3A). The latter comprising an 11-bp inverted repeat is present in two instances on ICEclc, in the inrR gene and the ssb gene (Fig. 3A, named bisS-1 and bisS-2, respectively). To test for the recognition of these putative target sites by mCherry-BisD, we cloned and inserted them in single copy using mini-Tn5 integration on the chromosome of a P. putida strain lacking ICEclc. Induction of an ectopic copy of the mCherry-bisD gene from the tac promoter indeed resulted in (mostly) a single fluorescent focus appearing in stationary phase cells, but only when the insert included the bisS-1 or the bisS-2 sequence (Fig. 3B). Cells with bisS-3 did not produce fluorescent foci. These experiments indicated, therefore, that BisD specifically recognizes two bisS GAGCTATCCCCnGGGGATAGCTC sites on ICEclc that lay 2 kb apart. AF3 models of BisD dimers bound to bisS-DNA suggest placement of the BisDact domain on ‘top’ of the BisDparB domain in comparison to B. subtilis ParB dimers on parS [4], and reorganisation of the BisDact-domains upon binding to achieve a characteristic swap of the BisDpar regions on either side of the bisS-DNA, which is different from BisD dimer models with random ds-DNA (Supplementary Fig. S4).

We then checked if BisD can hydrolyze any of the ribonucleotides it binds to. We utilized endpoint colorimetric detection of free inorganic phosphate released by nucleotide hydrolysis in the Malachite Green assay. During 1 h of incubation at 25°C, BisD showed low but appreciable levels of hydrolysis of CTP but not of ATP, GTP, or UTP (Fig. 7C). CTP hydrolysis was statistically significantly stimulated upon addition of an ICEclc 40-bp DNA fragment containing the bisS-1 site but not with a random 40-bp DNA sequence of the same length (Fig. 7C). This result suggests that BisD, although potentially binding all ribonucleotides, selectively hydrolyses CTP, a reaction which is stimulated by bisS DNA.

Replacement of wild-type bisD on a single copy of the ICE in P. putida by the bisD-CG283GC substitution (BisD-R95A) reduced ICE transfer rates by >200-fold (to below the detection limit, Fig. 7D; P-value= 0.02838), while it also reduced activation of a single copy PinR-egfp promoter fusion to the detection limit for epifluorescence microscopy (Fig. 7E; P-value= 0.0077). This indicates the key role of nucleotide binding for BisD functioning in ICEclc transfer.

DiscussionWe characterize here for the first time a unique natural hybrid protein with dual functionality encompassing ParB-clamping and –sliding, and transcription activation. This protein, named BisD, is encoded by the ICEclc element in P. putida and P. knackmussii, and, together with BisC, had been shown to be crucial for ICEclc transfer competence [34]. The N-terminal ParB-like domain of BisD had been noticed previously [15], but we firmly demonstrate here the hallmarks of known ParB-function: (i) predicted sequence and structural similarity to ParB proteins with conserved residues forming the CTP binding pocket, (ii) recognition of and spreading from specific DNA sites (bisS), and (iii) nucleotide hydrolysis in presence of bisS-DNA. We also show that BisD is necessary for transcription activation of ICE-promoters by BisC, which is dependent on its (C-terminal) BisDact domain. A BisD mutant abolishing CTP binding (BisD-R95A) is uncapable of ICE activation and transfer. BisD and BisC enrich and colocalize to promoter regions on ICEclc that have been shown to bistably express exclusively in transfer competent P. putida cells [31], and fluorescently tagged BisD forms distinct foci that are dependent on the presence of a bisS site. Our first hint for enrichment of BisD on ICE-DNA came from epifluorescence microscopy images of P. putida with a single integrated ICEclc grown to stationary phase on 3-CBA. Under these conditions, 3–5% of individual cells develop ICE transfer competence and only those cells displayed clear mCherry-BisD fluorescent foci. The BisDParB domain alone is not sufficient to form foci, possibly because of absence of a dimerization interface that is formed by the BisD C-terminal domain (BisDact), although the long linker can be shortened (Fig. 5E).

CTP hydrolysis would eventually lead to opening of the BisD-clamp and its dissociation from the DNA, as has been reported for canonical ParB proteins [6–8]. The rate of CTP hydrolysis of BisD is slower than that of chromosomal ParB of Bacillus subtilis (around 8–10-fold lower), but other ParB-like proteins have been reported to exhibit a slow hydrolysis rate [56, 57] or even lack hydrolysis activity entirely [5]. Possibly, the slow CTP hydrolysis rate of BisD also enables it to distribute more extensively across the ICEclc DNA, which is advantageous for its regulatory mechanism of coactivating some 11 downstream ICE promoters. The 50-kb coverage by BisD is more than has been measured for other (wild-type) ParB proteins and their respective chromosomal target DNA. Slow hydrolysis would come with a risk of accumulating off-target BisD clamps, but in case of the ICEclc this seems to be avoided, perhaps again because BisD expression is restricted to stationary phase transfer competent cells that do not undergo cell division.

The ParB-like mechanism of BisD of sliding across DNA from the bisS sites is reminiscent for a partitioning-like mechanism. Indeed, a gene encoding for a ParA-like protein exists upstream of bisD on the ICE (Fig. 4A). Daughter cell partitioning of excised ICE-DNA is not very likely, as previous single cell microscopy data indicated that dividing transfer competent cells frequently lose the ICE [49], and their cell division is inhibited anyway by the Shi-system [32]. Active transport of excised ICEclc DNA molecules to conjugation complexes could be advantageous for transfer and avoid clogging of multiple ICE molecules with multiple conjugation pores around the cell envelope [35], but this would require some sort of recognition of the ICE-DNA-BisD complex by the type IV secretion system. In this context it is interesting to note that plasmid-expressed ParAICEclc-mCherry in P. putida (without ICE) locates to the nucleoid and concomitantly expressed Shi-eGFP localizes as foci in the cell envelope [32]. In addition to their inhibition of chromosome replication, ParA and Shi might thus be involved in pushing ICE-DNA away from the nucleoid to conjugation pores, of which there are multiple in transfer competent cells [35].
