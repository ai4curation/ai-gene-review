# Novel homozygous or compound heterozygous DNAH17 variants lead to male infertility characterized by multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella and asthenoteratozoospermia in humans.

Genetic variants in the dynein axonemal heavy chain 17 ( DNAH17 ) have been
implicated in multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella (MMAF)
and asthenoteratozoospermia (ATZS). The aim of this study is to identify novel
DNAH17 variants associated with MMAF and ATZS and to evaluate the impact of
these variants on sperm motility, sperm morphology, the ultrastructure of sperm
flagella, and the outcomes of intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). In the
present study, we identified one novel homozygous and three compound
heterozygous DNAH17 variants in one consanguineous and three nonconsanguineous
families. Semen analysis revealed significant reductions in sperm motility and
notable morphological changes. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed
the absence of outer dynein arms (ODAs), loss of peripheral doublet microtubules
(DMTs) 4-7, and multiple mitochondrial sheath (MS) abnormalities in the sperm
flagella. Functional analyses indicated that these variants disrupted the
expression and localization of the DNAH17 protein in the sperm flagella. Tandem
mass tagging proteomics and immunofluorescence demonstrated a significant
reduction in the expression of proteins associated with the assembly of
ODA-associated proteins into the axoneme from individuals with DNAH17 variants.
Notably, successful pregnancies were achieved through ICSI combined with
assisted oocyte activation (AOA) treatment in the female partner of these
probands. This study identified seven novel homozygous or compound heterozygous
DNAH17 variants in both consanguineous and nonconsanguineous families,
highlighting their detrimental effects on sperm motility, morphology, and
flagellar ultrastructure. These findings provide valuable insights for the
genetic diagnosis of MMAF and may enhance future genetic counseling strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Male infertility remains a significant global health concern and affects approximately 10.0%–20.0% of men of reproductive age.1 Abnormalities in sperm morphology and motility are particularly troubling causes of male infertility. A recent research has identified approximately 62 genes that contribute to multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella (MMAF),2 which highlights the genetic complexity that underlies sperm dysfunction. Mutations in genes involved in the structure and function of the axoneme are linked to conditions such as asthenozoospermia,34 which is characterized by reduced sperm motility, and teratozoospermia,56 in which morphologically abnormal sperm are present. These morphological defects are closely associated with impaired embryo quality, which establishes the direct relationship between genetic mutations and male infertility.78 A deeper understanding of the specific genetic variants responsible for these disorders is crucial for developing targeted therapeutic strategies and improving outcomes for affected individuals.

The sperm flagellum, with its distinctive “9 + 2” microtubule arrangement, is essential for sperm motility and flagellar morphology.910 The dynein axonemal heavy chain (DNAH) gene family plays a pivotal role in the formation and maintenance of this structure.1112 Members of the DNAH family contribute to the assembly of the axoneme, which is necessary for effective flagellar movement. For example, mutations in the DNAH1 gene are associated with asthenoteratozoospermia (ATZS),1314 in which sperm motility is impaired and structural abnormalities in the flagella are observed. These mutations disrupt dynein arm function, which prevents the generation of the force required for flagellar beating. Similarly, mutations in DNAH10 have been linked not only to sperm motility defects and MMAF1516 but also to broader ciliary dysfunctions, such as primary ciliary dyskinesia.17
DNAH2 mutations have also been shown to destabilize the axoneme, leading to significant structural defects that impair sperm motility and morphology.18 These examples underscore how mutations within the DNAH gene family can severely impact sperm motility and flagellar morphology.

Among the various DNAH family members, DNAH17 has been implicated in the morphology and motility of the sperm flagella. Research findings regarding mutations in DNAH17 and their impact on sperm characteristics show notable variability. Some mutations in DNAH17 lead to abnormal sperm flagella morphology, such as short, absent, bent, irregular, or coiled flagella, that result in reduced motility and compromised fertilization potential.19 However, a study reported cases of specific DNAH17 protein mutation, such as the p.C1803Y mutation, which causes reduced motility without a typical MMAF phenotype or ciliary-related symptoms.20 Furthermore, the effects of DNAH17 variants on the outcomes of intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) have shown considerable variation, with certain variants correlated with lower fertilization rates and poor embryo development.192122 This variability highlights the need for further investigation into the functional consequences of DNAH17 variants and their implications for male infertility and highlights an important avenue for future research.

In the current study, we examined three unrelated probands with MMAF to identify novel biallelic variants in DNAH17 and assess their impact on sperm motility, morphology, and the ultrastructure of sperm flagella. We also conducted preliminary mechanistic investigations to explore how DNAH17 deficiency influences the expression of axoneme assembly-related proteins. Finally, the outcomes of assisted reproductive technology (ART) in individuals with biallelic DNAH17 variants were evaluated.

Peptides were then separated using an EASY-nLC 1200 ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography system. To eliminate systematic bias between samples, raw data underwent median normalization before further statistical analysis: for each sample, the median intensity of all identified proteins was calculated, and each protein’s intensity was divided by this median to ensure comparability across samples. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified using the criterion log2(fold change)>1 with P < 0.05. Hierarchical clustering of DEPs was performed using the R (version 4.0.0; https://www.r-project.org/foundation/, last accessed on 2025 January 5) pretty heatmaps (pheatmap) package. Hypergeometric-based enrichment analysis with Gene Ontology (GO) was performed to annotate protein sequences. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA; version 4.1.0; https://www.gsea-msigdb.org, last accessed on 2025 February 27) software was used for gene set enrichment analysis to determine whether a set of genes was associated with a specific GO term. A value of normalized enrichment score (NES) >1, a nominal (NOM) P < 0.05, and a false discovery rate (FDR) value <0.25 were considered to indicate significant differences between the two groups.Statistical analysesStatistical analysis was performed using GraphPad PRISM 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical comparisons between the proband and control groups were performed using a Mann-Whitney U test (two groups) or Brown–Forsythe analysis of variance (ANOVA) test (three or more groups). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Whole exome sequencing (WES) and bioinformatic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Catalog No. 69504; QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was then subjected to WES to identify genetic variants. Library preparation, exome sequencing, and data analysis were performed by the AmCare Genomics Laboratory (Guangzhou, China) to assess genetic risk for participants and their offspring. Briefly, raw fast alignment search tool-quality score (FASTQ) reads were processed using fastp software (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp, last accessed on 2024 June 7) to remove adapter sequences, polyN/polyA tracts, and low-quality reads (Q score <20), generating clean reads for downstream analysis. The sequencing reads were aligned to the reference human reference genome GRCh37/hg19 using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA; version 0.7.15; https://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net/, last accessed on 2024 November 19). The clinical significance of the variants was assessed using publicly available databases (gnomAD [https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/, last accessed on 2024 June 7], ClinVar [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/, last accessed on 2024 November 19], and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man [OMIM; https://www.omim.org/, last accessed on 2024 June 7]), and peer-reviewed literature. Pathogenicity predictions were performed using rare exome variant ensemble learner (REVEL), Polyphen2, MutationTaster, and combined annotation-dependent depletion (CADD) to evaluate evolutionary conservation and structural/functional impacts. Variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines.25 Candidate pathogenic variants were validated by Sanger sequencing of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products using an ABI 3730XL sequencer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) and analyzed with Mutation Surveyor version 4.0.5 software (Softgenetics, State College, PA, USA). Specific PCR primers designed to target the mutation region in DNAH17 are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

RESULTSIdentification of DNAH17 variants in patients with male infertilityTo investigate the genetic basis of male infertility, we performed WES and bioinformatics analyses on a cohort of 3869 male patients with infertility. We identified 3 compound heterozygous and one homozygous DNAH17 variant in 4 MMAF patients, accounting for 4.3% (4/92) of the MMAF infertility cohort. We also identified 20 heterozygous variants of DNAH17 (GenBank: NM_173628) in 20 unrelated non-MAAF individuals, accounting for 0.5% (20/3777) of the non-MMAF infertile cohort (Supplementary Table 3). Following the identification of biallelic DNAH17 variants, we systematically excluded pathogenic variants in other known ATZS/MMAF-associated genes through comprehensive analysis of WES data, confirming that DNAH17 variants are the sole candidate causative alterations. DNAH17, located at 17q25.3, spans approximately 153 700 nucleotides and encodes a predicted 4462-amino acid protein containing six adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) domains and three tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) domains. To validate the biallelic DNAH17 variants, we performed a PCR-Sanger sequencing and segregation analysis in four families. Specifically, proband-1, harboring the compound heterozygous c.7618 C>T (p.L2540F) and c.13117 The T>G (p.Y4373D) variant, was found in nonconsanguineous family 1 (Figure 1a and 1b). Proband-2, with c.10543 G>A (p.G3515S) and c.12583_12585delGAG (p.E4195del), came from nonconsanguineous family 2 (Figure 1c and 1d). Proband-3, with homozygous c.5300_5302delTGG (p.V1767del), came from consanguineous family 3 (Figure 1e and 1f), and proband-4, with compound heterozygous c.2101dupC (p.E701Gfs*25) and c.5005 G>A (p.R1669W), came from nonconsanguineous family 4 (Figure 1g and 1h). Although DNA samples from the mother of proband-1 were unavailable because she had passed away, his father was heterozygous for the c.7618 C>T variant and did not carry c.13117 T>G variants. However, his sister did not carry either c.7618 C>T or c.13117 T>G variants. Thus, we inferred that the c.13117 T>G variant was inherited from the deceased mother of proband-1 or occurred as a de novo mutation. DISCUSSIONIn this study, we identified one novel homozygous and three compound heterozygous DNAH17 variants in one consanguineous and three nonconsanguineous families with autosomal recessive inheritance, characterized by ATZS and MMAF phenotypes. WES and Sanger sequencing revealed that these probands carried either homozygous or compound heterozygous DNAH17 variants, with no other known pathogenic mutations associated with ATZS or MMAF. Papanicolaou staining and SEM analysis demonstrated marked shortening and coiling of the sperm flagella. TEM further revealed multiple MS abnormalities in the midpiece; the absence of ODAs in the middle, principal, and end pieces; and the loss of DMTs 4–7 in the principal and end pieces of sperm flagella. Mechanistic analysis suggested that these novel biallelic DNAH17 variants disrupt established axonemal assembly regulators. Our findings support the hypothesis that biallelic DNAH17 variants contribute to MMAF and impaired sperm motility by regulating the assembly of MS, ODA, and DMTs 4–7 in sperm flagella (Figure 4c).DNAH17 encodes a protein component of the ODA, which is crucial for sperm flagellar motility.22 This gene is specifically expressed in the testes and sperm, emphasizing its role in male fertility. While DNAH17 is not detected in cilia, suggesting a function exclusive to sperm flagella,22 various DNAH17 variants have been linked to distinct sperm phenotypes, indicating its complex role in spermatogenesis.19 Among the 30 patients with homozygous or compound heterozygous DNAH17 variants reported in previous studies (Supplementary Table 6), 4 were diagnosed with nonobstructive azoospermia (NOA),2728 and 8 had sperm concentrations below the WHO5/6 reference levels (Supplementary Table 6 and 7).1921293031 Notably, our study revealed that the sperm count of the 4 probands who carried compound heterozygous DNAH17 missense variants or homozygous DNAH17 LoF variants was comparable to that of the controls. Interestingly, the mouse carried homozygous Dnah17 c.G5360A mutation resulted in a sperm count and morphology comparable to those of Dnah17+/+ mice,20 whereas Dnah17−/− mice presented a markedly reduced sperm count,31 suggesting a role for DNAH17 in spermatogenesis beyond axonemal assembly. In this study, we identified one novel homozygous and three compound heterozygous DNAH17 variants in one consanguineous and three nonconsanguineous families with autosomal recessive inheritance, characterized by ATZS and MMAF phenotypes. WES and Sanger sequencing revealed that these probands carried either homozygous or compound heterozygous DNAH17 variants, with no other known pathogenic mutations associated with ATZS or MMAF. Papanicolaou staining and SEM analysis demonstrated marked shortening and coiling of the sperm flagella. TEM further revealed multiple MS abnormalities in the midpiece; the absence of ODAs in the middle, principal, and end pieces; and the loss of DMTs 4–7 in the principal and end pieces of sperm flagella. Mechanistic analysis suggested that these novel biallelic DNAH17 variants disrupt established axonemal assembly regulators. Our findings support the hypothesis that biallelic DNAH17 variants contribute to MMAF and impaired sperm motility by regulating the assembly of MS, ODA, and DMTs 4–7 in sperm flagella (Figure 4c).

DNAH17 encodes a protein component of the ODA, which is crucial for sperm flagellar motility.22 This gene is specifically expressed in the testes and sperm, emphasizing its role in male fertility. While DNAH17 is not detected in cilia, suggesting a function exclusive to sperm flagella,22 various DNAH17 variants have been linked to distinct sperm phenotypes, indicating its complex role in spermatogenesis.19 Among the 30 patients with homozygous or compound heterozygous DNAH17 variants reported in previous studies (Supplementary Table 6), 4 were diagnosed with nonobstructive azoospermia (NOA),2728 and 8 had sperm concentrations below the WHO5/6 reference levels (Supplementary Table 6 and 7).1921293031 Notably, our study revealed that the sperm count of the 4 probands who carried compound heterozygous DNAH17 missense variants or homozygous DNAH17 LoF variants was comparable to that of the controls. Interestingly, the mouse carried homozygous Dnah17 c.G5360A mutation resulted in a sperm count and morphology comparable to those of Dnah17+/+ mice,20 whereas Dnah17−/− mice presented a markedly reduced sperm count,31 suggesting a role for DNAH17 in spermatogenesis beyond axonemal assembly. Further investigations are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanism involved.

MMAF: multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella

Biallelic DNAH17 variants are associated with asthenozoospermia, although the link to MMAF phenotypes remains debated. Previous studies of 30 patients, including 26 with asthenozoospermia, reported that 22 exhibited MMAF,19212229−33 whereas 4 had simple asthenozoospermia (Supplementary Table 7).2034 Notably, three patients carrying homozygous DNAH17 missense mutations presented a normal sperm morphology rate exceeding 70.0%,20 with a reference value of 4.0%. However, the high normal sperm morphology rate raises concerns about adherence to the sperm morphology evaluation criteria of the WHO5/6.2324 Subsequent studies from the same group confirmed MMAF phenotypes in patients with homozygous DNAH17 LoF variants,32 suggesting that the variant type might influence the manifestation of MMAF. Our findings, alongside others, demonstrate that homozygous or compound heterozygous DNAH17 missense variants often result in significant MMAF phenotypes. Interestingly, in our cohort, nearly 19.1%–49.2% of sperm flagella in patients with compound heterozygous DNAH17 variants retained normal morphology, indicating substantial variability within the same genetic mutation. Given the subjectivity of MMAF diagnosis and the lack of universally accepted standards, accurate sperm morphology assessment requires experienced personnel following the WHO5/6 guidelines2324 to ensure consistency across studies.

Another notable finding was that infertile male patients carrying heterozygous DNAH17 LoF variants exhibited no MMAF phenotypes. Specifically, no significant differences in sperm morphology or progressive motility were detected compared with those in the entire non-MMAF infertility cohort (Supplementary Table 1). These findings suggest that heterozygous DNAH17 LoF variants do not adversely affect axonemal assembly. The carrier frequency of DNAH17 LoF variants in the non-MMAF infertility cohort was 0.5% (20/3777), whereas the carrier frequency of DNAH17 pathogenic variants in the MMAF patients was 4.3% (4/92). The collective frequency of 1218 LoF variants of DNAH17 recorded in gnomAD version 4.1.0 is 0.3%. Thus, the carrier frequencies of DNAH17 pathogenic variants in both the non-MMAF infertility cohort and the MMAF patient cohort are higher than the collective frequency of DNAH17 LoF variants in gnomAD version 4.1.0. It is highly important to screen carriers of DNAH17 pathogenic variants in the infertile population, especially the MMAF population.

Sperm viability, assessed through eosin staining, has often been overlooked but plays a crucial role in distinguishing genetic causes of asthenozoospermia and MMAF. When there are more than 25.0%–30.0% immotile live sperm in a semen sample, a genetic flagellar disorder may be a possible cause.35 If the proportion of immotile live sperm in the semen is too low, the underlying causes of asthenozoospermia or MMAF are likely not genetic factors.35 Among nine prior studies on DNAH17 and asthenozoospermia, only two evaluated sperm viability, both of which reported at least 40% immotile live sperm (Supplementary Table 7).2122 Our study revealed varying viability rates (33.0%–72.0%) in patients with DNAH17 variants. Future studies should incorporate sperm viability assessments to rule out nongenetic causes of these conditions.

The expression of the DNAH17 protein in sperm flagella has been inconsistently reported. Zhang et al.20 reported detectable DNAH17 expression throughout the length of their sperm flagella in homozygous missense variant carriers, whereas homozygous LoF variants were not expressed.32 Tang et al.31 reported that DNAH17 was consistently localized throughout the entire sperm flagellum in control samples, but the protein was absent from the majority of the principal piece in spermatozoa from four probands carrying compound heterozygous missense or LoF variants. Other studies noted weak or partial expression in patients with compound heterozygous DNAH17 variants.33 In our study, patients with compound heterozygous missense variants exhibited DNAH17 expression predominantly in the midpiece and parts of the principal piece, whereas no expression was detected in individuals with homozygous LoF variants. These variations in protein expression may contribute to the differences in sperm motility and morphology observed across studies. In addition, our results revealed distinct genotype-dependent effects of DNAH17 variants on sperm flagella-associated protein expression. While both homozygous LoF and compound heterozygous variants led to significant reductions in DNAH17, DNAH8, DNAI1, ODAD2, ODAD4, and TOM20, only the homozygous LoF variant profoundly disrupted the expression of other axonemal assembly-associated proteins, including CFAP70, AKAP4, SPAG6, RSPH9, and DNALI1. In contrast, these proteins remained unaffected or slightly affected in spermatozoa from compound heterozygous carriers (proband-1 and -4), highlighting a dose-dependent relationship between DNAH17 dysfunction and structural compromise. Notably, IFT20 was markedly upregulated across all the biallelic DNAH17 variant carriers, suggesting a potential compensatory mechanism to counteract axonemal instability through enhanced retrograde transport. However, the precise mechanistic basis of this observation warrants further investigation.

The ultrastructural impact of DNAH17 variants on sperm flagella has been well documented. Early work by Whitfield et al.22 identified defects in the MS, the absence of ODAs, and the partial loss of DMTs. Zhang et al.20 reported significant loss of DMTs 4–7 in three Pakistani patients with homozygous DNAH17 missense variants, while ODAs were present. Another study from their team revealed that sperm flagella from Chinese patients with homozygous DNAH17 LoF variants presented no observable ODAs, whereas some sperm flagella from Pakistani patients with homozygous DNAH17 LoF variants still presented ODA.32 Tang et al.31 reported that malformed acrosomes, disorganized mitochondrial sheaths in large cytoplasmic bags, displaced ODF and/or DMT, and the absence of CP and ODA were observed in DNAH17-deficient spermatozoa. In our study, we observed widespread loss of DMTs 4–7 and a complete absence of ODAs in both homozygous and compound heterozygous DNAH17 variant carriers, which is consistent with previous reports.20 Ethnic differences in patient populations may account for some of the variability observed in the effects of DNAH17 variants on the sperm flagellar ultrastructure.

Given the relatively high total motile sperm count in these patients, we recommend ICSI combined with AOA as a preferable treatment over IVF or ICSI alone. A previous study has demonstrated poor outcomes with IVF in dynein arm knockout models.36 Although a recent study reported that fertilized embryos were obtained from four patients with MMAF induced by biallelic DNAH17 variants following ART via ICSI without AOA,31 more studies confirmed that ICSI alone in MMAF patients carrying DNAH17 variants often yields unsatisfactory results (Supplementary Table 8).192122 Notably, a study revealed that DNAH17 variants could cause total fertilization failure, and the use of donor sperm in IVF was an effective solution for affected patients.33 In contrast, ICSI combined with AOA has been shown to improve clinical outcomes, supporting its use in the treatment of DNAH17-related infertility.1933 Our findings further support the effectiveness of combining ICSI with AOA to achieve transplantable blastocysts and clinical pregnancy in patients with homozygous or compound heterozygous DNAH17 variants. Therefore, we recommend that the use of donor sperm should not be considered prior to attempting ICSI combined with AOA treatment.

CONCLUSIONWe identified novel homozygous and compound heterozygous DNAH17 variants in patients with male infertility and MMAF phenotypes. These variants lead to significant flagellar abnormalities, reduced motility, and altered axonemal structures, enhancing our understanding of the role of DNAH17 in male fertility. Our findings also suggest that ICSI combined with AOA should be considered for treating infertility in patients with DNAH17 variants.
