The function of many channels and transporters is enriched by the conformational
plasticity of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). Copper transporter 1
(Ctr1) is the main entry point for Cu(I) ions in eukaryotes and contains IDRs
both at its N-terminal (Nterm) and C-terminal ends. The former delivers copper
ions from the extracellular matrix to the selectivity filter in the Ctr1 lumen.
However, the molecular mechanism of this process remains elusive due to Nterm's
disordered nature. Here, we combine advanced molecular dynamics simulations and
circular dichroism experiments to show that Cu(I) ions and a lipidic environment
drive the insertion of the Nterm into the Ctr1 selectivity filter, causing its
opening. Through a lipid-aided conformational switch of one of the transmembrane
helices, the conformational change of the selectivity filter propagates down to
the cytosolic gate of Ctr1. Taken together, our results elucidate how
conformational variability of IDRs modulates ion transport.

## Full Text

Intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) are a widespread feature of integral membrane proteins. Genome-wide studies estimate that a disordered region occurs in ~40% of human membrane proteins (1). IDRs are most frequent in proteins associated with signaling, such as G-protein-coupled receptors (2), receptor tyrosine kinases (3) and c-AMP-dependent chloride channels (4), since their lack of a fixed structure enables them to promiscuously adapt to bind a range of protein partners and ligands. In addition to signaling, IDRs of membrane proteins were observed to play a role in trafficking, sensing, function regulation, and to affect the curvature and content of membrane vesicles (5).

The advancement of cryo-EM and protein crystallization methods brought about a surge in the number of determined membrane protein structures. However, this success has been limited to structured proteins and regions, while structural characterization of IDRs severely lags behind. NMR spectroscopy has proven to be an effective tool for investigating the conformational ensembles of IDRs in isolation, but structural and mechanistic studies of IDRs in the context of full-length membrane proteins are still difficult to perform. Additionally, the conformational ensembles of IDRs can be affected by posttranslational modifications and ligand binding, further complicating the picture. All-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are a powerful instrument to study the structure and function of intact membrane proteins embedded in a lipid bilayer, and therefore allow us to gain a complete picture of a membrane protein’s mechanism of action by dissecting the functional and regulatory role of its IDRs.

Here, we apply brute force and enhanced sampling MD simulations to investigate how the ID ectodomain regulates the function of the hCtr1, the main cellular entry point for Cu(I) ions in eukaryotes. hCtr1 is characterized by a homotrimeric architecture (Fig. 1) (6) with each subunit containing an approximately 50 amino acid-long ID N-terminal domain (Nterm), located in the extracellular matrix, three transmembrane (TM) helices (TM1-3), and a short intracellular C-terminal tail. The TM2 helix of each monomer contains the highly conserved 150MxxxM motif, which in the trimeric structure of hCtr1 forms the selectivity filter (7). The latter is located at the extracellular half of the TM domain, can host up to two Cu(I) ions, and represents the bottleneck of the transporter. Below the selectivity filter lies the positively charged central cavity, large enough to permit entry of all three C-terminal tails (8). As such, both the selectivity filter and the C-terminal tails can act as gates and together constitute the ion permeation pathway.

Due to its toxicity, the in-cell concentration of free Cu(I) is extremely low (<10−18 M) (9). Cu(I) trafficking is therefore mediated by a complex network of metal-protein and protein–protein interactions that tightly regulate Cu(I) homeostasis to prevent a plethora of diseases connected to Cu(I) imbalance, such as cancer, neurodegeneration, and metabolic diseases (10–12). Preliminary studies suggest the Nterm is central for acquiring Cu ions from the extracellular matrix. It utilizes the amino terminal Cu(II)- and Ni(II)-binding (ATCUN) motif (1MDH) to collect Cu(II) ions from blood carrier proteins (13, 14), mediates their reduction to Cu(I) and their subsequent transfer to the selectivity filter. The Nterm methionine-rich motif (40MMMMxM) was shown to play a key role in the Cu(I) internalization process (15, 16), with residues Met43 and Met45 being identified as key for copper uptake at low copper concentrations (17). Additionally, the hCtr1’s transport appears to be coupled to copper-dependent endocytosis, which dictates the amount of hCtr1 present at the plasma membrane and is severely hindered by Nterm truncation (18). However, despite these experimental studies, how the Nterm performs its diverse tasks from a mechanistic standpoint is unclear.

Here, we aim to expand the knowledge on the diverse roles played by IDRs of membrane proteins by resolving the functional role of the ID Nterm domain in hCtr1-mediated cellular copper import. To this end, we explore the Nterm conformational landscape by Hamiltonian replica exchange (HREX) MD and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy. We identify three low-energy structural ensembles and exploit this information to build a full-length hCtr1 model embedded in a lipid bilayer. Our simulations reveal that the conformational changes in the Nterm domain, mediated by metal–protein and lipid–protein interactions, regulate Cu(I) delivery to the selectivity filter. Namely, in the Cu(I)-transport conducive state, the Nterm directly interacts with the selectivity filter, triggering its conformational remodeling. This causes a lipid-aided conformational switch of TM2 helix from the bent to the linear state and affects the crosstalk between the selectivity filter and the C-terminal tail, which acts as the final hCtr1 entry gate. Taken together, this suggests the ID Nterm intricately regulates hCtr1’s function along the whole permeation pathway.

In most cases, this regulation predominantly occurs via a so-called ball-and-chain mechanism, where a short terminal segment binds into the channel cavity and blocks the ion flow. Such inactivation has been observed for the potassium channel MthK (22) and voltage-gated Shaker channels (23). However, emerging lines of evidence indicate that conformational changes in disordered regions can also affect ion transport by modulating the geometry and dynamics of the selectivity filter or other gates (24, 25). For example, the disordered Nterm domain of the human ether-à-go-go-related potassium channel interacts with its cytoplasmic gates, stabilizing the open state of the channel (26).Similarly, our simulations revealed that the intrinsically disordered Nterm domain exhibits a functional and regulatory role in hCtr1, facilitated by its toggling between different conformational states (Fig. 6). While the Nterm was unfolded in the apo state, Cu(I) binding triggered the formation of β-sheet fragments. The Nterm conformational equilibrium was additionally governed by lipid–protein interactions. Indeed, we showed that in the alpha state, which was stabilized by interaction with lipid molecules, the Met1, from one Nterm monomer, interacted with the top Met triad of the selectivity filter. This interaction induced a conformational transition of one of the TM2 helices from a bent to a linear state, which in turn resulted in the opening of the selectivity filter both at the top and bottom Met triad. This suggests that Met1 is a key residue for Cu delivery from the Nterm to the selectivity filter. Accordingly, truncation of the first 30 amino acids of the Nterm was reported to reduce the affinity of the transporter for Cu(I) ions (16). However, cell growth assays and 64Cu uptake experiments in HEK293 cells identified Met43 and Met45 as central for high-affinity copper uptake (17). In spite of these lines of experimental evidence, in our MD simulations none of the Met residues present in the Met-rich 40MMMMPM motif were sufficiently close to the selectivity filter to permit direct copper transfer. Likewise, in the Ctr1 crystal structure from Salmo salar, Met45, the only resolved residue from the Met-rich motif, is more than 10 Å away from the selectivity filter (6). Therefore, we posit that Met1 mediates the Cu(I) hand-off from the Met-rich motif to the selectivity filter, since it can easily span the distance due to its flexibility. The observed direct involvement of lipid molecules in the Nterm conformational equilibrium similarly suggests that the membrane is not a passive spectator in hCtr1-mediated copper transport. In addition to stabilizing the Nterm in the transport conducive alpha conformation, lipid molecules also promoted the shift of the TM2 helix from the bent to the linear state through the insertion of a hydrophobic lipid tail at the TM2/TM3 interface, consequently disrupting the interaction between two adjacent hCtr1 monomers. The breaking of hydrophobic contacts along the TM2/TM3 interface had two consequences. First, it altered the signal transduction pathway from the selectivity filter to the C-terminal tail, and second, it led to the unfolding of the C-terminal end of the TM3 helix. The latter augmented the length of the disordered C-terminal tail flanking the TM3 helix, increasing its flexibility and therefore potentially making it more fit to play its suggested regulatory role. Encouragingly, lengthening of the C-terminal tail during Cu(I) internalization was observed also by X-ray crystallography (6). More generally, our work contributes to the emerging view of IDRs not only as interaction hotspots, but important regulatory elements in ion channels and transporters that employ sophisticated and complex mechanisms to elegantly modulate metal ion transport in limited and healthy amounts.

The N-Terminal Domain Assumes Three Distinct Conformational States.

First, we performed a HREX simulation to determine the conformational landscape of the Nterm domain (hCtr1 residue range 1–52). Briefly, in HREX simulations 24 replicas of the system, differing in the scaling of specific force field terms (i.e., the parameters of dihedral angles, Lennard–Jones and Coulomb potentials), were run in parallel. At regular intervals, the coordinates of the systems were exchanged between neighboring replicas, enabling better sampling of the system’s conformational landscape (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Afterward, only the replica where the system was described with the “physical” force field (no scaling) was analyzed.

The free energy (G) surface of the Nterm conformational landscape explored in the HREX simulation was projected as a function of the radius of gyration (Rg), α-helical and β-sheet content (Fig. 2 A–C). Three distinct conformational minima, differing in the content of secondary structure elements, were identified and named as the alpha, beta, and unfolded states (Fig. 2 D–F). The alpha state (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Figs. S2–S4) contained both short α-helical (residue ranges 28–32, 44–49) and β-sheet fragments (residue ranges 6–9, 40–43), while the beta state was characterized by three β-strands (residue ranges 5–7, 39–41, 18–20) (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Figs. S2–S4). Finally, the unfolded state corresponded to an ensemble of highly disordered structures with almost no secondary structure elements (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Figs. S2–S4). In comparison with the alpha state, the unfolded and beta states laid at ΔG of 0.2 ± 0.7 and 4.7 ± 0.9 kJ/mol, respectively (Fig. 2C). The activation free energy barrier for the transition from the alpha to the unfolded state was 1.1 ± 0.7 kJ/mol. A similarly low barrier (5.0 ± 0.5 kJ/mol) was observed between the unfolded and beta states, suggesting the Nterm can easily transit between these distinct conformational states at physiological condition, consistently with its disordered nature (Fig. 2C).

This resulted in unfolding of TM3 at the C-terminal end and in an increase in the length of the C-terminal tail, indicating TM2/TM3 interaction is crucial for the stability of the TM3 helix. The observed lengthening of the disordered region might serve to promote an incipient movement of the C-terminal tail from the cytosol to the pore lumen, where it may recruit Cu(I) for subsequent internalization, and therefore suggests a functional link between the disordered N- and C-terminal regions.

The Na+ and Cl− ions were described using Joung and Chetham parameters (46).

The full-length models of hCtr1 were constructed with MODELLER (version 10.0) homology modeling software (41). Three model structures were built corresponding to the minima detected in the Nterm free energy landscape. Representative structures were cross-validated by utilizing two alternative similarity metrics: RMSD of backbone atom pair distances or Euclidean distance in a coordinate system defined by the distances between Cu(I)/Cu(II)-binding motifs present in the Nterm (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). First, simulated systems were equilibrated in the NVT ensemble for 10 ns using periodic boundary conditions and position restraints on the protein component and lipid head groups. Next, equilibration was continued in the NPT ensemble for another 10 ns. In the production phase, all position restrains were removed, and the system was simulated for 1 μs. The leap-frog algorithm was used to solve Newton’s equations of motion together with a 2-fs time step. Electrostatic interactions were evaluated using the particle mesh Ewald method (47). Temperature (T) was monitored using a Nosé–Hoover thermostat (T = 323 K, coupling constant was 0.5 ps) (48), while pressure was maintained at 1 bar with the Parrinello–Rahman barostat (time constant for pressure coupling was 2 ps). Python packages MDTraj and MDAnalysis were used for trajectory analysis (49, 50). The bending angle was calculated with HELENAL (51) as the angle between local helical axes defined by residues 147–150 and 151–154, respectively. Cpptraj was employed for principal component analysis and calculating Pearson’s correlation matrices (52). Signaling network analysis was performed using the VMD network-view plugin and the GNCOMMUNITIES and SUBOPT tools (53). Three replicas of hCtr1 with the Nterms in the alpha state were run and displayed similar behavior. In simulations where TM2 was maintained in the linear state, position restraints were placed on backbone atoms of TM2 and a harmonic bond (2.1 Å) was added between atom pairs Val143O-Tyr147H and Val144O-Phe148H.

QM/MM MD Simulations.Binding of Cu(I) to the N-terminal domain of hCtr1 was predicted with a hybrid quantum mechanics (QM)/molecular mechanics (MM) MD simulations. The Cu(I)-bound Nterm was solvated in TIP3P water (box size 59 Å × 52 Å × 52 Å) and Cl− ions were added to achieve neutrality. QM/MM MD simulations were performed with cp2k (version 7.1) (54). The QM portion was composed of Cu(I) and the side chains of residues Met9, Met12, Met40, Met42, Met43, and Met45. In all cases, the QM/MM boundary was across the Cβ–Cα bond. The quantum portion was described with the Becke–Lee–Yang–Parr density functional theory (DFT/BLYP) (55, 56) using the double-ζ molecularly optimized basis set along with Goedecker–Teter–Hutter pseudopotentials (DZVP-MOLOPT-GTH) (57). For the copper ion, the short-range version of the basis set was used (DZVP-MOLOPT-SR-GTH). The system was initially minimized in the microcanonical (NVE) ensemble and slowly heated to 300 K using the NVT ensemble. After equilibration, the trajectory was followed for 2.5 ps. Binding sites were reparametrized based on the final geometry using Metal Center Parameter Builder (MCPB) (58) and Gaussian09 (59) to derive Cu-site-specific force-field parameters. Force constants and Merz–Kollman-restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) charges were obtained with the B3LYP functional (55, 60) and 6-31G* basis set (61). Modified force-field parameters were used in subsequent classical MD runs of Cu(I)-Nterm, which otherwise used the same force field and simulation setup described above.
