The vast majority of fast inhibitory transmission in the brain is mediated by
GABA acting on GABAA receptors (GABAARs), which provides inhibitory balance to
excitatory drive and controls neuronal output. GABAARs are also effectively
targeted by clinically important drugs for treatment in a number of neurological
disorders. It has long been hypothesized that function and pharmacology of
GABAARs are determined by the channel pore-forming subunits. However, recent
studies have provided new dimensions in studying GABAARs due to several
transmembrane proteins that interact with GABAARs and modulate their trafficking
and function. In this review, we summarize recent findings on these novel GABAAR
transmembrane regulators and highlight a potential avenue to develop new GABAAR
psychopharmacology by targeting these receptor-associated membrane proteins.

Auxiliary Subunits of Ionotropic ReceptorsOver the past two decades, significant progress has been made toward identifying novel proteins that interact with ionotropic glutamate receptors (iGluRs) in the CNS [26–30]. A growing list of these proteins have been classified as auxiliary subunits because they not only regulate receptor trafficking, but also modulate their functional and pharmacological properties [26–29]. Historically, auxiliary subunits associated with AMPA receptors (AMPARs) have been the most characterized. Numerous studies have identified >12 AMPAR auxiliary subunit families, including transmembrane AMPAR regulatory proteins (TARPs), cornichons (CNIHs), Shisa/cysteine-knot AMPAR modulating proteins (Shisa/CKAMPs), ferric chelate reductase 1-like protein (FRRS1L), and germ cell specific gene 1-like protein (GSG1L) [31–40]. Stabilization of GABAARs at Synapses by LH4LH4 (Lhfpl4 or GARLH4), a four-pass transmembrane protein (Figure 1A), was initially identified in affinity-purified cortical α1-containing GABAAR complexes [23] and was subsequently discovered as a component of both the hippocampal α2-containing and cerebellar α1-containing GABAAR proteomes [18,22]. However, the function of LH4 in neurons was not established until recent studies confirmed that LH4 was highly enriched at inhibitory synapses and its interaction with GABAARs was critical for GABAergic transmission both in vitro and in vivo [18,19]. Indeed, knockdown (KD) of LH4 strongly decreased GABAergic transmission in hippocampal neurons, without altering excitatory transmission [18]. Consistent with these observations, GABAergic transmission in hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neurons was also significantly reduced in LH4 knockout (KO) mice [19]. LH4 regulated GABAergic transmission in an input- and cell-specific manner. Specifically, fast, but not slow, miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs), that originated from distinct interneuron inputs, were reduced in LH4 KO CA1 pyramidal neurons [19]. Additionally, inhibitory transmission onto LH4 KO hippocampal interneurons was unaffected [19]. Thus, LH4-independent mechanisms also contribute to the modulation of inhibitory transmission in the hippocampus. Mechanistically, LH4 anchors GABAARs at inhibitory synapses, thereby controlling receptor synaptic abundance [18–20]. Supporting this observation, synaptic clustering of GABAARs was strongly decreased in both LH4 KD and KO hippocampal neurons [18–20]. Tonic inhibition mediated by extrasynaptic GABAARs was enhanced in LH4 KO CA1 pyramidal neurons; likely due to compensatory adaptation because of reduced fast phasic currents [19]. Given that tonic and phasic inhibition are largely mediated by different GABAAR subtypes [49,50], these data indicated LH4 may regulate GABAARs in a subunit-dependent manner. Similarly in cerebellum, γ2-containing GABAARs associated with LH4 were targeted to synapses [18,21], By contrast, δ-containing GABAARs which lacked LH4 were localized extrasynaptically [21], The presence of the δ subunit prevented incorporation of the γ2 subunit into GABAARs and highlighted an important mechanism of GABAAR assembly that defines distinct subcellular distributions of different GABAAR subtypes [21] (Figure 1B). Although LH4 is critical for inhibitory transmission, loss of LH4 did not change mIPSC decay kinetics in hippocampal neurons [18,19]. Additionally, despite observations that LH4 did not alter GABAAR sensitivity to GABA, 4,5,6,7-tetrahydroisoxazolo(5,4-c)pyridin-3-ol (THIP), or picrotoxin in Xenopus oocytes [18], it remains to be investigated whether these pharmacological observations extrapolate to other GABAAR-targeting compounds at different binding sites on the receptor (see Outstanding Questions). Collectively, these studies show that LH4 controls synaptic clustering of GABAARs in a subunit-, input-, and cell-specific manner without affecting cell surface trafficking or GABAAR properties.Further observations have demonstrated that LH4 works in concert with neuroligin2 (NL2), a cell adhesion protein important for inhibitory synapse development and function [51–53], to regulate GABAergic synapses. Both proteomic and biochemical data have demonstrated that LH4 is an integral component of the native GABAAR complex and interacts with NL2 [18–21]. Similarly, LH4 has been identified as a major NL2 binding partner in a NL2 proteomic screening [20]. In neurons, NL2 and LH4 were highly colocalized at inhibitory synapses and regulated each other’s synaptic clustering [18,20,21], In line with this relationship, NL2 clusters and GABAergic synapse density are strongly reduced in both hippocampal and cerebellar neurons lacking LH4, indicating a critical role of LH4 in the regulation of GABAergic synapse development [18–20]. Additionally, in NL2 KO neurons, LH4 punctaare significantly decreased [20], Notably, region- and cell-specific deficits of NL2 and GABAAR clusters are observed in the cerebellum from LH4 KO mice [20], which reinforces the conclusion in hippocampus that LH4 regulates GABAergic transmission onto excitatory pyramidal neurons, but not inhibitory interneurons [19]. This observation poses an interesting question as to whether LH4 regulation of GABAergic transmission is exclusive to excitatory neurons across the CNS or if subpopulations of GABAergic interneurons are also regulated. Although LH4 is important for GABAAR retention at inhibitory synapses, it did not appear to regulate forward trafficking and cell surface expression of GABAARs. Indeed, in heterologous cells, coexpression of LH4 with GABAARs did not increase surface expression of receptors [18,19]. Similarly, GABA-evoked GABAAR-mediated whole-cell currents and surface expression of GABAARs were not altered in LH4 KD or KO hippocampal neurons [18,19]. Lastly, LH4 KO has been reported to induce premature death, as well as motor deficiencies and increased seizure susceptibility [20]. However, another study reported normal viability of LH4 KO mice [19]. Differences between the two studies could be attributed to different gene KO strategies and/or mouse strains. Altogether, the findings discussed above indicate that LH4 critically regulates GABAergic synaptogenesis, likely in conjunction with NL2, and stabilizes GABAARs and other inhibitory proteins at synapses.

Clptm1 Negatively Regulates Early Forward Trafficking of GABAARsIn addition to mechanisms that contribute to anchoring of receptors at synapses, intracellular events, such as regulation of early GABAARs trafficking, can influence inhibitory synaptic strength. Of note, a recent study identified the transmembrane protein Clptm1 (Figure 1A) as a negative regulator of early GABAAR forward trafficking and GABAergic transmission [24]. Cleft lip/palate are developmental disorders that affect the formation of the face with various degrees of symmetry and severity [54], which can be due to chromosomal translocation of Clptm1 [55]. Pharmacological manipulation of GABAAR-mediated signaling can promote cleft palate facial deformities in rodent models indicating possible GABAAR involvement [56,57]. Using a proteomic approach to identify Y2-containing GABAAR binding partners, Clptm1 was discovered as a candidate and shown subsequently to decrease GABAAR-mediated whole-cell currents in heterologous cells [24], indicating negative regulation of GABAAR trafficking. Furthermore, Clptm1 was associated with both synaptic and extrasynaptic GABAAR subunits [24], suggesting a lack of subunit specificity required for receptor interaction. Unlike LH4, which promoted GABAAR synaptic retention independent of receptor forward trafficking [18,19], Clptm1 negatively regulated early forward trafficking of GABAARs through increased intracellular confinement, specifically in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and, to a lesser degree, in the Golgi apparatus [24] Figure 1B). As a result, GABAAR surface expression was reduced by Clptm1 in both heterologous cells and hippocampal neurons. Physiologically, Clptm1 bidirectionally regulated GABAergic transmission both in vitro and in vivo; overexpression reduced and KD enhanced the amplitude of mIPSCs, respectively. Additionally, tonic inhibitory currents generated by extrasynaptic GABAARs were similarly affected. Importantly, both glycine-receptor-mediated currents in heterologous cells and excitatory transmission in hippocampal neurons were unaltered by Clptm1 manipulation [24], which showed Clptm1 -specific regulation of GABAARs. Taken together, Clptm1 modulates both phasic and tonic inhibition through negative regulation of synaptic and extrasynaptic GABAARs, respectively.Intriguingly, Clptm1 also modulated bidirectional activity-dependent inhibitory synaptic scaling; likely due to interactions with GABAARs in a subunit-independent manner and thus uniformly regulates all postsynaptic GABAARs [24]. Indeed, overexpression of Clptm1 mimicked homeostatic downregulation of inhibitory transmission, and conversely, Clptm1 KD induced homeostatic up-regulation of inhibitory transmission. Notably, both KD and overexpression occluded homeostatic compensation in the same direction, but not the opposite direction, indicating the existence of Clptm1-independent pathways of inhibitory homeostatic regulation.

Shisa7 Interacts with GABAARs to Regulate GABAergic TransmissionGiven the similar homology of Shisa6–9 family proteins (Box 1), one might expect Shisa7 association with excitatory synapses. An early study reported that Shisa7 regulated total and surface expression of AMPAR subunits, without modulating kinetic properties, including deactivation, desensitization, and recovery from desensitization in human embryonic kidney (HEK) cells [48]. However, a subsequent study found that Shisa7 could modulate AMPAR desensitization and recovery from desensitization, but not deactivation, in HEK cells and prolonged miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) in CA1 pyramidal neurons [58]. Currently, it remains unclear what accounts for the discrepancies between these two studies. Intrigued by these observations, we further investigated Shisa7; counterintuitively, we found that unlike other Shisas localized at glutamatergic synapses [34,35], both overexpressed and endogenous Shisa7 localized to GABAergic synapses [25]. Biochemical studies in heterologous cells further showed that Shisa7 interacted with GABAARs in a subunit-dependent manner. For instance, Shisa7 could be coimmunoprecipitated with α1, α2 or γ2, but not β2 or β3 subunits, which demonstrated a subunit specificity for Shisa7 interaction, as well as possible variable stoichiometry of the GABAAR–Shisa7 complex. Furthermore, mutagenesis demonstrated that an N-terminal 20-amino-acid stretch unique to Shisa7 was critical for GABAAR interaction [25]. In addition, endogenous Shisa7 associated with GABAARs in the hippocampus [25], consistent with a recent GABAAR proteomic study [22]. Taken together, although all Shisa6–9 contain the AMPAR binding domain in their C termini, and can interact with AMPARs in heterologous cells when they are coexpressed [48,58], our work shows that endogenous Shisa7 is localized at GABAergic synapses and associates with GABAARs.Functionally, Shisa7 promoted GABAAR trafficking to the cell surface in heterologous cells [25]. In hippocampal cultures, KO of Shisa7 significantly reduces GABAAR abundance at the cell surface and inhibitory synapses [25] (Figure 2). Consistently, both single-cell genetic deletion and global KO of Shisa7 strongly reduced GABAergic transmission onto hippocampal CA1 neurons [25], highlighting that Shisa7 is critical for GABAAR-mediated synaptic currents. It is noteworthy that Shisa7 regulated diverse GABAergic inputs onto CA1 pyramidal neurons. Indeed, both soma-and dendrite-targeting inhibitory inputs were decreased in CA1 pyramidal neurons lacking Shisa7 [25]. Consistent with a previous report [58], excitatory transmission was not altered in Shisa7 KO hippocampal neurons [25]. Finally, given that Shisa7 regulates inhibitory synaptic transmission through GABAAR interaction, it elicits the question as to whether Shisa7 regulates tonic inhibition mediated by extrasynaptic GABAARs (Figure 1B and see Outstanding Questions).While the Shisa7 study by our group was in progress, a report identified that overexpressed Shisa7 over 1 week colocalized with Homer1 in hippocampal neurons, and thus localized to excitatory glutamatergic synapses [58]. Notably, the prolonged overexpression of Shisa7 resulted in dendritic spine loss, raising the question about the structural basis underlying the colocalization of overexpressed Shisa7 and Homer1. In our work, Shisa7 was overexpressed for <24 h or under the control of neuronal synapsin1 promoter for mild expression [25]. Under these conditions, we have consistently observed that Shisa7 colocalized with inhibitory, but not excitatory synaptic markers [25]. We have also noticed that prolonged Shisa7 overexpression (i.e., >48 h), similar to that previously reported [58], often results in neuronal degeneration, such as cell death or morphological abnormalities. In addition to overexpression, our work has shown that endogenous Shisa7 in hippocampal neurons largely colocalized with gephyrin and GABAAR subunits [25]. Currently, the reason for the discrepancies between these two studies is unclear. It is possible that different overexpression conditions may explain the differences and we also cannot rule out the possibility that subcellular localization of Shisa7 could be dynamically regulated.

While the Shisa7 study by our group was in progress, a report identified that overexpressed Shisa7 over 1 week colocalized with Homer1 in hippocampal neurons, and thus localized to excitatory glutamatergic synapses [58]. Notably, the prolonged overexpression of Shisa7 resulted in dendritic spine loss, raising the question about the structural basis underlying the colocalization of overexpressed Shisa7 and Homer1. In our work, Shisa7 was overexpressed for <24 h or under the control of neuronal synapsin1 promoter for mild expression [25]. Under these conditions, we have consistently observed that Shisa7 colocalized with inhibitory, but not excitatory synaptic markers [25]. We have also noticed that prolonged Shisa7 overexpression (i.e., >48 h), similar to that previously reported [58], often results in neuronal degeneration, such as cell death or morphological abnormalities. In addition to overexpression, our work has shown that endogenous Shisa7 in hippocampal neurons largely colocalized with gephyrin and GABAAR subunits [25]. Currently, the reason for the discrepancies between these two studies is unclear. It is possible that different overexpression conditions may explain the differences and we also cannot rule out the possibility that subcellular localization of Shisa7 could be dynamically regulated.

The strength of inhibitory synapses is primarily determined both by presynaptic release of GABA and activation of postsynaptic GABAARs [4]. Inhibitory postsynaptic responses can be influenced by GABAAR abundance at synapses [59,60] and different subunit compositions of GABAARs that ultimately determine the ion conductance and GABAAR kinetic properties that shape IPSCs [61–64]. Specifically, KO of Shisa7 increased the weighted Tdecay of IPSCs in hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neurons [25], indicating the possibility that Shisa7 normally speeds up IPSC decay kinetics. Indeed, in a coculture model, coexpression of Shisa7 reduced the weighted Tdecay of IPSCs produced by α1β2γ2 or α2β3γ2 GABAARs. In addition, Shisa7 decreased the weighted time constant, as well as both fast and slow components of deactivation of these GABAARs expressed in HEK cells. However, desensitization of α1β2γ2 GABAARs is unaffected by Shisa7 [25]. These observations support the hypothesis that Shisa7 can speed up decay kinetics of GABAARs. Although these data demonstrate Shisa7-dependent modulation of both α1- and α2-containing GABAARs, it is currently unknown whether Shisa7 affects other synaptic and extrasynaptic GABAARs. Additionally, it remains to be seen whether Shisa7 regulates other GABAAR kinetic properties, such as channel activation and recovery from desensitization. Nevertheless, the finding of Shisa7 modulation of GABAAR gating provides new insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of inhibitory transmission. Future studies on the molecular basis of Shisa7 interaction with GABAARs, using mutagenesis to probe Shisa7 functional domains underlying modulation of GABAAR kinetics, and structural determination of the GABAAR-Shisa7 complex will further provide mechanistic understanding of the regulation of GABAAR gating by Shisa7 (see Outstanding Questions).

Shisa7 Modulation of GABAAR PharmacologyGABAARs are major therapeutic targets for drugs such as benzodiazepines, barbiturates, neurosteroids, and general anesthetics [65] (Figure 3). Among them, benzodiazepines, one of the most frequently prescribed medicines for treatment of anxiety and sleep disorders [66,67], bind to GABAARs as positive allosteric modulators (PAMs) and increase the responsiveness of the receptor to endogenous GABA and other GABAAR agonists [68] (Box 2). Indeed, diazepam (DZ), the prototype of the benzodiazepines, can significantly potentiate GABA-induced whole-cell currents in vitro [2]. In addition, benzodiazepines modulate both synaptic and extrasynaptic GABAARs in vivo through a binding site residing at the interface between the α (i.e., α1–3 and α5) and γ2 subunits and a secondary binding site in the β/α interface [69–73]. Thus, both phasic and tonic inhibition can be modulated by benzodiazepines [74].Our recent work has revealed a critical role for Shisa7 in the regulation of DZ pharmacology. Specifically, our data show that Shisa7 can significantly enhance DZ-induced potentiation of GABAAR currents in heterologous cells [25]. Conversely, in hippocampal neurons, KO of Shisa7 strongly diminished the potentiating effects of DZ on GABA-evoked whole-cell currents and GABAergic transmission, suggesting that Shisa7 is involved in the physiological action of DZ in neurons. Supporting this notion, the hypnotic/sedative and anxiolytic-like effects of DZ in Shisa7 KO mice were severely blunted, demonstrating that Shisa7 modulation of GABAARs contributes to the therapeutic effects of DZ in vivo. It is worth noting that although Shisa7 can modulate DZ effects both in vitro and in vivo, it is not an absolute requirement for benzodiazepines’ actions, as benzodiazepines can still potentiate GABAAR response in heterologous cells transfected with GABAAR subunits without Shisa7 [25,75–77]. Additional experiments will be required to determine whether Shisa7 modulation of DZ effects in vivo is GABAAR subunit specific. Furthermore, whether Shisa7 modulates other compounds targeting GABAARs currently remains largely unknown, although Shisa7 does not alter GABA affinity of the α2β3γ2 GABAARs (see supplemental information in [25]). Since Shisa7 interacts with both α and γ,2 subunits and the classic benzodiazepine binding site is also located at the α and γ2 subunit interface, it is possible that other compounds interacting with GABAARs at the similar site, such as zolpidem and other non-benzodiazepines (Figure 3) [78], might also be subject to Shisa7 regulation. Nevertheless, these observations highlight that GABAAR pharmacological properties can be regulated by an auxiliary subunit, providing a new avenue for examining and developing GABAAR psychopharmacology in the future.

Concluding Remarks and Future PerspectivesAdvances in our understanding of the molecular mechanisms regulating excitatory transmission and synaptic plasticity have been furthered with the realization that iGluRs are critically regulated by auxiliary subunits. Recent evidence has now expanded upon these observations by revealing a set of GABAAR auxiliary subunits that can modulate the function of these receptors. As discussed, transmembrane accessory proteins, LH4, Clptm1, and Shisa7, all interact with GABAARs and regulate inhibitory transmission. Since GABAergic dysfunction has been implicated in a variety of CNS diseases [7,79,80], the discovery and characterization of GABAAR accessory proteins highlight a new aspect of research into the regulation of GABAARs in both healthy and abnormal function, as well as the possibility of these auxiliary subunits being druggable targets (see Outstanding Questions).Although GABAAR-targeting medications produce beneficial and clinically desired effects, their use can still result in adverse effects that include sedation, amnesia, physical dependence, and abuse liability. Indeed, benzodiazepines are highly addictive, and misuse/abuse of benzodiazepines have resulted in serious health consequences globally [81,82]. Therefore, it is important to actively seek novel compounds targeting GABAARs that are nonaddictive and/or have fewer side effects. As has been exemplified with auxiliary subunits for other receptors and ion channels [44], it is possible that GABAAR-associated transmembrane proteins could represent a new class of druggable proteins that can be exploited in GABAAR pharmaceutical development. Indeed, LH4, Clptm1, and Shisa7 can regulate surface and/or synaptic abundance of GABAARs, albeit by different mechanisms, and thus it is plausible that they could modulate sensitivity to compounds targeting surface or synaptic GABAARs. Therefore, development of novel pharmacological reagents that can modulate trafficking and targeting of GABAARs mediated by these proteins could be a promising approach. In addition, the observation of Shisa7 modulation of DZ pharmacology indicates that future drug screening and pharmacological studies on the GABAAR–Shisa7 complex could be utilized to improve benzodiazepine drug efficacy and mitigate adverse effects. Further testing of potential LH4 and/or Clptm1 regulation of GABAAR pharmacology might also generate additional opportunities for GABAAR pharmacology development. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the interaction of these novel transmembrane proteins with GABAARs and the regulation of GABAAR trafficking and/or pharmacology by these proteins are incompletely understood. Therefore, caution should be exercised in developing drug development strategies based on our current understanding of these GABAAR accessory proteins.GABAAR trafficking plays a fundamental role in the regulation of inhibitory synaptic strength, and abnormal trafficking of GABAARs has been observed in a variety of brain disorders [4,80,83]. Thus, it will continue to be important to determine the molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of GABAAR biology and trafficking by its accessory proteins. While LH4 promotes retention of GABAARs at synapses [18–21], Clptm1 negatively regulates GABAARs through inhibition of forward trafficking in the ER and Golgi apparatus [24]. It appears that there is dynamic regulation of GABAAR assembly by non-αβ subunits in cerebellum which influences not only their localization, but also whether LH4 association occurs [21]. Although incorporation of the δ subunit into α6-containing GABAARs promotes extrasynaptic GABAAR trafficking by limiting incorporation of γ2 and LH4 in the cerebellum, it remains to be seen whether this competition between non-αβ subunits is generalizable across the brain or whether this observation is brain-region specific. In contrast to LH4, Clptm1 regulates GABAARs regardless of subunit composition and is not directly involved in the stabilization of receptors at synaptic or extrasynaptic domains [24]. Although these initial studies have provided the foundation for further characterization of GABAAR transmembrane accessory proteins, a gap of knowledge exists as to whether mutations and/or changes in expression of these accessory proteins contribute to the pathophysiology of human neurological diseases, such as through alterations in GABAAR trafficking. It is also unknown whether LH4, Clptm1, and Shisa7 synergize with one another, work in parallel, or operate separately to regulate GABAAR trafficking and inhibitory transmission (see Outstanding Questions). Moreover, how these transmembrane accessory proteins functionally interact with other cytosolic proteins binding to GABAARs, such as gephyrin and GABAAR-associated protein (GABARAP) [83,84], to regulate receptor trafficking and function remains largely unclear. In addition, understanding the role of the auxiliary proteins in the context of native GABAAR complexes with other binding proteins will provide additional insight into molecular mechanisms regulating neural inhibition. Furthermore, defining how these accessory proteins contribute to various forms of GABAergic plasticity may reveal undiscovered mechanisms underlying the dynamic regulation of neuronal inhibition. Finally, future research using both genetic screening in humans and postmortem studies could prove invaluable in contributing to our understanding of GABAAR auxiliary proteins in human physiology, as well as generate interest in the possibility of developing new GABAAR-directed therapies by targeting these novel accessory proteins in complex with the receptor.
