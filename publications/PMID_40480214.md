TMEM63A, -B, and -C constitute a mammalian family of mechanosensitive ion
channels that are mutated in neurodevelopmental disorders. The molecular
mechanisms underlying TMEM63 activation by force and the impact of
disease-associated mutations have not been clarified. Here, we elucidate the
structural and functional bases of a prevalent TMEM63B mutation p.V44M. We first
found that TMEM63B p.V44M and the homologous TMEM63A p.V53M are gain-of-function
mutations that do not enhance channel activity but instead evoke constitutive
lipid scramblase activity. We then solved TMEM63A p.V53M mutant structures in
both closed and lipid-open states, which revealed major rearrangements of
pore-lining helices, creating a lateral cleft across the membrane. Simulation
studies revealed lipid scrambling through this cleft. The structural
rearrangements were triggered by disruption of a surface-proximal hydrophobic
latch, a putative force-sensing module that includes a cluster of disease
mutation sites. Our findings provide mechanistic insight into TMEM63
channelopathies and suggest a possible force-sensing mechanism.

INTRODUCTION

Mechanosensitive (MS) ion channels are pore-forming membrane proteins that detect and transduce mechanical forces into electrical signals1–4. They play critical roles in a broad range of physiological and pathological processes5–7. In mammals, three families of MS channels have been identified, including PIEZOs, Transmembrane channel-like (TMC) proteins and TMEM63s. PIEZOs, including PIEZO1 and PIEZO2, are involved in diverse physiological processes, such as the sense of touch, blood pressure regulation, vascular development and interoception8–10. TMCs, particularly TMC1 and TMC2, are receptors transducing sound-evoked mechanical information into electrical signals in sensory hair cells of the inner ear and are critical for hearing and balance11–13. TMEM63s, including TMEM63A, -B and -C, are broadly expressed high-threshold force sensors, with physiological roles yet to be fully revealed14–17.

Recent clinical studies have highlighted important roles of TMEM63s in brain development, as evident by their disease association in human neurodevelopmental disorders. For example, TMEM63A heterozygous variants have been identified in patients with hypo-myelinating leukodystrophies, associated with global developmental delays and seizures18–21. TMEM63C biallelic variants were reported in humans with hereditary spastic paraplegias22. All these patients have delayed motor development, with some showing mild intellectual disability. Most notably, 10 heterozygous mutations of TMEM63B were recently identified in patients with severe early-onset developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE)23,24. These patients displayed a wide range of neurodevelopmental symptoms, including intellectual disability, motor and cortical visual impairment, poor language skills, epilepsy and dysphagia. Despite identification of disease-associated mutations in TMEM63s, the precise molecular mechanisms by which these mutations affect ion channel structure and function remain largely unknown.

TMEM63s and their homologs in plants (OSCA channels) share a common protein fold with the TMCs and Ca2+-activated TMEM16 family of ion channels and scramblases7. The protein fold features an unusual proteolipidic pore25, with the cytoplasmic side exposed laterally to the membrane. This structural fold enables the TMEM16s to passively move ions and/or phospholipids across the membrane bilayer26,27. The scramblase activity of TMEM16s has been shown to regulate blood coagulation28,29, cell-cell fusion30,31, bone formation32, angiogenesis33 and viral infection34. Recently, we and others reported that TMEM63s and TMCs also exhibit scramblase activity under certain circumstances35–38, but it is unclear how these ion channels fulfill scramblase activity and whether this has any physiological significance.

The molecular mechanism of force sensing is a fundamental question for MS ion channels. In PIEZO channels, numerous structural and functional analyses have pointed to an apparent force-sensing mechanism involving membrane curvature39–43. In the resting state, the giant trimeric PIEZO structure adopts a bowl shape in the cell membrane, resulting in a spontaneous membrane curvature. In the presence of physical force, this membrane curvature is believed to be flattened to induce conformational changes in PIEZOs, leading to opening of the channel pore. Compared to PIEZOs, TMEM63s are much smaller and function as monomers15,35,44,45, which are unlikely to deform the cell membrane in the resting state. As such, TMEM63s likely use a distinct mechanism to sense force.

In this study, we explored the structural and functional basis of TMEM63 channelopathies, with a focus on the human (h) TMEM63B p.V44M mutation, which has been linked to severe DEE in humans23,24. We show that hTMEM63B p.V44M and corresponding hTMEM63A p.V53M are gain-of-function (GOF) mutations inducing significant cell toxicity in heterologous cells. Electrophysiological recordings detect little enhanced ion channel activity by either mutation. Instead, we found that both p.V44M in hTMEM63B and p.V53M in hTMEM63A evoke constitutive lipid scramblase activity. To uncover the underlying mechanism, we determined cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of hTMEM63A p.V53M mutant in both closed state (3.6 Å) and lipid-open state (2.9 Å, bound with extensive lipids). Structural analyses revealed major rearrangements of pore-lining transmembrane (TM) helices, including outward bending of TM4a and kinks of TM3a and TM5a, which create a lateral lipid translocation cleft. Coarse-grained molecular dynamics (MD) simulations detected spontaneous lipid scrambling events in the lipid-open state of the mutant, but not in the closed wildtype (WT) protein. Further structural analyses identified the V44 residue in the WT hTMEM63B and the V53 residue in the WT hTMEM63A as part of a conserved hydrophobic network, the “hydrophobic latch”, which maintains the protein in the closed state. Notably, the p.V44M and many other disease mutations in both hTMEM63B and hTMEM63A cluster within or near this hydrophobic latch. We propose that the hydrophobic latch is a structural element affected in many TMEM63 channelopathies. Furthermore, the latch is located just beneath the surface of the membrane, a location that may allow it to sense membrane thickness. We propose a model in which the hydrophobic latch serves as a force-sensing element for WT TMEM63 activation.

RESULTSThe hTMEM63B p.V44M is a GOF disease mutationThe hTMEM63B p.V44M mutation site is localized at the extracellular end of TM0, with proximity to the pore-lining TM4 (Figure 1A). Another disease-causing missense mutation p.T481N in TM4 was identified in the same region, the narrowest part of the presumed pore (Figure 1A). Both p.V44 and p.T481 are largely conserved within the human TMEM63 family (Figure 1A and B), indicating their importance in protein folding, trafficking or function. Given that the hTMEM63B p.V44M causes disease in heterozygous human patients23,24 and heterozygous TMEM63B knockout (KO) mice are asymptomatic16,17, p.V44M could be either a dominant-negative or a dominant GOF mutation. To test these possibilities, we overexpressed the hTMEM63B p.V44M mutant or the corresponding hTMEM63A p.V53M mutant in HEK293T cells and assessed its cytotoxicity. We first employed the MultiTox-Fluor Multiplex cytotoxicity assay, in which viable and dead cells are differentially detected with two different fluorescent reagents (Figure 1C). Compared to WT hTMEM63B, the p.V44M mutant exhibited significantly higher cytotoxicity (Figure 1D, Left). When combined with the p.T481N disease mutation, the p.V44M/p.T481N double mutant (DM) exhibited a further increase in cytotoxicity (Figure 1D, Left). Similarly, the p.V53M mutation in hTMEM63A induced significantly higher cell toxicity (Figure 1D, Right), which was further increased by combining with the p.T469N mutation (Figure 1D, Right). As a positive control, the GOF mutant of the cold receptor TRPM8 ion channel in rat (p.V976S46) induced substantial cell toxicity (Figure 1D).We further confirmed these results with a cell death assay using CYTOX orange nucleic acid stain, which penetrates and labels dead cells with compromised plasma membrane integrity and does not cross the membranes of viable cells. Expression of the hTMEM63B p.V44M mutant led to substantial cell death, compared to the WT hTMEM63B (Figure 1E). The p.V44M/p.T481N DM evoked a further increase in cell death, to a level compared to that induced by rTRPM8 p.V976S GOF mutant (Figure 1E). Similar results were observed for hTMEM63A mutants (Figure 1F). Together, these observations suggest a GOF nature for disease mutations p.V44M and p.T481N in hTMEM63B.The hTMEM63B p.V44M mutation causes little enhanced channel activityTo elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the GOF phenotype of the hTMEM63B p.V44M mutation, we examined whether and how this mutation evokes enhanced hTMEM63B channel activity. First, we investigated constitutive leak currents through hTMEM63B in the absence of mechanical stimuli. For this, we performed whole-cell electrophysiological recordings in HEK293T cells (Figure 2A). Relative to hTMEM63B WT channels, the overexpression of either p.V44M single mutant or p.V44M/p.T481N DM elicits little detectable leak currents (Figure 2A and B). Similarly, no leak current was detected with the expression of hTMEM63A p.V53M single mutant or p.V53M/p.T469N DM (Figure 2A and B). In contrast, the positive control rTRPM8 pV976S GOF mutant displayed significant leak currents (Figure 2A and B). These data suggest that the p.V44M mutation induces little constitutive channel activity in the absence of mechanical stimulation.Next, we assessed hTMEM63B MS channel activity. Using cell-attached electrophysiological recordings, negative pressures were applied within the recording pipette to induce membrane stretch (Figure 2C). For these experiments we used WT HEK293T cells that express endogenous PIEZO1. Consistent with our prior cell-attached recordings15, we found no confounding background currents activated by negative pressure. The membrane potential was held constant at +80 mV, which evokes substantially larger outward currents than inward currents at −80 mV. We detected significant stretch-activated currents in HEK293T cells expressing WT hTMEM63B (Imax = 102.9 ± 11.3 pA; n = 6) (Figure 2C and D) or hTMEM63A (Imax = 122.3 ± 16.1 pA; n = 7) (Figure 2F and G). Surprisingly, reduced stretch-activated currents were detected in both the hTMEM63B p.V44M mutant (Imax = 45.8 ± 4.6 pA; n = 6) (Figure 2C and D) and the hTMEM63A p.V53M mutant (Imax = 55.2 ± 6.5 pA; n = 5) (Figure 2F and G). The currents were further reduced in the hTMEM63B p.V44M/p.T481N DM (Imax = 29.0 ± 4.5 pA; n = 4) (Figure 2C and D) and the hTMEM63A p.V53M/p.T469N DM (Imax = 31.5 ± 4.9 pA; n = 4) (Figure 2F and G). We also generated activation curves (current vs. pressure) for these mutants and found that mechanosensitivity was not affected (Figure 2E and H).To assess the effect of protein trafficking, we performed a biotinylation assay to examine protein expression at the cell surface. In both hTMEM63B and hTMEM63A, either the single mutant or the DM displayed substantially reduced cell surface expression (Figure 2I), which was most likely due to reduced total protein expression (Figure 2I). These findings suggest that the p.V44M mutation in hTMEM63B leads to diminished total and cell surface expression, thereby resulting in reduced stretch-activated current (Figure 2D). Overall, our electrophysiological characterizations do not support changes in ion channel activity and mechanosensitivity as an explanation for the dominant GOF phenotype caused by the hTMEM63B p.V44M mutation.The p.V44M mutation confers constitutive lipid scramblase activity in hTMEM63BTMEM63s share a conserved protein fold with TMEM16s and TMCs. Some TMEM16 members (e.g., TMEM16E, TMEM16F and TMEM16K) have dual functions as ion channels and lipid scramblases7. Scramblases facilitate the movement of phospholipids across the membrane bilayer and can expose phosphatidylserine (PS) to the outer layer of the cell surface, which could induce apoptosis47. Therefore, we next examined whether the p.V44M mutation confers lipid scramblase activity to hTMEM63B. For this, we measured scramblase activity with an established assay38 that detects PS exposure with fluorescently tagged Annexin V (AnV) using confocal microscopy. PS is predominantly localized in the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane and can only be labelled by extracellular AnV when exposed to the outer leaflet. As shown in Figure 3A and B, we detected little PS exposure with overexpression of either hTMEM63B or hTMEM63A WT proteins in TMEM16F KO HEK293T cells. Remarkably, substantial spontaneous PS exposure was observed in cells expressing the hTMEM63B p.V44M or the TMEM63A p.V53M single mutant (Figure 3A and B). Additionally, the spontaneous PS exposure was further increased with expression of the hTMEM63B DM or the hTMEM63A DM (Figure 3A and B), which is well aligned with the GOF phenotypes in the cell toxicity assay (Figure 1D-F). Moreover, plasma membrane localization was notably reduced for the mutants compared to WT proteins (Figure 3A and B), in line with our biotinylation assay (Figure 2I). Together, these data indicate that, despite reduced plasma membrane localization, the p.V44M mutation enables constitutive scramblase activity in hTMEM63B, which may account for its GOF nature.Cryo-EM structures of the hTMEM63A p.V53M mutant reveal a lipid translocation cleftTo unravel the structural basis of the GOF scramblase activity in the hTMEM63B p.V44M disease mutant, we attempted to determine its atomic structure with single-particle cryo-EM, but no high-resolution reconstruction was achieved in either detergent lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG)/cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS) or nanodiscs. However, we successfully determined the cryo-EM structure of the hTMEM63A p.V53M mutant in LMNG/CHS (Figure S1, Table S1). Within a single dataset, we obtained 3D reconstructions in two distinct conformations, which we assigned as a closed state (3.6 Å resolution) and a lipid-open state (2.9 Å resolution), respectively (Figure 4A and S1). These high-resolution maps enabled us to unambiguously model most of the protein (Figure 4B and S2), including the intracellular IL2 domain in the lipid-open state (Figure S2A), which had not been clearly resolved in previous TMEM63 structures15,35,44,45. Like the TMEM63A WT structure15,45, the p.V53M mutant structures in both states retained a monomeric architecture that consists of a transmembrane domain (TMD), containing 11 TMs (TM0-10), and a large cytoplasmic IL2 domain (the second intracellular loop between TM2 and TM3) (Figure 4B). The closed state structure closely resembles our previously reported hTMEM63A WT protein structure (PDB: 8EHW, all atom room mean square deviation (RMSD) 0.72 Å) (Figure S3A). In this state, lipid densities were observed within the TMD, particularly in the cytoplasmic pore cleft that is exposed to the membrane (Figure 4A and B). The extracellular part of the pore (the gate region) remains sealed by TM4 through tight packing against TM0 and TM6 (Figure 4C, Left).Strikingly, in the lipid-open state, the gate region becomes exposed to the membrane, thereby creating a continuous lateral cleft across the membrane bilayer plane (Figure 4C, Right), analogous to the Ca2+-induced opening of the lipid translocation pathway in nhTMEM16 (lipid scramblase in fungus Nectria haematococca)48 (Figure 4D). Lipid densities were observed extending through this cleft (Figure 4B and C), supporting its role as a lipid translocation pathway. The cleft is largely lined by hydrophilic or aromatic residues in TM4 (T469, W473, S476, S474, S481 and Y485), TM6 (N558, S563, G567, E571, R574, G577 and R584) and TM0 (D57 and C60) (Figure S3B). These residues together create a hydrophilic environment that likely hosts the phospholipid head groups during lipid scrambling, leaving the lipid tails in the membrane. Additionally, the lipid-open state displayed a shortened TMD (44 Å height), compared to the closed state (50 Å height) (Figure 4A), which may further facilitate lipid scrambling across the membrane bilayer.Accompanying the lateral cleft opening, the putative ion permeation pathway, lined by TM3-7, is enlarged in the lipid-open state (Figure S3C and D). However, due to the existence of lipids in the cleft, this enlarged ion permeation pathway is mostly restricted (Figure S3E), which potentially explains the lack of leak current in hTMEM63B p.V44M and hTMEM63A p.V53M mutants (Figure 2A and B).MD simulations predict lipid scrambling by hTMEM63A p.V53M in the lipid-open stateWe next employed MD simulations to further explore lipid scrambling by the hTMEM63A p.V53M mutant in the lipid-open state. First, we performed all-atom MD simulations, in which the model was embedded in a pure POPC bilayer and solvated in TIP3P water with 150 mM KCl (Figure 5A, Left). To better understand the impact of the p.V53M mutation, we modeled a lipid-open state of hTMEM63A WT from the p.V53M mutant structure with the native valine residue restored. In a 2 μs-long equilibrium simulation (Table S2), we observed up to three lipids residing in the lateral cleft in hTMEM63A p.V53M for more than 1.2 μs that collectively explore the full z-extent of the cleft (Figure 5B, Left). For hTMEM63A WT modeled in the lipid-open state, we also observed three lipids stably lodging in the cleft for up to 1 μs (Figure 5B, Right). At this timescale, although we did not observe any complete scrambling events, a continuous path is evident in the lipid head group densities (Figure 5A, Right), suggesting that the lipid-open state structure of hTMEM63A p.V53M represents a scrambling-competent state.All-atom MD simulations are limited by the short timescales that can be reached, which makes it difficult to capture lipid scrambling events. Therefore, we next performed coarse-grained (CG) MD simulation with Martini349 to increase sampling by reaching longer timescales. We selected Martini3 because it accurately describes protein surface geometry and has shown scrambling for TMEM16 family members50 and insertases51. Focusing on hTMEM63A, we coarse-grained the lipid-open structures simulated in our all-atom MD and a closed-state structure of hTMEM63A WT (PDB: 8EHW). We embedded each Martini3-resolution model in pure POPC solvated with regular type water and 150 mM NaCl, and then simulated for at least 10 μs (Table S3).To identify scrambling events, we tracked the average orientation of each lipid over time (Figure S4A and B, Right). An overlay of the positions of all lipid head groups during all scrambling events revealed numerous lipids in the lateral cleft in the lipid-open state of hTMEM63A p.V53M and WT, but not in closed state of hTMEM63A WT (Figure 5C). During a typical scrambling event, a lipid passed directly along the lateral cleft (Figure 5D, Left, Video S1). Cumulatively, within 10 μs, we recorded 58 events for hTMEM63A p.V53M (5.8 ± 2.2 events⋅μs−1) but only 18 (1.9 ± 1.4 events⋅μs−1) for hTMEM63A WT (Figure 5D, Middle), suggesting that the p.V53M mutation may increase scrambling rates in hTMEM63A. Each scrambling event took a shorter time on average for the p.V53M mutant (334 ns) compared with the WT protein (665 ns) (Figure 5D, Right). In addition, we observed a low-probability scrambling pathway outside the canonical cleft (Figure S4B, Video S2) that may allow out-of-the-cleft scrambling for lipids with large headgroups, a phenomenon documented in scramblases of the TMEM16 family7. Overall, our CG results suggest that lipid scrambling can occur through the lateral cleft in the lipid-open structure of the hTMEM63A p.V53M.Outward bending of TM4 leads to the opening of the lipid translocation cleftNext, we sought to reveal the protein conformational changes leading to the opening of the lipid translocation cleft. Compared to the closed state of hTMEM63A p.V53M mutant, the lipid-open state exhibited major rearrangements in the pore-lining TM3-5, with other TMs remaining largely unchanged (Figure 6A; Video S3). These rearrangements involved the outward bending of the extracellular part of TM4 (TM4a) and kinks of TM3a and TM5a (Figure 6B and C). The outward bending of TM4a detached it from TM0 and TM6 at the gate region, directly leading to the opening of the lipid translocation cleft (Figure 6B). The kinks of TM3a and TM5a occur at conserved P439 (TM3) and G521 (TM5) residues, respectively (Figure 6C), and cause outward inclines of the TM3a-Loop34 (the loop between TM3 and TM4) and the TM5a-Loop56 to be nearly parallel to the membrane plane (Figure 6A and C). The membrane-facing sides of the TM3a-Loop34-TM4a and the TM5a-Loop56 are highly hydrophobic, while the extracellular sides are predominantly hydrophilic (Figure 6C). This amphipathic nature allows these regions to closely associate with the membrane and may help stabilize the lipid-open state.To test whether these conformational changes account for the constitutive scramblase activity and GOF phenotype, we introduced mutations to the kink residue proline that breaks α-helices or to glycine that has high flexibility. Both residues allow sharp bending of α-helices. We reasoned that mutating these residues would prevent the kinks of TM3a and TM5a, thereby reducing the constitutive scramblase activity and GOF phenotype. Indeed, these replacements in hTMEM63A (p.P439V in TM3 and p.G521A in TM5) or hTMEM63B (p.P451V in TM3 and p.G533A in TM5) dramatically reduced the constitutive scramblase activity of the double mutants (Figure 6D). Similarly, the cytotoxicity GOF phenotype of hTMEM63A or hTMEM63B double mutants was rescued by these replacements (Figure 6E). Our confocal imaging (Figure 6D) and biotinylation assay (Figure S5A) showed that all these substitutions have either little effect or enhanced effect on the protein surface expression, ruling out the possibility that the loss of scramblase activities or cytotoxicity is caused by impaired protein trafficking. Together, these data suggest that the outward bending of TM4a and associated kinks of TM3a and TM5a drive the opening of the lipid translocation cleft, leading to the constitutive scramblase activity and GOF phenotype of hTMEM63B p.V44M and hTMEM63A p.V53M mutants. In WT TMEM63s, the V53 in hTMEM63A and the V44 in hTMEM63B are part of a conserved hydrophobic network at the gate region (Figure 7A). This network involves hydrophobic residues in TM0 (V53/I56 in hTMEM63A; V44/L47 in hTMEM63B), TM4 (I462/I463/F466/F467/L470/L471 in hTMEM63A; I474/I475/F478/F479/L482/L483 in hTMEM63B) and TM6 (A562/I566 in TMEM6A; A575/I579 in hTMEM63B) (Figure 7A). We hypothesized that this network, termed the hydrophobic latch, tightens TM4a against TM0 and TM6 in the closed state. However, replacing the valine with methionine would reduce the distance to be less than 2.5 Å (Figure S6B), leading to a steric clash. This clash may cause a subtle local movement of TM4 to maintain an allowable distance with F466, as observed in the hTMEM63A p.V53M mutant structure of the closed state (Figure S6B). Alternatively, it may cause a more dramatic bending movement of TM4a (collapse of the hydrophobic latch), as in the mutant structure of the lipid-open state (Figure S6B). Notably, most disease mutations identified thus far in hTMEM63A (p.I462N, p.G553V, p.Y559H and p.G567S)18–21 and hTMEM63B (p.V44M,p.T481N, p.I475del, p.G580S and p.G580C)23,24 cluster within or near the hydrophobic latch (Figure 7B), suggesting that it may be a common structural element causing TMEM63 channelopathies.Intriguingly, in the absence of force, the hydrophobic latch in hTMEM63s is located just beneath the outer surface of the membrane (Figure 7A and C, Left), in a position that might allow it to sense membrane thickness. Therefore, we propose a model for how TMEM63s could sense and respond to physical force. If force induces membrane thinning, the membrane surface-proximal part of the protein, including the hydrophobic latch, would be exposed to the hydrophilic extracellular environment, causing hydrophobic mismatch (Figure 7C, Middle). This energetically unfavorable hydrophobic mismatch may cause the protein to undergo conformational changes and transition to a more energy-favorable state that matches the thinned membrane. Indeed, in the hTMEM63A V53M lipid-open state, we observed diminished hydrophobic mismatch with re-embedding of the hydrophobic latch into the thinned membrane (Figure 7C, Right; Video S4), due to the outward bending of TM4a and kinks of TM3a and TM5a. As illustrated in Figure 7D, we hypothesize that the hydrophobic latch biases WT TMEM63s toward the closed conformation in the resting state. Membrane stretch, evoked by physical force applied to the membrane, induces membrane thinning, which exposes the hydrophobic latch and causes hydrophobic mismatch. This destabilizes the closed state and evokes rearrangements of TMs, i.e., outward bending of TM4a and kinks of TM3a and TM5a. Consequently, these conformational changes are predicted to open the gate region, allowing ion and lipid permeation. The kinked TM3a and TM5a may help stabilize the open state of TMEM63s in the thinned membrane in an energetically favorable state. Cumulatively, within 10 μs, we recorded 58 events for hTMEM63A p.V53M (5.8 ± 2.2 events⋅μs−1) but only 18 (1.9 ± 1.4 events⋅μs−1) for hTMEM63A WT (Figure 5D, Middle), suggesting that the p.V53M mutation may increase scrambling rates in hTMEM63A. Each scrambling event took a shorter time on average for the p.V53M mutant (334 ns) compared with the WT protein (665 ns) (Figure 5D, Right). In addition, we observed a low-probability scrambling pathway outside the canonical cleft (Figure S4B, Video S2) that may allow out-of-the-cleft scrambling for lipids with large headgroups, a phenomenon documented in scramblases of the TMEM16 family7. Overall, our CG results suggest that lipid scrambling can occur through the lateral cleft in the lipid-open structure of the hTMEM63A p.V53M.

A hydrophobic latch mechanism may underly TMEM63 channelopathy and force sensationWe next sought to understand how the outward bending of TM4a is triggered by the V to M replacement in hTMEM63s. In WT TMEM63s, the V53 in hTMEM63A and the V44 in hTMEM63B are part of a conserved hydrophobic network at the gate region (Figure 7A). This network involves hydrophobic residues in TM0 (V53/I56 in hTMEM63A; V44/L47 in hTMEM63B), TM4 (I462/I463/F466/F467/L470/L471 in hTMEM63A; I474/I475/F478/F479/L482/L483 in hTMEM63B) and TM6 (A562/I566 in TMEM6A; A575/I579 in hTMEM63B) (Figure 7A). In WT hTMEM63A or hTMEM63B, the V53 or V44 residue is positioned ~3.6 Å adjacent to F466 or F478 in TM4 (Figure S6A), allowing a hydrophobic interaction without steric clash (the van der Waals radius of a carbon atom is 1.7 Å). However, replacing the valine with methionine would reduce the distance to be less than 2.5 Å (Figure S6B), leading to a steric clash. This clash may cause a subtle local movement of TM4 to maintain an allowable distance with F466, as observed in the hTMEM63A p.V53M mutant structure of the closed state (Figure S6B). Alternatively, it may cause a more dramatic bending movement of TM4a (collapse of the hydrophobic latch), as in the mutant structure of the lipid-open state (Figure S6B). Notably, most disease mutations identified thus far in hTMEM63A (p.I462N, p.G553V, p.Y559H and p.G567S)18–21 and hTMEM63B (p.V44M,p.T481N, p.I475del, p.G580S and p.G580C)23,24 cluster within or near the hydrophobic latch (Figure 7B), suggesting that it may be a common structural element causing TMEM63 channelopathies.Intriguingly, in the absence of force, the hydrophobic latch in hTMEM63s is located just beneath the outer surface of the membrane (Figure 7A and C, Left), in a position that might allow it to sense membrane thickness. If force induces membrane thinning, the membrane surface-proximal part of the protein, including the hydrophobic latch, would be exposed to the hydrophilic extracellular environment, causing hydrophobic mismatch (Figure 7C, Middle). This energetically unfavorable hydrophobic mismatch may cause the protein to undergo conformational changes and transition to a more energy-favorable state that matches the thinned membrane. Indeed, in the hTMEM63A V53M lipid-open state, we observed diminished hydrophobic mismatch with re-embedding of the hydrophobic latch into the thinned membrane (Figure 7C, Right; Video S4), due to the outward bending of TM4a and kinks of TM3a and TM5a. As illustrated in Figure 7D, we hypothesize that the hydrophobic latch biases WT TMEM63s toward the closed conformation in the resting state. Membrane stretch, evoked by physical force applied to the membrane, induces membrane thinning, which exposes the hydrophobic latch and causes hydrophobic mismatch. This destabilizes the closed state and evokes rearrangements of TMs, i.e., outward bending of TM4a and kinks of TM3a and TM5a. Consequently, these conformational changes are predicted to open the gate region, allowing ion and lipid permeation. The kinked TM3a and TM5a may help stabilize the open state of TMEM63s in the thinned membrane in an energetically favorable state. TMEM63 disease mutations likely cause collapse of the hydrophobic latch, thus allowing the opening of the gate region in the absence of force (Figure 7D).

DISCUSSIONIn this study, we showed that the disease mutation p.V44M evokes constitutive scramblase activity in the mechanosensitive ion channel TMEM63B. By cryo-EM analysis of the corresponding hTMEM63A p.V53M mutant, we revealed the structural basis of this scramblase activity, which involved rearrangements of pore-lining helices to create a lateral lipid translocation cleft. Recently, the p.V44M mutation in the mouse TMEM63B was reported to evoke constitutive lipid scrambling35. p.V44 is located within a hydrophobic latch, which is surrounded by many other disease-causing mutations. Given that the hydrophobic latch localizes just beneath the external surface of the membrane, it may also serve as a force-sensing module to detect membrane thickness through a hydrophobic mismatch mechanism, which has been proposed for other MS channels, including gramicidin A52, MscL53 and TREK-254. We propose that the hydrophobic latch in TMEM63s is a general structural element involved in both channelopathies and force-induced activation. Our proposed force-sensing mechanism might be shared by the auditory mechanosensory transduction channel TMC1, given the structural similarity. Unlike the well-studied mechanosensitive PIEZO channels, which sense membrane curvature39–43, TMEM63s may distinctively sense the membrane thickness.By structural, functional and simulation studies of TMEM16F55–58 and nhTMEM1648,59,60, a credit card model of lipid scrambling has been proposed7, i.e., a hydrophilic cleft in scramblases, analogous to a credit card swipe, allows movement of lipid polar head-groups across the membrane bilayer, leaving the hydrophobic lipid tail in the membrane interior. Our hTMEM63A p.V53M structure in the lipid-open state and other recent studies of TMEM63s and OSCAs25,35,38,61 provide strong support that this credit card model may extend to TMEM63s and OSCAs. In our hTMEM63A p.V53M mutant structure in the lipid-open state, we observed a hydrophilic lipid translocation cleft, analogous to that reported in nhTMEM16 (Figure 4C and D). However, the conformational changes associated with opening of the lipid translocation cleft are different, i.e., hTMEM63A involves bending of TM4a and kinks of TM3a and TM5a (Figure 6A-C), while nhTMEM16 mainly involves movement of TM4 without kink of TM3 or TM548,59,60. An activating mutant of mammalian TMEM16F displayed movement of TM4 and kink of TM3, but little change of TM557. This difference between TMEM63s and TMEM16s might reflect their distinct activation modalities, i.e., TMEM63s respond to physical force, while TMEM16s mainly detect Ca2+.A hallmark of TMEM16/TMC/TMEM63/OSCA families, termed the transmembrane channel scramblase (TCS) superfamily26, is their potential dual functions as ion channels and lipid scramblases, with some cases in which this dual function has been reported for the same protein7. How does one protein fulfill these two functions? Are ion channel activity and scramblase activity evoked concomitantly or independently? In TMEM63s, the kinks of TM3a and TM5a are important for both activation of lipid scramblase (Figure 6D) and ion channel (Figure S5B) activities, suggesting some shared protein conformational changes are required for both functionalities. On the other hand, hTMEM63B p.V44M and hTMEM63A p.V53M mutants exhibit constitutive scramblase activity without inducing leak currents or altered mechanosensitivity (Figure 2 and 3), indicating that these two functionalities can be disentangled. Consistently, our hTMEM63A p.V53M cryo-EM analysis captured a lipid-open state with an opened lipid translocation cleft (Figure 4C) and an ion permeation pathway restricted by lipids (Figure S3D and E). Since hTMEM63A p.V53M, hTMEM63B p.V44M and OSCA1.1 p.F516A mutants61 are still able to be mechanically activated to conduct ions (Figure 2C-H), we speculate that TMEM63s and OSCAs have a yet-to-be-determined ion-open state, in which the ion permeation pathway is enlarged. Another recent structural study25 of WT OSCA1.2 in reconstituted liposomes captured a state in which both the lipid cleft and ion permeation pathway appear enlarged, based on which, a proteolipidic pore was proposed. It is unclear whether the OSCA1.2 WT channels in such reconstituted liposomes exhibited constitutive leak currents, but simulations indicate that it may conduct ions at the experimentally measured rates25. In mouse, heterozygous KO of Tmem63b causes little phenotype16,17 and heterozygous humans who carry the p.Arg325* truncating variant of TMEM63B seem to be asymptomatic23. These observations argue against haploinsufficiency as a pathogenic mechanism. Therefore, the heterozygous p.V44M mutation, which causes DEE in humans, is most likely due to GOF constitutive scramblase activity, and not due to reduced protein expression or ionic currents. TMEM63B has high expression in cortical neurons, both excitatory and inhibitory neurons, according to the single-nucleus transcriptome dataset (http://resources.sestanlab.org/PFC/). We predict constitutive scramblase activity of hTMEM63B p.V44M mutant would cause long-term exposure of PS lipids in the outer leaflet of plasma membrane of neurons. This uncontrolled PS exposure may have several detrimental effects on neurons, since inner leaflet localization of PS is coupled to various cellular functions and outer leaflet PS exposure serves as an important ‘eat me’ signal for phagocytes62. First, PS exposure in the synaptic membrane could induce elimination of the synapse by microglia, the macrophages in the brain, resulting in loss of synapses. Degenerating dendrites and dendritic spines have been demonstrated to be engulfed and removed by microglia in a PS-dependent manner63,64. Second, PS externalization in the soma surface membrane may evoke insults by microglia, causing neuronal cell death. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans of human patients carrying the TMEM63B p.V44M mutation show brain shrinkage along with white matter changes, signs of neuronal loss and defective myelination23. Elevated neuronal death has also been reported in mouse models of DEE with mutations in ALG13, Tuba1a and INPP4A65. Third, disruption of membrane asymmetry in neurons might induce abnormal signaling pathways involved in cell growth, migration and differentiation62. Cytotoxicity assayTo assess the gain-of-function effects of hTMEM63A and hTMEM63B mutations, we performed a cytotoxicity assay. HEK293T cells were grown in a 6-well plate to around 70% confluence. Transfections were performed using lipofectamine 3000 according to the manual instructions. Cells in each well were collected 30 h after transfection using trypsin and were resuspended in 0.5 mL cell culture medium. We first measured cell toxicity/viability using the MultiTox-Fluor Multiplex Cytotoxicity Assay kit (Promega, Madison, WI). Briefly, 50 µL of resuspended cells were mixed with equal volume of the MultiTox-Fluor reagent in 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Then the fluorescence at Ex400/Em505 (indicating dead cells or cytotoxicity) and Ex485/Em520 (indicating viable cells or viability) were measured with the BioTek Synergy™ Neo microplate reader (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). We also performed a cell death assay with CYTOX orange nucleic acid stain (S-11368, Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR), which can penetrate dead cells with compromised plasma membranes and yet will not cross the membranes of viable cells. The collected and resuspended cells were stained for 10 min at room temperature with CYTOX (1 µM, labeling dead cells) and Hoechst (5 µg/mL, labeling total cells including both dead and viable cells). The CYTOX-positive cells and total cells were quantified, and the ratio of CYTOX-positive cells/total cells were used to indicate the cytotoxicity.

Due to missing densities, several fragments in the closed state map (M1-V45, G79-C114, Y285-E318, T452-L458, F533-L541, C711-A807) and lipid-open state map (M1-Q43, E86-G112, K729-A807) were excluded from final models. Generated models were subsequently refined in PHENIX using phenix.real_space_refine77,87 and manually inspected in Coot for several iterations. The model geometry was evaluated with the MolProbity Web Server88. Figures were prepared with ChimeraX75.

All-atom MD simulations using Anton 3The final states after 10 ns of backbone-constrained equilibration in NAMD 3.0.1 were converted to dms format using NAMDtoDMS.py from the Pittsburgh Supercomputing Center and simulated on Anton 3. Simulations (Table S2) were completed in two stages: 1) 1 µs with constraints applied to all Cα atoms except loops from AF2, and 2) 1 µs with constraints applied to Cα atoms of TM helices. CG MD simulationsAll-atom models without palmitoylation modifications were converted to CG resolution using Martinize2-vermouth94 (vermouth-0.10.1.dev93). Elastic network model bonds were added with force constants of 700 kJ⋅mol−1⋅nm−2 and bond lengths between 5 and 8 Å. A new POPC bilayer was generated by Insane95 (insane-1.2.0) and the systems were solvated in regular waters (name W) and 150 mM salt in systems with final dimensions 184 × 184 × 220 Å3 (Table S3). All CG MD simulations were performed in GROMACS 2024.281 with the New-RF parameter set96 and using a 20 fs timestep and reaction-field electrostatics. To synchronize neighbor list updates and avoid artifacts97, all nstX variables were set to 20 steps, Verlet-buffer-tolerance was disabled, and a neighbor list cutoff of 15 Å was used. Temperature was maintained at 310 K using the v-rescale thermostat98 and pressure was set to 1 bar semi-isotropically by a Berendsen barostat98 (coupling constant 4 ps−1). Pre-equilibration followed the Martinate protocol99: we performed 1) up to 500 steps of position-restrained vacuum minimization; 2) up to 500 steps of position-restrained minimization after Insane; 3) 10 ps of position-restrained NVT; and 4) 25, 50, and 300 ps of unrestrained NpT with timesteps of 5, 10, and 20 fs. We simulated all systems for at least 10 μs (Table S3) and all coordinates were saved every 100 ps.

MD simulation analysisDensities of phosphate atoms and water molecules in all-atom simulations were computed using VMD’s VolMap tool using CHARMM radii and in density mode, which applies a Gaussian smoothing to the mass-weighted atomic coordinates. Densities were computed using all frames (every 120 ps) and visualized in ChimeraX (Figure 5A, Right). In CG simulations, a lipid orientation vector between the phosphate atom (CG name PO4) and the average position of the hydrocarbon tail ends (CG name C4A C4B) was defined at each timestep. We defined an orientation angle as the angle between the orientation vector and the z-axis and computed the running average of this angle over a 100 ns sliding window (Figure S4). Lipids with initial orientation angles below and above 90° were assigned to the cytoplasmic and exoplasmic leaflets, respectively, and a transition was recorded if the average orientation angle of lipids in the cytoplasmic or exoplasmic leaflets crossed 145° or 35°, respectively. After an event was initially identified using the 100 ns sliding window, we computed a more accurate time per scrambling event by averaging angles over a 10 ns window and using stricter angle cutoffs of 152.5° and 27.5°, respectively. Phosphate positions and angles were recorded or computed in MDAnalysis100 and plotted using Seaborn/Matplotlib python packages. Head group positions during scrambling events were written to PDB files and visualized in VMD. Time-lapse images (Figure 5D, Left and S4) were rendered in VMD using the built-in trajectory display setting for representations.
