The maturation of spermatids to fertilization-competent sperm is a sophisticated
process required for sexual reproduction, and biogenesis of the cytoplasmic
droplet (CD) is a key event. Here, we demonstrate that two proteins with
testis-specific expression, arrestin domain-containing 5 (ARRDC5) and testis
expressed 38 (TEX38), physically interact, are required for normal sperm
maturation, and play a critical role in CD formation and function. In the CDs of
Arrdc5-/- or Tex38-/- mice, a key aspect of saccular element biogenesis is
deficient, the proteome is abnormal, and migration from the neck down the
flagellum does not occur. Furthermore, comparative proteomic profiling between
sperm CDs and spermatids from knockouts and wild-type mice suggests that ARRDC5
and TEX38 facilitate cargo loading during CD biogenesis and deposition of
proteins such as translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 40 (TOMM40) from
the CD to the sperm mitochondrial sheath during epididymal maturation. This
protein-delivery mechanism is likely a key event in enabling sperm motility and
therefore fertilization capacity.

INTRODUCTION

Spermatogenesis is a sophisticated developmental process that occurs within seminiferous tubules of testicular tissue and involves three main phases: mitotic proliferation of spermatogonia, meiotic divisions of spermatocytes, and morphological transformation of round spermatids to produce spermatozoa (or sperm).1–3 The final phase is termed spermiogenesis and is a unique developmental process in which a round cell undergoes dramatic changes to condense nuclear DNA, form an acrosomal vesicle, grow a long flagellum, and reposition organelles to generate a neck and midpiece composed of a mitochondrial sheath that will be required for motility after ejaculation.4–6 The formation of these structures creates a streamlined sperm shape, protects the nuclear DNA from damage, and endows eventual fertilization capacity.4–6 To become mature, spermatozoa are released from the seminiferous tubule epithelium and transported to the epididymis, where they sequentially transit through the caput, corpus, and cauda regions.7–9 Only cauda spermatozoa are motile and capable of natural fertilization.7–9

Achieving the morphological transformation from spermatid to sperm during spermiogenesis requires most of the cytoplasm and many organelles that are not necessary for locomotion or fertilization to be removed.4–6 Conventional understanding is that cytoplasm and organelle elimination are achieved via the formation and shedding of a residual body (RB) initially and then a cytoplasmic droplet (CD).10–13 Interestingly, unlike the RB, which is eliminated in the seminiferous epithelium before sperm are released for transport to the epididymis where final maturation occurs, the CD remains attached at the sperm neck and migrates down the midpiece.10,14,15 Failure of CD migration is consistently accompanied by a “bent-neck” sperm phenotype and reduced motility, both of which compromise the sperm’s fertilizing capacity.16–23 In addition, several recent studies have raised an intriguing prospect that the cargo contained in the CD has important biological roles in the final steps of sperm maturation.14,15,19,23

First described in 1909 as an organelle unique to sperm, the CD has been observed on epididymal sperm of most vertebrate species studied to date.15 Structurally, the CD is known to consist of saccular elements that derive from the vesicular components of the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus.12,23 Functionally, the potential role of CDs in sperm remains speculative. Proteomic analysis of CDs isolated from epididymal sperm of laboratory mice identified enzymes involved in energy metabolism, intracellular transport, and cytoskeleton structure.12,15 In addition, recent studies with mice revealed that key metabolic enzymes are partitioned into the CD via a Golgi-borne vesicle pathway during spermiogenesis,23 and a plethora of non-coding RNAs are contained in the CDs of epididymal sperm that may contribute to fertilization capacity.24–27 Despite these important findings suggesting that the CD contains cargo associated with sperm fertility, cause-and-effect evidence for the CD’s importance is lacking, and the existence of mechanisms under-pinning cargo loading to the CD and deposition from the CD to sperm remain undetermined.

Over the last two decades, various mouse models of male infertility have been reported to possess a phenotype of impaired sperm CD migration including those with knockout for the genes calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase type IV (Camk4),17
casein kinase 2 alpha 2 (Csnk2a2),18
spermatid maturation 1 (Spem1),19
coiled-coil domain-containing 62 (Ccdc62),20
capping protein (actin filament) muscle Z-line, alpha (Capza3),21
homeodomain-interacting protein kinase 4 (Hipk4),22 and synaptophysin-like protein 1 (Sypl1).23 Of these models, only Spyl1−/− mice have been investigated directly for abnormalities in the CD, which revealed an essential role for the encoded protein in saccular structure formation.23 Outside of saccular element biogenesis, the impacts of any gene knockout on establishment of the CD cargo, including the proteome, has not been reported in the peer-reviewed scientific literature.

In a previous study, we reported that the testis-enriched molecule arrestin domain-containing 5 (ARRDC5) is required for normal sperm morphogenesis and fertility in mice.28 Sperm generated by Arrdc5−/− mice have an array of abnormalities including misshapen heads (bent necks and retention of cytoplasm) and severely reduced motility.28,29 Interestingly, ARRDC5 protein was reported to be highly enriched in the CDs of sperm isolated from the epididymis of wild-type mice.15 Although ARRDC5 is now well known to be an essential regulator of spermiogenesis,28,29 mechanisms of action are undefined, and a potential role in CD formation or function has not been investigated. In this study, we identify testis expressed 38 (TEX38) as a direct interacting protein of ARRDC5 and demonstrate that knockout of the Tex38 gene in mice leads to a male-specific sterility due to malformed and immotile spermatozoa that is nearly identical to the Arrdc5−/− phenotype. Among the ARRDC5-interacting candidates, TEX38 was selected for focused analysis due to its testis-specific expression pattern, similar to that of ARRDC5. In contrast, many of the other candidates exhibited broader expression across multiple tissues. While completing experiments for this study, other reports on the generation and phenotyping of different Tex38−/− mouse lines were published.30,31 Like the Tex38−/− mouse model generated in the current study, the other studies30,31 discovered that TEX38 plays an essential role in sperm morphogenesis and is required specifically for male fertility. In addition to abnormal sperm morphogenesis, in this study we found that spermatozoa generated by both Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice have impaired biogenesis of the CD including deficient saccular element formation and failed migration during epididymal maturation. Furthermore, proteomic profiling of CDs and spermatids from Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice revealed abnormalities in CD cargo loading and unloading. Moreover, evidence is provided that the CD functions as a key organelle for the final steps in sperm maturation by delivering proteins to the mitochondrial sheath.

RESULTSARRDC5 and TEX38 are interacting proteinsRecent studies have revealed that expression of ARRDC5 is enriched in, if not exclusive to, the spermatogenic lineage across Mammalia and required for normal spermiogenesis and male fertility in mice.28,29 Considering that core functions of arrestin family molecules are carried out via protein-protein interactions,32–34 we sought to identify a key interactor of ARRDC5. Initial mining of the STRING consortium database (STRING: https://string-db.org/). for potential interacting proteins of ARRDC5 in human, mouse, and pig revealed the molecule TEX38 as a candidate (Figure 1A).35 In addition, the AlphaFold artificial intelligence program was able to generate logical protein-protein interaction models for ARRDC5 and TEX38 of the three mammalian species (Figure 1B).36 Like ARRDC5, the TEX38 gene is evolutionarily conserved as a 1:1 ortholog across mammalian species (Figure S1A). The protein sequence similarity for TEX38 is 74%, 71%, 72%, and 66% conserved between human and bovine, ovine, porcine, and murine, respectively (Figure S1A).37,38Mining of the mouse ENCODE (ENCODE database: https://www.encodeproject.org/) and human GTEx (GTEx database: https://gtexportal.org/home/) databases showed that, like ARRDC5, TEX38 gene expression is restricted to testicular tissue (Figure S1B) and this was confirmed by RT-PCR analysis of several tissues from mice, pigs, cattle, and sheep (Figure 1C).39,40 To further define overlap in the cell-type expression profiles for the two molecules, we mined previously reported single-cell RNA transcriptome datasets of testicular tissue from adult humans, mice, and pigs.41,42 For all three species, TEX38 transcripts were detectable across cell populations expressing tell-tale biomarkers of both early (SPACA1, ACRV1, EQTN, and CATSPER3) and late (PRM1, SPEM1, TSSK6, and TNP1) spermatids, which is a similar expression pattern to ARRDC5 (Figures S2A and S2B).43–46 The Human Protein Atlas database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) confirmed that both ARRDC5 and TEX38 are enriched in early and late spermatids based on single-cell RNA-sequencing data.47,48Next, to assess a direct protein-protein interaction between ARRDC5 and TEX38 in a mammalian cell system, we constructed a split-luciferase expression construct based on the human coding sequences (Figure 1D).49 Driven by the CAG promoter, a fusion transcript was produced from this construct that, when translated, would produce TEX38 fused to the N-terminal portion of firefly luciferase and ARRDC5 fused to the C-terminal portion of firefly luciferase (Figure 1D). Also, these two fusion transcripts would be separated at translation due to the in-frame p2A cleavage peptide (Figure 1D); thus, subsequent interaction was required for full complementation of the firefly luciferase molecule and light emittance. A control construct was also generated in which the TEX38 coding sequence was replaced with green fluorescent protein (GFP) coding sequence (Figure 1D). Transient transfection of the split-luciferase constructs in HEK293 cells confirmed the STRING prediction that ARRDC5 and TEX38 physically interact (Figure 1E).35 Furthermore, site-directed mutagenesis of TEX38 within the region predicted by AlphaFold to mediate interaction abolished the luciferase signal, thus demonstrating loss of interaction with ARRDC5 (Figure S3).Collectively, these findings demonstrate that, in mammalian species, ARRDC5 and TEX38 are co-expressed specifically in late stages of the spermatogenic lineage and physically interact, suggesting a cooperative role in regulating key events of spermiogenesis.Arrdc5- and Tex38-knockout mice have similar male-specific sterility phenotypesGiven the similarity in expression profile and demonstrated interaction, we postulated that ARRDC5 and TEX38 have similar biological roles in regulating spermiogenesis. To investigate this, CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing technology was used to engineer a Tex38-knockout mouse line (Figure 2A).50 An N0 founder female with a 518-bp deletion allele of Tex38 (Tex38Δ518) was generated and mated with a wild-type C57BL6/J male to yield N1 heterozygous (Tex38Δ518/+) offspring (Figure 2A). To generate a line for experimentation, the N1 heterozygous animals were back crossed with wild-type C57BL6/J mice to produce an N2 generation (Figure 2A). The N2 Tex38Δ518/+ animals were then intercrossed to produce Tex38Δ518/Δ518 mice as knockouts (Tex38−/−) (Figure 2A). Both male and female Tex38−/− mice from this line were generated at expected Mendelian ratios (Figure 2A). During the phenotyping of this line, another study was published reporting the generation and characterization of a different Tex38-knockout mouse line.30 Regardless, we continued assessment of the mouse line generated here for direct comparison to Arrdc5-knockout mice and confirmation of the previous findings. The Tex38−/− mice generated for this study were found to be of grossly normal size, structure, and health, with body and organ wet weights at 4 months of age that are not different compared to wild-type littermates (Figures 2B–2D). Furthermore, testis gross morphology appeared normal for Tex38−/− mice, and wet weight was not different compared to wild-type littermates (Figures 2E and 2F). Likewise, the wet weights of epididymides, and seminal vesicles were measured to be not different between adult Tex38−/− and wild-type mice (Figure S4), indicating that development of the male reproductive tract was normal including testosterone production.51,52 Collectively, these findings demonstrate that, like ARRDC5, TEX38 function is not required for normal viability and physiology including proper function of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis.Next, to assess the fertility status of Tex38−/− female and male mice, breeding trials were conducted by pairing with wild-type counterparts. Over a 4-month period, the number of offspring produced by Tex38−/− females (n = 3 different mice) was measured to be not different compared to Tex38+/+ littermates (Figure 2G). In contrast, Tex38−/− males (n = 4 different mice) produced zero offspring even though mating occurred, shown by the presence of vaginal plugs, while Tex38+/+ littermates displayed a normal level of fecundity (Figure 2H).The normal size of testes indicated that the seminiferous epithelium was intact for Tex38−/− mice and this was confirmed via cross-sectional histology (Figure 3A). Similarly, abnormalities were not observed for cross-sections of cauda epididymis from Tex38−/− mice, and spermatozoa were clearly present (Figure 3A). Thus, to further investigate the cause of sterility, cauda epididymal sperm of adult males (2–3 months of age) were examined using light microscopy and computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA). Microscopic imaging revealed an array of abnormalities for sperm of Tex38−/− mice that were not observed for Tex38+/+ mice, including bent necks, malformed heads, and macrosperm (Figure 3B). The sperm concentration for Tex38−/− males (n = 3 different mice) was measured to be significantly (p = 0.003) reduced to only 38.5% of normal when compared to wild-type littermates (Figure 3C). In addition, the percentage of motile spermatozoa for Tex38−/− males (n = 3 different mice) was calculated to be 49.4% ± 12.1% (mean ± SEM), which was significantly (p = 0.016) decreased compared to the 80.4% ± 5.9% motile spermatozoa for Tex38+/+ littermates (Figures 3D and S5A). Furthermore, nearly all the sperm (99.7% ± 0.1%, mean ± SEM) from Tex38−/− males (n = 3 different mice) were measured to have abnormal morphology, which was significantly different (p < 0.0001) from the 7.7% ± 6.3% of spermatozoa with abnormal morphology in epididymides of Tex38+/+ littermates (Figure 3E). Interestingly, the major morphological abnormality for sperm produced by Tex38−/− mice was bent necks, observed for 93.4% ± 2.7% of sperm (Figure 3B).To further assess the fertilization capacity of those abnormal sperm, in vitro fertilization (IVF) was performed with denuded oocytes from wild-type female mice and sperm from either Tex38−/− or wild-type littermate males. Following incubation for 4 h, the number of spermatozoa bound per oocyte was calculated to be 13.5 ± 2.2 for wild-type (mean ± SEM; n = 3 different mice), which was significantly (p = 0.0002) greater compared to 1.3 ± 1.0 for Tex38−/− mice (Figures 3F and 3G). At 18 h, 47.1% ± 1.1% (mean ± SEM; n = 3 sessions) of oocytes incubated with spermatozoa from wild-type mice progressed to the two-cell stage, and subsequently 42.2% ± 1.6% reached the eight-cell stage (Figures S5B and S5C). In contrast, none of the oocytes incubated with spermatozoa from Tex38−/− mice reached the two-cell stage (Figures S5B and S5C). These results demonstrate that, like the functional importance of ARRDC5, spermatozoa generated in the absence of TEX38 exhibited abnormal morphology and reduced motility, thereby rendering them incapable of fertilizing oocytes.CD position and ultrastructure are abnormal in sperm of Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− miceThe strikingly similar phenotypes of knockout mouse models for Arrdc5 and Tex38 and their direct physical interaction suggest cooperation between the two encoded molecules for regulation of spermiogenesis. In addition, a previous study reported that ARRDC5 is abundant in the CD of epididymal spermatozoa.15 Also, epididymal spermatozoa of Arrdc5−/− mice are known to have excess cytoplasm in the sperm head.28 Given these pieces of knowledge, we next aimed to further define the commonality of spermiogenesis defects by studying the ultrastructure of both testicular and epididymal spermatozoa produced by Arrdc5−/−, Tex38−/−, and wild-type mice using electron microscopy with particular attention on the CD. Surprisingly, the morphology of spermatozoa in seminiferous tubules appeared to be normal and indistinguishable between Arrdc5−/−, Tex38−/−, and wild-type mice under scanning electron microscopy (Figure 4A). However, in the caput epididymis of Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice, spermatozoa with both bent and unbent necks were observed, while, in the cauda epididymis, the majority exhibited bent necks (Figure 4A). Caput spermatozoa with unbent necks from Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice exhibit a clearly distinguishable bulb in the neck region, whereas, in wild-type mice, the CDs in most caput sperm has already migrated to the flagellar body (Figure 4A). Indeed, we determined that most spermatozoa (~80%) in the cauda epididymis of wild-type littermates had a CD properly positioned at the midpiece (Figure 4B). In contrast, in the cauda epididymides of Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice, none of the sperm had CDs properly positioned at the midpiece, with most (>90%) retained at the neck region (Figures 4A and 4B).During spermiogenesis, the CD develops to contain saccular elements that derive from vesicular components of the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus.12,23 In addition, the CD contains an array of biomolecular cargo in the form of proteins and RNAs.12,15,27 Strikingly, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging of epididymal sperm from Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice revealed that the retained CDs remained attached to sperm heads and lacked saccular elements (Figure 4C). However, vesicular components were observable in the cytoplasm of spermatids that formed in seminiferous tubules of both Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice with no apparent differences compared to spermatids of wild-type mice (Figure 4C). Lastly, TEM imaging also revealed the presence of sperm clumps in the epididymis of both Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice in which multiple heads and tails were observed to be enclosed within a membrane (Figure S6); this abnormality was not observed for epididymal sperm of wild-type littermates. Taken together, these findings suggest that, in the absence of ARRDC5 or TEX38, CD formation initiates during spermiogenesis but incorporation of saccular elements is impaired, and proper migration is blocked during epididymal transit.Abnormal proteome in CDs of Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− miceConsidering the common abnormal position and ultrastructure of CDs generated by Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice, we next aimed to assess whether the protein cargo loaded into them is also altered. To achieve this, CDs were isolated from sperm generated by Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice as well as wild-type mice using an established protocol (Figure S7)12,15 and analyzed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). In total 2,112, 2,155, and 2,087 proteins were detected at high confidence in CD samples from Arrdc5−/−, Tex38−/−, and wild-type mice, respectively (Figure 5A; Table S1). The complete list of measured proteins for all samples is available in Table S1. In the knockout mice, 28 and 72 proteins were uniquely detected in Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− samples, respectively (Table S1). Among these, no statistically significant Gene Ontology (GO) terms were enriched (Table S1). Interestingly, the total protein expression pattern revealed that CDs from Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice clustered closely with each other and distinctly from CDs of wild-type mice (Figure 5A).Comparative analysis identified 234 and 187 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) (p < 0.05, fold change ± 1.5) in CDs of Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice compared to CDs of wild-type mice, respectively (Table S2). Of these, 65.8% and 73.8% were found to be increased in abundance and 34.2% and 26.2% decreased in abundance in the CDs of Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice compared to wild-type, respectively (Figure 5B; Table S2). Collectively, 90 proteins were commonly DEPs for the two knockout models compared to wild-type mice (Figure 5C; Table S3). Further analysis of the common DEPs by GO revealed functional categories such as “cytoskeletal proteins,” “metabolite interconversion enzyme,” “mitochondrial respiratory chain complex III,” “organelle organization,” “mitochondrial electron transport,” and “cilium or flagellum-dependent cell motility” (Figure 5D; Table S3).53,54 Together, these findings suggest that the proteomes of CDs in Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice are similar to each other but distinct from the CD proteome of wild-type mice.Abnormal proteome initiates in round spermatids of Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− micePossible explanations for altered proteomes of sperm CDs generated by Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice compared to wild-type mice include (1) abnormalities of protein generation/stability in developing spermatids, and (2) normal levels of typical CD proteins in spermatids but impaired loading and/or unloading during development. To explore these possibilities, we profiled the proteomes of spermatids generated by Arrdc5−/−, Tex38−/−, and wild-type mice for comparison to their CD proteomes. Spermatids were isolated from testes of Arrdc5−/−, Tex38−/−, and wild-type mice (n = 3 different animals for each genotype) using previously validated methodology and analyzed by LC-MS.55 In total, 1,367 proteins were identified with high confidence across all samples (Table S4). Analysis of the full spermatid proteome dataset by principal-component analysis (PCA) spatially resolved the Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− profiles as similar to each other but distinct from the wild-type profile (Figure 6A).56 In addition, correlation analysis of the spermatid proteomes between the two knockout and wild-type mice revealed a stronger association between Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− lines (R = 0.55, p < 2.2e−16) compared to associations between either of the knockout models and wild-type (R = 0.42 and 0.49 for Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/−, respectively; p < 2.2e−16) (Figure 6B; Table S4). As a control, correlations between biological replicates within wild-type, Arrdc5−/−, and Tex38−/− mice were R = 0.62, 0.55, and 0.51, respectively (p < 2.2e−16) (Figures 6B and S8A; Table S4).Compared to wild-type, 6.1% and 1.7% of proteins in spermatids were measured to be abnormally reduced in abundance from Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice, respectively (Figure 6C; Table S4). In contrast, 13.3% and 12.1% of proteins were found to be abnormally increased in abundance for Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− spermatids compared to wild-type, respectively (Figure 6C; Table S4). Collectively, 116 and 10 proteins were determined to be commonly increased or decreased as DEPs for spermatids of the Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice compared to wild-type spermatids, respectively (Figure 6D; Table S4). GO analysis of the common DEPs revealed enrichment in several significant categories, including “positive regulation of microtubule plus-end binding,” “formation of cytoplasmic translation initiation complex,” “microtubule cytoskeleton organization,” and “organelle assembly” (Figure 6E). Together, these findings demonstrate that lack of either ARRDC5 or TEX38 leads to proteome abnormalities beginning at the spermatid stage and suggest that the two molecules share common molecular functions, likely related to “microtubule cytoskeleton organization.”Next, to determine whether the abnormally abundant proteins in CDs of the knockouts were generated at normal or altered levels in spermatids, we conducted a detailed comparison of the DEP profiles for CDs and spermatids of Arrdc5−/−, Tex38−/−, and wild-type mice. First, the total number of DEPs in spermatids was found to be similar to the number of DEPs in CDs for both Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− sperm (266 vs. 234 and 188 vs. 187), respectively (Tables S2 and S4). Second, these analyses revealed that only 8.1% (19) and 4.8% (9) of the DEPs measured for CDs were also detected to be differentially abundant in spermatids for Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice, respectively (Figures S8B and S8C; Table S5). Of the 70 proteins (Table S3) found to be commonly increased in abundance in CDs of Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− sperm, only two proteins (2.9%, HOOK1 and OTUB2) were also measured to be increased in spermatids of the knockouts compared to wild-type (Figure 5C; Table S5). In comparison, of the 20 proteins found to be commonly decreased in abundance in CDs of Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice, only one protein (5%, GAA) was likewise reduced in spermatids (Table S5). Taken together, these findings indicate that, although the number of DEPs in spermatids is comparable to that in CDs, the specific DEPs present in the CDs of sperm generated by the two knockout models differ from those in spermatids. First, proteomic datasets for CDs from knockout spermatozoa, which fail to migrate during epididymal maturation, were mined for proteins with increased abundance compared to CDs of wild-type mice, which do migrate but were not different in spermatids across the genotypes (Figure 7B). Thus, we focused on assessing whether proteins with known roles in mitochondrial function, which were measured to be abundant in CDs that fail to migrate, are repositioned along the sperm flagellum during epididymal maturation. We found that several proteins known to associate with the mitochondrial protein-import pathway were captured in the analysis (Figure S10B): translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 40 (TOMM40), translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 70 (TOMM70), translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 34 (TOMM34), peptidase, mitochondrial processing subunit beta (PMPCB), heat shock 90-kDa protein 1-alpha (HSP90AA1), heat shock 70-kDa protein 4-like (HSPA4L), and heat shock 70-kDa protein 2 (HSPA2) (Figures 7D and S7).57,58 These proteins have been discovered and described previously as having important roles in mitochondrial protein import and activity, as illustrated in Figure S10C.57,58Next, we postulated that the core function of the CD could be shuttling protein cargo to mitochondria during epididymal maturation. To investigate this, we focused on tracking movement of the molecules TOMM40 and TOMM70 in the CD and sperm body during the epididymal maturation phase. Both Tomm40 and Tomm70 genes are in the nuclear, rather than mitochondrial, genome; thus, their proteins are translated in the cytosol and must be transported to the mitochondria where they incorporate as a key element of the core pore-forming subunit of the preprotein-translocation channel.58–60 During spermiogenesis, mitochondria in round spermatids are repositioned as a sheath to make up the midpiece of sperm; concomitantly, components of the cytosol are eliminated as an RB or sequestered into the CD. It stands to reason that proteins present in the cytosol of transforming spermatids that will be required for function in the repositioned mitochondria during post-testicular maturation are loaded into the CD for depositing during migration. As measured by proteomics, the relative abundance of TOMM40 and TOMM70 were found to be >8-fold higher in non-migrated CDs isolated from sperm of the cauda epididymides of knockout mice compared to migrated CDs in wild-type mice but was not different in spermatids across the genotypes (Figure 7D). Next, to track positioning of both proteins in the CD and spermatozoa during epididymal maturation, immunofluorescent staining with super-resolution microscopy imaging was performed on spermatozoa isolated from either the caput or cauda epididymis of wild-type, Arrdc5−/−, and Tex38−/− mice. Intriguingly, both TOMM40 and TOMM70 staining were distinctly detected only in the CD and were absent in the mitochondrial sheath of wild-type spermatozoa positioned in the caput when the CD was located at the neck (Figures 7E, S11A, and S11B). In contrast, the staining of these proteins became detectable in both the mitochondrial sheath and CDs of cauda-positioned sperm after the CDs had migrated across the midpiece (Figures 7E, S11A, and S11B). For spermatozoa in both the caput and cauda epididymides of Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice, intense staining for TOMM40 and TOMM70 was observed in the sperm neck where underdeveloped CDs resided but was undetectable in the mitochondrial sheaths (Figure S11B). Based on immunoblotting analysis, we measured a significant reduction of 1.8-fold in TOMM40 and 1.3-fold in TOMM70 levels in caput epididymal CD-less sperm compared to cauda epididymal CD-less sperm of wild-type mice (Figures 7F, 7G, and S11C). In corroboration, immunoblot analysis revealed that abundance of TOMM40 and TOMM70 in CDs isolated from cauda epididymides of both knockout models was significantly greater by an average of 2.2-fold and 2.1-fold compared to CDs of wild-type mice, respectively (Figures 7H, 7I, and S11D). Collectively, these findings support a concept that key proteins regulating final sperm function, including those that activate the mitochondrial sheath for requisite motility such as TOMM40 and TOMM70, are unloaded from the CD to main structures of the sperm flagellum during epididymal maturation.

Next, to track positioning of both proteins in the CD and spermatozoa during epididymal maturation, immunofluorescent staining with super-resolution microscopy imaging was performed on spermatozoa isolated from either the caput or cauda epididymis of wild-type, Arrdc5−/−, and Tex38−/− mice. Intriguingly, both TOMM40 and TOMM70 staining were distinctly detected only in the CD and were absent in the mitochondrial sheath of wild-type spermatozoa positioned in the caput when the CD was located at the neck (Figures 7E, S11A, and S11B). In contrast, the staining of these proteins became detectable in both the mitochondrial sheath and CDs of cauda-positioned sperm after the CDs had migrated across the midpiece (Figures 7E, S11A, and S11B). For spermatozoa in both the caput and cauda epididymides of Arrdc5−/− and Tex38−/− mice, intense staining for TOMM40 and TOMM70 was observed in the sperm neck where underdeveloped CDs resided but was undetectable in the mitochondrial sheaths (Figure S11B). Based on immunoblotting analysis, we measured a significant reduction of 1.8-fold in TOMM40 and 1.3-fold in TOMM70 levels in caput epididymal CD-less sperm compared to cauda epididymal CD-less sperm of wild-type mice (Figures 7F, 7G, and S11C). In corroboration, immunoblot analysis revealed that abundance of TOMM40 and TOMM70 in CDs isolated from cauda epididymides of both knockout models was significantly greater by an average of 2.2-fold and 2.1-fold compared to CDs of wild-type mice, respectively (Figures 7H, 7I, and S11D). Collectively, these findings support a concept that key proteins regulating final sperm function, including those that activate the mitochondrial sheath for requisite motility such as TOMM40 and TOMM70, are unloaded from the CD to main structures of the sperm flagellum during epididymal maturation.

During spermiogenesis, organelles and cytosolic components that are not needed for sperm function must be eliminated.10 Conventional understanding is that this is accomplished within the seminiferous epithelium first through generation of an RB by the developing spermatid and then biogenesis of a CD.10,11,13 Unlike the RB, which is shed in the seminiferous epithelium, the CD persists on spermatozoa after release and during epididymal transit.10,14,15 In addition, impaired migration of the CD from a position at the neck, along the mitochondrial sheath, and finally down the midpiece is associated with sperm that have bent necks and other head deformities as well as reduced motility.16–23 For these reasons, the CD has gained attention in recent studies as a structure that may contribute to final steps in sperm maturation.

An interesting aspect of spermiogenesis that is associated with CD formation and migration is the major repositioning of mitochondria as a sheath in the sperm midpiece where they serve an essential role in driving motility.61,62 After release from the seminiferous epithelium, sperm enter the caput region of the epididymis, progressively transit through the corpus, and then arrive in the cauda regions where they reside prior to ejaculation.7–9 Only sperm in the cauda region have the capacity for motility.7–9 To achieve this, the mitochondrial sheath must be activated, and, at present, the mechanisms by which this occurs are largely undefined and may include the delivery of activating factors via CD migration.

In the current study, we identify 50 proteins present in CDs located at the sperm neck that are then lowly abundant in CDs positioned at the principal piece. One possible explanation for this change in abundance is the unloading of proteins from the CD to other sperm organelles during the CD’s migration along the flagellum. While the functions of many of these proteins have been studied in broader biological contexts, their roles in sperm biology are largely unexplored. Interestingly, 7 of the 50 proteins potentially unloaded from the CD to other sperm organelles can be classified as having key roles in mitochondrial protein import. These include TOMM40, TOMM70, TOMM34, PMPCB, HSP90AA1, HSPA4L, and HSPA2. Of particular interest, the mature form of TOMM40 creates a dimeric pore embedded in the outer mitochondrial membrane, serving as the sole channel for the translocation of mitochondrial precursor proteins across the outer membrane.59,60

Mammalian mitochondria contain approximately 1,300 different proteins, 99% of which are encoded by nuclear DNA, translated in the cytosol, and subsequently transported into the mitochondria through a channel formed in part by TOMM40 and TOMM70.63,64 This continuous import of proteins into mitochondria is crucial for maintaining functionality. In the present study, we demonstrate that impaired migration of the CD is associated with low levels of TOMM40 and TOMM70 in the mitochondrial sheath of cauda epididymal sperm and severely reduced motility. Moreover, we discovered that, prior to its migration, TOMM40 and TOMM70 are enriched in the CD but minimal in the mitochondrial sheath and then become observable in mitochondria post migration. These findings suggest that the CD has a previously unrecognized role in depositing activating proteins that were translated in the cytoplasm of spermatids during spermiogenesis to the mitochondrial sheath during epididymal maturation, thereby endowing capacity for motility.

In the present study, we demonstrate that CD biogenesis and migration require the function of both ARRDC5 and TEX38. In the absence of either molecule, CDs lack characteristic structures, and sperm adopt abnormal morphologies during epididymal transit, including misshapen heads and bent necks. In addition, the CD fails to migrate on sperm of both knockout models, and, in association, the sperm have severely impaired motility. Furthermore, we discovered that ARRDC5 and TEX38 are able to interact directly. Taken together with the strikingly similar knockout mouse phenotype of male-specific sterility due to malformed and immotile sperm, these findings indicate that ARRDC5 and TEX38 are mechanistically linked as core regulators of mammalian spermiogenesis. Based on the overlapping alterations in proteomes of CDs for spermatozoa of the knockout mice, we postulate that a key mechanistic function of the ARRDC5-TEX38 complex is the loading and unloading of protein cargo during CD biogenesis and migration. The role of ARRDC5 in sperm epididymal maturation is supported by ShinyApp data, which indicate that a significant increase in expression occurs during the process of epididymal transit.65 Vacuolar Protein Sorting-Associated Protein 26C (VPS26C), one of the predicted interactors of the ARRDC5-TEX38 complex, is known to play a crucial role in endosomal trafficking pathways. This raises the possibility that ARRDC5-TEX38, together with VPS26C, facilitates the transport of saccular elements and other proteins from spermatids to the CD. Further research is warranted to explore this potential mechanism. Given that both Arrdc5-and Tex38-knockout male and female mice appear to be in normal health, the potential for drug interference with the ARRDC5-TEX38 interaction to affect physiological systems or early stages of spermatogenesis, apart from spermiogenesis, is likely minimal. For these reasons, targeting the ARRDC5-TEX38 interaction with a small-molecule inhibitor may offer a promising approach for novel male contraceptive development.

Limitations of the studyDue to the lack of a specific antibody and difficulty in engineering ARRDC5/TEX38-tagged animal models, several gaps in knowledge are unfilled. First, although ARRDC5 is known to be an abundant protein in the CDs of mice, the abundance of TEX38 is undefined. Likewise, while ARRDC5 and TEX38 are testis specific across Mammalia, whether either are present in the CDs of sperm other than mice remains to be defined. Second, physical interaction between ARRDC5 and TEX38 was verified in a mammalian cell line, but whether this is also the case in spermatids and/or CDs requires further study. Third, although findings of this study provide associative evidence that key proteins, including TOMM40 and TOMM70, are shuttled from the CD to the mitochondrial sheath during epididymal maturation, whether this is functionally required for sperm motility requires additional investigation.

Spermatid and cytoplasmic droplet isolationSpermatids were isolated from single cell suspensions of testicular tissue using a discontinuous BSA gradient method, as described and validated by Kim et al. (2021).55 Briefly, single-cell suspensions of testes were prepared and mixed with 1 mL of 0.5% BSA medium. The suspension was gently layered using serological pipettes onto a BSA density-gradient column composed of 5% BSA at the bottom and 4%, 3%, 2%, and 1% BSA sequentially toward the top. (1970) and Yuan et al. (2013).12,15 Briefly, 2 mL epididymal sperm suspension was layered over a discontinuous sucrose gradient consisting of 2 mL of 0.25 M sucrose on top of 3 mL of 1 M sucrose. After centrifugation at 1,500g for 20 min, the top 1 mL of the 0.25 M sucrose layer was removed. The CDs were enriched within the remaining 0.25 M sucrose layer and at the interface between the 0.25 M and 1 M sucrose layers. These fractions were carefully transferred to a new 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1,200g for 10 min. The resulting CD-enriched supernatant was then transferred to a separate 1.5 mL tube and centrifuged at 12,500g for 20 min at 4°C to obtain the CD pellet. The pellet located at the bottom of the 1 M sucrose layer, which contained CD-depleted spermatozoa, was also collected into a new 1.5 mL tube for downstream analyses.
