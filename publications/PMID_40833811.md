Guidance of cell growth or movement in response to chemical cues in the
environment is critical for many cell behaviors. Budding yeast orientation of
polarized growth in response to gradients of mating pheromones provides a
tractable model to address how cells accurately assess small spatial differences
in chemical concentrations. Pheromones bind to receptors that act through
heterotrimeric G proteins to promote activation of the MAPK Fus3. Active Fus3
binds to Gα, which is thought to enhance local phosphorylation of relevant MAPK
substrates to promote orientation of polarity toward high-pheromone regions.
Polarity is oriented by a pathway in which Gβγ binds the scaffold protein Far1
to activate the conserved polarity regulator Cdc42, which activates the formin
Bni1 to orient actin and hence growth. Gβγ, Far1, and Bni1 are all MAPK
substrates whose phosphorylation could improve orientation toward high-pheromone
regions. Here, we show that the Gα-MAPK interaction can enhance the efficiency
of polarity-site alignment between mating partners, particularly under
conditions with high Fus3 activity. Surprisingly, however, we find no evidence
that phosphorylation of Gβγ, Far1, or Bni1 contribute to the benefit conferred
by Gα-MAPK interaction. The precise role of this interaction remains mysterious.

INTRODUCTION

Movement or growth guided by chemical cues (chemotaxis and chemotropism, respectively) is common to many cell types. For example, neutrophils migrate toward bacteria (Petri and Sanz, 2018), sperm swim toward eggs (Kaupp et al., 2008), axons grow toward synaptic targets, pollen tubes grow toward ovules (Palanivelu and Preuss, 2000), and fungi orient toward mating partners (Clark-Cotton et al., 2022), all by recognizing specific chemical cues. Active Cdc42 recruits effectors to the polarity site, which orient the cytoskeleton and vesicle traffic to direct growth or movement in that direction (Pruyne and Bretscher, 2000; Heasman and Ridley, 2008).

To properly orient polarity, cells decode the spatial information present in concentration gradients of extracellular chemicals and communicate that information to the polarity machinery (Cai and Devreotes, 2011; Ghose et al., 2022). As cells and their targets change position, this information can be updated by moving or reorienting the polarity site. Currently, the mechanism whereby extracellular gradients influence polarity is best understood in the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae.

Budding yeast cells occur in two haploid mating types—a and α (Herskowitz, 1988). Each mating type releases a specific peptide pheromone (a-factor and α-factor) that is recognized by G-protein–coupled receptors (GPCR) on the surface of the opposite mating type (Alvaro and Thorner, 2016). Gβγ then goes on to recruit a scaffold protein called Ste5, which promotes activation of a MAPK (Fus3) that leads to transcription of mating specific genes, cell-cycle arrest, and polarization of Cdc42 (Figure 1A) (Atay and Skotheim, 2017). In addition, Gβγ recruits a second scaffold protein, Far1, from the cytoplasm. Far1 binds to the GEF for Cdc42, leading to Cdc42 activation at sites of Gβγ release (i.e., sites of pheromone binding) (Figure 1B). The Far1 pathway is essential for guiding the movement of the polarity site to the right location (Martin, 2019; Ghose et al., 2022).

Computational models that combine what is known about the yeast polarity circuit with a representation of the Far1 pathway can relocate polarity sites in a manner that is biased by external pheromone gradients (Ghose et al., 2021; Guan et al., 2023). However, the bias is quite weak, and the models do not perform as well as yeast cells, suggesting that additional pathways may impact gradient tracking in budding yeast. One potential pathway involves an interaction between Gα and the MAPK (Figure 1A), which was reported to enhance mating efficiency (Metodiev et al., 2002). Here, we confirm that this interaction can improve gradient tracking by the polarity site and explore potential mechanisms by which this pathway might enhance polarity site guidance.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONThe MAPK Fus3 binds to multiple substrates and regulators by recognizing a docking site motif that is also present in Gpa1 (Metodiev et al., 2002). Previous studies showed that a gpa1DSD (DSD, Docking Site Disrupted mutant, K21E K22E) blocks Gpa1–Fus3 interaction and exhibits a mating defect (Metodiev et al., 2002; Errede et al., 2015). A mating defect could occur because of reduced efficiency in orienting toward a partner, or reduced ability to mate following orientation. To distinguish the defective step(s) in the mating process, we mixed gpa1DSD mutants of opposite mating types with fluorescent polarity markers and compared their mating with that of wild-type (WT) cells. Recent work has shown that mating cells undergo an initial “indecisive phase” in which polarity clusters (visualized using the polarity marker Bem1) can form and disperse at different locations, before two partner cells’ polarity sites come into stable alignment during a “committed phase” that leads to fusion (Henderson et al., 2019; Clark-Cotton et al., 2021). We observed similar behavior in our time-lapse movies (Figure 1C). Compared with WT controls, gpa1DSD mutants mated with similar efficiency (Figure 1D) and took a similar time to align their polarity sites (Figure 1E). Following commitment, fusion occurred with similar kinetics in mutant and WT cells (Figure 1F). A mating defect could occur because of reduced efficiency in orienting toward a partner, or reduced ability to mate following orientation. To distinguish the defective step(s) in the mating process, we mixed gpa1DSD mutants of opposite mating types with fluorescent polarity markers and compared their mating with that of wild-type (WT) cells. Recent work has shown that mating cells undergo an initial “indecisive phase” in which polarity clusters (visualized using the polarity marker Bem1) can form and disperse at different locations, before two partner cells’ polarity sites come into stable alignment during a “committed phase” that leads to fusion (Henderson et al., 2019; Clark-Cotton et al., 2021). We observed similar behavior in our time-lapse movies (Figure 1C). Compared with WT controls, gpa1DSD mutants mated with similar efficiency (Figure 1D) and took a similar time to align their polarity sites (Figure 1E). Following commitment, fusion occurred with similar kinetics in mutant and WT cells (Figure 1F). Thus, in these conditions, the gpa1DSD mutants have no obvious defect in polarity site alignment and mating.

A potential explanation for the weak effect of gpa1DSD is that Fus3 localization to sites of receptor activation can be accomplished not only via Gpa1–Fus3 interaction but also, perhaps redundantly, via Ste5–Fus3 interaction (Bhattacharyya et al., 2006) (Figure 1A). To test that hypothesis, we also compared mating dynamics in ste5ND mutants (ND, nondocking mutant, which cannot bind Fus3: Q292A I294A Y295A L307A P310A N315A) and in ste5ND
gpa1DSD double mutants. We found that ste5ND mutants mated well, with dynamics similar to WT cells (Figure 1, D–F). ste5ND
gpa1DSD double mutants mated less rapidly, perhaps, indicating some redundancy (Figure 1, D–F). This effect was statistically significant, but quite small in magnitude.

Another possible explanation for the weak effect of gpa1DSD in our imaging conditions compared with filter matings that are more crowded (Metodiev et al., 2002) or exposure to synthetic pheromone gradients in microfluidic devices (Errede et al., 2015) is that pheromone levels are higher in those conditions, leading to higher levels of active Fus3. To test whether gpa1DSD may show a greater defect in conditions with higher Fus3 activation, we used strains in which Ste5 is targeted to the membrane via a C-terminal transmembrane (CTM) domain (Figure 2A) (Pryciak and Huntress, 1998). High-level expression of Ste5-CTM results in hyperactivation of Fus3 without the need for pheromone binding (Pryciak and Huntress, 1998), while allowing efficient mating.

Cells expressing Ste5-CTM arrest the cell cycle in G1 and exhibit a strong polarity site that is mobile until it orients toward a mating partner (Figure 2B) (McClure et al., 2015; Ghose et al., 2021). As these cells are already arrested in G1 at the time of mixing with opposite-mating-type partners, we cannot score indecisive phase duration (from birth to commitment) in the same manner. Instead, we scored the time from initial mixing of partners until two cells’ polarity sites first became aligned (time to first encounter: See Methods). In cells expressing Ste5-CTM, gpa1DSD mutants showed significantly reduced mating efficiency (Figure 2C) and took significantly longer to align polarity toward a partner (Figure 2D). Surprisingly, in some cases the polarity sites did not stop moving upon reaching alignment, instead making “kiss-and-run” contacts (Jacobs et al., 2022) (Figure 2E). Thus, in cells expressing Ste5-CTM, gpa1DSD mutants have significant defects in orienting polarity and in maintaining orientation of polarity sites toward mating partners.

Kiss-and-run events have been observed in scenarios where mating partners secrete reduced levels of pheromone (Jacobs et al., 2022). However, pheromone secretion was normal in gpa1DSD mutants (Figure 2F), and gpa1DSD mutants did not display other mating defects (like failing to maintain G1 arrest) characteristic of reduced pheromone secretion (Jacobs et al., 2022). Thus, the kiss-and-run events observed in gpa1DSD mutants are unlikely to reflect decreased pheromone secretion and may instead reflect a decreased ability to interpret the pheromone gradient to orient polarity.

Fus3 is known to phosphorylate many proteins that are localized to sites enriched for free Gpa1, including Gβ (Ste4; Li et al., 1998), Gγ (Ste18; Choudhury et al., 2018), Far1 (Peter et al., 1993) and the formin Bni1 (Matheos et al., 2004) (Figure 3A). All these substrates play roles in polarity site movement, guidance, and stabilization (Ghose et al., 2022). In particular, Ste4 phosphorylation, enhanced by Gpa1–Fus3 interaction, was proposed to regulate pheromone receptor phosphorylation and thereby contribute to polarity site guidance (Abdul-Ganiyu et al., 2021). To test this hypothesis, we replaced endogenous Ste4 with the nonphosphorylatable ste43TA,SA (T318A T320A T322A S335A) (Abdul-Ganiyu et al., 2021) and imaged mating mixes of cells expressing Ste5-CTM. Surprisingly, ste43TA,SA mutants appeared to align their polarity sites even more rapidly than the WT cells (Figure 3B). This may correspond to the observation that polarity sites in mutant cells could rapidly “jump” toward partners (Abdul-Ganiyu et al., 2021). Mating efficiency was unaffected (Figure 3C). We conclude that Ste4 phosphorylation is not required for polarity site alignment and mating.

Ste4 (Gβ) forms a tight complex with Ste18 (Gγ), which is also phosphorylated by Fus3 (Choudhury et al., 2018). Thus, it seemed possible that Ste18 phosphorylation might compensate for the lack of Ste4 phosphorylation in the ste43TA,S mutants. The role (if any) of Ste18 phosphorylation in polarity site guidance or stabilization has not yet been addressed. To ask whether Ste18 phosphorylation contributes to these processes, we replaced endogenous Ste18 with the nonphosphorylatable ste183A (T2A S3A S7A) (Choudhury et al., 2018). Mutant cells exhibited no difference from WT cells in the timing of polarity site alignment (Figure 3B) or the efficiency of mating (Figure 3C). Moreover, a strain in which neither Ste4 nor Ste18 could be phosphorylated behaved indistinguishably from the ste43TA,SA mutant alone (Figure 3, B and C). In all these experiments, both mating partners were mutant, so efficient mating cannot be attributed to a WT partner compensating for a defect in the mutant partner. Moreover, all strains expressed Ste5-CTM, a context in which gpa1DSD mutants did exhibit a defect in polarity site alignment (Figure 2). A similar lack of effect on polarity site alignment was found for strains that did not express Ste5-CTM (Figure 3D). We conclude that phosphorylation of Ste4 and/or Ste18 does not account for the role of the Gpa1–Fus3 interaction in this process.

The role of Gβγ in polarity site guidance is to recruit the scaffold protein Far1 to sites of pheromone binding (Butty et al., 1998; Nern and Arkowitz, 1999; Ghose et al., 2021; Jacobs and Lew, 2022). Far1 plays genetically separable roles in cell-cycle arrest and in polarity site guidance (Valtz et al., 1995). Fus3 phosphorylates Far1 at T306, and far1T306A mutants cannot arrest the cell cycle in G1 (Gartner et al., 1998), which has precluded analysis of whether that phosphorylation is also important for the role Far1 in polarity site guidance. To circumvent this issue, we took advantage of a well-characterized C-terminal truncation of Far1, far1-H7, which is phosphorylated by Fus3 and effectively arrests the cell cycle but cannot bind to the Cdc42 GEF and is therefore defective for polarity site guidance and mating (Figure 4, A–C) (Valtz et al., 1995; Butty et al., 1998). The presence of an additional copy of full-length Far1 (in a strain that has far1-H7) enables polarity site guidance and effective mating (Figure 4, B and C). Replacement of the full-length Far1 with nonphosphorylatable far1T306A had no effect on the timing of polarity site alignment (Figure 4C), and alignment was generally followed by commitment and fusion (not kiss-and-run events) (Figure 4D). We conclude that Far1 phosphorylation by Fus3 at T306 cannot account for the role of the Gpa1–Fus3 interaction.

Previous work implicated the formin Bni1 as another Fus3 substrate and suggested that Bni1 phosphorylation might promote mating (Matheos et al., 2004). The Bni1 phosphorylation sites have yet to be determined, so we took a different approach to ask whether the Gpa1–Fus3 interaction may act via Bni1. If Bni1 regulation is responsible for the polarity site alignment defect of gpa1DSD mutants, then gpa1DSD would have no further effect in cells lacking functional Bni1. WT, bni1Δ, gpa1DSD, and gpa1DSD
bni1Δ strains were induced to express Ste5-CTM and mating mixes were imaged as above. bni1Δ mutants were able to align their polarity sites and commit to partners, but subsequent fusion was delayed, as expected for mutants unable to focus actin cables properly (Gammie et al., 1998) (Figure 5, A–C). The polarity alignment defect of gpa1DSD
bni1Δ double mutants was comparable with that of gpa1DSD single mutants (Figure 5D). Thus, gpa1DSD leads to defective gradient tracking even in cells lacking Bni1. We conclude that the Gpa1–Fus3 interaction acts in parallel with Bni1, and not simply through Bni1 regulation, to orient polarity.

CONCLUSIONOur results support the conclusion that the binding of Gpa1 to Fus3 contributes to polarity site guidance by pheromone. When this interaction was disrupted by the gpa1DSD mutation, cells took longer to align their polarity sites and often failed to halt movement once aligned. These defects were only severe in cells that expressed Ste5-CTM, which have maximally activated the Fus3 MAPK (Pryciak and Huntress, 1998; McClure et al., 2015), so it may be that Gpa1–Fus3 interaction becomes more important at higher levels of Fus3 activity. That may also explain why the defect appears to be more severe at the high levels of pheromone used in microfluidics experiments (Errede et al., 2015) and perhaps in crowded filter mating assays (Metodiev et al., 2002). The more rapid gradient tracking provided by the Gpa1–Fus3 interaction may be biologically significant in conditions where external factors, like rainfall or displacement due to animals, might cut short a mating attempt.Because the Gpa1–Fus3 interaction would serve to localize a pool of active Fus3 to the site of G protein activation, an obvious consequence of that interaction would be the enhanced phosphorylation of Fus3 substrates in the vicinity of active receptors. Indeed, previous studies had suggested that Gβγ (DeFlorio et al., 2013; Abdul-Ganiyu et al., 2021) and Bni1 (Matheos et al., 2004) phosphorylation by Fus3 might be important for mating. However, our studies did not identify any role for these phosphorylations in polarity site alignment. Our findings suggest that phosphorylation of Ste4, Ste18, Far1, and Bni1 by Fus3 does not account for the role of the Gpa1–Fus3 interaction in mating. A caveat to that conclusion is that the different substrates may act redundantly so that eliminating one or two has no effect. Other potential targets of Gpa1–Fus3 have been identified by phosphoproteomics (Waszczak et al., 2019), and it also remains possible that the interaction plays a role that does not involve Fus3 kinase activity. The actual function of this interaction remains to be determined.

Following an encounter, the polarity sites generally stop moving and remain oriented toward each other (commitment) but in some cases they continue moving (kiss-and-run). We scored the percentage of encounters that produced kiss-and-run events versus commitment. For partners that committed, the committed phase duration was scored as above (time from commitment to fusion).

Halo assays to measure pheromone secretionPheromone from one mating type causes cells of the opposite mating type to arrest in G1. In the halo assay (Wilson and Herskowitz, 1984), a lawn (3.5 × 105 cells) of supersensitive cells is spread evenly on a YEPD (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 2% dextrose) plate. A spot of 2 × 106 cells of the opposite mating type is pipetted onto the lawn, and pheromone diffusing away from the spot promotes G1 arrest, creating a zone of growth inhibition (the halo) in the lawn whose area reflects the amount of pheromone secreted from the spot. Supersensitive strains were DLY8993 (MATa
bar1Δ) to detect α-factor and DLY15660 (MATα sst2Δ) to detect a-factor. Pheromone secretion is induced by MAPK activation, so we added β-estradiol (100 nM) to induce expression of Ste5-CTM and MAPK activation in the cells of the spot.
