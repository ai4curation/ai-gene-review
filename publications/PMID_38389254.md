    J Mol Cell Biol. 2024 Oct 21;16(5):mjae022. doi: 10.1093/jmcb/mjae022.

Although the dynamic instability of microtubules (MTs) is fundamental to many
cellular functions, quiescent MTs with unattached free distal ends are commonly
present and play important roles in various events to power cellular dynamics.
However, how these free MT tips are stabilized remains poorly understood. Here,
we report that centrosome and spindle pole protein 1 (CSPP1) caps and stabilizes
both plus and minus ends of static MTs. Real-time imaging of laser-ablated MTs
in live cells showed deposition of CSPP1 at the newly generated MT ends, whose
dynamic instability was concomitantly suppressed. Consistently, MT ends in
CSPP1-overexpressing cells were hyper-stabilized, while those in CSPP1-depleted
cells were much more dynamic. This CSPP1-elicited stabilization of MTs was
demonstrated to be achieved by suppressing intrinsic MT catastrophe and
restricting polymerization. Importantly, CSPP1-bound MTs were resistant to
mitotic centromere-associated kinesin-mediated depolymerization. These findings
delineate a previously uncharacterized CSPP1 activity that integrates MT end
capping to orchestrate quiescent MTs.

Introduction

As polymers of the αβ-tubulin heterodimer, microtubules (MTs) exhibit characteristics of dynamic instability (Mitchison and Kirschner, 1984), which is fundamental for many cellular functions in both interphase and mitotic cells (Gudimchuk and McIntosh, 2021). Nevertheless, quiescent centrosomal and non-centrosomal MTs that do not present dynamic growth or shortening dominate in the interphase of both cycling and specialized cells, such as epithelial cells, neurons, and myotubes (Kapitein and Hoogenraad, 2015; Meiring et al., 2020). For example, MT plus ends in fully contacted lung epithelial cells spend 87.4% of the time in a static state (Waterman-Storer et al., 2000), suggesting a suppression of the dynamic instability at MT ends.

Numerous MT tip-associated proteins regulate dynamic instability, which can be grouped into three main categories according to their end binding and functional properties: (i) MT plus end tracking proteins (+TIPs); (ii) MT minus end capping proteins; and (iii) MT end accumulating motor proteins (Akhmanova and Steinmetz, 2008, 2015). Among +TIPs, end-binding (EB) proteins specifically recognize MT plus tips that are growing, where they serve as hubs and recruit a variety of other +TIPs via protein–protein interactions (Honnappa et al., 2006; Weisbrich et al., 2007; Jiang et al., 2012). By in vitro reconstitution assays, EB proteins were demonstrated to regulate MT dynamics by increasing the frequency of catastrophe (Vitre et al., 2008); in cells, they promote persistent MT growth (Komarova et al., 2009), reflecting an integrated function of the +TIP complex in vivo. It is widely accepted that MT minus ends are much less dynamic than the plus ends because of the protection provided by MT minus end capping proteins (Akhmanova and Hoogenraad, 2015). One such protein is γ-tubulin ring complex (γ-TuRC), which serves as an MT nucleating factor on the centrosome and mitotic spindle (Moritz et al., 1995; Wiese and Zheng, 2000; Petry et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2022). CAMSAP proteins (Meng et al., 2008; Tanaka et al., 2012; Jiang et al., 2014) and the invertebrate ortholog Patronin (Goodwin and Vale, 2010; Hendershott and Vale, 2014) also directly cap and stabilize the MT minus end, which is independent of γ-TuRC. The third group of proteins that directly regulate MT tip dynamics includes motor proteins of the kinesin-13 and kinesin-8 families. Mitotic centromere-associated kinesin (MCAK), a kinesin-13 family member, is an MT depolymerase that hydrolyzes adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) to remove αβ-tubulin subunits from both plus and minus ends (Hunter et al., 2003; Helenius et al., 2006). The depolymerization force applied to MT ends correlates with the number of MCAK molecules (Oguchi et al., 2011). Different from MCAK, which does not walk along the MT lattice, kinesin-8 family members move processively toward the MT plus end, where they depolymerize the MT (Varga et al., 2009; Gardner et al., 2011).

Nevertheless, how MTs maintain a quiescent state was not sufficiently explained (Liu et al., 2020). Although MT minus end capping proteins contribute to the stabilization of the corresponding minus end, neither +TIPs nor motor regulators could keep the plus ends in a static state. Centrosome and spindle pole protein 1 (CSPP1) is a centrosome-associated protein (Patzke et al., 2005, 2006, 2010; Frikstad et al., 2019). CSPP1 mutations in humans are linked to Joubert syndrome. Patients affected with Joubert syndrome suffer malfunctions of multiple systems, such as malformations of the brain (Akizu et al., 2014; Shaheen et al., 2014; Tuz et al., 2014). The mitotic functions of CSPP1 on the central spindle and kinetochore have been investigated (Asiedu et al., 2009; Zhu et al., 2015). Here, we report that CSPP1 caps both plus and minus ends of static MTs and stabilizes MT tips by suppressing intrinsic catastrophe and simultaneously restricting elongation. In addition, CSPP1 protects MTs from depolymerization by MCAK. These discoveries add to the three categories of the aforementioned MT end regulators and help explain how the commonly present quiescent MTs are stabilized.

To explore the potential role of CSPP1 in regulating MT dynamics, we took advantage of deconvolution microscopy and examined CSPP1 localization in interphase cells. Specifically, live cell imaging was performed with HeLa cells expressing GFP-tagged CSPP1 (GFP-CSPP1) and mCherry-tagged α-tubulin (mCherry-MT), which was indicative of cellular MTs. Indeed, CSPP1 appeared at the distal ends of MTs that were initially growing and marked by EB3. This association of CSPP1 with the MT plus ends was concomitant with EB3 dissociation (Figure 1B). To investigate direct roles of CSPP1 in the control of MT dynamics, we first monitored in vitro MT polymerization at 340 nm absorbance in the absence or presence of CSPP1-M and found that inclusion of 100 nM purified CSPP1-M accelerated MT polymerization (Supplementary Figure S3E). To visualize CSPP1-promoted MT polymerization, an in vitro reconstitution of MT growth was performed under TIRFM. In the absence of CSPP1-M, MTs elongated primarily at the plus ends, while the addition of CSPP1-M allowed MT growth from both plus and minus ends. The MT elongation from the minus end was dependent on CSPP1-M concentration, and the growth length from the minus end within 10 min became comparable to that from the plus end as the concentration of CSPP1-M increased (Supplementary Figure S3F and G). Furthermore, the addition of CSPP1-M allowed MTs to grow continuously from both ends without a catastrophic event, in contrast to control MTs that showed dynamic growth and shrinkage (Figure 3B), suggesting that CSPP1-M suppressed MT catastrophe. To demonstrate this further, we introduced EB1 at the high concentration, as a catastrophe promoting factor (Vitre et al., 2008), into the in vitro MT growth assay. EB1 (600 nM) completely restrained MT elongation due to the infinite catastrophe frequency. Inclusion of CSPP1-M (180 nM) suppressed EB1-promoted catastrophe and permitted MT growth from both ends (Supplementary Figure S3H and I). In the absence of CSPP1-M, occasional flashing of the recombinant GFP-tagged EB1 (EB1-GFP) was recorded at MT ends, despite the inability of MT growth, but the tip tracking of EB1 was completely inhibited by CSPP1-M (Supplementary Figure S3H), consistent with the previous observation that, in cells, overexpression of CSPP1 suppressed MT plus end tracking of EB1 (Supplementary Figure S2). In addition, CSPP1-M at high concentrations decreased the growth rate of MT plus ends (Supplementary Figure S3J).

Next, we extended these assessments to full-length CSPP1 and three MT-associated domains at various concentrations and compared their activities. All four CSPP1 preparations achieved complete suppression of MT catastrophe, but at substantially different protein concentrations, i.e. full-length CSPP1 at 20 nM, CSPP1-M at 50 nM, CSPP1-M1 at 500 nM, and CSPP1-M2 at 1 μM, suggesting that CSPP1-M1 and CSPP1-M2 constitute a functional entity to suppress MT catastrophe (Figure 3B and C). In addition, CSPP1-M2 showed a prominent MT plus end tracking property in vitro, in contrast with full-length CSPP1, CSPP1-M, and CSPP1-M1, which are associated with the newly polymerized MT lattice (Figure 3B; Supplementary Figure S4). This finding indicates that CSPP1-M2 is additionally responsible for the recognition of MT ends. In terms of MT growth, full-length CSPP1 was most effective, reducing the growth rate by 80% at 160 nm, and CSPP1-M restricted the growth rate to ∼50% at similar concentrations, while CSPP1-M1 and CSPP1-M2 only reduced the growth rate by 19.6% and 44.8%, respectively, at a concentration of 1 μM (Figure 3D). Our in vitro discoveries of MT catastrophe suppression and growth rate restriction by CSPP1 are consistent with the in vivo observations that CSPP1 stabilizes the capped MT ends, although complete growth inhibition was not achieved in vitro.

DiscussionIn the present study, we report that CSPP1 possesses a non-canonical activity to cap both plus and minus ends of cytoplasmic MTs and functions as a novel stabilization factor. Mechanistically, CSPP1 not only suppresses intrinsic catastrophe and GTP-driven polymerization of MTs but also prevents the depolymerization of bound MTs by MCAK. To our knowledge, CSPP1 is the first protein identified to cap plus ends of cytoplasmic MTs. Our findings about CSPP1 explain the common presence of unattached free MT ends in various settings, such as quiescent MT plus ends in regular cells and stabilized non-centrosomal MTs in terminally differentiated cells.Different from CAMSAP/Patronin family members, which specifically cap and stabilize the MT minus end, CSPP1 recognizes both plus and minus ends. It is not yet known whether CSPP1 and CAMSAP proteins are mutually exclusive in capping the minus end or they synergistically work together to stabilize the distal end. The capacity of CSPP1 to bind to both ends of MTs suggests that exposure of the α-tubulin subunit at the distal minus end and the β-tubulin subunit at the plus end is not relevant for the recognition of the ends. The structural determinant for the end binding of CSPP1 requires further investigation. Our data showed that free tubulin dimers promoted the association of CSPP1 with MT ends (Figure 3A). Perhaps CSPP1 alone was not able to recognize the end structures of the sheared GMPCPP-stabilized MTs, but free tubulin dimers worked together with CSPP1 and formed favored structures at both ends for binding. The affinity between CSPP1 and the free tubulin dimer was demonstrated by the formation of a coacervate in vitro (Supplementary Figure S3D).In our in vitro MT reconstitution assays, CSPP1 exhibited a binding preference for the newly assembled GTP/GDP-bound MT lattice (Figure 3B; Supplementary Figure S3C) over the preformed GMPCPP-stabilized MT seeds. We reason that either CSPP1 distinguishes the structural difference between lattices of GTP/GDP-bound and GMPCPP-bound MTs, or the CSPP1–tubulin association allows CSPP1 incorporation into the newly polymerized MTs, which would not happen on the preformed MT seeds. During the revision of this article, van der Berg et al. (2023) reported that CSPP1 resided in the lumen of MTs. The biased decoration of the newly polymerized MTs over the preformed MT seeds (Figure 3B; Supplementary Figure S3C) precluded the possibility that CSPP1 voluntarily entered the lumen but supported the co-polymerization model. Indeed, the MTs imaged in their study were either polymerized in the presence of purified proteins in vitro or from CSPP1-overexpressing cells (van den Berg et al., 2023). Under physiological conditions, CSPP1 tends to localize to the centrosome and cytoplasmic MT ends rather than the MT lattice. Another way that CSPP1 enters the lumen would be MT damage repair, as proposed by van der Berg et al. (2023). In this regard, the CSPP1–tubulin condensate (Supplementary Figure S3D) may additionally play an important role during the process of repair, as the coacervate not only stops the damaged MT from depolymerization but also provides the αβ-tubulin heterodimer for repair.Almost all CSPP1-capped MTs present a static state in cells (Figure 2A and B). The variable effects of CSPP1 at MT plus ends in interphase cells and on kinetochores in mitotic cells suggest a regulation of its activity during mitosis. Protein expression and purificationTo express full-length CSPP1 tagged with 6×His or 6×His-GFP, baculovirus-infected Sf9 cells were cultured for 3 days. For the 6×His-GFP-tagged CSPP1 domains, the proteins were induced to express in the BL21-Gold (DE3) strain with 0.5 mM IPTG at 16°C overnight. Then, the proteins were centrifuged and incubated with Ni-NTA agarose beads for 1 h, washed with wash buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 1 M KCl, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM MgCl2, 30 mM imidazole, and 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol) three times, and eluted with elution buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 1 M KCl, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM MgCl2, 300 mM imidazole, and 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol). Subsequently, the eluted proteins were subjected to either gel filtration through a Superose 6 10/300 column or buffer exchange into BRB80 buffer (80 mM PIPES, pH 6.8, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA) containing 50 mM KCl and 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol. MCAK purification was performed according to a previously described protocol (Friel and Howard, 2011).

In vitro MT reconstitution assaysFor MT growth assays, GMPCPP (Jena Bioscience, NU-405)-stabilized MT seeds (polymerized from 15 μM unlabelled tubulin dimer, 1 μM rhodamine-labelled tubulin dimer, and 1 μM biotinylated tubulin dimer) were attached to a silanized glass chamber that was coated with anti-biotin antibody (Sigma, B7653). ImmunofluorescenceHeLa or hTERT-RPE-1 cells growing on coverslips were fixed with warmed paraformaldehyde that was diluted to 3.7% in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for another 10 min.
