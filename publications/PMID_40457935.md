The syndrome of multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella
(MMAF) is one of the most serious kinds of sperm defects, leading to
asthenoteratozoospermia and male infertility. In this study, we use whole-exome
sequencing to identify genetic factors that account for male infertility in a
patient born from a consanguineous Pakistani couple. A homozygous frameshift
mutation (c.1399_1402del; p.Gln468ArgfsTer2) in axonemal dynein light chain
domain containing 1 ( AXDND1 ) was identified in the patient. Sanger sequencing
data showed that the mutation was cosegregated recessively with male infertility
in this family. Papanicolaou staining and scanning electron microscopy analysis
of the sperm revealed severely abnormal flagellar morphology in the patient.
Immunofluorescence and western blot showed undetectable AXDND1 expression in the
sperm of the patient. Transmission electron microscopy analysis showed
disorganized sperm axonemal structure in the patient, particularly missing the
central pair of microtubules. Immunofluorescence staining showed the absence of
sperm-associated antigen 6 (SPAG6) and dynein axonemal light intermediate chain
1 (DNALI1) signals in the sperm flagella of the patient. These findings indicate
that AXDND1 is essential for the organization of flagellar axoneme and provide
direct evidence that AXDND1 is a MMAF gene in humans, thus expanding the
phenotypic spectrum of AXDND1 frameshift mutations.

RESULTSClinical investigation of the patient IV:9 from a consanguineous Pakistani familyIn this study, a consanguineous Pakistani family was recruited. An infertile man (IV:9, 31 years old) born from a consanguineous couple was unable to conceive after 9 years of marriage (Figure 1a). A fresh semen sample from the patient was collected by masturbation after 5–7 days of abstinence; the semen volume and sperm concentration of the patient (IV:9) were within the normal range of the WHO guidelines.24 However, the patient exhibited asthenoteratozoospermia, characterized by low sperm motility and abnormal sperm morphology (Table 1). Sperm morphological analysis of the spermatozoa was performed after Papanicolaou staining for a fertile control and IV:9. DISCUSSIONIn the present study, by WES, we identified a homozygous AXDND1 frameshift variant (ENST00000367618.3, c.1399_1402del; p.Gln468ArgfsTer2) in a consanguineous Pakistani family with an infertile man, who was diagnosed with MMAF. Sanger sequencing indicated that the variant cosegregates recessively with the MMAF in the family. Papanicolaou staining and SEM analysis revealed severely compromised sperm flagellar morphology in the patient. Immunofluorescence staining and western blot showed undetectable AXDND1 expression in sperm flagella of patient IV:9. TEM analysis showed disorganized sperm axonemal structure in the patient, particularly missing the central pair of microtubules. Immunofluorescence staining showed the absence of central pair protein SPAG6 and inner dynein arm protein DNALI1 signals in the sperm flagella of the patient. Taken together, these findings indicate that AXDND1 is essential for the organization of the axonemal ultrastructure. Our data provide direct evidence for the link of AXDND1 as an MMAF-related gene in humans, thus expanding the phenotypic spectrum of AXDND1 frameshift mutations.AXDND1 encodes a protein belonging to the dynein light chain family, which has been proven to involve various cellular processes. The dynein light chain family proteins may regulate the kinetic properties of dynein by interacting with intermediate chain and other intracellular proteins and play important roles in multiple biological processes such as intracellular transport, cell division, and neuronal development.32 Intriguingly, AXDND1 was involved in the sperm flagella formation process as well as a regulator of the spermatogonia commitment.22 However, the potential molecular mechanism of AXDND1 in the guarantee of long-term maintenance of the spermatogonia pool is poorly understood. Here, we found that patient IV:9 exhibited normal sperm count through two examinations at the age of 30 years; the phenotype is milder when compared to the knockout mice. One possible explanation is that deficiency in dynein proteins usually causes a more severe phenotype regarding sperm count when compared to human orthologs. For instance, it has been reported that deficiency in inner dynein arm protein DNAH127 or outer dynein arm proteins – DNAH1714 and DNAH833 causes a more severe phenotype in knockout mice, compared with those of human patients with biallelic DNAH12, DNAH17, or DNAH8 mutations. The differences in environmental factors and genetic backgrounds could lead to phenotypic divergence between humans and mice. Factors such as differences in protein expression levels, regulated pathways, genetic redundancy, and functional compensation may also contribute to the varying phenotypes observed in humans and mice. Nevertheless, AXDND1 plays an important role in the development of the sperm flagellum in both species. In the present study, by WES, we identified a homozygous AXDND1 frameshift variant (ENST00000367618.3, c.1399_1402del; p.Gln468ArgfsTer2) in a consanguineous Pakistani family with an infertile man, who was diagnosed with MMAF. Sanger sequencing indicated that the variant cosegregates recessively with the MMAF in the family. Papanicolaou staining and SEM analysis revealed severely compromised sperm flagellar morphology in the patient. Immunofluorescence staining and western blot showed undetectable AXDND1 expression in sperm flagella of patient IV:9. TEM analysis showed disorganized sperm axonemal structure in the patient, particularly missing the central pair of microtubules. Immunofluorescence staining showed the absence of central pair protein SPAG6 and inner dynein arm protein DNALI1 signals in the sperm flagella of the patient. Taken together, these findings indicate that AXDND1 is essential for the organization of the axonemal ultrastructure. Our data provide direct evidence for the link of AXDND1 as an MMAF-related gene in humans, thus expanding the phenotypic spectrum of AXDND1 frameshift mutations.

AXDND1 encodes a protein belonging to the dynein light chain family, which has been proven to involve various cellular processes. The dynein light chain family proteins may regulate the kinetic properties of dynein by interacting with intermediate chain and other intracellular proteins and play important roles in multiple biological processes such as intracellular transport, cell division, and neuronal development.32 Intriguingly, AXDND1 was involved in the sperm flagella formation process as well as a regulator of the spermatogonia commitment.22 However, the potential molecular mechanism of AXDND1 in the guarantee of long-term maintenance of the spermatogonia pool is poorly understood. Here, we found that patient IV:9 exhibited normal sperm count through two examinations at the age of 30 years; the phenotype is milder when compared to the knockout mice. One possible explanation is that deficiency in dynein proteins usually causes a more severe phenotype regarding sperm count when compared to human orthologs. For instance, it has been reported that deficiency in inner dynein arm protein DNAH127 or outer dynein arm proteins – DNAH1714 and DNAH833 causes a more severe phenotype in knockout mice, compared with those of human patients with biallelic DNAH12, DNAH17, or DNAH8 mutations. The differences in environmental factors and genetic backgrounds could lead to phenotypic divergence between humans and mice. Factors such as differences in protein expression levels, regulated pathways, genetic redundancy, and functional compensation may also contribute to the varying phenotypes observed in humans and mice. Nevertheless, AXDND1 plays an important role in the development of the sperm flagellum in both species. In human sperm samples, the severity of the mutation may vary depending on environmental factors, genetic diversity, or the modifying effects of other genes. Future studies, including detailed viability and acrosome reaction examinations of the patient, could further contribute to understanding the role of AXDND1 in sperm development.

Besides, it seems that the AXDND1 mutations may lead to accelerated reproductive aging disease, which may explain that the patient carrying a homozygous AXDND1 stop-gain variant (c.937T>C; p.R313X) could suffer from azoospermia.22 Genetic screening of more clinical infertility cases could contribute to deepening the understanding of the function of AXDND1 in humans. In clinical practice, intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) may be considered an optional assisted reproductive technique to overcome severe infertility problems like MMAF. Considering that AXDND1 may cause a more severe phenotype with aging, these findings also suggest that patients carrying the AXDND1 mutations could be suited for personalized ICSI treatment in clinical practice.
