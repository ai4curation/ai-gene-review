Lipoprotein trafficking is essential for Gram-negative bacteria to maintain the
outer membrane, a permeability barrier critical for survival and antibiotic
resistance. While most bacteria employ the canonical LolCDE transporter,
Acinetobacter utilizes a noncanonical LolDF system whose mechanism has remained
unclear. Here we determine cryo-EM structures of Acinetobacter baylyi LolDF in
substrate-bound, AMP-PNP-bound, and LolA-bound states. LolDF forms a symmetric
homodimer with a central cavity that accommodates three acyl chains at the
membrane level, suggesting lateral extraction of lipoproteins directly from the
inner membrane. AMP-PNP-driven conformational changes induce a coordinated
symmetric constriction of the cavity that promotes substrate expulsion, distinct
from the asymmetric mechanism observed in LolCDE. Functional studies further
identify key door-bar residues required for transport and reveal that charged
residues at the +3 position function as an inner membrane retention signal.
Together, these findings reveal a mechanistically distinct lipoprotein
trafficking system and provide a framework for targeting multidrug-resistant
Gram-negative pathogens.

Multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacterial pathogens pose a significant threat to global health, primarily due to the intrinsic barrier properties of the outer membrane (OM)1–4. The structural and functional integrity of the OM depends on lipoproteins, which stabilize the OM through anchoring to the peptidoglycan layer and coordinate critical processes, such as β-barrel protein assembly5–10, lipopolysaccharide transport11–16, and lipid asymmetry maintenance17–23. Lipoproteins exhibit remarkable diversity in sequence, structure, and function, with the composition and roles of lipoprotein families varying markedly across species. Evolutionary pressures such as host interaction, antibiotic stress, or niche specialization drive the expansion and reduction of lipoprotein repertoires, contributing to bacterial fitness and pathogenic potential44–46. Unlike E. coli, which employs the canonical LolCDE pathway, organisms such as Acinetobacter species utilize a noncanonical system mediated by the homologous LolDF complex, indicating a distinct lipoprotein-sorting strategy47,48 (Fig. 1a). Acinetobacter baumannii is a major multidrug-resistant pathogen in critical care, and its remarkable resilience is closely linked to OM homeostasis49,50. Elucidating how the LolDF system contributes to OM integrity and antibiotic resistance addresses a fundamental gap in current therapeutic strategies, underscoring the urgent need for antibacterial targets tailored to this recalcitrant pathogen. Previously, we elucidated the mechanism of the E. coli LolCDE complex, demonstrating that the transmembrane subunits LolE and LolC perform distinct roles in lipoprotein recognition and mechanotransduction, respectively26,28. In contrast, the Acinetobacter LolDF complex contains a homodimeric LolF, implying dual functional capacity.

Here, cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) analyses resolve multiple conformational states of the Acinetobacter baylyi (A. baylyi) LolDF complex, revealing both conserved and divergent mechanistic features related to the E. coli LolCDE system. Functional assays identify key residues critical for substrate recognition and trafficking, highlighting the essential role of the LolDF-LolA pathway in A. baylyi OM homeostasis. Collectively, our findings uncover species-specific adaptation in lipoprotein transport and provide a structural framework for the rational design of selective therapeutics against multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter species.

ResultsCryo-EM structures of the A. baylyi LolDF complex in multiple functional statesTo investigate the structural mechanism of lipoprotein trafficking in Acinetobacter, we first isolated the LolDF complex from A. baylyi. While A. baumannii is the primary clinical pathogen, A. baylyi serves as an ideal model system as it shares 87% sequence identity and conserved LolDF function with A. baumannii (Suppl. Fig. 1a), yet is non-pathogenic and genetically tractable51,52. To isolate the endogenous transporter in its native state, we engineered the A. baylyi ADP1 strain to carry a C-terminal 8×His-Flag tag on the chromosomal lolD gene together with a chloramphenicol-resistance cassette (Fig. 1b). Notably, this insertion did not impair the biological function of the complex, as the resulting strain exhibited growth kinetics comparable to those of the wild-type strain (Fig. 1c). The endogenous A. baylyi LolDF complex was purified and its composition confirmed by mass spectrometry (Suppl. Fig. 1b–f). We validated the complex’s functionality in vitro by demonstrating robust ATPase activity in detergent micelles (Fig. 1d).To capture the transporter in distinct functional states, the purified complex was further incubated either with purified A. baylyi LolA (yielding the LolA-bound state) (Suppl. Fig. 1g, h) or with the non-hydrolysable ATP analog AMP-PNP (capturing the ATP-bound state) (Suppl. Fig. 1b–d). We determined cryo-EM structures of the lipoprotein-like substrate-bound, LolA-bound, and AMP-PNP-bound complexes to resolutions of 3.15 Å, 3.00 Å, and 3.11 Å, respectively (Fig. 1e, f and Suppl. Figs. 2, 3). These maps allowed for unambiguous modeling of the LolF transmembrane domains (TMDs) and the cytoplasmic LolD subunits (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3). In regions where the periplasmic domains (PDs) exhibited flexibility, models were guided by the LolA-bound LolDF structure, where the PDs are better resolved (Suppl. Fig. 2).The LolDF complex forms a homodimer that adopts a characteristic type VII ABC transporter architecture (Fig. 1e, f and Suppl. Fig. 4a–c). LolF shares 38% sequence identity and a conserved architecture with its E. coli homologs, LolC and LolE (Suppl. Fig. 4d). Each LolF monomer comprises four transmembrane helices (TM1-TM4) and a large periplasmic domain (residues Arg58-Asn252) (Suppl. Fig. 4a, b). The long TM1 and TM2 helices from opposing monomers form a central cavity, with their periplasmic ends connecting to the PD composed of small α- and β- rich extracytoplasmic (Sabre) subdomains and β-α-β motifs (Porter) subdomains (Suppl. Fig. 4a, b). The shorter TM3 and TM4 wrap around the periphery of TM1 and TM2, and the segment between TM3 and TM4 folds into a ‘door-bar’ structure (residues Ser342-Trp374), which extends toward the lateral gates of the central cavity (Suppl. Fig. 4a, b). On the cytoplasmic side, the LolD dimer interacts with LolF via the coupling helices situated between TM2 and TM3 (Suppl. Fig. 4b). LolD exhibits the conserved ABC transporter nucleotide-binding domain (NBD) fold, consisting of a RecA-like and a helical subdomain for ATP binding53 (Suppl. To investigate the substrate recognition by LolDF, we introduced Asp or Lys substitutions at positions +2 to +6 of the lipoprotein BamE (Fig. 2f, g). Wild-type or mutant A. baylyi BamE proteins were co-expressed with recombinant LolDF carrying His- and Flag-tags. The LolDF complex was purified by nickel-affinity chromatography, and co-purified BamE was detected by immunoblotting. Negative substitutions at positions +2 to +4, as well as a positive substitution at position +3, abolished substrate binding by LolDF (Fig. 2f, g). Importantly, the mutant BamE proteins was expressed at levels comparable to the wild-type, indicating that the observed difference arises from impaired extraction by LolDF rather than altered protein expression (Fig. 2f, g). These results indicate that positively charged residues at the +3 position can influence LolDF-mediated sorting of BamE in A. baylyi, suggesting that charge-dependent sorting determinants may extend beyond the canonical negative +2 to +4 rule. However, the broader lipoprotein sorting rules governing the LolDF pathway in A. baylyi remain incompletely defined and will require investigation across additional substrates.

Bacterial lipoprotein transport systems have diversified across species, reflecting adaptations to distinct ecological niches and levels of pathogenicity44–48,64,65. In Gram-negative bacteria, proper sorting and localization of lipoproteins are essential for maintaining OM integrity, enabling nutrient acquisition and modulating host-pathogen interactions44–46,64,65. While the E. coli LolCDE complex has served as the canonical model for lipoprotein trafficking, our structural and functional analysis of the A. baylyi LolDF complex reveals notable mechanistic differences.

Structural comparisons reveal differences in substrate positioning between the two systems (Suppl. Fig. 5d). In LolCDE, the lipoprotein resides near the entrance of the central cavity above the membrane plane, consistent with models in which substrate extraction involves local membrane deformation during ATP-driven conformational resetting25–28,66 (Suppl. Fig. 5d). By contrast, in LolDF the bound lipoprotein-like substrate is positioned closer to the transmembrane region, suggesting that extraction may occur laterally from the inner membrane without pronounced membrane elevation (Figs. 1f, 2b and Suppl. Fig. 5d). Conformational morphing analyses further highlight distinct transporter dynamics (Supplementary movies 1 and 2). In LolDF, nucleotide binding promotes symmetric inward movement of the two LolF protomers as the NBDs close, whereas ATP binding in LolCDE induces rotational displacement centered on LolC that produces an asymmetric rearrangement of the transporter (Supplementary movies 1 and 2). Although a post-hydrolysis structure of LolDF was not captured in this study, the symmetric architecture and inward compressive motion observed upon NBDs closure suggest that reopening of the transporter during the catalytic cycle likely proceeds through a largely symmetric reset mechanism, contrasting with the pronounced asymmetric conformational change observed in LolCDE.

The widened cavity of LolDF accommodates acyl chains with fewer hydrophobic contacts than in LolCDE25–28,30 (Fig. 2b). The centrally positioned acyl chain forms the most extensive interactions and corresponds to one of the thioester-linked chains (Fig. 2b). Both diacylated and triacylated lipoproteins retain this configuration, suggesting that LolDF may tolerate substrates with different acylation states. In contrast, lipoprotein recognition in LolCDE is largely mediated by LolE, where the TM2 periplasmic segment engages the peptide linker and the door-bar accommodates the amide-linked R3 acyl chain25–28,30 (Suppl. Fig. 5d). In LolDF, the lower positioning of the bound substrate results in the door-bar primarily engaging the peptide region while the R3 acyl chain extends toward the peripheral space of the cavity (Suppl. Fig. 5d, e). Such structural flexibility may explain why the N-acyltransferase Lnt is dispensable in organisms that rely on the LoDF system47,59,67 (Fig. 2d, e).

The ability to traffic diacylated lipoproteins to the outer membrane may provide physiological advantages to Acinetobacter. Reduced triacylation can destabilize membrane packing and promote the formation of outer membrane vesicles (OMVs)64 (Fig. 2e). These vesicles can carry membrane-anchored β-lactamases and release them extracellularly, thereby facilitating degradation of β-lactam antibiotics68,69. In addition, diacylated lipoproteins are preferentially recognized by host Toll-like receptor 2/6 heterodimers44,45,70, triggering acute inflammatory responses rather than sustained clearance46,71. Together, these features highlight a lipoprotein trafficking pathway in Acinetobacter that is both mechanistically distinct and physiologically adaptable.

Despite these mechanistic differences, LolDF retains core mechanotransduction features characteristic of ABC transporters28 (Suppl. Fig. 10a–e), including allosteric coupling between NBDs and TMDs via the Q-loop, coupling helices, and elbow helices, as well as communication between TMDs and PDs mediated by the conserved TM2 tryptophan residue (Fig. 3). Previous studies established functional asymmetry in the LolCDE transporter, in which LolE primarily mediates lipoprotein binding, whereas LolC recruits the periplasmic acceptor LolA26,28. In contrast, our structural and phylogenic analyses indicate that the homodimeric LolF protein integrates structural motifs corresponding to both LolE and LolC, suggesting that each protomer contributes to both substrate binding and LolA engagement (Fig. 1e,f, and Suppl. Fig. 9a).

Consistent with this model, superimposition of the LolA-bound structures reveals a similar substrate-transfer configuration in LolDF and LolCDE, in which LolA and the bound lipoprotein engage opposite transmembrane domains. These observations suggest that although LolDF may extract lipoproteins through either lateral gate, subsequent transfer to LolA likely follows a conserved spatial arrangement. However, the conformational transitions underlying lipoprotein release differ between the two systems. In LolCDE, substrate transfer is driven by asymmetric rotational motion that repositions the cavity towards the LolC PD and aligns LolA for lipoprotein capture26,28. In contrast, LolDF undergoes a coordinated symmetrical compression in which both the TM helices move inwards to close the central cavity, elevating the periplasmic interface and facilitating substrate transfer without large-scale repositioning of LolA (Fig. 4b). Notably, LolA binding appears to stabilize a primed transporter conformation characterized by a narrowed cavity and higher positioning of the lipoprotein, potentially facilitating efficient substrate transfer (Fig. 4a and Suppl. Fig. 2n).

Overall, our results reveal that the LolDF transporter operates through a structurally and mechanistically distinct framework for lipoprotein trafficking. Compared with the asymmetric LolCDE system, LolDF exhibits a symmetric architecture that integrates substrate binding and LolA recruitment within a homodimeric transporter and allows flexible accommodation of lipoproteins with different acylation states. This divergence likely reflects adaptation of lipoprotein trafficking pathways to distinct physiological and ecological pressures. More broadly, these findings highlight the Lol pathway as a central determinant of OM homeostasis and a potential vulnerability for the developing narrow-spectrum antimicrobials against multidrug-resistant pathogens.

Image processing

Cryo-EM data were processed using a combination of RELION75 and cryoSPARC76. Beam-induced motion in image stacks was corrected using MotionCor277, and contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated with Gctf78 or CTFFIND4 to ensure high-quality micrographs for downstream analysis.

For the substrate-bound LolDF complex, initial particle picking yielded 453,597 particles, which were subjected to three-dimensional (3D) classification. Two distinct classes were resolved: one corresponding to a dimerization state and the other to the substrate-bound LolDF state. Particles from the latter class were selected for further refinement. Subsequent post-processing with a B-factor of −55.2 Å2 produced a final cryo-EM map at an overall resolution of 3.15 Å. The data processing workflow is summarized in Suppl. Fig. 2a–d.

For the LolA-bound LolDF complex, initial particle picking yielded 185,087 particles, which were subjected to three-dimensional (3D) classification. A major class corresponding to the LolA-bound state was identified, and 84,003 particles from this class were selected for further refinement. Post-processing with a B-factor of −83.7 Å2 produced a final cryo-EM map at an overall resolution of 3.00 Å. The data processing workflow is summarized in Suppl. Fig. 2g–j.

For nucleotide-bound LolDF states, a total of 5,381,272 particles were processed in cryoSPARC76 through 3D classification., Of these, 177,670 particles were selected for non-uniform refinement, yielding final maps for the AMP-PNP-bound LolDF complex with closed NBD conformations at resolutions of 3.11 Å, with B-factors of −102.3 Å2. No symmetry constraints (C1) were applied. The corresponding workflow is shown in Suppl. Fig. 3.

All final resolutions were estimated using the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) criterion at a threshold of 0.143.
