Malaria, which is caused by Plasmodium parasites, is transmitted by anopheline
mosquitoes. When gametocytes, the precursor cells of Plasmodium gametes, are
transferred to a mosquito, they fertilize and proliferate, which render the
mosquito infectious to the next vertebrate host. Although the fertilization of
malaria parasites has been considered as a rational target for
transmission-blocking vaccines, the underlying mechanism is poorly understood.
Here, we show that the rodent malaria parasite gene Plasmodium berghei
GENERATIVE CELL SPECIFIC 1 (PbGCS1) plays a central role in its gametic
interaction. PbGCS1 knockout parasites show male sterility, resulting in
unsuccessful fertilization. Because such a male-specific function of GCS1 has
been observed in angiosperms, this indicates, for the first time, that parasite
sexual reproduction is controlled by a machinery common to flowering plants. Our
present findings provide a new viewpoint for understanding the parasitic
fertilization system and important clues for novel strategies to attack
life-threatening parasites.
