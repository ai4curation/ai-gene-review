Arp2/3 complex generates branched actin networks essential for numerous motile
functions of the cell. It comprises seven subunits: actin-related proteins
(Arps) 2 and 3 and five scaffolding subunits (ArpC1-5). The complex adopts two
major conformations: inactive, with the Arps interacting end-to-end, and active,
with the Arps aligned side-by-side like subunits in the actin filament.
Activation involves several cofactors, including ATP, WASP-family
nucleation-promoting factors (NPFs), actin monomers, and the mother actin
filament. NPFs bind to two sites, one on Arp2-ArpC1 and one on Arp3, delivering
actin subunits at the barbed end of the Arps to initiate branch elongation.
However, the mechanisms by which each NPF drives the equilibrium toward
activation remain unclear. We present two cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM)
structures of Arp2/3 complex at 2.9-Å resolution: one with NPFs bound to Arp3
and ArpC1 but not Arp2 and another with NPFs bound to Arp3 and Arp2-ArpC1. The
structures reveal that NPF binding to Arp2 is allosterically linked to the
release of ArpC5's N-terminal tail from Arp2 and conformational changes in Arp2,
including closure of its ATP-binding cleft and partial rotation and translation
toward its position in the active complex at the branch. Previous work
identified another allosteric switch linking NPF binding to Arp3 with the
release of its inhibitory C-terminal tail, which we also observe. In summary,
both NPF-binding sites induce allosteric changes in Arp2/3 complex, collectively
shifting the equilibrium toward activation.

## Full Text

Arp2/3 complex consists of seven subunits, including actin-related proteins (Arps) 2 and 3 and scaffolding subunits (ArpC1-5). It nucleates branched actin networks that drive processes ranging from cytokinesis to organelle and cell motility (1). The complex adopts two major conformations: inactive in isolation (2) and active at branch junctions (3). In the inactive state, the Arps interact end-to-end, whereas in the active state, they align side-by-side, analogous to actin subunits in the short-pitch helix of the actin filament. Activation provides a platform for growth of the branch (daughter) filament through monomer addition at the barbed end of the Arps. Several cofactors drive the equilibrium toward activation, including ATP binding to the Arps, WASP-family nucleation-promoting factors (NPFs), actin monomers, and the mother actin filament. Although NPFs are generally unrelated, they share a conserved C-terminal W-C-A sequence, comprising one to three actin monomer-binding WH2 domains (W), and central (C) and acidic (A) domains that bind Arp2/3 complex. Two NPFs are required for activation (4, 5): one binds to Arp2–ArpC1 and the other to Arp3. NPFs are thought to perform two main functions: a) contribute the first monomers to initiate branch elongation at the barbed end of the Arps and b) shift the equilibrium of Arp2/3 complex toward the active state. Recently, we described the structure of a transitional state of Arp2/3 complex activation with actin bound to an artificially dimerized NPF construct (6). In this structure, Arp2/3 complex adopted an intermediate conformation between inactive and active states, while the Arps underwent partial flattening, displaying a conformation intermediate between monomeric (G) and filamentous (F) actin. These changes affected Arp2/3 complex globally, obscuring the individual contributions of each NPF-binding site and actin. Here, we present two cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of NPF-bound Arp2/3 complex—one with NPFs bound to Arp3 and ArpC1 but not Arp2, and one with NPFs bound to Arp3 and Arp2–ArpC1. The structures reveal that each NPF-binding site is responsible for allosteric changes in the Arps that collectively drive the equilibrium toward activation, without globally affecting the structure of the complex.

On the Arp3 side, the NPF binds identically in both structures (Fig. 1A). This interaction is consistent with previous work (6, 9), although the current structures provide further details. Residues S461–E483, including the entire C domain (NPF-C) and five residues of the A domain (NPF-A), fold into a single α-helix that binds in the hydrophobic cleft at the barbed end of Arp3 (Fig. 1C). This helix fits between Arp3 residues H371–E389 and A150–R161 on each side of the hydrophobic cleft and also contacts the loop 371HM372 at its C-terminus and F414 at its N terminus. F414 is the last observed residue of Arp3; the remaining C-terminal residues (415GVMS418) are displaced by NPF-C and, as a consequence, become disordered (see below). NPF-A residues D484–E490 were not observed, whereas residues E491–D501, including the conserved W499, bind in a pocket formed by Arp3 residues R329–R341 and Y233–E241. Several basic residues line the path of NPF-A on Arp3, including K251, R329, R333, R337, and R341 (Fig. 1C).

On the Arp2–ArpC1 side, NPF-A residues D497–D501 bind ArpC1 in both structures, with W499 inserting within a pocket formed by the loop K174–F191, which links the third and fourth β-strands of blade 4 of the 7-bladed β-propeller (Fig. 1D). In one structure (Fig. 1A), other NPF residues are not observed, whereas the N-terminal tail of ArpC5 (S7-E21) is bound along a cleft formed between subdomains 3 and 4 of Arp2 (Fig. 1E). In this structure, Arp2 is highly flexible and subdomains 1 to 2 are poorly defined in the map (Fig. 1B). In the other structure, the N-terminal region of ArpC5, up to residue G49, is mostly disordered, even at low signal-to-noise ratios. In contrast, NPF residues S461–D487 are observed bound at the interface between Arp2 and ArpC1 (Fig. 1A). Binding of the NPF to Arp2 helps connect it to the rest of Arp2/3 complex, compensating for the lost interactions with ArpC5. Most of NPF-C is folded as an α-helix (A466–I478), occupying the front end of the hydrophobic cleft of Arp2. The chain then kinks at residue H479, followed by NPF-A residues S480–D487, folded as a second α-helix (Fig. 1D). Residues D488–D496 connecting this α-helix to residues 497D–D501 were not observed. Opposite Arp2, NPF-A interacts with residues E126–R142 of the third blade of ArpC1, and its binding path includes several positively charged side chains on both ArpC1 (R94, K135, K138, K139, K174, K179, K188) and Arp2 (K341, R343) (Fig. 1D).

Compared to the structure with NPFs bound to Arp3 and ArpC1, NPF binding to Arp2 triggers the closure of its nucleotide-binding cleft, with subdomains 1 to 2 rotating 19° relative to subdomains 3 to 4 (Fig. 2A and Movie S2). This closure differs from subunit flattening, which occurs perpendicular to it and is an additional conformational change that actin and the Arps undergo during polymerization and branch formation (3, 20–24). Indeed, in the current structures, the Arps continue to display the twisted conformation characteristic of monomeric actin, which is incompatible with hydrolysis (20). As a result, both Arps retain Mg2+-ATP bound in their catalytic site. NPF binding to Arp2 also imparts rigidity, with subdomains 1 to 2 becoming better defined in the cryo-EM map (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, compared to its position in the structure of Mg2+-ATP-bound Arp2/3 complex (25), Arp2 is rotated ~13° and translated ~7 Å toward its activated position at the branch (3) (Fig. 2B and Movie S2).

A previous study identified another allosteric switch involving the C-terminal tail of Arp3 (26), which in the inactive complex occupies the hydrophobic cleft of Arp3 (2, 25). A crosslinking assay that monitors the Arps transition into the short-pitch conformation revealed that deleting this tail shifts the equilibrium toward activation, suggesting it plays an autoinhibitory role (26). NPF-C was subsequently shown to compete with the Arp3 tail for binding to the Arp3 cleft, supporting a model in which NPF-induced displacement of the Arp3 tail allosterically relieves autoinhibition (27). The structures confirm that NPF-C binding partially displaces the Arp3 tail (6, 9) (Fig. 2C), whereas the inhibitor Arpin stabilizes it in the autoinhibited conformation (8). Since one of our structures contains NPF bound to Arp3 but not Arp2, we asked whether Arp3’s allosteric switch induces additional changes in Arp2/3 complex. Yet, compared to the structure of Mg2+-ATP-bound Arp2/3 complex (25), the only change observed was a ~2-Å translation of Arp2, pushed by the N-terminal end of the Arp3 tail that contacts subdomain 4 of Arp2 (Fig. 2C). While Arp3’s nucleotide cleft closes upon ATP binding (25), unlike Arp2, it does not undergo further closure upon NPF binding. These relatively minor changes appear nonetheless sufficient to trigger dynamic motions within Arp2/3 complex, which more frequently visits the short-pitch state when the tail of Arp3 is either deleted or competitively displaced by NPF binding, as detected by the crosslinking assay (26).

While we have observed here that the N-terminal tail of ArpC5 is allosterically released upon NPF binding to Arp2, cryo-EM structures of Arp2/3 complex at the branch junction show this tail either bound to Arp2 (21–24) or unresolved in a lower-resolution cryo-electron tomography structure (3), likely as a consequence of NPF dissociation upon branch formation (28, 29). In contrast, the C-terminal tail of Arp3 remains unbound in these structures, as it is displaced by the D-loop of the actin subunit bound at the barbed end of Arp3.

The two NPF-binding sites of mammalian Arp2/3 complex exhibit different affinities (Kd of 20 vs. 182 nM), suggesting that they bind in a stepwise manner (30). However, it remains unclear which site binds first. Previously, we found that glia maturation factor (GMF), known to bind Arp2 (31), did not bind to a preformed 1:1:1 actin–NPF–Arp2/3 complex, whereas Arpin C-A, known to bind Arp3, bound this complex (8). These results suggested that the first and higher-affinity NPF-binding site was on Arp2–ArpC1 (30). However, the structures determined here suggest that the first NPF binds to Arp3 and that this site has higher affinity than the one on Arp2–ArpC1. The conflicting nature of these results underscores the need for further investigation, possibly including determining the structure of the 1:1:1 actin–NPF–Arp2/3 complex, to conclusively resolve which NPF binds first and whether this triggers a conformational change that facilitates binding of the second NPF.

Arp2/3 complex subunits Arp3, ArpC1, and ArpC5 exist as two isoforms, with sequence identities of 91%, 67%, and 67%, respectively. Isoform variations correlate with different activities in vitro and in cells (32, 33), which may be partially due to different interactions with NPFs. On Arp3, the NPF and C-terminal tail binding sites are minimally affected by isoform variations. In contrast, ArpC5’s N-terminal tail and the NPF-binding site on Arp2–ArpC1 are considerably impacted by sequence variation between ArpC1 and ArpC5 isoforms (Fig. 2 D–G). The reported expression ratios of ArpC5 to ArpC5L and ArpC1A to ArpC1B in the bovine cerebral cortex are both approximately 2:1 (34). Analysis of the cryo-EM maps for side chains that differ substantially in size between isoforms suggests that ArpC1A is by far the predominant species present in our bovine brain-purified Arp2/3 complex (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), whereas the ArpC5 to ArpC5L ratio appears more consistent with the reported 2:1 value (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

In summary, this work demonstrates that allosteric pathways associated with both NPF-binding sites on Arp2/3 complex collectively drive the complex toward activation. Isoform variations among Arp2/3 complex subunits are likely to influence activation through the ArpC5–Arp2–ArpC1 pathway but not the Arp3 pathway.
