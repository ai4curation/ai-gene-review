Sensory transduction in auditory hair cells gates mechanosensitive ion channels,
converting sound information into electrical signals (Zheng and Holt, 2021).
Previously, we found that Transmembrane channel (TMC) proteins 1 and 2 form the
pore of hair cell transduction channels (Pan et al., 2013; 2018). The structure
of C. elegans TMC proteins (Jeong et al., 2022; Clark et al., 2024) and
predicted mammalian TMC structures (Hahn et al., 2009; Ballesteros et al., 2018;
Pan et al., 2018) are reminiscent of TMEM16 proteins, which function as
Ca2+-activated ion channels and lipid scramblases. Here, we investigated lipid
scramblase activity in live auditory hair cells with pharmacologic or genetic
disruption of TMC1, extending work reported by Ballesteros and Swartz (2022). We
used annexin-V to label phosphatidylserine (PS) localized in the outer leaflet
of hair cell stereocilia membranes. PS externalization was triggered by
disruption of sensory transduction using the blocker, benzamil, or by genetic
mutations that affect TMC1 permeation properties. We found that expression of
either TMC1 or TMC2, was essential for PS externalization. Tmc1/Tmc2 knockout
mice and Tmie mutant mice lacked PS externalization completely. We also
determined that expression of exogenous human TMCs (hTMC1 or hTMC2) in Tmc1/Tmc2
knockout mice induced PS externalization. Lastly, we demonstrated that
expression of a dominant mutation in Tmc1 evoked constitutive PS
externalization, while a recessive mutation eliminated PS externalization. Our
data suggest that disruption of sensory transduction may lead to dysregulation
of membrane homeostasis in hair cells and thus may contribute to auditory
dysfunction in mice and humans.

Human TMC proteins can trigger PS externalizationNext, we investigated whether expression of exogenous human TMC proteins could restore PS externalization and AnnV-647 labeling. Δ/Δ; Tmc2

If TMCs are not directly involved in lipid scrambling in hair cells, what proteins are directly involved and what essential role do TMCs play in modulating or facilitating the activity of those proteins? One potential answer may reside within the TMC superfamily, which includes TMEM63s and TMEM16s (Jan and Jan, 2024), with several TMEM16s functioning as calcium-activated chloride channels and lipid scramblases. Alternatively, inactivation of a flippase, responsible for transporting phospholipids, like phosphatidylserine (PS), from the outer leaflet to the inner leaflet of the bilayer, could also lead to an accumulation of PS in the external leaflet of the membrane.Conversely, the notion that TMCs serve a dual function as mechanosensitive ion channels and lipid scramblases seems plausible. Since TMCs are members of a protein superfamily, in which some members function as both ion channels and lipid scramblases (Jan and Jan, 2024), this dual function property could extend to TMC1 and TMC2, and perhaps other members of the TMC family. However, evidence for TMCs serving a direct role as lipid scramblases is lacking. Direct evidence could come in the form of induced PS externalization in heterologous cells expressing TMCs. Although heterologous TMC expression in the plasma membranes of cell lines has proven challenging, a recent report provided evidence of mechanosensitive currents recorded from HEK293 cells expressing mutant forms of mouse TMC1 or TMC2 or in HEK293 cells with UROD knock-down transfected with human TMC1 or TMC2 (Fu et al., 2024). If reproducible, these expression protocols could be used to investigate the putative scramblase activity of TMCs in isolation. Of course, it remains possible that co-expression of other TMC binding partners may be necessary to evoke scramblase activity in heterologous cells. If TMCs directly mediate scramblase activity, it will be of interest to determine if lipid and ion permeation share the same pathway, or if lipid permeation occurs through a distinct region of the protein or MT complex. Whether PS externalization and plasma membrane homeostasis in hair cells depends on indirect or direct action of the pore-forming subunits of the MT channel complex, TMC1 and TMC2, their roles in lipid scramblase activity and genetic hearing loss remain to be determined.

Thus, factors that inhibit sensory transduction current in hair cells, whether pharmacologic or genetic, promote PS externalization, whereas genetic factors that eliminate TMC function abolish PS externalization. Whether PS externalization has a physiological role in healthy hair cells or is a pathological mechanism in damaged hair cells is not clear. As discussed by Ballesteros and Swartz (2022), we support the notion that changes in membrane homeostasis due to altered scramblase activity may contribute to the hearing loss phenotypes in patients with TMC1 mutations.

To determine whether functional auditory transduction is required for PS externalization or if expression of wild-type TMC1 or TMC2 alone is sufficient to promote scramblase activity, we examined Tmie mutant mice, which lack sensory transduction but retain mislocalized TMC1 and TMC2 expression (Cunningham et al., 2020). Tmie mutant hair cells lacked annexin labeling, even after benzamil application, suggesting that functional and properly localized TMCs, are required for PS externalization.

Throughout the course of this study, we noticed a strong correlation between FM1–43 uptake and the ability of hair cells to externalize PS. Genetic mutations that eliminated FM1–43 uptake, such as Tmc1
Δ/Δ;Tmc2
Δ/Δ double knockouts, Tmc1
N193I/N193I;Tmc2Δ/Δ mice and Tmie mutant mice, also eliminated AnnV-647 labelling entirely. Mutations that permitted FM1–43 uptake, such as single Tmc knockouts, p.D569N mutants and application of benzamil, also permitted PS externalization and AnnV-647 labelling. Since FM1–43 has been characterized as a permeant blocker of hair cell transduction channels (Gale et al., 2002), the correlation between FM1–43 uptake and PS externalization establishes a tighter link between TMC1/2 ion permeation and scramblase activity raising the possibility ions, FM1–43 and lipids may share a common permeation pathway.

However, it remains unclear whether TMC1 and TMC2 have a direct role in lipid scrambling or an essential but indirect role, perhaps by providing a signal or environment that enables lipid scrambling. If TMCs are not directly involved in lipid scrambling in hair cells, what proteins are directly involved and what essential role do TMCs play in modulating or facilitating the activity of those proteins? One potential answer may reside within the TMC superfamily, which includes TMEM63s and TMEM16s (Jan and Jan, 2024), with several TMEM16s functioning as calcium-activated chloride channels and lipid scramblases. Alternatively, inactivation of a flippase, responsible for transporting phospholipids, like phosphatidylserine (PS), from the outer leaflet to the inner leaflet of the bilayer, could also lead to an accumulation of PS in the external leaflet of the membrane.

Conversely, the notion that TMCs serve a dual function as mechanosensitive ion channels and lipid scramblases seems plausible. Since TMCs are members of a protein superfamily, in which some members function as both ion channels and lipid scramblases (Jan and Jan, 2024), this dual function property could extend to TMC1 and TMC2, and perhaps other members of the TMC family. However, evidence for TMCs serving a direct role as lipid scramblases is lacking. Direct evidence could come in the form of induced PS externalization in heterologous cells expressing TMCs. Although heterologous TMC expression in the plasma membranes of cell lines has proven challenging, a recent report provided evidence of mechanosensitive currents recorded from HEK293 cells expressing mutant forms of mouse TMC1 or TMC2 or in HEK293 cells with UROD knock-down transfected with human TMC1 or TMC2 (Fu et al., 2024). If reproducible, these expression protocols could be used to investigate the putative scramblase activity of TMCs in isolation. Of course, it remains possible that co-expression of other TMC binding partners may be necessary to evoke scramblase activity in heterologous cells. If TMCs directly mediate scramblase activity, it will be of interest to determine if lipid and ion permeation share the same pathway, or if lipid permeation occurs through a distinct region of the protein or MT complex. Whether PS externalization and plasma membrane homeostasis in hair cells depends on indirect or direct action of the pore-forming subunits of the MT channel complex, TMC1 and TMC2, their roles in lipid scramblase activity and genetic hearing loss remain to be determined.
