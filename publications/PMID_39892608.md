INTRODUCTION: The synthesis and regulation of ergosterol are vital for fungal
growth and stress adaptation. While ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation is a
recognized mechanism controlling sterol biosynthesis in fungi, prior research
suggests the presence of additional regulatory mechanisms. However, the
specifics of the alternative regulatory mechanisms have not been systematically
investigated.
OBJECTIVES: We proposed that a regulatory network is likely to discern
disturbances in sterol biosynthesis and trigger responses accordingly. This
study aimed to validate the hypothesis and investigate the regulatory
mechanisms.
METHODS: Quantitative Real-time PCR and HPLC-MS/MS were used to explore and
compare the regulation of sterol biosynthesis in different fungi. Key
transcription factors involved in the alternative regulatory mechanism in
Neurospora crassa were identified by phenotypic profiling of a transcription
factor mutant library. ChIP-qPCR, fluorescence confocal imaging, RNA sequencing,
and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were used to reveal the mechanism of
each transcription factor.
RESULTS: Unlike the canonical ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation in fungi
like C. neoformans, our study demonstrated that the inhibitions of ergosterol
biosynthesis at specific steps triggered distinct transcriptional responses of
erg genes in fungi, including N. crassa and Aspergillus fumigatus. In N. crassa,
the responses were orchestrated by different transcription factors.
Specifically, the inhibition of ERG24 and ERG2 activated transcription factors
SAH-2 and AtrR, resulting in the upregulation of erg24, erg2, erg25, and erg3.
Furthermore, the inhibition of ERG11/CYP51 activated transcription factor NcSR,
leading to the upregulation of erg11 and erg6. Phenotypic profiles of mutants of
various N. crassa erg genes and the aforementioned transcription factors implied
that the targeted regulation of ergosterol biosynthesis could fortify fungal
viability within complex habitats.
CONCLUSION: Our study reveals a novel regulatory mechanism in fungi: targeted
upregulation of specific sterol biosynthesis genes in response to given
perturbations in ergosterol biosynthesis, exhibiting a higher degree of
precision and sophistication in sterol biosynthesis regulation.

Introduction

Sterols are crucial lipid components of eukaryotic cells, essential for maintaining membrane dynamics and ensuring membrane functions. Deficiencies in sterols can result in impaired membrane fluidity and permeability, the destabilization of membrane microdomains, and incorrect distribution of membrane proteins. These disruptions can affect a range of biological processes, including the cell cycle, cell proliferation, cell–cell communication, mating, vesicle trafficking, and stress responses [1], [2], [3], [4]. Ergosterol is the predominant sterol in fungi, analogous to cholesterol in mammals. Its biosynthetic pathway represents a pivotal target for widely used antifungal agents in both clinical and agricultural settings [5].

To support normal cellular activities and stress adaptation, cells must maintain sterol homeostasis. A critical mechanism is the transcriptional regulation of sterol biosynthesis genes. In fungi, when ergosterol biosynthesis is inhibited by hypoxia or sterol biosynthesis inhibitors like azoles, key sterol biosynthesis genes such as erg11(CYP51) are transcriptionally upregulated to attenuate the resulting toxicity [6], [7]. Similar phenomena are observed in mammals [8]. Current knowledge attributes these transcriptional responses primarily to several key transcription factors, including SREBP (sterol regulatory element binding protein), Upc2p (sterol uptake control protein 2), and FgSR. SREBP is a major regulator of sterol biosynthesis, conserved from mammals to fungi [9]. Upc2p serves as a functional homolog of SREBP in Saccharomyces fungi [10], and FgSR is a recently identified transcription factor in Fusarium
[11]. Additional transcription factors that regulate sterol biosynthesis have also been reported, such as Ndt80 and Adr in yeast [12], [13], and AtrR and SltA in A. fumigatus
[14], [15], indicating a complex regulatory network controlling sterol biosynthesis.

Ergosterol and cholesterol, the end products of sterol biosynthesis, contribute the most in activating these transcription factors and triggering the expression of sterol biosynthesis genes. For SREBP, the presence of ergosterol or cholesterol anchors it to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane. Upon ergosterol depletion, SREBP detaches from the ER membrane and is cleaved at the Golgi apparatus to release the N-terminal transcription factor domain, which is transferred into the nucleus to activate sterol biosynthesis genes [16], [17]. Similarly, Upc2p is sequestered in the cytosol by ergosterol and is released upon ergosterol depletion [18], [19]. Although not fully understood, FgSR is also believed to be activated by insufficient ergosterol levels [11]. Obviously, these examples illustrate a typical end-product-mediated feedback regulation mechanism. Regardless of which step in ergosterol biosynthesis is inhibited, the pathway employs a consistent transcriptional response strategy to address ergosterol depletion.

Despite extensive research on ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation, the mechanisms governing the regulation of erg genes across the majority of fungal species remain largely unexplored. Unexpectedly, a previous study in N. crassa revealed that conditions leading to ergosterol depletion do not consistently trigger the upregulation of erg genes [20]. Several erg genes exhibited varied transcriptional responses to different kinds of ergosterol biosynthesis inhibitors in N. crassa and Trichophyton rubrum
[20], [21], [22]. These suggest the presence of additional regulatory mechanisms distinct from ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation. In both fungi and mammals, the biosynthesis of sterols is a complex process involving multiple enzymes. Disruption of a single step in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway leads to the depletion of the final product, ergosterol, and the accumulation of one or more sterol intermediates with differing chemical bonds [3]. These intermediates exhibit specific functions within the membrane, rather than indiscriminately disturbing membrane functions, thereby eliciting distinct phenotypes in defined cellular processes [23], [24], [25]. Furthermore, mutants of certain ergosterol biosynthesis genes (erg) are viable, and the severity of growth defects caused by the deletion of different erg genes varies among fungi [20], [26], [27]. Notably, under specific stress conditions such as heat and high glucose, some erg mutants exhibit superior growth compared to the wild-type strain [27], [28]. These results imply that the cells need to distinguish perturbations at different steps of sterol biosynthesis and mount diverse responses. Therefore, we hypothesize that a regulatory mechanism capable of discerning perturbations in sterol biosynthesis may be integral to governing this process in fungi like N. crassa. However, the existence of this mechanism, and its underlying principles, calls for further detailed investigation.

In this study, following a comprehensive investigation, we corroborated that inhibition of ergosterol biosynthesis at discrete steps triggered distinct transcriptional responses of erg genes in not only N. crassa but also A. fumigatus. This underscores the existence of targeted regulation of erg genes in response to specific perturbations in ergosterol biosynthesis. The results that the responses of erg genes are separately activated by different transcription factors not only further support the existence of the mechanism but also provide insights into the underlying regulatory networks. This finely tuned regulatory mechanism allows fungi to adapt effectively to complex environments, enhancing their survival strategies. In brief, our study illuminates a sophisticated regulatory framework for fungal sterol biosynthesis that deviates from the canonical ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation, shedding new light on the maintenance of sterol homeostasis.

ResultsSterol biosynthesis genes respond differently to ergosterol biosynthesis inhibitors in Neurospora crassaTo test our hypothesis, we conducted further investigations informed by our previous results in N. crassa that several erg genes exhibited varied transcriptional responses to different kinds of ergosterol biosynthesis inhibitors [20]. We first analyzed genome-wide gene expression profiles of N. crassa treated with three distinct sterol biosynthesis inhibitors (SBIs): terbinafine (TERB) targeting squalene epoxidase ERG1, ketoconazole (KTC) targeting sterol C14 demethylase ERG11, and amorolfine (AMO) targeting sterol C14 reductase ERG24/sterol C8 isomerase ERG2. Each SBI inhibited the hyphal extension of the wild-type N. crassa strain at the minimum inhibitory concentration (Fig. S1A). Sterol profiling revealed that each SBI reduced ergosterol content and led to the accumulation of specific sterol intermediates (Fig. S1B).RNA-seq analysis revealed diverse genome-wide transcriptional changes induced by these inhibitors (Fig. 1A). Unexpectedly, despite the unique effects of each SBI, a substantial number of genes (893 genes) were commonly affected by all three inhibitors (Fig. 1B). GO enrichment analysis suggests that these genes likely correspond to general stress responses, possibly related with impaired membrane functions due to abnormal sterol biosynthesis (File S2). Except that, a series of genes were found to be specifically affected by each SBI (Fig. 1B). Sterol biosynthesis genes, which are required for adaptation to sterol biosynthesis inhibition, were enriched among genes specifically upregulated by ketoconazole or amorolfine, as revealed by pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. S2 and File S2), indicating differential responses of erg genes to distinct inhibitors. KTC treatment dramatically induced the expression of erg11, erg6, and erg24, while AMO treatment significantly induced erg24, erg2, erg25, and erg3 (Fig. 1C). In contrast, TERB treatment did not induce these erg genes. These results were further confirmed by qRT-PCR (Fig. S1C). For comparison with responses typically seen in ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation, we also analyzed the responses of erg genes to the three SBIs in Cryptococcus neoformans. As with N. crassa, the growth of wild-type C. neoformans strain was inhibited by SBIs, resulting in reduced ergosterol content and the accumulation of sterol intermediates (Fig. S1D and E). Similar to SAH-2, a decrease in basal expression of erg24 was observed in atrRKO strain, consistent with the capacity of AtrR to bind to the erg24 promoter under normal conditions (Fig. 4F). Unlike SAH-2, AtrR predominantly localized in the cytosol under normal conditions and translocated to the nucleus upon AMO treatment (Fig. 4D), underscoring its role as a putative primary transcription factor responsive to AMO.

However, the expression of erg24 remained upregulated following AMO treatment in both sah-2KO and atrRKO strains (Fig. 4F). Given a coordinated regulation of SREBP and AtrR in gene expression in A. fumigatus
[37], we postulated that similar coordination occurs in the regulation of erg24 expression in N. crassa. Consequently, we constructed the double mutant of sah-2 and atrR. Plate drug sensitivity assay revealed that the double mutant exhibited increased sensitivity to AMO compared to the single deletion mutants (Fig. 4E). Analysis of gene expression demonstrated a complete loss of erg24 response to AMO in the double mutant (Fig. 4F). These results suggest a collaborative role of AtrR and SAH-2 in the transcriptional regulation of erg24 in N. crassa.

DiscussionUnderstanding how sterol homeostasis is achieved through the transcriptional regulation of sterol biosynthesis genes holds broad implications for cellular physiology, fungal pathogen control, and evolutionary biology. Prior studies in fungi and mammals have firmly established the well-documented end-product feedback regulation of sterol biosynthesis, in which the upregulation of erg genes is triggered by a key transcription factor upon ergosterol/cholesterol depletion [9], [40]. In yeast, for example, conditions, such as hypoxia, sterol biosynthesis inhibitors, and erg gene disruptions, can activate Upc2p, inducing the expression of erg11, erg2, erg25, erg5, and others [41], [42], [43]. While it has been believed that ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation of sterol biosynthesis is conserved across fungi, our study reveals that erg genes can be separately regulated by multiple transcription factors in response to perturbations at different steps of sterol biosynthesis (Fig. 8). Given that target overexpression is a major mechanism for antifungal resistance [44], our results indicate that fungi can selectively induce the expression of required erg genes rather than all of them, hinting at a more intelligent regulation strategy. Interestingly, the genetic disruption of erg3, erg4, or erg5 in the late steps of ergosterol biosynthesis, which had a slight or even negligible impact on growth, exhibited a minimal effect on the expression of examined erg genes (Fig. 1E, 6A). This suggests that fungi only need to address defects that pose a significant threat to their survival by modulating sterol biosynthesis. More importantly, differential responses of erg genes to different SBIs are also observed in A. fumigatus in our study, as well as T. rubrum in previous studies [21], [22], confirming the widespread nature of this sophisticated regulatory mechanism.Fig. 8A proposed model for the targeted regulation of sterol biosynthesis in Neurospora crassa. Perturbations at different steps of the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway activate distinct transcription factors to regulate corresponding ergosterol biosynthesis genes for maintaining sterol homeostasis. In N. crassa, inhibition of sterol C14 demethylase ERG11 by KTC activates NcSR, leading to the upregulation of erg11 and erg6. On the other hand, inhibition of sterol C14 reductase ERG24 and sterol C8 isomerase ERG2 by AMO activate SAH-2 and AtrR, resulting in the upregulation of erg24, erg2, erg25, and erg3. The accumulation of specific sterol intermediates is proposed to trigger the activation of the responses. Draw by FigDraw.It is evident from our aggregated data that finely tuned regulatory mechanisms according to inhibited steps in sterol biosynthesis are present in particular fungal strains. Although the role of ergosterol depletion has not been definitively ruled out, the observed responses could be predominantly attributed to the accumulation of specific sterol intermediates. Different SBI treatments resulted in the accumulation of distinct sterol intermediates along with ergosterol depletion in fungi (Fig. 7 and Fig. S1). In line with this, N. crassa and A. fumigatus exhibited variable transcriptional responses of erg genes to different SBI treatments. Typical ergosterol feedback regulation in fungi like C. neoformans yields a uniform transcriptional response of erg genes. Interestingly, recent studies on A. fumigatus also suggest that the accumulation of particular intermediates like eburicol is involved in the toxicity of azoles and the activation of a specific protein Hmg1 [45], [46], [47]. Sterol intermediates are believed to activate cellular responses [24], [48], [49]. In mammals and yeast, the accumulation of sterol intermediate lanosterol promotes the degradation of HMG CoA reductase, an enzyme upstream in the sterol biosynthesis pathway [9], suggesting that sterol intermediates can regulate sterol biosynthesis. However, it remains largely unknown which sterol intermediates are responsible for triggering the responses observed in our study. KTC treatment results in the accumulation of 14-methyl sterols with defective chemical bonds at C14, C7, C24, and C22, while AMO treatment leads to defects at C14 and C7. The shared C14 and C7 defects may explain why KTC also induced the upregulation of erg24, albeit to a lesser extent than the AMO-induced level (Fig. 1C and D). The relevance of the C7 double bond to the AMO response seems more pronounced. Since the responses of erg genes are similar in erg24KO and erg2KO strains (Fig. 1E), the deletion of erg24 leads to defects in both C14 and C7, while erg2 deletion only affects C7. The roles of other chemical bonds in sterols remain unclear due to the complexity of the sterol biosynthetic pathway. Why do different sterol intermediates activate distinct transcriptional responses? We hypothesize that sterol intermediates may be specifically detected by the identified transcription factors like NcSR and AtrR. In mammals, steroid hormones are detected by a class of transcription factors known as nuclear receptors, such as estrogen receptors [50]. In yeast and Candida species, the transcription factor Upc2p, which is essential for the regulation of sterol biosynthesis, interacts with ergosterol [18], [19]. Interestingly, according to protein structure search by Foldseek [51], the C-terminal of NcSR exhibits a high degree of structural similarity to the ergosterol-binding region of Upc2p, and AtrR also shares a certain degree of structural similarity with the ligand binding region of Upc2p and estrogen receptor, thus providing a critical support for the hypothesis. Nevertheless, this hypothesis, along with its underlying mechanisms, necessitates additional exploration and validation.Our findings reemphasize the rewiring in sterol biosynthesis regulation. The regulatory mechanism of sterol biosynthesis has been demonstrated to vary across the phylogenetic tree of fungi. Typically, i) Upc2p replaces SREBP to regulate erg genes in Saccharomycotina [10]; ii) in Fusarium, a novel transcription factor FgSR, but not SREBP, functions to activate the expression of erg genes [11]. Even within the SREBP pathway, differences in the proteinases responsible for SREBP cleavage and the SCAP protein that binds ergosterol are observed among different fungal species [52]. Despite these variations, ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation remains a fundamental mechanism. Our study reveals an extensive alteration with a shift from ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation in species like fission yeast, C. neoformans, budding yeast, and Candida to targeted regulation based on disruptions at specific steps in fungi such as N. crassa and A. fumigatus
[36], [40], [53]. The regulatory mechanisms in other fungi remain largely unexplored, but our results give a glimpse into the evolving paradigm of sterol biosynthesis regulation among fungi. As supposed by our results, this kind of regulation could potentiate the survival of fungi in complex environments. However, the situation within certain fungi is complex, characterized by the presence of multiple copies of erg genes and the differential tolerance to the accumulated specific sterol intermediates [20], [45], [54]. The underlying reasons and mechanisms by which the rewiring of sterol biosynthesis regulation confers benefits to fungi warrant further exploration.Remarkably, as our understanding of the regulatory framework of sterol biosynthesis deepens, transcription factors like SREBP, AtrR, and FgSR, which are initially identified in other fungi [11], [14], [55], continue to be the prominent players in the emerging model, highlighting their significance. However, their subcellular localization, responses to sterol biosynthesis inhibitors, and the genes they regulate have changed across fungi, indicating functional rewiring of these regulators. For instance, AtrR and SREBP (SAH-2) directly bind to the promoters of erg24 and erg2 to control their expression in N. crassa (Fig. 4). However, in A. fumigatus, there are no recognized binding sites for AtrR and SREBP (SrbA) on the erg24 and erg2 promoters. Instead, they directly regulate the expression of other erg genes, such as erg11 and erg1[14], [38]. These findings indicate that, although similar differential responses are observed in A. fumigatus and N. crassa, the transcription factors acting for a given perturbation may differ. It is plausible as the rewiring of transcription factors frequently occurs in species [10], [13], [56]. However, this greatly complicates our understanding of how transcription factors are associated with various perturbations, which is largely unknown. Anyway, our results may give an example of phenotypic innovations through rewiring in sterol biosynthesis regulation. Phenotypic innovations enhance the adaptability of fungi to changing environments [57]. Thus, while the underlying mechanisms are not yet fully elucidated, our findings suggest implications for environmental adaptation via the modulation of sterol biosynthesis regulation.

Understanding how sterol homeostasis is achieved through the transcriptional regulation of sterol biosynthesis genes holds broad implications for cellular physiology, fungal pathogen control, and evolutionary biology. Prior studies in fungi and mammals have firmly established the well-documented end-product feedback regulation of sterol biosynthesis, in which the upregulation of erg genes is triggered by a key transcription factor upon ergosterol/cholesterol depletion [9], [40]. In yeast, for example, conditions, such as hypoxia, sterol biosynthesis inhibitors, and erg gene disruptions, can activate Upc2p, inducing the expression of erg11, erg2, erg25, erg5, and others [41], [42], [43]. While it has been believed that ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation of sterol biosynthesis is conserved across fungi, our study reveals that erg genes can be separately regulated by multiple transcription factors in response to perturbations at different steps of sterol biosynthesis (Fig. 8). Given that target overexpression is a major mechanism for antifungal resistance [44], our results indicate that fungi can selectively induce the expression of required erg genes rather than all of them, hinting at a more intelligent regulation strategy. Interestingly, the genetic disruption of erg3, erg4, or erg5 in the late steps of ergosterol biosynthesis, which had a slight or even negligible impact on growth, exhibited a minimal effect on the expression of examined erg genes (Fig. 1E, 6A). This suggests that fungi only need to address defects that pose a significant threat to their survival by modulating sterol biosynthesis. It is evident from our aggregated data that finely tuned regulatory mechanisms according to inhibited steps in sterol biosynthesis are present in particular fungal strains. Although the role of ergosterol depletion has not been definitively ruled out, the observed responses could be predominantly attributed to the accumulation of specific sterol intermediates. Different SBI treatments resulted in the accumulation of distinct sterol intermediates along with ergosterol depletion in fungi (Fig. 7 and Fig. S1). In line with this, N. crassa and A. fumigatus exhibited variable transcriptional responses of erg genes to different SBI treatments. Typical ergosterol feedback regulation in fungi like C. neoformans yields a uniform transcriptional response of erg genes. Interestingly, recent studies on A. fumigatus also suggest that the accumulation of particular intermediates like eburicol is involved in the toxicity of azoles and the activation of a specific protein Hmg1 [45], [46], [47]. Sterol intermediates are believed to activate cellular responses [24], [48], [49]. In mammals and yeast, the accumulation of sterol intermediate lanosterol promotes the degradation of HMG CoA reductase, an enzyme upstream in the sterol biosynthesis pathway [9], suggesting that sterol intermediates can regulate sterol biosynthesis. However, it remains largely unknown which sterol intermediates are responsible for triggering the responses observed in our study. KTC treatment results in the accumulation of 14-methyl sterols with defective chemical bonds at C14, C7, C24, and C22, while AMO treatment leads to defects at C14 and C7. The shared C14 and C7 defects may explain why KTC also induced the upregulation of erg24, albeit to a lesser extent than the AMO-induced level (Fig. 1C and D). The relevance of the C7 double bond to the AMO response seems more pronounced. Since the responses of erg genes are similar in erg24KO and erg2KO strains (Fig. 1E), the deletion of erg24 leads to defects in both C14 and C7, while erg2 deletion only affects C7. The roles of other chemical bonds in sterols remain unclear due to the complexity of the sterol biosynthetic pathway. Why do different sterol intermediates activate distinct transcriptional responses? We hypothesize that sterol intermediates may be specifically detected by the identified transcription factors like NcSR and AtrR. In mammals, steroid hormones are detected by a class of transcription factors known as nuclear receptors, such as estrogen receptors [50]. In yeast and Candida species, the transcription factor Upc2p, which is essential for the regulation of sterol biosynthesis, interacts with ergosterol [18], [19]. Interestingly, according to protein structure search by Foldseek [51], the C-terminal of NcSR exhibits a high degree of structural similarity to the ergosterol-binding region of Upc2p, and AtrR also shares a certain degree of structural similarity with the ligand binding region of Upc2p and estrogen receptor, thus providing a critical support for the hypothesis. Nevertheless, this hypothesis, along with its underlying mechanisms, necessitates additional exploration and validation.

Our findings reemphasize the rewiring in sterol biosynthesis regulation. The regulatory mechanism of sterol biosynthesis has been demonstrated to vary across the phylogenetic tree of fungi. Typically, i) Upc2p replaces SREBP to regulate erg genes in Saccharomycotina [10]; ii) in Fusarium, a novel transcription factor FgSR, but not SREBP, functions to activate the expression of erg genes [11]. Even within the SREBP pathway, differences in the proteinases responsible for SREBP cleavage and the SCAP protein that binds ergosterol are observed among different fungal species [52]. Despite these variations, ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation remains a fundamental mechanism. Our study reveals an extensive alteration with a shift from ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation in species like fission yeast, C. neoformans, budding yeast, and Candida to targeted regulation based on disruptions at specific steps in fungi such as N. crassa and A. fumigatus
[36], [40], [53]. The regulatory mechanisms in other fungi remain largely unexplored, but our results give a glimpse into the evolving paradigm of sterol biosynthesis regulation among fungi. As supposed by our results, this kind of regulation could potentiate the survival of fungi in complex environments. However, the situation within certain fungi is complex, characterized by the presence of multiple copies of erg genes and the differential tolerance to the accumulated specific sterol intermediates [20], [45], [54]. ConclusionOur study discloses a nuanced transcriptional regulation mechanism for sterol biosynthesis, extending beyond the already-known ergosterol-mediated feedback regulation. This mechanism arises from the functional rewiring of several conserved transcription factors and enables fungi to address perturbations in sterol biosynthesis with increased precision and potentially greater efficiency, thereby enhancing fungal resilience in complex environments.
