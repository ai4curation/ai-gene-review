ABSTRACT

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a multiprotein complex that plays a vital role in the innate immune system in response to microbial infections and endogenous danger signals. Aberrant activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome is implicated in a spectrum of inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, emphasizing the necessity for precise regulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome to maintain immune homeostasis. The protein level of NLRP3 is a limiting step for inflammasome activation, which must be tightly controlled to avoid detrimental consequences. Here, we demonstrate that ABHD8, a member of the α/β-hydrolase domain-containing (ABHD) family, interacts with NLRP3 and promotes its degradation through the chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) pathway. ABHD8 acts as a scaffold to recruit palmitoyltransferase ZDHHC12 to NLRP3 for its palmitoylation as well as subsequent CMA-mediated degradation. Notably, ABHD8 deficiency results in the stabilization of NLRP3 protein and promotes NLRP3 inflammasome activation. We further confirm that ABHD8 overexpression ameliorates LPS- or alum-triggered NLRP3 inflammasome activation in vivo . Interestingly, the nucleocapsid (N) protein of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) impairs the ABHD8-NLRP3 association, resulting in an elevation in NLRP3 protein level and excessive inflammasome activation. These findings demonstrate that ABHD8 May represent a potential therapeutic target in conditions associated with NLRP3 inflammasome dysregulation.

Introduction

NLRP3 (NLR family pyrin domain containing 3) is a crucial pattern recognition receptor that detects a variety of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or danger/damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) by triggering the assembly of inflammasome to induce CASP1 (caspase 1) activation, IL1B (interleukin 1 beta) and IL18 maturation and secretion, and pyroptosis [ 1 , 2 ]. Dysregulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome has been implicated in various inflammatory and autoimmune diseases [ 3 ], including gout [ 4 ], type 2 diabetes [ 5 ], rheumatoid arthritis [ 6 ], atherosclerosis [ 7 ], and Alzheimer disease [ 8 ]. Consequently, it is imperative to tightly regulate the activity of the NLRP3 inflammasome to prevent deleterious consequences and uphold immune balance.

The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome involves two critical processes: priming and activation [ 2 ]. The priming step, which encompasses the upregulation of NLRP3 gene expression and pro-IL1B synthesis in response to TLR (toll like receptor) ligands, is crucial for sensitizing the cells and preparing them for subsequent inflammasome activation [ 9 ]. Hence, maintaining an appropriate protein level of NLRP3 is a key step in inflammasome activation. While the regulation of NLRP3 expression can occur at the transcriptional level by modifying NLRP3 mRNA expression, increasing evidence suggest that post-translational modifications, such as ubiquitination and phosphorylation, play crucial roles in the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [ 10 ]. The ABHD (α/β-hydrolase domain-containing) enzymes are characterized by the presence of the conserved α/β-hydrolase fold and are involved in various biological processes, including lipid metabolism [ 11–13 ], endocannabinoid signaling [ 12 ], and autophagy [ 14–16 ]. Previous studies demonstrated that ABHD8 deletion has a modest effect on bone mineral density in females [ 17 ], and NLRP3 inflammasome activation were closely related to increase the risk of osteoporosis, characterized by a decrease in bone mineral density and an increased risk of fracture [ 18 ]. However, whether ABHD8 is involved in the regulation of NLRP3 inflammasome activation remains unknown.

Here, we demonstrate that ABHD8 interacts with NLRP3, subsequently promoting NLRP3 degradation through chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) pathway. ABHD8 works as a scaffold to recruit palmitoyltransferase ZDHHC12 to promote the palmitoylation and CMA-mediated degradation of NLRP3. Accordingly, ABHD8 deficiency stabilizes NLRP3 protein and promotes NLRP3 inflammasome activation. We further confirm that ABHD8 significantly decreases LPS or alum-triggered NLRP3 inflammasome activation in vivo . Notably, the nucleocapsid (N) protein in severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) hinders the association between ABHD8 and NLRP3, resulting in an upregulation of NLRP3 protein level and excessive inflammasome activation. These findings collectively suggest that ABHD8 might be a potential target for the treatment of NLRP3 inflammasome-related diseases.

Results

ABHD8 interacts with NLRP3

To investigate the potential involvement of ABHD8 in the regulation of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, an immunoprecipitation (IP) assay was conducted ( Figure 1A ). This finding was further supported by confocal analysis and Förster/fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assay, which demonstrated a strong association between NLRP3 and ABHD8 ( Figure 1B,C ). Additionally, the association between NLRP3 and ABHD8 was significantly enhanced by NLRP3 inflammasome inducers such as ATP, nigericin, silica, or alum ( Figure 1D,F ). Further investigation revealed that the NOD (nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain) and LRR (leucine-rich repeat) domains of NLRP3 were involved in the interaction with ABHD8 ( Figure 1F,G ). Moreover, it was observed that ABHD8 did not interact with PYCARD/ASC, pro-CASP1 ( Figure 1H ), or other receptors such as NLRP1, AIM2, or NLRC4 ( Figure 1I ). Taken together, these findings indicate a specific interaction between ABHD8 and NLRP3.

Figure 1.

ABHD8 specifically attenuates NLRP3 inflammasome activation

To explore the potential effect of ABHD8 during NLRP3 inflammasome activation, we designed two small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting ABHD8 to downregulate its expression. We observed that ABHD8 knockdown notably enhanced CASP1 activation, IL1B maturation and production, and cell death in response to NLRP3 inflammasome inducers but not AIM2 inflammasome activator poly(dA:dT) (Figure S1). To substantiate these findings, two ABHD8 -knockout (KO) THP-1 cells were constructed by means of the CRISPR-Cas9 technique. Consistently, ATP, nigericin, silica, or alum-triggered NLRP3 inflammasome activation but not poly(dA:dT)-induced AIM2 inflammasome activation was significantly increased in ABHD8 -deficient THP-1 derived macrophages ( Figure 2A,B ). Similarly, transfection of Abhd8 siRNA in bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) resulted in a marked and specific enhancement of NLPR3 inflammasome activation ( Figure 2C,D ). In contrast, ABHD8 deficiency had no inhibitory effects on the production of inflammasome-independent proinflammatory cytokines IL6 or TNF/TNF-α (Figure S2). Taken together, these data indicate that ABHD8 specifically attenuates NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

Figure 2.

ABHD8 mediates NLRP3 degradation in a lysosome-dependent manner

To dissect the inhibitory function of ABHD8 in inflammasome activation through its interaction with NLRP3, we examined the effect of ABHD8 on the stability of NLRP3. Our findings revealed that an increasing amount of ABHD8 significantly reduced the protein level of NLRP3 without affecting NLRP3 mRNA abundance ( Figure 3A–C ). Additionally, through a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay, we demonstrated that the degradation rate of endogenous NLRP3, but not PYCARD or pro-CASP1, was notably attenuated by ABHD8 deletion (Figure S3A, B and S3D-G). These data suggest that ABHD8 reduces NLRP3 protein level by facilitating its degradation.

Figure 3.

In eukaryotic cells, the ubiquitin-proteasome system and the autophagy-lysosomal system are the major quality control system responsible for protein degradation [ 19 ]. To identify which degradation system is substantially involved in ABHD8-mediated NLRP3 degradation, we employed various related inhibitors and found that the degradation of NLRP3 mediated by ABHD8 was impeded by lysosome inhibitors ammonium chloride (NH 4 Cl) and chloroquine (CQ) but not by the proteasome inhibitor carfilzomib (CFZ) or macroautophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA) (Figure S3C, D). Additionally, the lysosome inhibitor treatment could reverse the effects of ABHD8 deletion on NLRP3 protein level in THP-1-derived macrophages or BMDMs, while the proteasome inhibitor or the macroautophagy inhibitor did not have the same effect ( Figure 3H–K ). Furthermore, ABHD8 was still able to promote NLRP3 degradation in BECN1 - or ATG5 -deficient HEK293T cells, in which macroautophagy was impaired ( Figure 3L,M ). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ABHD8-mediated NLRP3 degradation primarily occurs through the lysosome pathway, rather than the proteasome or macroautophagic pathways.

ABHD8 promotes NLRP3 degradation through the CMA pathway

CMA is a lysosome-dependent selective degradation pathway that targets substrates containing the Lys-Phe-Glu-Arg-Gln (KFERQ)-like motif. These substrates are recognized by HSPA8/HSC70 (heat shock protein family A (Hsp70) member 8), which subsequently delivers them to lysosomes for degradation by interacting with LAMP2A (lysosomal associated membrane protein 2A) [ 20 ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that NLRP3 is a substrate of CMA [ 21 , 22 ]. Therefore, we investigated whether ABHD8-mediated NLRP3 degradation occurs through the CMA pathway. We observed that ABHD8 markedly promoted the association between NLRP3 and HSPA8 or LAMP2A (Figure S4A, B). We subsequently demonstrated that the NLRP3-HSPA8-LAMP2A interaction was strongly impaired in ABHD8 deficient THP-1-derived macrophages ( Figure 4A,B ). Additionally, knockdown of HSPA8 or LAMP2A prevented ABHD8-mediated NLRP3 degradation in HEK293T cells (Figure S4C-F). Consistently, we found that the NLRP3 protein levels were upregulated in ABHD8 -KO THP-1-derived macrophages, and knockdown of HSPA8 or LAMP2A could reverse the effects of ABHD8 deficiency on NLRP3 protein level in THP-1-derived macrophages ( Figure 4C–F ). Collectively, these results suggest that ABHD8 promotes NLRP3 degradation through the CMA pathway.

Figure 4.

ABHD8 recruits ZDHHC12 to palmitoylate NLRP3 for facilitating its degradation

Since palmitoylation of NLRP3 is critical for NLRP3 degradation through the CMA pathway [ 22 ] and several members of ABHD family (e.g., ABHD10 and ABHD17) have been implicated in the regulation of protein palmitoylation [ 23 , 24 ], we speculated that ABHD8 might influence the palmitoylation level of NLRP3. We observed that ABHD8 overexpression enhanced the palmitoylation level of NLRP3 ( Figure 5A,B ), while ABHD8 deletion reserved this effect ( Figure 5C,D ). Notably, ABHD8 lacks of the acyltransferase activity motif (HXXXXD), so we hypothesized that ABHD8 works as a scaffold protein to recruit other palmitoyltransferase to palmitoylate NLRP3. Since we have demonstrated that ZDHHC12 is a major palmitoyltransferase to mediate NLRP3 palmitoylation in the late sate of inflammasome activation [ 22 ], we sought to investigate whether ABHD8-mediated the enhancement of NLRP3 palmitoylation and degradation through recruiting ZDHHC12. Through IP assay, we identified the interaction between ZDHHC12 and ABHD8 ( Figure 5E ). Notably, ABHD8 deletion significantly impaired the NLRP3-ZDHHC12 interaction ( Figure 5F,G ). Consistently, overexpression of ZDHHC12 could promote the palmitoylation and degradation of NLRP3, while ABHD8 deficiency abolished these effects, confirming that ABHD8 is critical for ZDHHC12-mediated NLRP3 palmitoylation and degradation ( Figure 5H–K ). Meanwhile, ZDHHC12 deficiency impaired ABHD8-mediated the promotion of NLRP3 palmitoylation and degradation ( Figure 5L–O ). Taken together, these results demonstrate that ABHD8 plays an important role in the recruitment of ZDHHC12 to NLRP3, subsequently facilitating NLRP3 palmitoylation and degradation.

Figure 5.

ABHD8 negatively regulates LPS- or alum-induced inflammation in vivo

To further elucidate the biological significance of ABHD8, we established an ABHD8 overexpression model by administering an expression plasmid via injection. Utilizing an endotoxic shock model induced by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of E. coli LPS, we observed that ABHD8 overexpression remarkedly inhibited LPS challenge-induced IL1B secretion, but not IL6 or TNF/TNF-α secretion in the plasma in vivo ( Figure 6A ). In addition, the NLRP3 protein levels and the injury of lung tissues were significantly decreased in ABHD8 overexpression mice relative to control mice ( Figure 6B,C ). Moreover, alum challenge apparently reduced the IL1B production but not IL6 or TNF/TNF-α in the peritoneal lavage fluids of ABHD8 overexpression mice compared with control mice ( Figure 6D ). And the NLRP3 protein levels were dramatically decreased in the peritoneal exudate cells from ABHD8 overexpression mice ( Figure 6E ). These results suggest that ABHD8 plays a crucial role in suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation in vivo.

Figure 6.

SARS-CoV-2 N protein impairs the NLRP3-ABHD8 association

SARS-CoV-2 infection-induced excessive inflammasome activation is associated with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) severity, which causes great harm to public health problem and social development [ 25 ]. Hence, unraveling the pathogenesis of SARS-CoV-2 stands as a critical and urgent scientific puzzle that needs to be solved.

As previously reported [ 25 ], we confirmed that the nucleocapsid (N) protein of SARS-CoV-2 could promote NLRP3 inflammasome activation ( Figure 7A ). Notably, we observed that an increasing amount of N enhanced the protein level of NLRP3 ( Figure 7B ). Thus, in addition to its known role in promoting the association between NLRP3 and PYCARD [ 25 ], our finding provide support to the hypothesis that the N protein may also interact with other molecules to influence the stability of NLRP3. Interestingly, we found that both NLRP3 and ABHD8 interacted with the N protein ( Figure 7C,D ), and N protein could rescue ABHD8-mediated NLRP3 degradation ( Figure 7E ), suggesting that N protein might impair the NLRP3-ABHD8 interaction. We next found that N protein significantly decreased the association between NLRP3 and ABHD8 ( Figure 7E ). In addition, when ABHD8 is knocked out, there is a noticeable increase in the NLRP3 protein level, but this effect is observed only in the absence of the N protein ( Figure 7F ). Correspondingly, N protein could not further significantly promote the production of IL1B in ABHD8 -deficient cells ( Figure 7G ). Therefore, combining the results from previous studies with our observations, it can be concluded that the N protein of SARS-CoV-2 disrupts the NLRP3-ABHD8 association to enhance NLRP3 stability, ultimately leading to increased inflammasome activation.

Discussion

Given that uncontrolled NLRP3 inflammasome activation is closely related to inflammation-related diseases [ 3 ], including rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, asthma, atherosclerosis, and cancer, the protein level and activity of NLRP3 must be tightly controlled to avoid aberrant consequences and maintain homeostasis. Previous studies demonstrated that the ABHD family plays crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and modulating physiological process [ 26 ]. For instance, ABHD5/CGI-58, is involved in lipid droplet formation and lipolysis, and its dysfunction can lead to lipid storage disorders [ 27 ]; ABHD6 can regulate endocannabinoid signaling [ 28 , 29 ] and has been implicated in various physiological processes, including pain sensation [ 30 ], neuroprotection [ 31 ], and insulin secretion [ 32 ]. Thus, continued research on the ABHD family is essential for understanding their specific mechanisms of action and their potential as therapeutic targets for various diseases. Here, we uncover the function of ABHD8, a member of the ABHD family, in the regulation of NLRP3 inflammasome activation: ABHD8 targets NLRP3 and promoting its degradation, resulting in impaired NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

Autophagy represents a fundamental and highly conserved cellular degradation system within eukaryotic cells [ 33 ]. Within this framework, CMA stands as a selective form of autophagy, responsible for the degradation and recycling of damaged or unnecessary cellular components [ 34 ]. Dysregulation of CMA has been implicated in various diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders and cancer [ 35 , 36 ]. For instance, the deletion of LAMP2A has been shown to impede the degradation of NLRP3 through the CMA pathway, ultimately promoting NLRP3 inflammasome activation and contributing to atherosclerosis [ 21 ]. Additionally, our previous research has demonstrated the critical role of palmitoyltransferase ZDHHC12-mediated NLRP3 palmitoylation in NLRP3 degradation via the CMA pathway in the late sate of inflammasome activation, and there existed a strong correlation between defects in ZDHHC12-mediated NLRP3 palmitoylation level and human pathological conditions with excessive inflammation [ 22 ]. Hence, the interplay between palmitoylation and CMA highlights the intricate regulation of protein degradation pathways and their connections to post-translational modifications. Notably, in this study, we discover that ABHD8, lacking an acyltransferase activity motif, serves as a scaffold protein recruiting palmitoyltransferase ZDHHC12 to NLRP3. This action facilitates palmitoylation followed by CMA-mediated degradation of NLRP3. SARS-CoV-2 infection causes severe inflammatory responses and has become the most serious public health problem worldwide. Emerging evidence suggests that NLRP3 inflammasome are activated upon SARS-CoV-2 infection and are associated with COVID-19 severity [ 37 , 38 ]. Here, we also demonstrate that SARS-CoV-2 N protein disrupts the ABHD8-NLRP3 association to upregulate the NLRP3 protein level and elevate inflammasome activation. Collectively, our previous and recent studies have underscored the vital nature of NLRP3 palmitoylation in maintaining the balance of NLRP3 protein levels and overall cellular homeostasis. These findings provide valuable insights into the regulatory mechanisms governing NLRP3 protein level and offer potential targets for therapeutic intervention in diseases associated with NLRP3 dysregulation and inflammation.

In summary, we identified ABHD8 as a negative regulator of NLRP3 inflammasome by promoting the CMA-mediated degradation of NLRP3. ABHD8 recruits ZDHHC12 to facilitate the palmitoylation of NLRP3, which enhances its recognition by HSPA8. HSPA8 then delivers NLRP3 to interact with LAMP2A, leading to its subsequent degradation in the lysosome. By downregulating the protein abundance of NLRP3, ABHD8 effectively prevents excessive NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby avoiding uncontrolled inflammatory diseases ( Figure 8 ). These findings establish a link between ABHD8, ZDHHC12, and CMA, forming a complex regulatory network that helps regulate NLRP3 inflammasome activation and maintain homeostasis. Considering the significant role of ABHD8 in modulating NLRP3 inflammasome activation, targeting ABHD8 pharmacologically could be a promising therapeutic approach for inflammation-related diseases.

Materials and methods

Mice

All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (Authorization number: SYXK (YUE) 2023-0313, Guangzhou, China). C57BL/6 WT (GDMLAC-07) mice were obtained from the Guangzhou Medical Laboratory Animal Center. Mice aged 6 to 8 weeks were selected for the experiments, as this age range is commonly used in many research studies involving mice. The mice were housed in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility at Sun Yat-sen University. The facility maintained standard conditions, including a temperature range of 20-26°C, relative humidity between 40-70%, and a strict 12-h light-dark cycle (lights on at 08:00 a.m. and off at 08:00 p.m.). During the euthanasia process, the mice were euthanized using CO 2 from compressed gas cylinders.

Cell culture and transfection

The HEK293T cell line were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection/ATCC (CRL-11268) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modification of Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Corning, 10-013-CVR) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 1099–141) and 1% L-glutamine (Gibco, 25030-081). BECN1 -KO, ATG5 -KO, HSPA8/HSC70 -KO and ZDHHC12 -KO cell lines have been described previously [ 22 , 39 ]. The human monocytic THP-1-cell line obtained from American Type Culture Collection (TIB-202) was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, C22400500BT) with 10% FBS and 1% L-glutamine. To differentiate THP-1 cells into macrophages, the cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium containing phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA; Sigma-Aldrich, P1585) for 12 h. After a 24-h resting period, the macrophages were treated with the indicated stimuli. Bone marrow cells were isolated from randomly chosen 6-week-old mice, and then differentiated into bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) by culturing them in 10 ml of conditioned medium (DMEM supplemented with 100 ng/ml macrophage colony-stimulating factor (MCSF; PeproTech, AF-315-02), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), 10% FBS, and 1% L-glutamine), as described previously [ 22 ]. All indicated cells were grown at 37°C in a 5% CO 2 incubator.

To achieve ABHD8 -KO cell lines, HEK293T or THP-1 cells were seeded and subsequently exposed to lentiviruses expressing Cas9 and specific sgRNAs, along with polybrene (Hexadimethrine Bromide, 4 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, H9268) for 24 h. Following this transduction period, cells were subjected to puromycin selection (1 μg/ml) for 3 days to generate polyclonal cell pools for subsequent experiments. The knockout efficiency was evaluated by immunoblot analysis.

For quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis, the 2× PolarSignal SYBR Green mix Taq (with Tli RNaseH) from MIKX (MKG900) was utilized. To ensure accurate comparisons, all data were normalized to the expression level of human GAPDH . The following primers were used:

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The production of cytokines was detected with human IL1B/IL-1β kit (BD Biosciences, 557953), human TNF/TNF-α kit (BD Biosciences, 555212), human IL6 kit (BD Biosciences, 555220), mouse IL1B/IL-1β kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88-7013-76), mouse TNF/TNF-α kit (BD Biosciences, 558534), and mouse IL6 kit (BD Biosciences, 555240), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Immunoblot analysis

HEK293T cells, THP-1-derived macrophages, and BMDMs underwent transfection or stimulation as indicated. Subsequently, the cells were collected using a low-salt lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1.5 mM MgCl 2 , 10% glycerol, and 1% Triton X-100 [Sigma-Aldrich, T9284]) supplemented with phosphatase inhibitor (Roche Applied Science, 04906837001) and EDTA-free protease inhibitor (Biosharp, BL630B). The cell lysates were then placed on a rocker with ice for 30 min and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 g at 4°C for 5 min to obtain the whole-cell lysates. These lysates were boiled with 5× SDS loading buffer at 100°C for 5 min and separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). For immunoprecipitation: the indicated whole-cell lysates were incubated with anti-Flag M2 affinity gel (Sigma-Aldrich, A2220) or appropriate antibodies along with Protein A/G beads (Pierce, 20333/20399) on a 3D shaker at 4°C overnight. The beads were washed three to five times with low-salt lysis buffer, and the immunoprecipitates were eluted with 2× SDS loading buffer for subsequent SDS-PAGE. For supernatant precipitation: After priming with LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, L4524) for 3 h, the related cell culture medium was changed to Opti-MEM (Gibco, 51985091) without FBS before the cells were treated with or without indicated inflammasome activators. The supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 800 g at 4°C for 5 min. Supernatants for each sample were precipitated with methanol: chloroform (supernatants: methanol: chloroform with a volume ratio of 4:4:1), vortexed, and centrifuged at 16,000 g at 4°C for 15 min. The upper supernatants were discarded, and the same volume of methanol was added, vortexed and then centrifuged as before. The supernatants were discarded and the protein pellets were dried at 65°C for 5 min, followed by dissolved in 2× loading buffer, incubated at 100°C for 10 min and resolved by SDS-PAGE. Following this, proteins were transferred to an Immobilon®-PSQ Transfer Membrane (Millipore, ISEQ00010) and incubated with the appropriate antibodies. Protein detection was carried out using Immobilon®-Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore, WBKLS0500). The resulting images were analyzed using the ChemiDoc MP System (Bio-Rad Laboratories) and Image Lab version 6.0 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., USA).

Confocal analysis

HEK293T cells at a concentration of 2 × 10 4 /ml were cultured on glass bottom culture dishes (Nest Scientific, 801002) and subsequently transfected or treated as indicated. Following this, the culture medium was gently removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS (Corning, 21-040-CV) before being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After three subsequent washes, pre-cooled methyl alcohol at −20°C was added to the dishes for 20 min, followed by three additional washes with PBS. The cells were then blocked in 6% goat serum (Boster Biological, AR1009) at room temperature for 1 h and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies, which were diluted in 6% goat serum, at 4°C overnight. After another round of washing with PBS three times, the cells were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488- or Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated antibodies against mouse or rabbit) for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, D9542) for 5 min. Confocal images were captured using a microscope (TCS-SP8, Leica) equipped with a 100 × 1.40 NA oil objective and processed for gamma adjustments using Leica AS Lite. The colocalization was quantified (quantification of n = 12 cells) by Pearson’s correlation coefficient using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Förster/fluorescence resonance energy transfer analysis

The HEK293T cells were plated in glass-bottom culture dishes and subsequently transfected or treated as indicated. Following this, the cells underwent two washes with PBS and were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min before being mounted in PBS. A Förster/fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assay was conducted using the FRET SE (sensitized emission) Leica software on a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscopy system. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad software, California, USA).

Acyl-biotin exchange (ABE) assay

The indicated cells were harvested in low-salt lysis buffer and then placed on a rocker with ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 g at 4°C for 15 min. Subsequently, the cell lysates were treated with TCEP (500 mM; Sangon Biotech, 51805-45-9) at 55°C for 1 h, and then incubated with NEM (50 mM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23030) at 4°C overnight. Removal of NEM from the cell lysates was achieved through repeated methanol-chloroform precipitation. The protein pellets were dried at room temperature and dissolved in SDS buffer containing 4% SDS, 5 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, and protease inhibitor at 37°C. Each sample was divided into two parts and mixed with the thiol-reactive biotin molecule HPDP-biotin (0.4 mM; Thermo Fisher Scientific; 21341), respectively. The portion excluding the hydroxylamine hydrochloride (HAM; Sigma-Aldrich, 379921) cleavage step (−HAM) was supplemented with lysis buffer, while the portion including the HAM step (+HAM) was supplemented with 1 M HAM. After 5 h of incubation at 4°C, the samples were pelleted by methanol-chloroform precipitation, and then resuspended in 4% SDS buffer. Following dissolution, 1/5 of the samples was eluted with 5× SDS loading buffer and boiled at 100°C for 5 min for the “input”, while the remaining samples were incubated with neutravidin agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 29200) at 4°C overnight. The beads were washed four times with lysis buffer and eluted with 2× SDS loading buffer, and subsequently resolved by SDS-PAGE.

In vivo studies

For i n vivo transfection: empty vector (EV) or Flag-mouse ABHD8 (Flag-mABHD8, 40 μg/mouse; constructed in this study) was injected into the tail veins of mice in the presence of in vivo DNA transfection reagent (Entranster-in vivo; Engreen, 18668-11-2), according to the manufacturer’s protocols and previous studies [ 40 , 41 ]. For LPS-induced endotoxemia model: the indicated mice were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 25 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, L2630) for 12 h. Blood samples were obtained from the angular vein and Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 500 g at 4°C for 15 min and stored at −80°C until analysis by ELISA. Then all mice were euthanized by CO 2 exposure and the lung tissues were harvested for hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining and immunoblot analysis. For in vivo alum-induced peritonitis model, the indicated mice were i.p. injection of 700 μg alum (InvivoGen, tlrl-aloh) for 12 h. The mice were euthanized by CO 2 exposure, and peritoneal cavities were washed with 4 ml of cold PBS and centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min. The peritoneal fluids were harvested and concentrated for ELISA analysis and the cell pellets were collected for by immunoblot analysis.

Statistical methods

All quantitative data were subjected to analysis using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA). The data are presented as the mean value ± SEM (standard error of the mean) or ± SD (standard deviation) as indicated in the figure legends. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used to determine the statistical significance between groups.
