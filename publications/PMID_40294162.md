# Proximity labeling identification of plasma membrane eisosome proteins in Candida albicans.

The ability of Candida albicans to resist stressful conditions in the host and
grow invasively into tissues contributes to the virulence of this human fungal
pathogen. Plasma membrane subdomains known as the MCC (membrane compartment of
Can1) or eisosomes are important for these processes. MCC/eisosome domains are
furrow-shaped invaginations of the plasma membrane that are about 250 nm long
and 50 nm deep. To identify proteins that localize to these domains, a proximity
labeling method was used in which the TurboID variant of the BirA biotin ligase
was fused to Sur7 and Lsp1, 2 proteins that localize to eisosomes and are
important for virulence. This resulted in biotinylation of nearby proteins,
permitting their identification. Analysis of 19 candidate proteins by tagging
with the green fluorescent protein identified 7 proteins that detectably
overlapped with MCC/eisosomes. Deletion mutant analysis showed that one of
these, a poorly studied protein known as Ker1, was important for hyphal growth
in liquid culture, invasive growth into agar medium, and resistance to stress
caused by copper and cell wall perturbing agents. Altogether, these approaches
identified novel MCC/eisosome proteins and show that TurboID can be applied to
better define the molecular mechanisms of C. albicans pathogenesis and aid in
discovery of targets for novel therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methodsGrowth media and strain construction
C. albicans cultures were grown and maintained in either rich YPD medium containing yeast extract, peptone, and dextrose or in synthetic medium containing yeast nitrogen base, dextrose, uridine, and amino acids for auxotrophic strains (Sherman 1991). A comprehensive list of the strains used in this study can be found in Supplementary File 1.To create strains with gene fusions to TurboID, GFPγ, or mScarlet, a cassette was generated by PCR, electroporation was used to introduce it into the appropriate C. albicans strain, and then the corresponding selectable marker was used to select for homologous recombination events that created the gene fusion (De Backer et al. 1999; Zhang and Konopka 2010; Frazer et al. 2019). The forward primer contained 80 bases of homology to the 3′ end of the open reading frame (ORF), excluding the stop codon, and 20 bases of homology to the appropriate plasmid containing the gene fusion cassette. The reverse primer contained 80 nucleotides of homology to the area around 50 bases after the targeted ORF and 20 nucleotides of homology to the plasmid containing the cassette, as previously described (Zhang and Konopka 2010; Duenas-Santero et al. 2019). The PCR products were purified with the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) before transformation. Primer sequences are included in Supplementary File 2.Gene deletion mutants were constructed using the transient CRISPR-Cas9 system, as described (Min et al. 2016), with the SAT1 flipper as template DNA for the healing fragment (Reuss et al. 2004). The healing fragment primers possess 20 nucleotides of homology to SAT1 flipper cassette (also known as NAT for nourseothricin resistance) and 80 nucleotides of homology directly upstream or downstream of the targeted ORF. To select for transformants, the electroporated cells were plated on YPD plus 200 μg/ml NAT (Gold Biotechnology). To remove the SAT1 flipper from the correct mutants, colonies were streaked onto maltose agar medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 2% maltose) and grown for 2–3 days. Selected colonies were plated onto YPD plus 200 μg/ml NAT plates to test whether the cells were still resistant or if they had gained sensitivity indicating the SAT1 flipper was lost by homologous recombination. The sensitive colonies were confirmed by PCR.Genomic DNA isolated by phenol–chloroform extraction was used as a template to assess genotypes by PCR. To verify that gene fusions were properly integrated, a forward primer located in the ORF and a reverse primer in the selectable marker gene were used in a PCR and the resulting products were assessed to confirm the expected product size. Strains carrying the TurboID tags were also confirmed by Western blot analysis, as detailed below. To verify the gene deletion mutants, multiple PCR primer combinations were used. This included oligonucleotide primers that flanked the outside of the deletion cassette, the 5′ and 3′ junctions, and the correct position of the SAT1 flipper cassette. Primers inside the targeted ORF were also used to confirm its absence. Plasmids used to complement deletion mutant strains were constructed as described previously (Gerami-Nejad et al. 2013) using primers containing 70 nucleotides of homology to the pDIS3 plasmid and 30 base pairs of homology to the complementing gene (Gerami-Nejad et al. 2013). The resulting purified PCR product was combined with Sma1-digested pDIS3 and introduced by electroporation into the E. coli strain DH5α for gap-repair cloning as described (Jacobus and Gross 2015). C. albicans cultures were grown and maintained in either rich YPD medium containing yeast extract, peptone, and dextrose or in synthetic medium containing yeast nitrogen base, dextrose, uridine, and amino acids for auxotrophic strains (Sherman 1991). A comprehensive list of the strains used in this study can be found in Supplementary File 1.

To create strains with gene fusions to TurboID, GFPγ, or mScarlet, a cassette was generated by PCR, electroporation was used to introduce it into the appropriate C. albicans strain, and then the corresponding selectable marker was used to select for homologous recombination events that created the gene fusion (De Backer et al. 1999; Zhang and Konopka 2010; Frazer et al. 2019). The forward primer contained 80 bases of homology to the 3′ end of the open reading frame (ORF), excluding the stop codon, and 20 bases of homology to the appropriate plasmid containing the gene fusion cassette. The reverse primer contained 80 nucleotides of homology to the area around 50 bases after the targeted ORF and 20 nucleotides of homology to the plasmid containing the cassette, as previously described (Zhang and Konopka 2010; Duenas-Santero et al. 2019). The PCR products were purified with the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) before transformation. Primer sequences are included in Supplementary File 2.

Gene deletion mutants were constructed using the transient CRISPR-Cas9 system, as described (Min et al. 2016), with the SAT1 flipper as template DNA for the healing fragment (Reuss et al. 2004). The healing fragment primers possess 20 nucleotides of homology to SAT1 flipper cassette (also known as NAT for nourseothricin resistance) and 80 nucleotides of homology directly upstream or downstream of the targeted ORF. To select for transformants, the electroporated cells were plated on YPD plus 200 μg/ml NAT (Gold Biotechnology). To remove the SAT1 flipper from the correct mutants, colonies were streaked onto maltose agar medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 2% maltose) and grown for 2–3 days. Selected colonies were plated onto YPD plus 200 μg/ml NAT plates to test whether the cells were still resistant or if they had gained sensitivity indicating the SAT1 flipper was lost by homologous recombination. The sensitive colonies were confirmed by PCR.

Genomic DNA isolated by phenol–chloroform extraction was used as a template to assess genotypes by PCR. To verify that gene fusions were properly integrated, a forward primer located in the ORF and a reverse primer in the selectable marker gene were used in a PCR and the resulting products were assessed to confirm the expected product size. Strains carrying the TurboID tags were also confirmed by Western blot analysis, as detailed below. To verify the gene deletion mutants, multiple PCR primer combinations were used. This included oligonucleotide primers that flanked the outside of the deletion cassette, the 5′ and 3′ junctions, and the correct position of the SAT1 flipper cassette. Primers inside the targeted ORF were also used to confirm its absence. Plasmids used to complement deletion mutant strains were constructed as described previously (Gerami-Nejad et al. 2013) using primers containing 70 nucleotides of homology to the pDIS3 plasmid and 30 base pairs of homology to the complementing gene (Gerami-Nejad et al. 2013). The resulting purified PCR product was combined with Sma1-digested pDIS3 and introduced by electroporation into the E. coli strain DH5α for gap-repair cloning as described (Jacobus and Gross 2015). The resulting plasmids were digested with Sfi I (New England Biolabs) and then introduced by electroporation into C. albicans. for integration into the Neut5L locus.

The efficiency of biotin release was further improved by directed evolution procedures to create a variant form of BirA that was named TurboID (Branon et al. 2018). We adapted TurboID for C. albicans by synthesizing the corresponding gene using codons optimized for C. albicans and inserting it into a cassette that also included a NAT resistance gene to select for gene fusions (Fig. 1b). This enables the cassette to be PCR amplified and targeted for integration at the 3′ end of an ORF to create TurboID fusions in C. albicans. Eis1, Seg1, and Ker1 were chosen because they were readily detected by TurboID, their GFP fusions colocalized with eisosomes, and they have not been characterized well in C. albicans. Orf19.1964 was selected for further study because it is a novel eisosome protein identified by TurboID, with no orthologs in S. cerevisiae. Although Srd1-GFP was not detected in eisosomes, it was chosen because it is in the Sur7/PalI family of proteins (Zhu et al. 2020) and Sur7 is functionally very important for cell wall organization and stress resistance in C. albicans (Wang et al. 2016; Lanze and Konopka 2024).
C. albicans mutants lacking both copies of the corresponding genes were created using CRISPR/Cas9 approaches and then assayed to determine if they showed defects in resistance to plasma membrane and cell wall stress similar to Sur7 (Wang et al. 2016; Lanze et al. 2021; Lanze and Konopka 2024). Susceptibility to stress was assayed by spotting dilutions of cells on agar medium and then incubating the plate at high temperature (42°) or by spotting the cells onto agar medium that contained agents that exacerbate cell wall and plasma membrane stress including Calcofluor White, Congo Red, and SDS (Fig. 5). While none of the mutants displayed the same level of growth inhibition to these agents as sur7Δ/Δ, the eis1Δ/Δ, seg1Δ/Δ, and ker1Δ/Δ mutants showed a slight increase in sensitivity to Calcofluor White, and ker1Δ/Δ was also slightly more sensitive to Congo Red and SDS (Fig. 5a). Adapting TurboID for C. albicans

Proximity labeling methods take advantage of mutant forms of the E. coli biotin protein ligase (BirA) that more readily release the highly reactive intermediate biotinyl-5′-AMP, resulting in addition of biotin to Lys residues on nearby proteins (Choi-Rhee et al. 2004; Roux et al. 2012). We adapted TurboID for C. albicans by synthesizing the corresponding gene using codons optimized for C. albicans and inserting it into a cassette that also included a NAT resistance gene to select for gene fusions (Fig. 1b). This enables the cassette to be PCR amplified and targeted for integration at the 3′ end of an ORF to create TurboID fusions in C. albicans. The use of the NAT resistance gene as a selectable marker permits this approach to also be used with clinical isolates of C. albicans.

C. albicans mutants lacking both copies of the corresponding genes were created using CRISPR/Cas9 approaches and then assayed to determine if they showed defects in resistance to plasma membrane and cell wall stress similar to Sur7 (Wang et al. 2016; Lanze et al. 2021; Lanze and Konopka 2024). Susceptibility to stress was assayed by spotting dilutions of cells on agar medium and then incubating the plate at high temperature (42°) or by spotting the cells onto agar medium that contained agents that exacerbate cell wall and plasma membrane stress including Calcofluor White, Congo Red, and SDS (Fig. 5). While none of the mutants displayed the same level of growth inhibition to these agents as sur7Δ/Δ, the eis1Δ/Δ, seg1Δ/Δ, and ker1Δ/Δ mutants showed a slight increase in sensitivity to Calcofluor White, and ker1Δ/Δ was also slightly more sensitive to Congo Red and SDS (Fig. 5a). The results agreed with an older study that found that a ker1Δ mutant generated with the “ura-blaster” technique showed increased susceptibility to Calcofluor White and Congo Red (Galan et al. 2004).

The mutants were also tested to see if they showed increased sensitivity to copper, as seen for the sur7Δ/Δ mutant (Douglas and Konopka 2019). This elevated sensitivity to copper is thought to underlie the increased killing of sur7Δ/Δ by macrophages, which import copper into the phagosome (Douglas et al. 2012). The copper sensitivity of the sur7Δ/Δ mutant is thought to be due to increased exposure of phosphatidylserine on the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane (Douglas and Konopka 2019), a lipid normally enriched on the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane (Pomorski and Menon 2016). Copper binds phosphatidylserine with high affinity and can damage the plasma membrane (Monson et al. 2012). The use of disk diffusion assays showed that the ker1Δ/Δ mutant displayed elevated sensitivity to copper similar to the sur7Δ/Δ mutant (Fig. 5b). To search for synergistic effects, we also made double mutant strains with sur7Δ/Δ. Note that we could not isolate a sur7Δ/Δ eis1Δ/Δ double mutant, suggesting there could be a synthetic lethal interaction between these mutations. The sur7Δ/Δ eis1Δ/EIS1 and sur7Δ/Δ seg1Δ/Δ mutants displayed an increased susceptibility to copper when compared to sur7Δ/Δ, whereas the sur7Δ/Δ ker1Δ/Δ did not show a significant increase in susceptibility (Fig. 5b). This suggests that Ker1 may function in the same pathway as Sur7 to promote copper resistance. Sur7-GFP still localized to punctate eisosome patches in the plasma membrane of a ker1Δ mutant strain (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating that the increased copper sensitivity of a ker1Δ strain is not due to the failure of Sur7-GFP to localize properly. These results raise the possibility that there may be a functional relationship between Sur7 and Ker1. Control studies showed that ker1Δ/Δ mutant phenotypes were rescued by reintroducing a copy of wild-type KER1. Altogether, these data identify the novel eisosome proteins Eis1, Seg1, and Ker1 as contributors to stress resistance in C. albicans. Eis1 and Seg1 had minor roles, but Ker1 played a more significant role, especially in promoting resistance to copper.

DiscussionTurboID proximity labeling was successful in identifying new eisosome proteins that promote stress resistance and invasive hyphal growth that are important for C. albicans virulence (Dantas Ada et al. 2015; Arkowitz and Bassilana 2019; Lanze et al. 2020). TurboID is well suited for the analysis of eisosomes that are comprised of a large network of integral and peripheral membrane proteins. The need to use strong detergents to solubilize membranes limits the use of many standard co-precipitation methods for detecting membrane protein complexes. In contrast, the biotinylation of proteins proximal to TurboID allows for the use of strong detergents while they are being harvested with streptavidin beads. A potential limitation is that the proximal proteins that are identified are not necessarily directly interacting with the TurboID fusion protein. However, the observation that Lsp1-Turbo and Sur7-Turbo identified different proteins suggests that it may be possible in future studies to map out spatial positions in the eisosome to some degree. Deletion mutant analysis for 5 proteins identified by TurboID revealed that the ker1Δ/Δ mutant had to strongest defects. The ker1Δ/Δ mutant was more susceptible to stressful conditions and had the strongest defect in hyphal morphogenesis and invasive growth (Figs. 5–7). A previous study identified KER1 by probing a bacteriophage expression library of C. albicans genes with a polyclonal antibody raised against C. albicans germ tubes (Galan et al. 2004). Consistent with this, Ker1 was not needed for eisosome formation since Sur7-GFP and Lsp1-mScarlet still formed typical punctate eisosome patches in a ker1Δ/Δ mutant strain (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Proteins that were detected by mass spectrometry with a strong signal, such as Ker1, are good candidates for future studies. However, we found that it is important to keep in mind that proteins detected with a weak signal may also be significant. Ker1 is distinctive in that it is a large protein (134 kD), which increases the probability that peptides can be detected by mass spectrometry. In addition, it is highly enriched in Lys residues that are a target for biotinylation as it contains 173 Lys residues. Thus, we also analyzed other candidates, such as Rta3 that gave a weaker signal in mass spectrometry but has been shown to play a role in promoting plasma membrane lipid asymmetry (Whaley et al. 2016; Srivastava et al. 2017). This was interesting because previous studies indicated that Sur7 is important for promoting plasma membrane asymmetry (Douglas and Konopka 2019).

Ribosomal proteins were also recovered on the streptavidin beads (Supplementary Files 4 and 5). This is likely due to nonspecific interactions since ribosomal proteins are very abundant. Alternatively, it could be indicative of specific interactions since the TurboID proteins must all be translated on ribosomes and eisosomes have been reported to contact cortical ER in S. pombe (Ng et al. 2020). To improve the identification of eisosome proteins in our assays, we analyzed a nontagged control strain so that nonspecific proteins could be eliminated. An additional control described in recent reports involves the use of a strain that produces TurboID fused to a cytoplasmic protein or free TurboID to help identify the background of nonspecifically biotinylated proteins (Hollstein et al. 2022; Pfannenstein et al. 2024). Future studies may be able to enrich for specific proximal proteins by assessing multiple types of control strains.

Altogether, these studies demonstrate that proximity labeling was successful in identifying new eisosome proteins in C. albicans. This indicates that TurboID can be applied to study other important membrane complexes and can also be applied to other fungal pathogens. These approaches can help define the molecular mechanisms that regulate fungal pathogenesis and aid in discovery of new targets for antifungal drugs.
