Signaling downstream of Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase Kinase Kinases (MAP3K)
is promiscuous. In the vascular and immune systems the MAP3K, MEKK2, activates
different substrates, but the mechanisms of substrate targeting have not been
delineated. Here, we determine the 2.4 Å crystal structure of the MEKK2 kinase
domain in complex with the small molecule inhibitor, ponatinib. We find that
MEKK2 dimerizes by a surface centered on the αG helix and the C-terminal region
of the activation segment, that this surface is important for MEKK2
autophosphorylation and dimerization, and that this surface is conserved with
MEKK3. We then assess the importance of the surface for phosphorylation and
recruitment of two MAP2K substrates, MEK5 and MKK6. We find that both MEK5 and
MKK6 require the αG helix-mediated interaction for phosphorylation. In contrast,
MEKK2 recruitment of MEK5 is dependent on PB1 domain interactions but MKK6
recruitment is associated with the αG helix-mediated interaction. Our study
therefore provides a framework to understand diverse substrate targeting by the
MAP3Ks, MEKK2 and MEKK3.

MAP kinase signaling from the MAP3K layer to the MAP2K layer is context dependent, but like MEKK2 and MEKK3, most MAP3Ks activate some combination of ERK, p38 and JNK signaling pathways36. To achieve specificity MAP3Ks can use scaffold proteins; the most well studied examples are KSR in RAF signaling to MEK37, and JIP1 in signaling to the JNK pathway38. Specificity is also achieved by unique domain architectures that facilitate protein-protein interactions, binding partners, and cellular localization39–41, and the catalytic kinase domains can contribute to specificity42. Nonetheless, how targeting specificities contribute to specific MAP3K activation of MAP2K responses remains under-studied for many MAP3K-MAP2K pairs36,42. Here we investigate the role for the MEKK2/3 kinase domains in determining substrate specificities.

MEKK2 and MEKK3 are both multi-domain proteins that contain an N-terminal Phox Bem1p (PB1) domain and a C-terminal kinase domain separated by ~200 amino acids43–46 (Fig. 1b). Similarly, MEK5 contains an N-terminal PB1 domain ~50 amino acids distal to its C-terminal kinase domain43–45,47,48 (Fig. 1b). Substrate targeting of MEKK2/3 is largely thought to be dependent on the PB1 domains, with the kinase domains the center of catalytic activity45. Indeed, MEKK2/3 signaling through MEK5 is known to be driven by recruitment through a PB1-PB1 interaction, and phosphorylation of MEK5 by MEKK2/3 via the kinase domain43,46,49,50. In contrast, the MEKK2/3 signaling through the MKK6-p38 cascade cannot be dependent on PB1-PB1 mediated heterodimerization because MKK6 does not contain a PB1 domain; this MAP2K contains a kinase domain with only short N- and C-terminal tails51,52. There is therefore diversity in kinase-substrate recruitment and recognition for the MEKK2/3 MAP3Ks. This diversity is further supported by studies on the autoactivation of MEKK2/3 which indicate that auto-phosphorylation is driven by homotypic kinase domain-kinase domain interactions, and not by homotypic PB1-PB1 interactions53. Together, these results suggest that a better understanding of the diverse mechanisms of MAP3K signaling to MAP2Ks might be achieved by assessing kinase-substrate interactions of the MEKK2 and MEKK3 group.

There are no crystal structures of either the kinase domain of MEKK2 or of MEKK3. These STE group serine-threonine kinases are in total 67% identical to one another, but in the catalytic domain they are over 86% identical. As expected for such similar protein kinases, the recognition of short linear motifs important for determining substrate specificity are essentially indistinguishable between MEKK2 and MEKK3, with both showing a slight preference for threonine over serine at the phosphosite embedded in a typical MAP3K motif54, however, substrate recognition and recruitment for MEKK2 and MEKK3 has not been well studied outside of high-throughput analyses of short linear motifs. In contrast, at the structural level, the N-terminal PB1 domains are well studied. There are crystal and NMR structures that define these PB1 domains, their flexibility, their interactions with MEK555–59, and also their interactions with the adaptor protein CCM222,60. Overall, the majority of prior molecular level studies have centered on the PB1 domains, but the catalytic domains are the centers of functional relevance, are clinically important, and are inhibited by an FDA approved small molecule, ponatinib.

Ponatinib (AP24534) is a broad specificity ATP-competitive type II kinase inhibitor61–63. It was developed to target Abl tyrosine kinase with mutation in the gatekeeper residue, T315I, and is approved for treatment of Philadelphia chromosome-positive leukemias64. Although its target in development was focused, ponatinib inhibits a range of tyrosine kinases and over 100 kinases in total65. Among these are MEKK2 and MEKK3, and ponatinib shows functional utility targeting both14,20,66–69; MEKK2 in the context of cancer20,66 and MEKK3 in the context of the vascular disorder, cerebral cavernous malformations20. Nonetheless, despite its nanomolar affinity and potential as either an inhibitor or a foundation for inhibitor development, no crystal structures are available that demonstrate the binding of ponatinib to either MEKK2 or MEKK3.

In this study, we determine a crystal structure of the catalytic domain of MEKK2. This structure provides a molecular basis for ponatinib binding to both MEKK2 and MEKK3. Unexpectedly, the structure also provides insight into the mechanism of substrate recognition by these MAP3Ks. We find that the MEKK2 kinase domain homo-dimerizes by a surface centered on the αG helix and the C-terminal region of the activation segment, and that mutation of this interface disrupts both MEKK2 dimerization and autophosphorylation. In contrast, although phosphorylation of MEK5 is dependent on intact αG helix surfaces of both kinases, recruitment is driven by interactions of the PB1 domains. This mode of substrate recognition and phosphorylation is not, however, conserved for MEKK2 signaling to MKK6, where intact αG helixes are required for both MKK6 phosphorylation and recruitment. We therefore provide a framework for understanding the molecular basis of MEKK2 and MEKK3 inhibition, autophosphorylation, and substrate targeting.

ResultsCrystal structure of the MAP3K, MEKK2, in complex with ponatinibWe began by determining the 2.4 Å co-crystal structure of the catalytic domain of MEKK2 in complex with ponatinib (Supplementary Table 1, Fig. 1c). As expected, MEKK2 exhibits the canonical Ser/Thr protein kinase fold with a β-sheet rich N-lobe and an α-helical rich C-lobe, together defining a catalytic cleft where ponatinib is unambiguously bound (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Ponatinib coordinates MEKK2 as a type IIA inhibitor70 with the kinase in a DFG-out conformation with a dihedral assignment of BBAminus71. As with previous structures of kinase domains bound to ponatinib, the activation loop is in an inactive orientation but largely unstructured. Ponatinib coordination is achieved by hydrogen bonding between N1 nitrogen of the imidazo[1,2-b]pyridazine heterocycle with the N-H group of the backbone amide of Met440, the N-H group of the amide linker with the carboxylate of αC helix Glu406, the carbonyl oxygen of the linker with the N-H group of the DFG motif Asp501, polar contact of the thrifluoromethyl group with the imidazole of His481, and contacts between the methylpiperazine and carbonyl groups of Val480 and His481 (Fig, 1d, Supplementary Fig. 1b). This pose is similar to previous.structures of kinase domains in complex with ponatinib (Supplementary Fig. 1c)63,72–83. As MEKK2 is over 86% identical to MEKK3 in the catalytic domains, we mapped non-identical residues on its structure to assess where differences occur. We find that there are no sequence differences between MEKK2 and MEKK3 in any residue that coordinates ponatinib (Supplementary Fig. 2).
