AIMS: In humans, noncoding variants of PCSK2, the gene encoding prohormone
convertase 2 (PC2), have been previously associated with risk for and age of
onset of type 2 diabetes (T2D). The aims of this study were to identify coding
variants in PCSK2; to determine their possible association with glucose
handling; and to determine functional outcomes for coding variants in
biochemical studies.
METHODS: Exome-wide genotyping was performed on 1725 Old Order Amish (OOA)
subjects. PCSK2 coding variants were tested for association with
diabetes-related phenotypes. In vitro analyses using transfected human
PC2-encoding constructs were performed to determine the impact of each mutation
on PC2 activity.
RESULTS: We identified 10 rare missense coding variants in PCSK2 in various
genomic databases. R430W (rs200711626) is greatly enriched in the OOA population
(MAF 4.3%). This variant is almost twice as common (MAF 7.4%) in OOA individuals
with T2D as in OOA individuals with normal or with normal/impaired glucose
tolerance (MAF 3.9% and 2.9%, respectively; p=0.25 and p=0.10). In vitro
experiments revealed a broadening of the pH optimum for the R430W variant, which
may result in increased activity against PCSK2 substrates.
CONCLUSIONS: Although the association of the R430W variation with T2D in the OOA
population did not reach significance, based upon the broadened pH profile of
R430W PC2, we speculate that the presence of this substitution may result in
altered processing of PCSK2 substrates, ultimately leading to increased
conversion to diabetes.

1. Introduction

Prohormone processing of large precursors to bioactive species represents a carefully controlled series of catalytic events that culminates in the secretion of appropriate amounts of peptide hormones into the bloodstream. Any disturbance in the proteolytic processing of prohormones can result in endocrine pathology. For example, obesity, diabetes, and gastrointestinal dysfunction are associated with human and mouse prohormone convertase 1/3 (PC1/3) mutations and polymorphisms [1, 2]; reviewed in [3, 4]; and loss of the trimming carboxypeptidase E (CPE) results in obesity (= fat/fat mice) [5]. A rare human CPE variant with reduced activity, R283W, is associated with earlier age of onset of diabetes [6], while other CPE variants are associated with increased body mass index [7]. In mice, complete knockout of the prohormone convertase PC2 in the C57Bl/6j strain results in hypoglycemia due to the total absence of glucagon [8, 9]; PC2 knockout in the 129Sv background results in even larger hypoglycemic effects [10]. These mouse studies indicate that unlike other processing enzyme mutations, loss of PC2 activity may be associated with protection from diabetes and obesity rather than with susceptibility [8, 11, 12].

Increasing evidence from genome-wide association studies suggests that at least 9 common variants in the gene encoding human PC2, PCSK2, are associated with diabetes risk and related traits in human populations ([13–15]; summarized in [16]). The PCSK2 intronic variant rs2208203 was associated with lower fasting glucagon levels, as well as reduced insulin secretion, in a Scandinavian population [17]. A study of African-Americans with type 2 diabetes (T2D), showed association between diabetes and four intronic PCSK2 variants [18]. Work in a Chinese population confirmed association between rs2021785 and T2D risk, and a study in a Japanese population also found clear association between an intronic PCSK2 microsatellite and diabetes risk [13, 14]. In a longitudinal study, Chang et al. [16] found that individuals carrying certain intronic PCSK2 variants (rs6044695 and rs2284912) had a lower incidence of diabetes during a 5-year follow-up, suggesting a protective effect of these particular variants (or linked genes). The repeated association between PCSK2 variants and diabetes risk, either positive or negative, in a variety of human populations suggests that differences in PC2 activity, potentially affecting peptide hormone cleavage and thus glycemic control, may represent an important factor in the progression to T2D. However, the underlying biochemical mechanism for this increased risk- increased or decreased PC2 function- is as yet unclear, since no exonic variants of PCSK2 have been studied thus far.

Based on the mouse data showing that loss of PCSK2 is protective against the development of diabetes, as well as the human study showing a similar protective effect of the intronic variants rs6044695 and rs2284912, we hypothesized that coding variants associated with decreased enzyme activity might be beneficial, i.e. associated with protection from diabetes, while coding variants associated with increased activity might be deleterious, i.e. associated with increased conversion to T2D. However, human PCSK2 exonic variants are extremely rare, with the most frequent SNPs exhibiting minor allele frequencies (MAF) of less than 0.05% (1000Genomes and Exome Aggregation Consortium [ExAC] databases). Given this rarity, no associations have been found between any PCSK2 coding variants and diabetes or related traits thus far.

In the work presented below, we have identified a PCSK2 coding variant, R430W, which is present at a much higher frequency in the Old Order Amish (OOA) population than in the general population, and have evaluated its association with T2D and diabetes-related traits. Furthermore, in an effort to determine whether coding mutations cause a loss or gain of function, we have enzymatically characterized this variant as well as three other rare PCSK2 missense coding variants.

Study PopulationThe Lancaster OOA are a genetically well-defined founder population of European descent that immigrated to the US from Western Europe over a 50-year period starting in 1727. Today’s Lancaster Amish population of >30,000 individuals are descended from approximately 200 of these families. Because the OOA grew from a small number of founders with few outside individuals joining the population, certain variants that are rare in outbred populations have become common in the OOA, providing unique opportunities for genetic studies. All ten ExAC coding variants are very rare in all known outbred populations (MAFs between 0.0016% and 0.076%) (Table 1). The location of these ten variants, which span the five preproPC2 domains, is depicted in Fig. 1A.

The “genomic evolutionary rate profiling” (GERP) score, a measure of conservation using estimates of evolutionary constraint [28], indicates that the wild type alleles of all variants are conserved, given GERP scores between 3.3 and 5.93 (where values greater than 2 suggest evolutionary constraint), suggesting that all of these residues are important for PC2 function. The “sorting intolerant from tolerant” (SIFT) score is a homology-based tool that categorizes missense changes based on their likelihood to cause a phenotypic effect [29]; six PCSK2 variants are also rated as deleterious, with a score of 0.02 or less. PolyPhen2 is a third algorithm that predicts the outcome of a nonsynonymous substitution [30]; here, A267T and R430W are rated as “probably damaging”, with scores of 0.99 and 0.97, respectively. The concordance of three prediction algorithms indicate that there is a large likelihood that at least three of the ten substitutions, A267T, R430W, and M525V, will alter PC2 activity.

With regard to the OAA population, direct exome sequencing of 154 OOA individuals showed that 13 copies of the rs200711626 variant allele encoding the missense mutation R430W were found, which represents a MAF of 4.2%. This is greatly enriched compared to other populations, indicating a likely founder effect; only 16 copies are reported in 60,000 individuals in the Exome Aggregation Consortium (equivalent to an MAF of 0.013%; Table 1). None of the other ten coding variants were found in the OAA.

Of the ten rare coding variants confirmed in the ExAc database, we selected four for further analysis which are predicted to have deleterious effects and which span different domains (Table 1). The enzymatic activity against a fluorogenic substrate, and the protein expression of these four PCSK2 variants, A77D, A267T, R430W and M525V, is shown in Fig. 1B. In agreement, higher PC2 protein levels were found in the A267T cell lysates, indicative of intracellular accumulation, most likely as a result of misfolding (Fig. 1C).

3.2. Association of the common PCSK2 coding variant rs200711626 (R430W) and type 2 diabetes risk in the OOA population

As described above, using direct exome sequencing the PCSK2 coding variant R430W was found to exhibit a high frequency in an OOA population (MAF 4.2%). In agreement, using Human Exome chip data from 1909 OOA individuals that were participants in ongoing complex disease studies other than the AFDS, 187 copies of R430W were identified, yielding a MAF of 4.9%. Only two homozygotes were identified; one is known to have diabetes, while the diabetic status of the other individual is not known.

We then evaluated the allele frequency of R430W by glucose tolerance status in 1229 AFDS individuals. We found that R430W is almost twice as common (MAF 7.4%) among OOA individuals with T2D as OOA individuals with normal and/or with impaired glucose tolerance; however, these differences were not statistically significant (Table 2; p= 0.10, p=0.25, and p=0.25). 4. Discussion

Previous studies have found both positive and negative associations between intronic variants in PCSK2 and increased T2D risk [13, 14, 16–18]. Since coding variants in PCSK2 are very rare in outbred populations, no information exists on whether variants confer a loss or gain of function for this enzyme. While the repeated independent findings of association of PCSK2 variations with parameters involved in diabetes risk in a variety of different populations clearly implies functionality, whether these represent direct or indirect effects is not yet clear. Further work mapping possible chromatin interactions of intronic variants [32] represents an interesting avenue for further research which could lead to possible indirect gene targets. In this work we have attempted to approach this problem by examining coding variants for this enzyme, one of which, although rare in the general population, was found to be enriched in the Old Order Amish, suggesting a founder effect.

We enzymatically characterized four rare coding PCSK2 variants (A77D, A267T, R430W and M525V). These variants were chosen for their presence in multiple exome databases, including 1000Genomes phase 3, ESP, and ExAC; their distribution within proPC2; and their predicted lack of functionality. Coding variations can theoretically affect PC2 function in numerous ways, for example by affecting specific activity; substrate specificity; enzyme stability; and/or trafficking through the secretory pathway to the secretory granule (the locus of precursor cleavage). Coding mutations in the related convertase PCSK1 are known to affect many of these parameters, and nearly always result in impaired enzyme function (reviewed in [3]). Interestingly, however, a known human PCSK1 mutation, S357G, found in a lung cancer patient, results in a significantly more active PC1/3 enzyme [22]; thus, convertase coding mutations are not necessarily loss-of-function.

Given their predicted deleterious functional consequences, it is surprising that three of the four PCSK2 coding variants studied exhibited no gross alterations in secretion or catalytic activity under standard reaction conditions. Only the A267T substitution resulted in dramatic effects, with a profound reduction in medium PC2 protein and corresponding activity. Since cellular protein levels of this enzyme were increased relative to wild-type enzyme, this variant is likely subject to misfolding followed by retention within the endoplasmic reticulum, a known problem for secretory protein mutants such as PC1/3 [20, 33] or proinsulin (reviewed in [34]). Of note, this variant appears only twice in ExAC (Table 1) and twice in 1000Genomes phase 3, both in the heterozygous state.

With regard to the other PC2 variants, slight differences in activity, either positive or negative, can be difficult to detect in transfection experiments. For example, while a common PCSK1 variant, N221D (rs6232) results in a modest reduction in PC1/3 activity in similar transfection studies, the paired Q665E/S690T variant (MAFs of 15–30%) rs6234–rs6235, which is strongly associated with obesity risk in a variety of populations [3, 27, 28] exhibits enzyme activity identical to that of wild-type PC1/3 [20, 35]. In our experiments, variant PC2 enzymes which exhibit identical activity to wild-type enzyme could exhibit either increased or decreased activity in vivo. More active enzyme would likely lead to more efficient cleavage of prohormones. Since the phenotype of the PC2 null mouse is clearly dominated by the complete lack of glucagon [8, 36], changes in glucagon production might be expected to show the largest effect. Interestingly, the Pcsk2 null mouse is protected from dietary-induced weight gain, while genetically obese mice are known to express higher levels of Pcsk2, suggesting that that susceptibility to either obesity and/or diabetes could be associated with increased rather than with decreased PC2 activity [11, 37]. This idea is supported by our results on the R430W variant enzyme, whose broadened pH optimum supports the idea of increased activity in vivo. However, we did not detect R430W PC2-mediated alterations in glucagon production in proglucagon-transfected GH4C1 cells; we speculate that this cell system may already operate at maximal glucagon production. Studies using Pcsk2 gene-edited mice will likely be required to definitively establish the effects of rare coding variants on glucagon production and susceptibility to diabetes and obesity.

In our studies of the OOA population, we found that the PCSK2 variant R430W is twice as common among OOA individuals with T2D as in those with normal or impaired glucose tolerance. A power analysis suggests that the lack of significant association between carriers of R430W PCSK2 with glucose and insulin tolerance during the OGTT may be ascribed to the modest sample sizes (to have 80% power to detect a significant difference in R430W carrier frequency of 3.9% vs. 7.4% between NGT and diabetes groups with an alpha of 0.05, data from 230 diabetic individuals and 955 NGT individuals would be required). It is also possible that the effect of the R430W substitution is manifested only in those already exhibiting impaired glucose tolerance. Changes in PC2 activity, even if small, might be particularly important in individuals with impaired glucose tolerance. We speculate that individuals who are beginning to exhibit dysregulated glucose metabolism may be more likely to convert to T2D if they carry the R430W variation. Additional studies measuring fasting and postprandial glucagon levels in sufficient numbers of R430W carriers vs non-carriers are needed to better define the effect of this variant on peptide hormone synthesis, and could help to clarify how enzyme activity changes might underlie the observed trend of linkage of the R430W allele with diabetes risk. Of note, rs200711626/PCSK2 R430W is not associated with any eQTLs in any of the >70 tissues profiled and indexed at the GTEx portal (gtexportal.org).

In conclusion, understanding how convertase missense variants affect the activity of PC2 against proglucagon and other prohormone substrates will improve our functional knowledge of the role of this enzyme in controlling blood sugar. While missense variants of PCSK2 are rare in the general population, intronic variants are common and are clearly linked to metabolism. Since PC2 action is critical to glucose handling, achieving a full understanding how different PCSK2 variations generate gain- or loss-of-function enzymes could have important implications for differential treatment strategies in metabolic disease.
