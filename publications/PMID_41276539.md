Tumor-associated inflammation and macrophage polarization drive gastric cancer
(GC) progression. We aimed to identify inflammation-related biomarkers that
predict prognosis and modulate the tumor immune microenvironment, focusing on
LDL receptor-related protein 12 (LRP12). We integrated public datasets with an
in-house cohort to build an immune-related prognostic model based on
inflammatory response-related genes (IRRGs) and to nominate candidate genes.
Single-cell transcriptomics were used to map cell-type enrichment. Key findings
were validated by multiplex immunofluorescence/immunohistochemistry, LRP12
overexpression and knockdown in GC cell lines, in vitro proliferation and
invasion assays, western blotting for signaling pathways, and xenograft models.
LRP12 was identified as a differentially expressed IRRG-associated gene and
emerged as a robust prognostic marker, enriched in malignant cells and
macrophage populations. High LRP12 expression correlated with poorer overall
survival and with M2 macrophage markers (CD163, CD206). Functional studies using
both overexpression and knockdown showed that LRP12 promotes GC cell
proliferation and invasion, while xenografts of LRP12-knockdown cells exhibited
reduced tumor growth. Mechanistically, LRP12 overexpression activated the
AKT/mTOR pathway, reflected by increased phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR.
LRP12-associated signatures were linked to an immunosuppressive microenvironment
characterized by M2 macrophage infiltration and may indicate responsiveness to
therapies that repolarize M2-like tumor-associated macrophages. LRP12 is a
prognostic biomarker in GC that promotes tumor progression via AKT/mTOR
signaling and M2 macrophage-mediated immunosuppression, with potential to inform
personalized therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of global mortality, characterized by uncontrolled cell proliferation, immune surveillance evasion, and metastatic potential. Treatment paradigms have evolved from conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy to targeted therapies, immunotherapies, and precision medicine approaches1. Gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with over 1.08 million new cases and 760,000 deaths annually2. While radical gastrectomy combined with D2 lymphadenectomy and perioperative chemotherapy has demonstrated survival benefits3,4, GC’s pronounced heterogeneity frequently results in resistance to conventional therapies, accelerating disease progression. Consequently, research focus is shifting from tumor-intrinsic factors to the comprehensive tumor microenvironment.

Inflammation is widely recognized as a critical driver of cancer development, with the interplay between local immune responses and systemic inflammation playing pivotal roles in tumor progression and prognosis5,6. GC tissue heterogeneity is intricately linked to dynamic changes within the tumor microenvironment7. While previous immune-related prognostic models in GC have advanced our understanding—notably by demonstrating that the tumor microenvironment score is a key predictor of prognosis and therapeutic outcomes8—they often overlook novel modulators such as lipoprotein receptor-related proteins (LRPs). Although several LRP family members have been implicated in cancer immunology—notably LRP1 facilitates M2 macrophage polarization in GC and bladder cancer9,10—the function of LRP12 in GC remains largely unexplored.

LRP12 is a key transmembrane protein that facilitates neuroblast migration11 and influences neurological disease development12,13. It exhibits diverse expression patterns across tumor types14–16, enhancing carboplatin resistance in non-small cell lung cancer through promoter methylation17 and serving as an epigenetic marker in B-cell lymphoma18. However, LRP12’s role in GC progression and immune-inflammatory responses remains unclear. This study integrates bioinformatics analyses, clinical samples, functional assays, and in vivo xenograft experiments to establish LRP12 as a prognostic biomarker associated with M2 macrophage infiltration and AKT/mTOR signaling, potentially guiding personalized therapeutic strategies for GC patients.

DiscussionIn this study, we identify LRP12 as a novel and significant prognostic biomarker in GC. Our findings demonstrate that elevated LRP12 expression correlates with GC progression and poor patient survival by promoting tumor progression through the AKT/mTOR pathway and by fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) enriched with M2-polarized macrophages. This work positions LRP12 at the critical intersection of tumor-intrinsic signaling and immune modulation.Our findings on the prognostic value of LRP12 align with and significantly extend previous reports. While prior studies identified LRP12 hypermethylation as a biomarker in lung cancer and B-cell lymphoma17,18, our work is the first to demonstrate that its transcriptional upregulation in GC is a key driver of immune evasion. Mechanistically, we show that this effect is mediated by LRP12-induced activation of the AKT/mTOR pathway, a master regulator of macrophage biology. This pathway is not merely a switch for polarization but a central hub that governs macrophage survival, proliferation, migration, and adaptive responses to metabolic and inflammatory cues25. Extensive research has established that PI3K-AKT signaling is essential for M2 polarization in response to various stimuli, such as IL-4, often requiring crosstalk with other pathways like STAT626–28. Crucially, the inhibition of AKT/mTOR has been shown to abolish the upregulation of M2-specific genes, confirming its indispensable role in this process29,30. Therefore, our finding that LRP12 activates this pathway offers a mechanistic explanation for its role in promoting M2 macrophage infiltration.These LRP12-induced M2 macrophages actively construct an immunosuppressive TME. They cripple anti-tumor immunity not only by secreting suppressive cytokines but also by expressing immune checkpoints like PD-1, which directly inhibits their own phagocytic capacity and blunts the cytotoxic functions of T cells and NK cells31. This context is particularly relevant given the role of Helicobacter pylori infection, a primary driver of GC that promotes cell migration and shapes immune infiltration32. We propose a synergistic model where H. pylori-induced inflammation primes the TME, and subsequent LRP12 upregulation robustly drives M2 polarization via the PI3K-AKT pathway, creating a formidable barrier to effective anti-tumor immunity.Translating these findings into clinical practice, our data suggest that LRP12 could serve as a predictive biomarker to guide personalized treatment strategies. Specifically, patients with LRP12-high tumors exhibited increased sensitivity to cisplatin but resistance to both capecitabine and ICBs. This suggests a clear, actionable therapeutic hypothesis: LRP12-high patients may benefit from cisplatin-based chemotherapy, potentially combined with strategies to overcome immunotherapy resistance. Such strategies could include targeting the LRP12-PI3K axis with PI3K/AKT inhibitors, which have shown potential in modulating the immune response33, or employing agents that target tumor-associated macrophages (e.g., CSF1R inhibitors) to repolarize M2 macrophages and restore anti-tumor immunity34. This approach mirrors the established use of biomarkers like PD-L1 to guide pembrolizumab therapy in GC35 and offers a refined method for patient stratification.Methodologically, our study’s strength lies in its hybrid approach, integrating bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data. Bulk sequencing of large cohorts like TCGA provided the statistical power for robust prognostic modeling, an approach consistent with the pioneering pan-cancer analyses of signaling pathways by Liu et al.36. However, bulk data can mask cellular heterogeneity37. By complementing this with single-cell data, we pinpointed LRP12’s enrichment specifically within malignant cells and macrophages. This integrated strategy, which mitigates the respective limitations of each technology, has proven effective in other recent studies for tasks such as identifying biomarkers or profiling transcription factors38,39. High-throughput functional genomics, particularly CRISPR-based screening, offers a powerful strategy for systematically identifying genetic determinants of therapeutic resistance. For instance, a seminal study successfully integrated CRISPR screens with IC50 data to uncover key genes driving trametinib resistance40. Drawing inspiration from this approach, future CRISPR screens could be employed to systematically map the landscape of genetic dependencies associated with LRP12-driven resistance in GC.Beyond conventional drug development, our identification of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway as a key downstream effector of LRP12 opens avenues for exploring novel inhibitors from traditional medicine. Approaches like network pharmacology have successfully identified molecular targets for herbal compounds41, and compelling evidence shows that biomarker-guided traditional medicine can effectively modulate cancer progression42. Integrating our omics-based discovery with these empirical pharmacopeias could accelerate the identification of natural compounds that specifically inhibit the LRP12-PI3K axis, offering a promising strategy for developing next-generation cancer therapies.Despite the comprehensive nature of our study, several limitations remain. First, the direct molecular interaction between LRP12 and the signaling machinery that drives M2 polarization requires further clarification through dedicated mechanistic studies. Second, although our conclusions are supported by large-scale bioinformatics analyses and multiple in vitro and in vivo assays, we have not yet performed immune cell co-culture experiments that would provide causal evidence for LRP12’s immunomodulatory effects. Future work will therefore focus on detailed molecular dissection of LRP12’s role in macrophage polarization, the development of LRP12-modulated gastric cancer cell lines for macrophage co-culture and functional assays, and prospective clinical studies to validate its utility as a biomarker.

Therefore, our finding that LRP12 activates this pathway offers a mechanistic explanation for its role in promoting M2 macrophage infiltration.

These LRP12-induced M2 macrophages actively construct an immunosuppressive TME. They cripple anti-tumor immunity not only by secreting suppressive cytokines but also by expressing immune checkpoints like PD-1, which directly inhibits their own phagocytic capacity and blunts the cytotoxic functions of T cells and NK cells31. This context is particularly relevant given the role of Helicobacter pylori infection, a primary driver of GC that promotes cell migration and shapes immune infiltration32. We propose a synergistic model where H. pylori-induced inflammation primes the TME, and subsequent LRP12 upregulation robustly drives M2 polarization via the PI3K-AKT pathway, creating a formidable barrier to effective anti-tumor immunity.

Translating these findings into clinical practice, our data suggest that LRP12 could serve as a predictive biomarker to guide personalized treatment strategies. Specifically, patients with LRP12-high tumors exhibited increased sensitivity to cisplatin but resistance to both capecitabine and ICBs. This suggests a clear, actionable therapeutic hypothesis: LRP12-high patients may benefit from cisplatin-based chemotherapy, potentially combined with strategies to overcome immunotherapy resistance. Such strategies could include targeting the LRP12-PI3K axis with PI3K/AKT inhibitors, which have shown potential in modulating the immune response33, or employing agents that target tumor-associated macrophages (e.g., CSF1R inhibitors) to repolarize M2 macrophages and restore anti-tumor immunity34. This approach mirrors the established use of biomarkers like PD-L1 to guide pembrolizumab therapy in GC35 and offers a refined method for patient stratification.

Methodologically, our study’s strength lies in its hybrid approach, integrating bulk and single-cell transcriptomic data. Bulk sequencing of large cohorts like TCGA provided the statistical power for robust prognostic modeling, an approach consistent with the pioneering pan-cancer analyses of signaling pathways by Liu et al.36. However, bulk data can mask cellular heterogeneity37. By complementing this with single-cell data, we pinpointed LRP12’s enrichment specifically within malignant cells and macrophages. This integrated strategy, which mitigates the respective limitations of each technology, has proven effective in other recent studies for tasks such as identifying biomarkers or profiling transcription factors38,39. High-throughput functional genomics, particularly CRISPR-based screening, offers a powerful strategy for systematically identifying genetic determinants of therapeutic resistance. For instance, a seminal study successfully integrated CRISPR screens with IC50 data to uncover key genes driving trametinib resistance40. Drawing inspiration from this approach, future CRISPR screens could be employed to systematically map the landscape of genetic dependencies associated with LRP12-driven resistance in GC.

Beyond conventional drug development, our identification of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway as a key downstream effector of LRP12 opens avenues for exploring novel inhibitors from traditional medicine. Approaches like network pharmacology have successfully identified molecular targets for herbal compounds41, and compelling evidence shows that biomarker-guided traditional medicine can effectively modulate cancer progression42. Integrating our omics-based discovery with these empirical pharmacopeias could accelerate the identification of natural compounds that specifically inhibit the LRP12-PI3K axis, offering a promising strategy for developing next-generation cancer therapies.

Despite the comprehensive nature of our study, several limitations remain. First, the direct molecular interaction between LRP12 and the signaling machinery that drives M2 polarization requires further clarification through dedicated mechanistic studies. Second, although our conclusions are supported by large-scale bioinformatics analyses and multiple in vitro and in vivo assays, we have not yet performed immune cell co-culture experiments that would provide causal evidence for LRP12’s immunomodulatory effects. Future work will therefore focus on detailed molecular dissection of LRP12’s role in macrophage polarization, the development of LRP12-modulated gastric cancer cell lines for macrophage co-culture and functional assays, and prospective clinical studies to validate its utility as a biomarker.

ConclusionIn conclusion, LRP12 emerges as a significant prognostic biomarker in GC, correlating with poor patient outcomes and enhanced tumor progression through the AKT/mTOR pathway. Its association with M2 macrophage infiltration suggests a role in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment, potentially contributing to immunosuppression. These findings highlight LRP12 as a promising therapeutic target, offering new avenues for personalized treatment strategies in GC.
