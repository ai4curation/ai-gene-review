INTRODUCTION: Niemann-Pick C1 (NPC1), a lysosomal cholesterol transport protein,
is required for efficient efferocytosis. Patients with Npc1 mutation are
frequently accompanied with hepatic symptoms, including hepatomegaly, elevated
liver transaminases, or even acute liver failure, but the pathogenic mechanism
remains unknown.
OBJECTIVES: Our work aims to characterize the functional role of myeloid NPC1 in
liver injury and elucidate its underlying mechanism.
METHODS: Analyses of injured livers from patients with liver diseases and mouse
models were conducted to examine NPC1 expression. Myeloid cell-specific Npc1
knockout mice were constructed to determine the functional role of macrophage
NPC1 in liver injury. Isolated macrophages were subjected to in vitro
mechanistical assays.
RESULTS: We found that NPC1 is mainly expressed in hepatic macrophages. Its mRNA
and protein expression are significantly elevated in injured livers from both
patients and mouse models. Tissue-specific deletion of myeloid Npc1 increased
liver inflammation, levels of serum liver function enzymes, and liver fibrosis
in mouse models of liver injury induced by carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) injection
and methionine-and-choline-deficient (MCD) diets. Further analyses indicate that
Npc1 deficiency in mouse models of liver injury resulted in increased levels of
serum HMGB1 and mitochondrial DNA, promoted hepatic macrophage proinflammatory
activation, M1 polarization, led to overproduction of hepatic inflammatory
cytokines/chemokines, e.g. CCL2 and STING/NFκB pathway activation. In vitro
mechanistical studies reveal that Npc1-deficient macrophages exhibited
inefficient efferocytosis, partly due to impaired cargo degradation.
CONCLUSIONS: These findings indicate that elevated expression of myeloid NPC1 in
liver diseases protects liver from injury by promoting macrophage efferocytosis
of damaged cells. Dysfunction of NPC1 aggravates liver injury, suggesting that
NPC1 may be a potential therapeutic target for treating liver diseases.

Introduction

NPC1 is an intracellular cholesterol transporter, mainly localized in lysosome and late endosome. It appears ubiquitously expressed in our body, but mostly abundant in the liver and lung [1], [2]. NPC1 plays an important role in maintaining intracellular cholesterol homeostasis in mammals. Mutations of Npc1 or Npc2 in humans cause severe neurological disorders called Niemann–Pick Type C disease (with Npc1 mutations accounting for 95 %) [3], where hepatic symptoms have also been frequently observed [4], [5], [6]. Common hepatic presentations include hepatomegaly, elevated liver transaminases, prolonged neonatal jaundice, ascites, or even acute liver failure [7], [8], [9]. It is characterized by several histological hallmarks, including increased macrophage infiltration, inflammation, hepatocyte apoptosis and liver fibrosis [10], [11], [12], [13]. Moreover, NPC1 and lysosomal cholesterol accumulation have been implicated in inflammation, immune regulation and liver fibrosis [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]. However, as how Npc1 mutation leads to liver injury remains unknown. Furthermore, it is not known whether NPC1 plays a role in liver injury.

Hepatic residential macrophages, also called Kupffer cells play critical roles in maintaining liver tissue homeostasis, including modulating hepatic lipid metabolism. They also mediate inflammation by responding to stress signals released from injured parenchyma, such as proinflammatory cytokines and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). They activate stellate cells (HSCs) and promote liver fibrosis as well [19], [20], [21]. Furthermore, circulating monocytes can rapidly influx into the liver and further amplify these responses [22], [23], [24]. Recent studies have highlighted the beneficial roles played by macrophages in phagocytic clearance of dying or dead hepatocytes, a process known as efferocytosis, which prevents hepatocyte-derived DAMPs release and curbs fibrotic progression [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30]. Under normal physiologic homeostasis, efferocytosis is immunologically silent. However, when ineffective efferocytosis happens, this can cause tissue inflammation. But the mechanistic details remain to be elucidated. Most importantly, it is not known whether myeloid NPC1 plays any role in this response.

In this report, we investigate the functional role of myeloid NPC1 in liver injury in humans and mice. We found that Npc1 expression was increased in injured livers from patients with liver diseases and mouse models. Myeloid Npc1 tissue-specific deletion in mice promoted hepatic macrophages M1 (proinflammatory) transition, aggravated liver injury and worsened liver fibrosis. In vitro mechanistic studies revealed that deficiency of NPC1 led to increased accumulation of DAMPs and damaged hepatocytes, which was likely caused by impaired macrophage efferocytosis. These findings indicate that NPC1 plays a protective role in liver injury. Stimulation of its functional role may be a novel strategy for treating liver diseases.

This result may imply that ingestive cargoes cannot be effectively degraded, probably due to their accumulation inside the cells.

To further test this hypothesis, we performed an in vitro degradation assay by incubating PMs with PKH26-labeled ACs [35]. The fluorescence photographs demonstrated that non-fragmented ACs in Npc1ΔMφ PMs were obviously higher than those in Npc1WT PMs (Fig. 7E), further reflecting an abortive degradative process. To quantitatively characterize the degradative effect and exclude potential uptake effects after only 3-hours interval, an additional set of degradation experiment was conducted using flow cytometry. As illustrated in Fig. 7F, TAMRA MFI in the F4/80+ BMDM population was monitored up to 120 h after washing. TAMRA MFI ascended first until reached the peak after 24 h, likely because BMDMs continued to engulf the bound ACs that cannot be washed. Compared with control cells, Npc1ΔMφ BMDMs had lower TAMRA MFI at 24 h, whereas significantly higher fluorescence signals at 120 h, demonstrating that Npc1ΔMφ BMDMs exhibited a dramatically reduced rate of degradation (Fig. 7F).

To test whether defective cargo degradation can stimulate inflammatory response, we assayed the mRNA expression of inflammatory genes in efferocytic macrophages. As shown in Fig. 8A, qPCR revealed significantly higher levels of Il1b mRNA expression in ACs-treated Npc1ΔMφ BMDMs than in ACs-treated Npc1WT BMDMs at 5 h after washing. Furthermore, ACs-treated Npc1ΔMφ BMDMs tended to have higher levels of Ccl2 and Tgfb1 mRNA, but lower levels of Tnf and Il10 mRNA than ACs-treated Npc1WT BMDMs, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. We found that 1) Npc1 mRNA and protein expression were significantly elevated in the injured livers from both patients and mouse models (Fig. 1, Fig. 2); 2) Deficiency of myeloid NPC1 in mice exacerbated liver injury and increased liver fibrosis (Fig. 2, Fig. 3), which were associated with aggravated inflammatory response evidenced by increased macrophage infiltrates and expression of proinflammatory cytokines (Fig. 4, Fig. 5); 3) Flow cytometry analysis revealed that NPC1 deficiency in mice led to increased size and number of hepatic macrophages in CCl4-induced liver injury (Fig. 7); 4) Further biochemistry analysis indicated that there was excessive DAMP accumulation in the blood of CCl4-injured mice upon myeloid NPC1 deficiency (Fig. 6); and 5) in vitro assay in cell cultures demonstrated that deficiency of NPC1 exhibited insufficient efferocytosis, which led to increased expression of proinflammatory genes (Fig. 7, Fig. 8). Therefore, we conclude that hepatic myeloid NPC1 plays a protective role in liver diseases, and its upregulation in the injured livers is likely as an adaptive response to enhance efferocytosis for clean-up of cell debris, providing a mechanistic explanation for the role of hepatic myeloid NPC1 in the liver diseases (Fig. 8B).Efferocytosis is crucial for maintaining tissue homeostasis in health and diseases. Effective macrophagic efferocytosis reduces liver injury and fibrosis [25], [26], [45], [46]. In contrast, impaired efferocytosis can lead to liver inflammation [27]. However, the mechanistic details had not been elucidated. Here we demonstrated that deficiency of myeloid NPC1 exacerbated liver injury in mouse models by enhancing inflammation and fibrosis. Considering that macrophages have to deal with cellular debris or myelin upon efferocytosis, as well as impaired myelin turnover in Npc1-deficient microglia, we speculate that this process may also be deficient in hepatic macrophages, leading to increased intracellular lipid accumulation. This speculation is supported by a number of observations: 1) we detected increased levels of HMGB1 and mtDNA, two important DAMPs, in vivo in CCl4-injured mouse serum (Fig. 6), indicating insufficient clearance of cell debris in the Npc1ΔMφ animals; 2) deficiency of NPC1 impaired cargo degradation in macrophages in vitro in cell cultures, stimulating the expression of genes involved in inflammation and fibrogenesis (Fig. 7, Fig. 8); 3) consequently, increased liver inflammation and fibrosis (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). These observations are also consistent with previous reports [42], [47], [48], [49]. Studies from Schwerd et al. have demonstrated that Npc1 mutations impair bacterial handling in macrophages due to a defect in phagosome-autophagosome transition [47]. Additionally, lipid trafficking to the lysosome is compromised in Npc1-deficient microglia, whereas their lysosomes maintain normal proteolytic function [48]. Sarkar et al. attributed impaired autophagosome maturation to the failure of fusion between autophagosomes and late endosomes, because Npc1-deficient late endosomes were unable to recruit SNARE components [42]. Macrophages from Gaucher disease also had impaired efferocytosis caused by defective phagosome-lysosome fusion and phagosome maturation [49], although it is not known whether NPC1 function is impaired in these patients. Together, all these observations support our hypothesis, indicating the functional significance of NPC1 in efferocytosis.
