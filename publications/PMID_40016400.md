Adenylate kinase (AK), also referred to as adenosine triphosphate-adenosine
monophosphate phosphotransferase, serves an essential function in regulating
cellular energy metabolism. A deficiency in red blood cell adenylate kinase 1
(AK-1) is linked to congenital non-spherocytic hemolytic anemia as well as
delays in both mental and psychomotor development. This deficiency is an
extremely rare autosomal recessive genetic disorder, with 13 highly pathogenic
variants of the AK-1 gene documented globally through genetic testing.
Thalassemia, a disorder of hemoglobin production, is a common monogenic
inherited disease in southern China. Severe forms of thalassemia result in
defective hemoglobin synthesis, leading to hemolytic anemia due to the breakdown
of red blood cells. A molecular investigation was carried out on a proband with
severe hemolytic anemia from a family in Guangxi, China, to identify the
underlying cause of the anemia and associated clinical manifestations, offering
valuable information for family planning and prenatal counseling. Hematological,
biochemical, and thalassemia-specific genetic testing were performed on the
proband and family members in relation to chronic hemolytic anemia. Whole-exome
sequencing was utilized to detect genetic variants, followed by an analysis of
their pathogenic potential. Confirmation was conducted using Sanger sequencing.
Prenatal diagnosis was carried out by analyzing amniotic fluid during the
mother's second pregnancy. The proband, who presented with severe hemolytic
anemia, was diagnosed with the thalassemia genotype- SEA/αα compound
βCD41-42/βN. A homozygous mutation, c.464delA (p.Lys155Arg fs*39), was
identified in exon 6 of the AK-1 gene. A case of mild thalassemia with severe
anemia was investigated, leading to the identification of a novel mutation in
the AK-1 gene. Bioinformatics tools predicted the pathogenic nature of this
variant, linking it to AK deficiency, which may contribute to the observed
severe anemia and associated clinical symptoms.

IntroductionAdenylate kinase (AK) is ubiquitously present across various organisms, serving as a vital monomeric enzyme essential for normal cellular growth, differentiation, motility, and metabolic processes in living organisms [1]. As a pivotal enzyme in the regulation of cellular adenine nucleotide homeostasis, AK modulates the cell cycle, proliferation, and energy distribution through the AK→adenosine monophosphate (AMP)→AMP-activated protein kinase signaling cascade [2]. AK deficiency, an autosomal recessive inherited disorder, results in the depletion of adenine nucleotides in cells, leading to a deficit of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and ultimately contributing to chronic hemolytic anemia [3]. AK proteins that possess catalytic domains facilitate the reversible reaction: adenosine diphosphate (2ADP) ↔ AMP + ATP. To date, nine isoenzymes, denoted as AK1-AK9, have been identified in human tissues, each exhibiting distinct tissue-specific distributions [4–5]. The AK-1 gene, spanning 12 kb and located on chromosome 9q34, comprises 7 exons and encodes a protein consisting of 194 amino acid residues, with the highest expression levels observed in erythrocytes, skeletal muscle, and the brain [6]. Mutations in the AK-1 gene lead to decreased catalytic activity of type I AK and reduced protein stability, which may result in hemolytic anemia or psychomotor impairments [7]. Thalassemia, a prevalent monogenic inherited disorder, is particularly common in regions of southern China, such as Guangdong, Guangxi, and Hainan. Its pathogenesis is characterized by an imbalance in the production ratios of α-globin and β-globin chains, causing defects in hemoglobin synthesis and leading to erythrocyte rupture and subsequent hemolytic anemia. Clinical manifestations of thalassemia vary: carriers of mild forms exhibit only microcytic hypochromic anemia with minimal impact on daily life, while patients with moderate to severe forms face progressively worsening conditions, often requiring lifelong blood transfusions to manage chronic hemolytic anemia [8–9].A case of homozygous AK-1 gene mutation associated with mild thalassemia has been reported, representing the first documented instance worldwide of AK deficiency coexisting with mild thalassemia, which contributes to the exacerbation of hemolytic anemia. Molecular analysis of the AK-1 gene, along with prenatal diagnostic testing, was conducted within this family.

DiscussionThe proband presented with severe anemia, hepatosplenomegaly, and pallor. Due to the high prevalence of thalassemia in the Guangxi region, a preliminary diagnosis of severe thalassemia was considered. Initial genetic testing for thalassemia revealed only mild thalassemia (--SEA/αα with βCD41–42/βN), which did not fully explain the severe anemic symptoms observed. As a result, other potential causes of anemia were explored. Whole exome sequencing revealed a mutation in the AK-1 gene. A novel homozygous frameshift mutation, c.464delA(p.Lys155Argfs*39), was identified in exon 6 of the AK-1 gene. Frameshift mutations can lead to mRNA degradation or the creation of premature termination codons [19], ultimately resulting in partial protein translation failure and loss of gene function. According to ACMG pathogenicity classification guidelines, this variant was classified as likely pathogenic and is suspected to contribute to AK deficiency, thereby leading to congenital hemolytic anemia. Through the study of this case, it was discovered that due to the rarity of AK1 deficiency, clinicians may easily misdiagnose the hemolytic anemia caused by thalassemia, leading to an inability to identify the underlying etiology. This not only wastes medical resources but also increases the financial burden on patients. If a patient suffers from both AK1 deficiency and mild thalassemia, they might be misdiagnosed with severe thalassemia, affecting the management of thalassemia by healthcare institutions, delaying the patient’s diagnosis and treatment, and even influencing the family’s plans for future pregnancies. Since thalassemia is prevalent in regions like Guangxi, it is highly beneficial to enhance awareness of AK-1 gene mutations and to explore how clinicians should diagnose, treat, and manage such complex diseases.Based on reported cases of pathogenic AK-1 gene variants (Table 3), missense mutations in the AK-1 gene were found to be the most prevalent form of variation. Specifically, five variants were identified in exon 6, four in exon 5, and three in exon 4. This pattern suggests that mutations occurring within exons 4–6 of the AK-1 gene are commonly associated with pathogenicity. AK deficiency has been documented globally, with no discernible geographical trends. In cases 3, 4, 5, and 10, patients exhibited a complete lack of AK activity. However, with the exception of case 9, all other cases presented with mild anemia that did not necessitate blood transfusions. In the present study, the proband’s AK enzyme activity was approximately 60% of the normal value, yet severe anemia requiring blood transfusion was observed. Despite other cases exhibiting varying degrees of enzyme deficiency and anemia, no correlation between them, consistent with findings by Patrizia A [7]. This suggests that while AK-1 gene mutations can induce hemolytic anemia, the severity of anemia does not directly correlate with enzyme activity levels, indicating that further investigation into the underlying mechanisms is warranted. Edwin Janssen [20] identified two structurally distinct AK-1 gene products, AK1 and AK1β, in the mouse brain, both of which are highly expressed in neurons and differ only by 1,189 nucleotides in the 3’UTR region. These isoforms are both involved in energy-demanding processes, such as neurotransmitter shuttling and exocytosis. Research on AK1 and AK1β suggests that AK1 may serve a principal function in neurons, particularly in energy metabolism. Two similar expression products with divergent 3’UTRs have also been detected in the human AK-1 gene [21], with the 3’UTR regions serving essential functions in regulating mRNA stability, translation, transcription, and protein interactions [22]. Therefore, mutations in the AK-1 gene may disrupt mRNA function in neuronal cells, potentially contributing to psychomotor disorders in some patients. To date, five cases of neuromotor disorders have been reported, all exhibiting extremely low or absent AK enzyme activity. Three of these cases involved AK-1 gene mutations leading to premature protein translation termination, indicating that AK1 enzyme activity could have a substantial impact on mental development. Premature stop codons may represent a critical factor affecting enzyme activity. Clinically, cases 3 and 4 presented with psychomotor disorders and delayed language development but exhibited only mild anemia that did not require transfusion. Conversely, cases 9, 10, and 12 demonstrated psychomotor disorders alongside severe hemolytic anemia. In this study, the proband carried a homozygous frameshift mutation in the AK-1 gene. His reticulocyte was 0.29*10^12/L, which reference range is (0.024–0.084) *10^12/L, indicating active bone marrow hematopoiesis and hemolytic anemia, yet his AK enzyme activity was approximately 60% of normal AK enzyme activity, and showed no psychomotor disorders. This suggests that there is no clear correlation between psychomotor disorders and the severity of anemia. The proband’s AK enzyme activity was higher than that of other reported cases, additionally, although the proband’s mother is a carrier of the same AK-1 gene mutation, she displayed normal enzyme activity. it may be attributed to individual variability, compensatory mechanisms, or interactions with other diseases, which are directions for our future research.AK deficiency may coexist with other hemolytic disorders. Human erythrocytes produce ATP through anaerobic glycolysis, which is essential for their high-energy demands. Enzyme defects within erythrocytes result in disrupted energy metabolism, accelerating cell destruction and hemolysis, with patients commonly presenting with clinical symptoms such as hemolysis [23–24]. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, the most prevalent enzyme disorder worldwide, destabilizes red blood cells, leading to oxidative damage and subsequent hemolysis [25]. In 1969, Szeinberg et al. [26] reported an Arab family in which two individuals exhibited severe AK deficiency (enzyme activity < 5% of normal), though hemolysis was only observed in the patient with concurrent severe G6PD deficiency. A follow-up study by Tore et al. [27] in 1994 identified six additional individuals from the same family with low AK activity, three of whom also had G6PD deficiency. These three patients exhibited more pronounced anemia symptoms compared to those with isolated AK deficiency, necessitating blood transfusions. Analysis of hematological data from previously reported cases indicates that patients with isolated AK deficiency often present with varying degrees of anemia, most of whom require occasional transfusions or no regular transfusion support. In contrast, the proband in this study exhibited severe anemia that necessitated frequent blood transfusions, particularly during infections. While it is established that thalassemia leads to excessive globin deposition on cell membranes, resulting in erythrocyte membrane damage, further investigation is needed to determine whether AK deficiency and thalassemia act synergistically to accelerate erythrocyte destruction and lysis.After diagnosis, the patient received regular blood transfusions, leading to an improvement in anemia symptoms and overall condition. During the mother’s subsequent pregnancy, amniotic fluid was collected for prenatal diagnosis, which revealed no abnormalities in the AK-1 gene of the fetus. The fetus was found to be a mild thalassemia carrier, permitting the continuation of the pregnancy. For patients presenting with such conditions, the current approach primarily focuses on symptomatic treatment. For families with a desire for future pregnancies, it is crucial to identify the underlying cause as early as possible. Prenatal diagnosis or third-generation in vitro fertilization (IVF) techniques can be utilized to prevent the birth of affected children. This study emphasizes that when genetic testing does not fully account for clinical manifestations, alternative pathogenic factors should be considered. The results of this study enhance the understanding of AK-1 gene pathogenic variants and offer valuable insights for the diagnosis, treatment, and prenatal diagnosis of affected individuals and their family members.

Since thalassemia is prevalent in regions like Guangxi, it is highly beneficial to enhance awareness of AK-1 gene mutations and to explore how clinicians should diagnose, treat, and manage such complex diseases.

Based on reported cases of pathogenic AK-1 gene variants (Table 3), missense mutations in the AK-1 gene were found to be the most prevalent form of variation. Specifically, five variants were identified in exon 6, four in exon 5, and three in exon 4. This pattern suggests that mutations occurring within exons 4–6 of the AK-1 gene are commonly associated with pathogenicity. AK deficiency has been documented globally, with no discernible geographical trends. In cases 3, 4, 5, and 10, patients exhibited a complete lack of AK activity. However, with the exception of case 9, all other cases presented with mild anemia that did not necessitate blood transfusions. In the present study, the proband’s AK enzyme activity was approximately 60% of the normal value, yet severe anemia requiring blood transfusion was observed. Despite other cases exhibiting varying degrees of enzyme deficiency and anemia, no correlation between them, consistent with findings by Patrizia A [7]. This suggests that while AK-1 gene mutations can induce hemolytic anemia, the severity of anemia does not directly correlate with enzyme activity levels, indicating that further investigation into the underlying mechanisms is warranted. Edwin Janssen [20] identified two structurally distinct AK-1 gene products, AK1 and AK1β, in the mouse brain, both of which are highly expressed in neurons and differ only by 1,189 nucleotides in the 3’UTR region. These isoforms are both involved in energy-demanding processes, such as neurotransmitter shuttling and exocytosis. Research on AK1 and AK1β suggests that AK1 may serve a principal function in neurons, particularly in energy metabolism. Two similar expression products with divergent 3’UTRs have also been detected in the human AK-1 gene [21], with the 3’UTR regions serving essential functions in regulating mRNA stability, translation, transcription, and protein interactions [22]. Therefore, mutations in the AK-1 gene may disrupt mRNA function in neuronal cells, potentially contributing to psychomotor disorders in some patients. To date, five cases of neuromotor disorders have been reported, all exhibiting extremely low or absent AK enzyme activity. Three of these cases involved AK-1 gene mutations leading to premature protein translation termination, indicating that AK1 enzyme activity could have a substantial impact on mental development. Premature stop codons may represent a critical factor affecting enzyme activity. Clinically, cases 3 and 4 presented with psychomotor disorders and delayed language development but exhibited only mild anemia that did not require transfusion. Conversely, cases 9, 10, and 12 demonstrated psychomotor disorders alongside severe hemolytic anemia. In this study, the proband carried a homozygous frameshift mutation in the AK-1 gene. His reticulocyte was 0.29*10^12/L, which reference range is (0.024–0.084) *10^12/L, indicating active bone marrow hematopoiesis and hemolytic anemia, yet his AK enzyme activity was approximately 60% of normal AK enzyme activity, and showed no psychomotor disorders. This suggests that there is no clear correlation between psychomotor disorders and the severity of anemia. The proband’s AK enzyme activity was higher than that of other reported cases, additionally, although the proband’s mother is a carrier of the same AK-1 gene mutation, she displayed normal enzyme activity. it may be attributed to individual variability, compensatory mechanisms, or interactions with other diseases, which are directions for our future research.

AK deficiency may coexist with other hemolytic disorders. Human erythrocytes produce ATP through anaerobic glycolysis, which is essential for their high-energy demands. Enzyme defects within erythrocytes result in disrupted energy metabolism, accelerating cell destruction and hemolysis, with patients commonly presenting with clinical symptoms such as hemolysis [23–24]. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, the most prevalent enzyme disorder worldwide, destabilizes red blood cells, leading to oxidative damage and subsequent hemolysis [25]. In 1969, Szeinberg et al. [26] reported an Arab family in which two individuals exhibited severe AK deficiency (enzyme activity < 5% of normal), though hemolysis was only observed in the patient with concurrent severe G6PD deficiency. A follow-up study by Tore et al. [27] in 1994 identified six additional individuals from the same family with low AK activity, three of whom also had G6PD deficiency. These three patients exhibited more pronounced anemia symptoms compared to those with isolated AK deficiency, necessitating blood transfusions. Analysis of hematological data from previously reported cases indicates that patients with isolated AK deficiency often present with varying degrees of anemia, most of whom require occasional transfusions or no regular transfusion support. In contrast, the proband in this study exhibited severe anemia that necessitated frequent blood transfusions, particularly during infections. While it is established that thalassemia leads to excessive globin deposition on cell membranes, resulting in erythrocyte membrane damage, further investigation is needed to determine whether AK deficiency and thalassemia act synergistically to accelerate erythrocyte destruction and lysis.

After diagnosis, the patient received regular blood transfusions, leading to an improvement in anemia symptoms and overall condition. During the mother’s subsequent pregnancy, amniotic fluid was collected for prenatal diagnosis, which revealed no abnormalities in the AK-1 gene of the fetus. The fetus was found to be a mild thalassemia carrier, permitting the continuation of the pregnancy. For patients presenting with such conditions, the current approach primarily focuses on symptomatic treatment. For families with a desire for future pregnancies, it is crucial to identify the underlying cause as early as possible. Prenatal diagnosis or third-generation in vitro fertilization (IVF) techniques can be utilized to prevent the birth of affected children. This study emphasizes that when genetic testing does not fully account for clinical manifestations, alternative pathogenic factors should be considered. The results of this study enhance the understanding of AK-1 gene pathogenic variants and offer valuable insights for the diagnosis, treatment, and prenatal diagnosis of affected individuals and their family members.
