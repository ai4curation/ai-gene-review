## Full Text

Abstract

Pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins comprise a large family of helical repeat proteins that bind RNA and modulate organellar RNA metabolism. The mechanisms underlying the functions attributed to PPR proteins are unknown. We describe in vitro studies of the maize protein PPR10 that clarify how PPR10 modulates the stability and translation of specific chloroplast mRNAs. We show that recombinant PPR10 bound to its native binding site in the chloroplast atpI–atpH intergenic region ( i ) blocks both 5′→3′ and 3′→ 5 exoribonucleases in vitro; ( ii ) is sufficient to define the native processed atpH mRNA 5′-terminus in conjunction with a generic 5′→3′ exoribonuclease; and ( iii ) remodels the structure of the atpH ribosome-binding site in a manner that can account for PPR10’s ability to enhance atpH translation. In addition, we show that the minimal PPR10-binding site spans 17 nt. We propose that the site-specific barrier and RNA remodeling activities of PPR10 are a consequence of its unusually long, high-affinity interface with single-stranded RNA, that this interface provides a functional mimic to bacterial small RNAs, and that analogous activities underlie many of the biological functions that have been attributed to PPR proteins.

Keywords: mitochondria, plastid, RNA binding protein, RNA processing

Pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins are a recently recognized class of RNA-binding proteins that profoundly affect gene expression in mitochondria and chloroplasts (reviewed in 1 ). PPR proteins are defined by tandem arrays of a degenerate 35-aa repeat that is predicted to adopt a helical hairpin structure ( 2 ). A wealth of genetic data documents the importance of PPR proteins for organellar RNA metabolism: Mutations in PPR-encoding genes cause defects in the splicing, editing, stabilization, processing, or translation of subsets of organellar RNAs, with downstream defects in respiration, photosynthesis, and organismal development.

Despite the abundant genetic data implicating PPR proteins in organellar gene expression, little is known about the mechanisms through which they act. RNA coimmunoprecipitation assays have shown several PPR proteins to be associated with their genetically defined RNA targets in native extracts ( 3 – 7 ), and several PPR proteins have been shown to bind specifically to their native RNA ligands in vitro ( 5 , 8 – 10 ). By extrapolation, it is anticipated that most PPR proteins are sequence-specific RNA-binding proteins. However, PPRs do not resemble known RNA-binding motifs. In fact, they are closely related to a protein-binding motif, the tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) ( 2 ). TPR tracts bind protein ligands along a surface created by stacked helical repeating units ( 11 , 12 ). Structural modeling ( 2 ) and circular dichroism data ( 9 ) support the view that PPR tracts adopt a structure that is similar to that of TPR tracts. This structure is unusual for an RNA-binding domain, most of which are globular ( 13 ). It is reminiscent, however, of the Puf domain, which binds single-stranded RNA along a surface formed by the stacking of eight helical repeating units ( 14 ).

How PPR proteins recognize specific RNAs and how these interactions influence diverse steps in gene expression are central issues that are just starting to be explored. The maize protein PPR10 offers a promising model for exploring PPR mechanisms because its in vivo functions and RNA-binding sites are well characterized, and it binds in vitro with high affinity and specificity to its native RNA targets ( 5 ). PPR10 localizes to chloroplasts, where it interacts with intergenic RNA from two polycistronic transcription units. PPR10 stabilizes RNA segments that lie either upstream or downstream of its binding sites. In addition, bound PPR10 enhances the translational efficiency of a downstream ORF. PPR10 consists almost entirely of PPR motifs and lacks other functional domains. Thus, findings obtained with PPR10 are likely to elucidate general mechanisms through which PPR proteins act.

In this study we explore the mechanistic basis for PPR10’s effects on gene expression through in vitro assays with recombinant PPR10 (rPPR10). The results provide evidence that PPR10 binds single-stranded RNA along an unusually long RNA-binding surface, that this interaction is sufficient to block 5′→3′ and 3′→ 5′ exonucleases, and that PPR10 in conjunction with generic exonucleases can define the positions of the processed mRNA termini observed in vivo. In addition, we show that PPR10 remodels RNA structure in a manner that can account for its ability to enhance translation. We argue that the barrier and RNA remodeling activities observed for PPR10 are likely to be general features of long PPR tracts and that they may underlie many of the biological functions that have been attributed to members of this large and unusual family of RNA-binding proteins.

Results

Defining the Minimal PPR10-Binding Site.

PPR10 binds in vivo to two RNA segments, in the atpI – atpH and the psaJ–rpl33 intergenic regions ( 5 ). We refer to these sites as the “ atpH ” and “ psaJ ” sites, respectively. In vitro binding assays previously localized PPR10’s binding sites to a 29-nt segment in each region. The binding sites are found at the 5′ or 3′ terminus of RNAs that are stabilized by PPR10 in vivo ( 5 ). These features of the atpI–atpH region are illustrated in Fig. 1 A .

Fig. 1.

To understand how PPR10 influences gene expression, it is important to define the precise RNA sequences with which it interacts. We initially estimated the boundaries of the atpH -binding site by performing binding assays with rPPR10 and nested RNAs generated by partial alkali hydrolysis ( Fig. S1 ). The results placed the PPR10-binding site between approximately −45 and −26 with respect to the atpH start codon. We then defined the minimal binding site by using gel mobility shift assays with synthetic RNAs ( Fig. 1 B ). Truncations from the 5′ direction through residue −44 caused no more than a minor effect on binding (compare RNAs 1, 2, and 3), whereas removal of the G residue at −43 caused a substantial loss of binding (compare RNA 7 to RNAs 3 and 4). Truncations from the 3′ direction through residue −28 had little effect (compare RNAs 1–5), whereas removal of the C residue at −27 caused a substantial loss of binding (compare RNAs 4 and 6). Finally, the 17-mer that is bounded by these two important residues (G −43 through C −27) is sufficient for a high-affinity interaction with PPR10 (RNA 4), whereas a 14-mer lacking additional nucleotides at each end fails to bind (RNA 8). These results show that the 17-nt RNA segment mapping from −43 to −27 comprises the minimal site required for a high-affinity interaction with PPR10. rPPR10 binds the minimal atpH site with an equilibrium K d of ∼0.1 nM ( Fig. 1 C ).

rPPR10 binds the psaJ site with lower affinity than it does to the atpH site ( Fig. 2 ). Three C→U transitions and an insertion of a U residue distinguish the minimal atpH site from the corresponding region of psaJ . To determine which of these features accounts for the difference in affinity, the transitions and the insertion were introduced separately into the atpH sequence context. Each modification reduced binding affinity to a similar extent (compare mut 1 and mut 2 with the atpH 19-mer in Fig. 2 ). Therefore, PPR10 discriminates between a C and a U residue at one or more of these positions, and it also is sensitive to the spacing between the 5′ and 3′ halves of its binding site. Furthermore, a U→G substitution near the 3′ end of the minimal binding site causes a substantial loss of binding affinity (compare mut 3 with the atpH 17-mer in Fig. 2 ).

Fig. 2.

Together, these results show that the determinants for a high-affinity interaction with PPR10 span a 17-nt RNA segment and that multiple nucleotides within that segment contribute to binding affinity. The minimal atpH -binding site exhibits striking evolutionary conservation ( Fig. 1 D ), suggesting that most of the nucleotides within the site are recognized in a sequence-specific fashion. PPR10 has 18 PPR motifs and an additional degenerate PPR at the start of the PPR tract. Thus, the features of its minimal binding site are consistent with a “1-PPR/1-nucleotide” binding mechanism in which consecutive PPR motifs recognize consecutive nucleotides.

PPR10 Is Sufficient to Block 5′→3′ and 3′→ 5′ Exoribonucleases.

PPR10-binding sites are found at the termini of RNAs that fail to accumulate in ppr10 mutants ( Fig. 1 A ), suggesting that bound PPR10 defines the mature termini by blocking exonucleases approaching from either direction ( 5 ). To test this model, we performed in vitro assays with rPPR10, purified exonucleases, and the end-labeled RNAs shown in Fig. 3 A . Because a chloroplast 5′→3′ exoribonuclease has not been firmly identified, we used a commercially available 5′→ 3′ exonuclease, Terminator exonuclease. Incubation of a 3′-end–labeled RNA with Terminator produced low-molecular-weight products with no degradation intermediates ( Fig. 3 B , Left ). The addition of rPPR10 blocked RNA degradation, yielding an RNA whose 5′ end corresponds precisely to that of the PPR10-dependent atpH RNA in vivo. Therefore, PPR10 in conjunction with a generic 5′→3′ exoribonuclease is sufficient to generate the mature 5′ end of atpH mRNA.

Fig. 3.

Polynucleotide phosphorylase (PNPase) is one of several 3′→5′ exoribonucleases in chloroplasts that could contribute to the formation of the PPR10-dependent 3′ termini ( 15 ). Recombinant PNPase digested a 5′-end–labeled RNA spanning the PPR10-binding site to completion ( Fig. 3 B , Right ). The addition of rPPR10 blocked RNA degradation, yielding RNA fragments whose 3′ ends map ≈9 nt downstream of the major PPR10-dependent 3′ ends in vivo. The position at which PNPase was blocked by PPR10 is consistent with the fact that PNPase enzymes stall ∼9 nt from the base of RNA hairpins, reflecting the distance between the point of entry of single-stranded RNA into the enzyme's ring-like neck and its active site ( 16 , 17 ). The minimal PPR10-binding site ends ∼13 nt upstream of the termini that were protected from PNPase digestion. It is reasonable to assume that PPR10 protects several additional nucleotides adjacent to its minimal binding site. Thus, PPR10 impedes the progress of PNPase at a position similar to that at which an RNA hairpin would impede PNPase progress. These results imply that PNPase cooperates with a second 3′→5′ exonuclease to produce mature atpI 3′ termini in vivo. The final trimming might be performed by RNase II/R, which has been shown to cooperate with PNPase in this manner in plant mitochondria ( 18 ).

PPR10 Binding Releases the atpH Ribosome-Binding Site from a Sequestering Secondary Structure.

The unprocessed atpH mRNAs that accumulate in ppr10 mutants are translated less efficiently than are their counterparts in normal plants, indicating that PPR10 binding not only stabilizes processed atpH RNAs but also enhances atpH translation ( 5 ). A sequence that is similar to Shine–Dalgarno elements shown to enhance the translation of other chloroplast ORFs ( 19 , 20 ) is found in the atpH 5′ UTR and is predicted to pair with the PPR10-binding site ( Fig. 4 B ). Thus, a reasonable hypothesis is that PPR10 binding releases the Shine–Dalgarno element, thereby facilitating ribosome recruitment. Analogous models have been proposed previously to explain genetic data for translational activators in Chlamydomonas chloroplasts ( 21 , 22 ).

Fig. 4.

To test this hypothesis, the effect of rPPR10 on the structure of the atpH 5′ UTR was assayed by probing with ribonucleases T1 and V1 ( Fig. 4 A ). RNase T1 cleaves after guanosines found in a single-stranded context. RNase V1 cleaves RNA in which the bases are stacked because of their presence in an RNA duplex or because of other structural constraints. The cleavage patterns obtained in the absence of PPR10 support the predicted RNA structure: The three guanosines in the Shine–Dalgarno element were not cleaved by RNase T1, and the “anti-Shine–Dalgarno” element was cleaved efficiently by RNase V1. The addition of rPPR10 caused a dramatic change in the digestion patterns. The guanosines within and near the Shine–Dalgarno element were cleaved efficiently by RNase T1, indicating a substantial increase in their single-stranded character. Furthermore, RNase V1 ceased to cleave the anti-Shine–Dalgarno region; this region lies within the minimal PPR10-binding site and so presumably is protected by rPPR10. Finally, rPPR10 binding increased RNase V1 digestion at several positions just 3′ to the sequences that are protected by PPR10 in vivo, suggesting that PPR10 induces the stacking of nucleotides adjacent to those with which it interacts directly.

These results show that PPR10 binding changes the predominating RNA structure so that the predicted Shine–Dalgarno element is released from an RNA duplex. Furthermore, the PPR10-induced rearrangement is expected to enhance ribosome binding independent of a canonical Shine–Dalgarno interaction because initiating ribosomes must interact with ∼20 nt of single-stranded RNA upstream of the start codon ( 23 ). Our results support a model in which PPR10 captures its binding site in single-stranded form, thereby increasing the single-stranded character of the adjacent atpH ribosome-binding region and facilitating ribosome recruitment ( Fig. 4 C ).

Discussion

Results presented here define a minimal RNA segment required for interaction with a PPR protein and provide insight into the mechanisms by which a PPR protein activates translation and stabilizes specific RNAs in vivo. Our findings provide strong support for the model that PPR10 stabilizes RNA upstream and downstream from its binding sites by blocking exonucleolytic RNA degradation. They further show that PPR10 is sufficient to define the processed atpH 5′ end, with no additional factors required other than the exonuclease itself. Finally, they demonstrate that PPR10 binding alters the structure of the adjacent RNA and that this RNA remodeling provides a plausible mechanism by which PPR10 enhances atpH translation. This study shows that two seemingly disparate functions—RNA stabilization and translational activation—can be explained as a passive consequence of the ability of a PPR tract to bind with high affinity along an extended segment of single-stranded RNA. Because PPR10 consists of little more than its PPR tract, these simple but potent effects are likely to be typical of long PPR tracts. Thus, our findings suggest mechanisms that may account for many of the functions attributed to PPR proteins.

RNA Recognition by PPR Tracts.

The specificity of the RNA defects caused by mutations in genes encoding PPR proteins initially suggested that PPR tracts can bind specific RNAs (reviewed in ref. 1 ), but the mechanism of RNA recognition is unknown. The precise definition of PPR-binding sites is necessary for developing this understanding. Where analyzed, PPR tracts bind to single-stranded but not to double-stranded RNA ( 9 ). Only four PPR proteins have been reported to bind with specificity to their native RNA ligands in vitro , and of these, the PPR10-binding site had been mapped to the highest resolution ( 5 , 8 – 10 ). We show here that PPR10 requires a 17-nt RNA segment for a high-affinity interaction. This binding site shows striking evolutionary conservation ( Fig. 1 D ), and small modifications in sequence or in spacing decrease binding affinity ( Fig. 2 ). These observations imply that PPR10 interacts with many of the nucleotides in its 17-nt minimal binding site. The length of the protein/RNA interface implied by these data is unusually long for a sequence-specific RNA-binding domain (reviewed in ref. 13 ). That PPR10 has 18 or 19 PPR motifs and requires 17 nt for a high-affinity interaction is compatible with a 1-repeat/1-nucleotide recognition mechanism analogous to that described for an unrelated helical repeat motif, the Puf domain ( 14 ). Our identification of the precise binding site of a PPR protein lays a foundation for defining the determinants of sequence-specific RNA recognition by PPR tracts.

Role of PPR Proteins in RNA Stabilization.

PPR10 is one of many PPR proteins for which genetic data have demonstrated a role in the stabilization of specific organellar RNAs. That PPR10-binding sites map to the 5′ or 3′ termini of transcripts that require PPR10 for their accumulation suggested that PPR10 defines “mature” RNA termini by blocking RNA degradation from either the 5′ or 3′ direction ( 5 ). Results presented here confirm and extend this model: ( i ) rPPR10 is sufficient to block RNA degradation by both 5′→3′ and 3′→5′ exonucleases in vitro; ( ii ) the 5′ end produced by a generic 5′ exonuclease in conjunction with rPPR10 matches the native PPR10-dependent 5′ end; and ( iii ) the 3′ end produced by PNPase in the presence of rPPR10 maps several nucleotides downstream of the native PPR10-dependent 3′ termini. This last observation supports the view that a second 3′→5′ exonuclease trims termini left by PNPase in chloroplasts, as has been shown in plant mitochondria ( 18 ). In fact, this additional activity may reside in the same RNase II/R homolog that performs this function in plant mitochondria, because this protein also localizes to chloroplasts ( 24 , 25 ).

Analogous mechanisms could underlie the genetic data implicating other PPR-like proteins in specific RNA-stabilization events. For example, CRP1 and HCF152 are each required for the accumulation of processed chloroplast RNAs with a 5′ or 3′ terminus mapping in a specific intergenic region: CRP1 for RNA termini between petB and petD ( 26 ), and HCF152 for RNA termini between psbH and petB ( 27 ). The 5′ and 3′ termini of the transcripts that rely on each of these proteins overlap by ∼25 nt ( 5 , 26 ), suggesting a mechanism analogous to that of PPR10, in which the binding of the protein between ORFs blocks exonucleases from both directions. The chloroplast protein PPR38 also may act in this manner, because it is required for the accumulation of processed RNAs with a 5′ or 3′ terminus mapping between clpP and rps12 , and recombinant PPR38 protects clpP RNA from 3′→5′ degradation in vitro ( 10 , 28 ). It was proposed that PPR38 mediates the latter effect by stabilizing a 3′ terminal RNA hairpin ( 10 ). However, the positions of the PPR38-dependent 3′ termini and the PPR38-binding site were not mapped with sufficient resolution to address the possibility that the protein itself blocks the exonuclease. Many other PPR-like proteins have been shown to stabilize RNAs with specific 5′ termini in vivo (e.g., refs. 29 – 35 ), suggesting that they impose a block to 5′→3′ degradation.

Link Between RNA Stabilization and Translational Activation.

A dual role in RNA stabilization and translational activation is a common feature of PPR-like proteins in chloroplasts (reviewed in ref. 15 ), but the basis for this connection has not been clear. Our findings suggest a simple mechanism that can account for this link. PPR10 simultaneously stabilizes RNA adjacent to its binding site by blocking nuclease access and enhances translation by preventing the RNA to which it is bound from binding to the adjacent translation-initiation region. Thus, PPR10’s RNA stabilization and translation-activation functions both result from the sequestration of an RNA segment of ∼20 nt. It seems likely that any PPR/RNA interaction of sufficient length and placed at an analogous position with respect to a translation initiation region will have analogous effects. Even weak RNA structures surrounding a start codon can impair ribosome access, so a PPR protein bound a short distance upstream could ensure a clear ribosomal “landing pad.”

The placement of a PPR protein with respect to a start codon is likely to be tied closely to both its translation-enhancing and RNA-stabilizing effects. When PPR10 is bound to the atpH 5′ UTR, its downstream edge maps ∼25 nt upstream of the atpH start codon ( Fig. 1 A ). Bacterial ribosomes protect ∼20 nt upstream of the start codon from nuclease attack ( 23 ). Thus, only a few nucleotides are predicted to lie between bound PPR10 and an initiating ribosome. This spacing might optimize the balance between two opposing effects: steric interference with ribosome binding and enhancement of ribosome access by sequestration of RNA adjacent to the ribosome-binding site. This placement also may be particularly effective at stabilizing the RNA downstream, because it minimizes the length of RNA that is protected neither by ribosomes nor by the PPR protein. That this positioning of a PPR protein may be optimal for enhancing gene expression correlates well with genetic data: Many PPR-dependent 5′ RNA termini in chloroplasts map ∼50 nt upstream of a start codon ( 5 , 27 , 28 , 35 , 36 ), as does the PPR10-dependent atpH 5′ end.

PPR Tracts as Protein Analogs of Bacterial Small RNAs.

The basal translation and RNA-degradation machineries in chloroplasts are closely akin to those in bacteria. The effects of PPR10 have intriguing parallels with the effects of bacterial small RNAs (sRNAs) on these two processes. Most bacterial sRNAs influence gene expression simply by engaging mRNA in an RNA duplex. sRNA can activate translation by pairing with an RNA segment that otherwise would pair with a ribosome-binding site (reviewed in ref. 37 ). sRNA can stabilize RNA upstream of its binding site by blocking 3′→5′ RNA decay ( 38 ). PPR10’s effects are analogous and appear to be the direct consequence of the placement of the PPR10-binding site and the extended length of the protein/RNA interface. Prior in vivo data ( 5 ) and the in vitro experiments shown here present a consistent view of the size of PPR10’s footprint: PPR10 interacts with an RNA segment spanning at least 17 nt and protects several additional nucleotides from exonuclease attack. Thus, PPR10 sequesters ∼23 nt, a length comparable to that of typical sRNA/mRNA duplexes in bacteria ( 37 ). We suggest that the PPR/RNA interface is a functional mimic of an RNA/RNA duplex: When either type of interface is of sufficient length, analogous effects result. The hypothesis that sRNA and long PPR tracts provide two means to the same end predicts specific effects on bacterial gene expression when PPR proteins and their cognate binding sites are expressed ectopically in bacteria. Experiments to test this idea are in progress.

Site-Specific Barrier and RNA Remodeling Activities of PPR Tracts and Implications for PPR Mechanisms.

Our results demonstrate that two biochemical effects of PPR10 underlie its biological functions: PPR10 presents a site-specific barrier to interaction with other proteins (such as nucleases), and it remodels local RNA structure. These two activities are predicted to result from any PPR/RNA interaction of sufficient length and affinity and could influence gene expression in many ways. Genetic data have implicated “pure” PPR proteins (i.e., those lacking additional domains) not only in RNA stabilization and translational activation but also in site-specific RNA cleavage ( 39 , 40 ), group II intron splicing ( 6 , 41 – 43 ), and translational repression ( 44 , 45 ). It often has been suggested (e.g., ref. 1 ) that these different functions result from the recruitment of different effector proteins to specific RNAs by the PPR tract. Our findings suggest a more parsimonious alternative: that most or all of the functions attributed to pure PPR proteins result solely from their high-affinity interaction with an extended length of RNA, which imparts site-specific barrier and RNA-remodeling effects. Our in vitro data illustrate, for example, how an apparent RNA-processing activity could result from PPR-induced RNA remodeling: When PPR10 binds to the atpH 5′ UTR, it shifts the RNA structure toward conformers with increased susceptibility to ribonucleases at specific sites ( Fig. 4 ). Thus, the binding of a PPR protein could induce RNA processing by exposing RNA segments that are substrates for housekeeping endonucleases such as ribonuclease E. PPR tracts could enhance group II intron splicing by sequestering an RNA segment that otherwise would favor nonproductive intron conformers, and they could repress translation by binding a site that overlaps a ribosome-binding region. The unusual biochemical attributes of long PPR tracts are likely to have contributed to their remarkable evolutionary exploitation for the modulation of organellar RNA metabolism.
