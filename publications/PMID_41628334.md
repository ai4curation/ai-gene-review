Tyrosine nitration alters the structure, function, and cellular localization of
proteins and is implicated in the pathology of multiple diseases [G.
Ferrer-Sueta et al., Chem. Rev. 118, 1338-1408 (2018), H. Ischiropoulos, Arch.
Biochem. Biophys. 356, 1-11 (1998), I. Griswold-Prenner et al., J. Biol. Chem.
299, 105038-10554 (2023)]. Although protein nitration is assumed to proceed via
nonspecific chemical mechanisms, it is highly selective, suggesting the
possibility of enzymatic catalysis. Here, we showed that glyoxalase
domain-containing protein 4 (GLOD4), a previously uncharacterized protein, is an
enzyme that catalyzes selective protein nitration. A primary in vivo target for
GLOD4-mediated nitration is alpha-synuclein (α-syn), which is central to the
pathogenesis of Parkinson's disease (PD) and related disorders. We document
tyrosine nitration of α-syn by GLOD4 in vitro, in cells, and in a murine model
of synuclein pathology. The data identified a function of GLOD4 and other
structurally related proteins that catalyze the peroxynitrite-mediated selective
protein tyrosine nitration. This enzymatic catalysis of nitration may unearth
pathophysiological mechanisms and potential interventions in diseases such as
PD, cancer, and autoimmunity.

## Full Text

Tyrosine nitration is a protein posttranslational modification (PTM), and like tyrosine phosphorylation and sulfation, alters protein structure, function, and localization, with important consequences on cellular function (1–3). Nitration of specific proteins has been implicated in multiple disorders, such as cancer, autoimmunity, and neurodegeneration (1–3). Recently, we reviewed and compiled a list of more than 908 human proteins modified in vivo by tyrosine nitration, suggesting that nitration might affect a broad range of biological processes (3). Protein tyrosine nitration has been considered almost exclusively a chemical reaction under oxidative/nitrative stress conditions (1). However, nitration of tyrosine residues is selective, as only selective proteins and specific tyrosine residues within a protein are nitrated (4). Previously, the biological selectivity of protein nitration was attributed to a combination of nonenzymatic factors, including the local environment of specific tyrosine residues and the proximity of the protein to nitric oxide synthases and/or peroxidases that catalyze the generation of reactive nitrogen species (1–7). Other potential selective mechanisms included the autonitration of heme-containing proteins (8–11) and catalysis by metal-containing proteins such as SOD1 and SOD2 (12–17). However, none of these mechanisms has been unequivocally documented in vivo. Catalysis of tyrosine nitration by myeloperoxidase (MPO) and eosinophil peroxidase (EPO) was documented utilizing nitrite plus hydrogen peroxide to generate nitrating species (18–22). Studies in MPO null mice confirmed that this enzyme drives protein tyrosine nitration under some but not all inflammatory conditions in vivo (21, 22). The intriguing possibility for broader enzymatic catalysis of tyrosine nitration and the identification of enzymes and targets remains elusive.

Tyrosine nitrated α-syn is a major constituent of Lewy bodies and cytoplasmic inclusions in patients with PD and other neurodegenerative diseases (23, 24). Mutations in α-syn are responsible for familial PD (25), and aggregates of α-syn and other proteins known as Lewy bodies are found in the cytoplasm of susceptible neurons in virtually all PD patients and patients with other neurodegenerative disorders, such as dementia with Lewy body disease (26–32). Tyrosine nitration impacts the interaction of α-syn with lipids (33), aggregation (33, 34), the spreading of α-syn pathology (35–37), neuroinflammation (38), and promotes neuronal dysfunction and injury (39). Moreover, injection of nitrated α-syn, but not nonmodified α-syn, into the rat ventral mesencephalon results in the death of dopaminergic neurons and a decline in motor function (40). Collectively, the published data indicate that α-syn nitration may contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of α-syn-related neurodegenerative disorders. However, the possibility that the selective nitration of α-syn could be derived enzymatically has not been explored.

Therefore, we sought to determine whether specific enzymes could be responsible for selectively nitrating tyrosine residues in α-synuclein. Starting from an unbiased screen, our results revealed that the nitration of α-syn in vitro, in cells, and in vivo is catalyzed by GLOD4, a protein with unknown function (41). We have also identified GLOD4-structurally related proteins (41, 42), which mediate the selective nitration of protein substrates. Thus, we propose that GLOD4 is a putative member of a discovered enzyme family, which we tentatively refer to as nitrases based on their ability to catalyze self- or targeted protein tyrosine nitration. Understanding the role of GLOD4 and other nitrases in biology and diseases may provide insights for potential interventions.

To determine whether any other VOC superfamily members exhibit nitration activity, we relied on the additional finding that GLOD4 has self-directed or autonitration activity; that is, incubation of GLOD4 with peroxynitrite results in nitration of a tyrosine residue on GLOD4 (Fig. 7A). The Structural Properties of GLOD4 Suggest an Enzymatic Mechanism of Action.Since protein function can be inferred from structure, to aid in understanding the mechanism of action of GLOD4 for nitration of α-syn, we examined its crystal structure in the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) (3ZI1). The GLOD4 structure in the PDB did not contain metal in the active site. However, inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis revealed that Zn2+ was present in purified GLOD4 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). Based on this observation, we analyzed the structure of GLOD4 crystallized in the presence of Zn2+ (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The resolved structure was found to feature a Zn2+-containing active site like that of other members of the metallo-β-lactamase superfamily. Zn2+ is bound by E70 and H8 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Mutations at either site (H8A and E70A) abolished enzymatic nitration activity (Fig. 3D). In addition, one cysteine was found to protrude into the putative active site (C254, SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Mutation of this site (C254A) also abolished enzyme activity (Fig. 3D). Based on these observations, we propose that the zinc-bound form of the enzyme interacts with the relatively more stable cis-peroxynitrite conformation in which the negative charge is localized over the entire molecule. The cis-peroxynitrite molecule can be further stabilized by forming hydrogen bonds with two or three waters, activating peroxynitrite and facilitating the transfer of the nitro group to the tyrosine residues of the protein substrate.
