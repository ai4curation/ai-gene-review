## Full Text

Abstract

CD93 is a cell-surface glycoprotein that has been shown to influence defence collagen-enhanced Fc-receptor or CR1-mediated phagocytosis of suboptimally opsonized targets in vitro , and CD93-deficient mice are defective in the clearance of apoptotic cells in vivo . To investigate the mechanism of CD93 modulation of phagocytic activity, GST fusion proteins containing the 47 amino acid intracellular domain (GST-Cyto), or various mutants of the intracellular domain of CD93, were constructed and used to identify intracellular CD93-binding molecules. The intracellular protein moesin, well characterized for its role in linking transmembrane proteins to the cytoskeleton and in cytoskeletal remodelling, bound to GST-Cyto when either cell lysates or recombinant moesin were used as a source of interacting molecules. An association of moesin with CD93 within intact cells was confirmed by co-capping moesin with CD93 in human monocytes. The moesin-binding site on CD93 mapped to the first four positively charged amino acids in the juxtamembrane region of the CD93 cytoplasmic tail. Interestingly, deletion of the last 11 amino acids from the C terminus of CD93 (GST-Cyto-C11) dramatically increased moesin binding to the cytoplasmic tail of CD93 in the cell lysate assay, but not when the binding of purified recombinant moesin was assessed. Furthermore, moesin binding to CD93 was enhanced by the addition of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP 2 ). Taken together, these data suggest that the interaction of moesin with the CD93 cytoplasmic domain is modulated by binding of other intracellular molecules to the C11 region and implies that a PIP 2 signalling pathway is involved in CD93 function.

Keywords: C1qR P , CD93, GIPC, ERM, phagocytosis

Introduction

CD93 is a transmembrane glycoprotein that is expressed in cells of the myeloid lineage, endothelial cells, haematopoietic stem cells, natural killer cells and platelets. 1 – 4 CD93 was originally designated as C1qR P , a receptor or receptor component for defence collagens (a family of proteins that includes C1q, MBL and SPA), because monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) to this molecule were found to inhibit the enhancement of monocyte phagocytic activity induced by C1q and other defence collagens. 5 – 8 In addition, the adherence of cells to the anti-C1qR P mAb, R3, induced an enhanced phagocytic activity. 7 More recently, expression cloning of CD93 demonstrated that this protein was identical to C1qR P . 9 Subsequent studies have demonstrated that mouse macrophages lacking CD93 retain the ability to respond to C1q, with an enhancement of phagocytosis in vitro , although the in vivo clearance of apoptotic cells in these animals is compromised. 10 Thus, the function of CD93 remains unresolved, the mechanism by which CD93 influences these activities has not been defined, and the basis for the effect of the anti-CD93 immunoglobulin on enhancement of phagocytosis by defence collagen remains to be clarified.

Structural domain analysis of CD93 revealed a carbohydrate-recognition domain, five epidermal growth factor-like domains, a mucin domain, a single transmembrane domain and a 47-amino acid intracellular domain. 3 A possible function of CD93 as an adhesion molecule has been proposed based on its structural features. 11 The mouse homologue of CD93, AA4, which is 67% identical to human CD93, 11 , 12 is expressed by haematopoietic stem cells and endothelial cells in the developing embryo, consistent with a role for CD93/AA4 during stem cell homing and angiogenesis, processes that require intercellular adhesion. As an approach to clarify the roles of CD93 in biological processes, the study described here was designed to investigate whether the 47-amino acid intracellular domain of CD93 binds signalling or adaptor molecules.

An antibody to the C-terminal 11 amino acids (C11) of the intracellular domain of CD93 was previously shown to inhibit C1q-mediated enhanced phagocytosis, 12 , 13 suggesting a critical role for this domain in CD93 function. However, the highly positively charged juxtamembrane (JX) region of CD93, containing 15 amino acids, may also be functionally relevant as it is similar to that found in other adhesion molecules, such as CD43, CD44, intercellular adhesion molecules (ICAMs) and selectins. This JX region of these adhesion molecules has been shown to be critical for the binding of members of the ezrin/radixin/moesin (ERM) family. 14 – 16 The N-terminal region of moesin or other members of the ERM family directly or indirectly interacts with this JX region of adhesion molecules, while its C-terminal region, when activated, interacts with cytoskeletal actin. 17 In the unactivated state, an intramolecular association between the N-terminal and C-terminal regions of ERMs mutually masks the interaction sites for their binding partners, leaving the molecule in a ‘dormant’ status. 18 However, the binding of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP 2 ) to the N terminus activates the molecule, and subsequent phosphorylation of the C terminus stabilizes the molecule in an ‘active’ state. 19 Importantly, the linkage of integral plasma membrane proteins to cytoskeletal actin by ERMs contributes to a redistribution of the actin cytoskeleton that has been shown to be essential for phagocytosis, migration and adhesion. 17 , 20 Here, we present the first data demonstrating that the cytoplasmic tail of CD93 interacts with moesin and confirm the association, by using confocal microscopy, with human monocytes. Furthermore, we identify a motif, containing four positively charged amino acids that are critical for this interaction, in the JX region of the CD93 cytoplasmic domain, and show that PIP 2 enhances this interaction. In addition, our results indicate that other intracellular molecule(s) may modulate this interaction through binding to the C11 of CD93.

Materials and methods

Reagents, antibodies and cell culture

All restriction enzymes were from Gibco-Invitrogen (San Diego, CA). Mouse monoclonal anti-moesin immunoglobulin G (IgG) (38/87) was generously provided by Dr H. Furthmayr (Stanford University, CA). Mouse monoclonal anti-moesin IgG and goat polyclonal anti-moesin IgG and anti-ezrin were purchased from BD Transduction Laboratories (San Diego, CA) and Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA), respectively. Anti-V5 epitope mouse mAb was from Invitrogen. Peroxidase-, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)- or Cy3-conjugated secondary antibodies [F(ab′) 2 fragments], were from Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories (West Grove, PA). The generation and characterization of R3 and R139, mAbs specific for CD93, have been described previously. 6 , 21 Anti-flag monoclonal M2 was from Sigma (St Louis, MO). HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's minimal essential medium (DMEM) (Gibco-Invitrogen) containing 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Hyclone, Logan, UT), 100 units/ml of penicillin G sodium/100 µg/ml of streptomycin sulphate (Gibco-Invitrogen), 1× non-essential amino acids (Gibco-Invitrogen) and 10 m m Hepes, pH 7·4. All other reagents were purchased from Sigma unless stated otherwise.

Construction of expression vectors encoding GST—CD93 fusion proteins

The coding region of CD93 was cloned into pcDNA 3·1(+) following restriction with Eco RV and Not I [pcDNA 3·1(+)-CD93], as described previously. 7 Coding sequences for the full-length and truncated cytoplasmic region of CD93 were cloned into the pGEX-4T vector to create GST fusion proteins containing the entire cytoplasmic region, or mutated cytoplasmic variants, as shown in Fig. 1 . Specifically, a fragment flanked by an Eco RI and a Not I site was cloned into the pGEX-4T vector (pGEX-4T-Cyto) encoding a GST fusion protein with the entire cytoplasmic region (GST-Cyto). To delete the last 11 amino acids from the C terminus of the cytoplasmic region (C11), polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was performed using pGEX-4T-Cyto as a template and primers 5-GGCGACCATCCTCCAAAATC-3 and 5-TCACTCCATGGCCCTGCTCTCA-3. The PCR product was subcloned into pGEM-T (Promega, Madison, WI), removed by digestion with Eco RI and Not I, and inserted into pGEX-4T to encode the GST fusion protein (GST-Cyto-C11). To delete either the first five or 15 amino acids from the N-terminal JX domain, PCR primers 5-GCGAAGAGGGAGGAGAAGAAGGA-3 or 5-CCCCAGAATGCGGCAGACAGTT-3, respectively, and 5-CTAGAAGGCACAGTCGAGGCTG-3 were used to amplify the desired fragment from pcDNA 3·1-CD93. After digestion with Not I, the relevant fragments were cloned into pcDNA 3·1(+) using the Eco RV and Not I restriction sites. The fragment between Eco RI and Not I was inserted into pGEX-4T in-frame with GST (designated GST-Cyto-5Aa or GST-Cyto-JX). To further delete C11 from Cyto-5Aa, pGEX-4T-Cyto-5Aa was used as a template, and 5-GGCGACCATCCTCCAAAATC-3 and 5-TCACTCCATGGCCCTGCTCTCA-3 as primers, for PCR amplification. The PCR product was subcloned into pGEM-T, recovered by digestion with Eco RI and Not I, and inserted into pGEX-4T (GST-Cyto-5Aa-C11). In order to substitute four positively charged amino acids (from positions 2 to 5) in the JX region with four alanines, a PCR product was amplified using 5-TATGCGGCCGCGGCAGCGAAGAGGGAGGAGAAG-3 and 5-CTAGAAGGCACAGTCGAGGCTG-3 as primers from pcDNA 3·1(+)-CD93-C11, digested by Not I, and the fragment cloned into pcDNA 3·1(+) using the Eco RV and Not I restriction sites. The fragment cleaved out with Eco RI and Not I was inserted into pGEX-4T (GST-Cyto-4Ala-C11). All constructs were confirmed to be in-frame by sequencing (UCI DNA sequencing facility), and fusion proteins of the correct size were detected by Western blot using rabbit anti-GST polyclonal antibody (Sigma) (data not shown). The GST-Cyto-4Ala was constructed as described previously. 22

Construction, expression and purification of full-length moesin and of N-half moesin

The coding sequences for full-length moesin and for the N-terminal 312 amino acids of moesin (a gift from Dr H. Furthmayr, Stanford University, Palo Alto, CA), were cloned into the Gateway pENTR11 Vector (Invitrogen) via the Xmn I and Xho I restriction sites. The constructs were verified to be in-frame by sequence analysis. The inserted sequences were transferred into the His-tag containing pET-DEST42 (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer's instructions, to generate pET-DEST42-moesin-full and pET-DEST42-moesin-N-half. His-tagged full-length and N-half recombinant proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21-CodonPlus (DE3)-RIL-competent cells (Stratagene, San Diego, CA), in the presence of 1 m m isopropyl thio-β- d -galactoside (IPTG) at room temperature, and purified using the nickel column purification method following the manufacturer's instructions (Invitrogen). The size of the recombinant proteins was verified via sodium dodecyl sulphate—polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS—PAGE) and Western blotting using antibodies to moesin and the inserted V5 epitope.

PIP 2 liposome preparation

PIP 2 , dissolved in organic solvent (chloroform/methanol, 1:1), was aliquoted and dried under nitrogen stream in glass tubes. The dried PIP 2 was stored at −20° until resuspended at a concentration of 2 mg/ml in 20 m m Hepes, pH 7·4, containing 0·2 m m EGTA, 23 and further sonicated in an ice waterbath sonicator for 30 min before being added to binding assay samples.

GST pull-down assay

GST fusion proteins were produced in E. coli BL21 cells (Promega) and purified by binding to glutathione agarose beads, as described by the manufacturer. Briefly, the expression of GST fusion protein was induced for 3 hr in 50-ml bacterial cultures containing 1 m m IPTG. Bacterial pellets from 7 ml of culture were stored at −70° until used. GST fusion protein from 7 ml of culture was purified on 100 µl glutathione beads, and 60–100 µl of beads containing bound GST fusion protein were mixed with 1–10 mg of protein derived from lysates of HEK293T cells extracted in octyl glucoside lysis buffer [1% octyl glucoside (Calbiochem, La Jolla, CA), 10 m m triethanolamine, pH 7·4, 1 m m CaCl 2, 1 m m MgCl 2, 1 m m phenylmethylsulphonyl fluoride, 5 µg/ml of leupeptin, and 1 µg/ml of pepstatin]. In some experiments, HEK 293T cells were transiently transfected with flag-tagged Gα interacting protein (GIAP) interacting protein, C-terminus (GIPC) using lipofectamine, according to the manufacturer's protocol. After incubation at 4° overnight (or for 2 hr) with gentle rocking, the beads were washed extensively with 1% octyl glucoside lysis buffer and subsequently incubated with 1·5 mg/ml of glutathione in 50 m m Tris buffer, pH 8·0, for 1 hr to elute bound proteins. Aliquots of the eluate were subjected to electrophoresis on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels, transferred to poly(vinylidene difluoride) membrane, and immunoblotted with monoclonal anti-moesin (BD Transduction Laboratories).

Alternatively, 60–100 µl of GST fusion protein, containing beads purified as described above, was added to 0·1–10 µg of recombinant moesin in 400 µl of buffer D (10 m m Hepes buffer, pH 7·5, 40 or 100 m m KCl, 1 m m MgCl 2 , 1 m m EGTA, 1 m m dithiothreitol and 2 µg/ml of leupeptin) or to the mixture of recombinant moesin and PIP 2 , following a 30-min preincubation of recombinant moesin and PIP 2 in 400 µl of buffer D (100 m m KCl) at 4°. After incubation for 2 hr, or overnight, at 4°, the beads were washed with buffer D and eluted with glutathione. The eluates were assayed by Western blot, as described above, by using monoclonal anti-moesin or anti-V5 epitope immunoglobulin. In all pull-down assays, the amount of moesin (or GIPC) bound to the GST fusion protein was quantified by normalizing to the amount of non-degraded GST-fusion protein in each sample.

Co-capping of CD93 and moesin by immunofluorescence confocal analysis

Sterilized circular coverslips were placed in the wells of a 12-well plate. Human peripheral blood monocytes were isolated by countercurrent elutriation, as previously described, 24 and resuspended in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 5% (v/v) FBS and cultured (0·5 × 10 6 cells/well) overnight at 37°. After washing with RPMI, the cells were incubated with 5 µg/ml anti-CD93 (R3 or R139), or isotype-specific control mouse immunoglobulin, in 0·5 ml of RPMI for 30 min at 4°. After washing, fluorescence-conjugated goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin (2 µg/ml) was added, and the cells were incubated for various periods of time at 37° to cap CD93. 25 Cells were washed at 4°, fixed for 15 min in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 3·7% formaldehyde, washed with PBS, and permeabilized for 10 min with PBS containing 0·1% Triton-X-100. Cells were incubated with blocking solution (PBS containing 2% bovine serum albumin and 2% normal serum matching the secondary antibody) and subsequently with anti-moesin (1 µg/ml of mAb 38/87 or 10 µg/ml of goat polyclonal antibody) in blocking solution for 1 hr, washed with PBS and incubated with CY3-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG, Fcγ fragment-specific or FITC—anti-goat IgG for 1 hr. After washing with PBS, coverslips were mounted with vectashield medium (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA), and Z serial section images (0·5 µm) were captured by confocal microscopy (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA).

Results

CD93 cytoplasmic domain binds moesin via a positively charged amino acid motif within the JX region

To investigate potential adaptor molecules that bind to the cytoplasmic tail of CD93 and that may be involved in CD93 function, and to identify their binding sites in the cytoplasmic region of CD93, GST fusion proteins with native or mutant CD93 cytoplasmic tail were generated ( Fig. 1 ) and bound to glutathione-sepharose beads. HEK293T cell lysates (as a source of interacting proteins) were added to GST fusion-protein-coated beads. Bound proteins were eluted and subjected to Western blotting. Given that the CD93 cytoplasmic domain contains a highly positively charged JX region, similarly to other molecules (e.g. CD44, CD43) that have been previously shown to bind to the intracellular adaptor molecule moesin, 14 anti-moesin immunoglobulin was used to probe the transferred proteins. As shown in Fig. 2(a) , moesin was eluted from the GST fusion protein containing the intact cytoplasmic tail of CD93 (GST—Cyto). No interaction of moesin was found with GST alone. Deletion of the highly charged 15-amino acid JX region (GST-Cyto-JX) eliminated the binding of moesin ( Fig. 2a ). Therefore, moesin binding to CD93 requires a highly charged JX region.

Figure 2.

It has been indicated previously that three contiguous positively charged amino acids are critical for the binding of moesin to the cytoplasmic tail of CD44. 14 Deletion of amino acids 1–5 (YRKRR) from the N terminus of the CD93 JX cytoplasmic domain (GST-Cyto-5Aa, Fig. 1a ) resulted in the elimination of moesin binding ( Fig. 2a ). Thus, similarly to CD44, positively charged residues in the JX domain of CD93 are involved in binding moesin.

The C-terminal 11 amino acids of CD93 (C11) modulate moesin binding

An antibody directed against the 11 C-terminal amino acids of CD93 was previously shown to block the enhancement of phagocytic activity of both human and mouse phagocytic cells, 12 , 13 suggesting that this region of the cytoplasmic domain may be involved in the regulation of membrane movement and/or cytoskeletal rearrangement. Therefore, a fusion protein deleted of these C-terminal 11 amino acids was constructed (GST-Cyto-C11) and assayed for the binding of moesin from cytoplasmic extracts. Interestingly, deletion of the C11 region strikingly enhanced the interaction between CD93 and moesin ( Fig. 2 ). While the absolute fold increase in moesin binding to GST-Cyto-C11 when normalized to the amount of intact fusion protein co-eluted from the beads varied, to some extent, in multiple experiments ( n = 4), the increased binding was reproducible and significant ( Fig. 2b ).

To determine whether the enhanced binding to Cyto-C11 reflected the creation of a new interaction site or merely increased access to the JX domain, a GST fusion protein — GST-Cyto-5Aa-C11 (in which C11 was deleted from the construct lacking the first five amino acids of the JX domain) — was tested for moesin binding. While robust binding was detected in GST-Cyto-C11, the binding of moesin was completely eliminated by deleting the highly charged five-amino acid segment of the JX domain ( Fig. 2a ). Binding was not restored by replacing the four positively charged amino acids (RKRR) with four alanine residues (GST-Cyto-4Ala-C11), demonstrating that the loss of moesin binding was not simply the result of steric hindrance owing to proximity to the GST protein ( Fig. 2a ). Taken together, the data indicate that the positively charged amino acid motif (consisting of residues 2–5) in the JX region of CD93 is essential for moesin association with CD93, and that the presence of the C11 amino acids modulate that binding. Results were reproduced in three independent experiments.

Experiments using lysates from THP-1 cells, a human monocytic cell line, resulted in similar identification of moesin as a CD93-binding protein (data not shown), demonstrating that these binding characteristics were not limited to one cell type. In addition, ezrin, another member of ERM family, was also identified as being bound to the cytoplasmic tail of CD93 in THP-1 cell lysates (data not shown). Similarly to moesin, the interaction with ezrin was lost if the JX region of CD93 was deleted, while the binding was enhanced by the deletion of C11 (data not shown). Thus, both moesin and ezrin in cell lysates bind to the JX domain of CD93, and the presence of C11 interferes with that binding to CD93.

CD93 co-caps with moesin in human monocytes

To verify that moesin interacted with CD93 in intact cells, immunofluorescence microscopy was used to determine whether CD93 and moesin were co-localized in live cells. As it has been shown that CD93 is highly expressed in human monocytes, 6 , 26 monocytes were adhered to glass slides and incubated with R3, a monoclonal anti-human CD93 IgM. After fixation and permeabilization, the cells were incubated with monoclonal anti-moesin (38/87) followed by Cy3-anti-mouse IgG. As shown in Fig. 3 , as expected CD93 was found to be evenly distributed on the monocyte cell surface, as assessed by confocal microscopy. Moesin was also evenly distributed at or near the cell membrane. Merging of the two images demonstrated co-localization of these molecules (yellow, Fig. 3 , top, right). Incubation of cells with anti-CD93 (R3) followed by anti-mouse IgM at 37° for 30 min resulted in the capping of CD93 ( Fig. 3 , centre and bottom). Under these conditions, strong moesin staining was also concentrated in a cap-like structure. The co-localization of CD93 and moesin was obvious when the two images were merged ( Fig. 3 , yellow, middle and lower right). Similar co-localization/co-capping of these molecules was observed using another set of antibodies, R139 (mouse monoclonal anti-human CD93 IgG), and a goat polyclonal anti-moesin IgG (data not shown). Furthermore, moesin was also co-capped with CD93 in similar experiments investigating endothelial cells (data not shown). These data indicate that CD93 and moesin can associate in vivo .

The C11 amino acids do not directly inhibit moesin interaction with intact CD93

As the interaction between moesin and CD93, demonstrated in vitro ( Fig. 2 ), was confirmed in phagocytic cells ( Fig. 3 ), we wanted to characterize the biochemical regulation of this interaction and, specifically, the function of C11 ( Fig. 2 ). There are at least two possible mechanisms for the modulation of moesin/ezrin binding to CD93 via the C11 region. First, C11 may directly provide steric hindrance by folding either to mask the ERM-binding site in the JX region of CD93 or to limit access to the site. Alternatively, other intracellular molecules may bind to C11, sterically blocking the access of moesin/ezrin to its binding site on CD93.

To determine whether C11 could directly inhibit the interaction with moesin, both full-length and N-half recombinant moesin protein were expressed and assessed for direct binding to GST-CD93 cytoplasmic tail fusion proteins. As shown in Fig. 4(a) , the binding of full-length recombinant moesin directly to GST-Cyto was detected under low-ionic-strength conditions (40 m m KCl) in a dose-dependent manner. Similarly to the results using cell extracts as the source of moesin ( Fig. 2a ), purified recombinant moesin bound to GST-Cyto, and deletions in the JX region of CD93, eliminated that binding ( Fig. 4b ), further supporting the hypothesis that positively charged amino acids are critical for moesin binding to CD93. However, in contrast to the results using cell lysates, the deletion of C11 did not result in a significant increase in the binding of purified moesin to the cytoplasmic tail of CD93 ( Fig. 2c and 4b ). Similar results were obtained when the binding incubations were performed overnight or for 2 hr (data not shown). When N-half recombinant moesin (lacking the C-terminal domain that masks the self-interaction site) was used in the GST fusion protein-binding assay, while the specificities of binding were the same as for the full-length protein, binding was clearly detectable at lower concentrations (0·5–1 µg for N-half moesin versus 5 µg for full-length moesin) and higher ionic strength (100 m m KCl for N-half moesin versus 40 m m KCl for full-length moesin) than that of the intact molecule ( Fig. 4d–4f ). The results demonstrate that C11 does not directly interfere with the interaction between moesin and CD93, and suggests that other intracellular molecules are involved in the regulation of this interaction via the C11 domain.

Figure 4.

Previous studies have shown that GIPC, a PDZ domain-containing protein, interacts with the CD93 cytoplasmic tail and that the interaction requires a class I PDZ-binding domain found within C11. 22 In contrast to the enhanced binding of moesin to GST-Cyto-C11 ( Fig. 2a, 2b ), under the same conditions GIPC interacted preferentially with GST-Cyto and the interaction was not detectable in the absence of C11 ( Fig. 2c ). These data support a model in which other intracellular molecules bind to C11 and inhibit moesin binding.

PIP 2 regulates the interaction between moesin and CD93

The interaction between recombinant moesin (re-moesin) and CD93 characterized in the above experiments were conducted under low-ionic-strength conditions because the interaction between full moesin and CD93 was not detected under high-ionic-strength conditions (100 m m KCl), characteristic of native but ‘dormant’ moesin. However, the N-half fragment of recombinant moesin (lacking the intramolecular moesin interaction and thus with the N-terminal interaction site unmasked) bound CD93 under these more physiological ionic-strength conditions ( Fig. 4d–4f ). Phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP 2 ) has been observed to facilitate moesin binding to other adhesion molecules under physiological ionic strength via its binding to the N terminus of ERMs, thereby interrupting the intramolecular interaction between the N- and C-terminal domains and unmasking a binding site. 27 Therefore, 100 µg/ml of PIP 2 was incubated with full-length moesin in buffer D (100 m m KCl) for 30 min at 4° followed by the addition of GST-Cyto fusion protein-attached beads. As shown in Fig. 5(a) , an interaction between full-length moesin and CD93 was detected under a higher ionic-strength conditions (100 m m KCl) in the presence of 100 µg/ml of PIP 2 . Similar binding was detected with 50 µg/ml of PIP 2 (data not shown). Thus, PIP 2 facilitates the interaction of moesin and the cytoplasmic domain of CD93. Moesin binding to GST-Cyto-4Ala was not detected in the presence of PIP 2 ( Fig. 5b, 5c ), demonstrating that PIP 2 did not create a new CD93 interaction site, but rather maintained the requirement for the positively charged amino acid motif in the JX domain.

Discussion

The data presented here identifies moesin as a candidate adaptor molecule for CD93, a cell-surface molecule prominent on monocytes, microglia, neutrophils, endothelial cells and stem cells. The CD93 cytoplasmic tail directly associates with these ERM family members, and a motif of four positively charged amino acids in the JX region of CD93 has been identified as required for this binding. The demonstration of co-localization and, more importantly, co-capping of CD93 and moesin in human monocytes and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) provides evidence that the in vitro interaction between CD93 and moesin does occur in vivo . In whole-cell lysates, this interaction between moesin and CD93 is modulated by the cytoplasmic C-terminal 11 amino acids of CD93, implicating binding of additional intracellular proteins. Furthermore, PIP 2 increases the binding of moesin to CD93, implicating a PIP 2 signalling pathway involved in CD93 function, similar to other ERM—membrane protein interactions. Based on these observations, we propose that the CD93 cytoplasmic tail, via its highly charged JX domain and C11, co-ordinates signal transduction events involved in cytoskeletal rearrangement, adhesion and/or phagocytosis ( Fig. 6 ).

Figure 6.

Moesin, similarly to other ERM family members, has been suggested to be a cross-linker between plasma membrane proteins and actin filaments. 17 Phosphorylation of a threonine residue in the moesin C-terminal domain, 28 or PIP 2 binding to its N-terminal domain, disrupt an intramolecular interaction resulting in the generation of, and/or maintenance of, its active form. 17 , 29 It has been reported that the N-terminal half of moesin or of other ERMs interacts with adhesive molecules such as CD44, CD43, ICAM-1, -2, -3, and l -selectin, 16 , 17 and that the highly positively charged JX region in these transmembrane proteins is a common ERM-binding site. While, in vitro , the interaction between ERMs and adhesive molecules is sensitive to ionic strength, PIP 2 significantly facilitates these interactions under physiological ionic strength. The similar interaction between moesin and CD93, observed in this study, suggests that CD93 shares some properties of this group of adhesion molecules. Experiments reported by Ikewaki and colleagues showed that an anti-CD93 mAb, mNI-11, 30 induces intercellular adhesion of a lipopolysaccharide-stimulated monocyte-like cell line, U937, promotes U937 cell adhesion to an endothelial cell layer (HUVECs) 31 and enhances the rapid spread of HUVECs in vitro , 32 all consistent with a role for CD93 as an adhesion molecule.

ERM family members link plasma membrane adhesion molecules and actin, thus contributing to reorganization of the cytoskeleton, which is essential in both processes of adhesion and phagocytosis. 33 , 34 Our previous observations, that anti-CD93 mAbs inhibited the enhancement of phagocytosis of suboptimally opsonized antibody- or complement-tagged particles when monocytes were treated with the anti-CD93 immunoglobulins in fluid phase, 6 , 21 suggested that CD93 influences phagocytosis. In addition, ligation of CD93 with the anti-CD93 mAb, R3, resulted in an enhancement of phagocytosis when human monocytes were plated on R3 immobilized to a surface. 7 The identification of CD93 interacting with moesin is consistent with this involvement of CD93 in phagocytic events that require redistribution of cytoskeletal components. However, ablation of the CD93 gene in mice had no effect on the ability of phagocytes to respond to defence collagens with an enhancement of phagocytic activity. This lack of functional deficit may be explained by molecular redundancy and/or a requirement for appropriate experimental challenge. Indeed, in CD93 knockout mice, the in vivo uptake of apoptotic cells was adversely affected. That is, rapid phagocytosis of some particles may be modulated by CD93. PIP 2 has been found to be accumulated in the phagocytic sites. 35 Thus, PIP 2 , shown here to facilitate an interaction between moesin and CD93, may trigger this association under appropriate/selective conditions.

While the physiological role of CD93 has yet to be fully defined, the results described here demonstrate that the C-terminal 11 amino acids of CD93 can participate in the regulation of CD93 binding to moesin, and suggest that other intracellular molecules are involved in that regulation. This modulation by C11 is consistent with previous data that an anti-C11 immunoglobulin inhibits CD93-mediated enhancement of phagocytosis. 13 Recent studies in this laboratory have identified (by using both yeast two-hybrid and GST pull-down assays) the PDZ domain, containing protein GIPC, as a CD93 cytoplasmic tail-binding protein. 22 The interaction between GIPC and CD93 requires a class I PDZ-binding domain, identified as the last three amino acids of C11, as well as the highly positively charged JX region of the CD93 cytoplasmic tail. Furthermore, we demonstrated here that GIPC and moesin have contrasting binding preferences to GST-Cyto and GST-Cyto-C11 under the same binding conditions. It remains to be seen whether GIPC is the intracellular component that modulates moesin binding to CD93.

Recent findings, in our laboratory, have demonstrated that CD93 is shed upon the activation of cells by specific inflammatory stimuli (S. S. Bohlson, R. Silva, and A. J. Tenner, manuscript submitted). This ectodomain release has been reported for l -selectin and CD44, as well as for tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and other cytokines. 36 , 37 Mutation analysis has recently indicated that the ERM motif of l -selectin plays a critical role in this shedding. 38 Thus, the ERM motif may also regulate this function for CD93, a hypothesis to be tested in future studies.

In summary, the observed interaction of CD93 with moesin/ezrin is consistent with a role for CD93 in either adhesion or phagocytosis, or both, as redistribution of the cytoskeleton is required for both processes and ERM molecules have been associated with both processes. 20 , 39 The C11 region of CD93 may be a ‘switch’ that can be turned on or off, depending on selective conditions/associated molecules, contributing to the regulation of moesin binding within the microenvironment where phagocytosis/adhesion proceeds. Future studies should identify stimuli that trigger the association or dissociation of CD93 with moesin and help to clarify the consequences of this interaction in remodelling the cytoskeleton and in signalling. 40 The data presented here provides the basis for future investigations to uncover the mechanism by which CD93 influences adhesion and/or phagocytosis.
