Abstract

In bacterial flagellum biogenesis, secretion of the hook–filament junction proteins FlgK and FlgL and completion of the flagellum requires the FlgN chaperone. Similarly, the related FliT chaperone is necessary for the secretion of the filament cap protein FliD and binds the flagellar export gate protein FlhA and the flagellum ATPase FliI. FlgN and FliT require FliJ for effective substrate secretion. In Helicobacter pylori , neither FlgN, FliT, nor FliJ have been annotated. We demonstrate that the genome location of HP1120 is identical to that of flgN in other flagellated bacteria and that HP1120 is the homolog of Campylobacter jejuni FlgN. A modeled HP1120 structure contains three α‐helices and resembles the FliT chaperone, sharing a similar substrate‐binding pocket. Using pulldowns and thermophoresis, we show that both HP1120 and a HP1120Δ 126–144 deletion mutant bind to FlgK with nanomolar affinity, but not to the filament cap protein FliD, confirming that HP1120 is FlgN. Based on size‐exclusion chromatography and multi‐angle light scattering, H. pylori FlgN binds to FlgK with 1:1 stoichiometry. Overall structural similarities between FlgN and FliT suggest that substrate recognition on FlgN primarily involves an antiparallel coiled‐coil interface between the third helix of FlgN and the C‐terminal helix of the substrate. A FlgNΔ 126–144 N100A, Y103A, S111I triple mutant targeting this interface significantly impairs the binding of FlgK. Finally, we demonstrate that FlgNΔ 126–144 , like FliT, binds with sub‐micromolar affinity to the flagellum ATPase FliI or its N‐terminal domain. Hence FlgN and FliT likely couple delivery of low‐abundance export substrates to the flagellum ATPase FliI.

Keywords: chaperone, coiled‐coil, export apparatus, flagellum, thermophoresis

1. INTRODUCTION

Colonization of the human gastric mucosa by Helicobacter pylori is a known factor in several diseases including gastritis, duodenal ulcer, and gastric lymphoma (Cover & Blaser, 2009 ). Both colonization and persistence by H. pylori are attenuated in non‐motile mutants lacking a functional flagellum (Gu, 2017 ; Kavermann et al., 2003 ; O'Toole et al., 1994 , 2000 ; Ottemann & Lowenthal, 2002 ). However, some key flagellum biogenesis proteins have yet to be systematically annotated in the H. pylori genome including the hook–filament junction protein chaperone FlgN, the filament cap chaperone FliT, and the export apparatus component FliJ (Alm et al., 1999 ; Douillard et al., 2010 ; Tomb et al., 1997 ).

Bacterial flagellum biogenesis has been studied extensively in Salmonella enterica where the process proceeds first by assembly of the basal body, the MS‐C ring (rotor) complex, and export apparatus proteins in the cytosolic membrane, followed by sequential secretion of proteins making the rod (drive shaft), the hook (universal joint), the hook–filament junction, the filament cap and finally the filament subunits (reviewed in Aizawa, 1996 ; Chevance & Hughes, 2008 ; Halte & Erhardt, 2021 ; Minamino, 2013 ; Minamino, Kinoshita, Morimoto, & Namba, 2022 ; Minamino, Kinoshita, & Namba, 2022 ). Secretion of flagellum components utilizes a specialized Type III secretion system that is an integral part of the flagellum basal body (Abrusci et al., 2013 ; Halte & Erhardt, 2021 ; Kuhlen et al., 2018 , 2020 ). Closely associated with the export apparatus, and essential for efficient secretion of substrates, are the flagellum ATPase FliI, its binding partner FliH and FliJ (Fraser et al., 2003 ; Gonzalez‐Pedrajo et al., 2002 ; Imada et al., 2016 ; Lane et al., 2006 ; Minamino, Kinoshita, & Namba, 2022 ; Minamino & MacNab, 2000 ; Minamino & Namba, 2008 ; Rossi et al., 2023 ).

All secreted flagellum components share conserved α‐helices situated at their extreme N‐ and C‐termini, which form an antiparallel coiled‐coil structure, and facilitate subsequent assembly of 11 protofilaments to form a repeating helical array that makes up the walls of the central hollow core of assembled axial flagellum structures (Johnson et al., 2021 ; Matsunami et al., 2016 ; Saijo‐Hamano et al., 2019 ; Shibata et al., 2019 ; Yonekura et al., 2003 ). Conservation of these helices is evident in the primary sequence in addition to their secondary and tertiary structures. Substrates secreted later in flagellum assembly, including the hook–filament junction proteins (FlgK and FlgL), the filament cap (FliD), and the filament (FliC or FlaA,B in H. pylori ) all require chaperones for their secretion in S. enterica and other related systems (Al‐Otaibi et al., 2020 ; Altegoer et al., 2018 ; Evdokimov et al., 2003 ; Khanra et al., 2016 ; Kim et al., 2017 ; Kinoshita et al., 2016 ; Minamino et al., 2021 ; Xing et al., 2018 ). Deletion of the genes for these chaperones in S. enterica generally results in termination of the flagellum at the hook structure, composed of the FlgE protein. After hook completion, a small integral multiple of 11 subunits of the hook–filament junction proteins, FlgK and FlgL, form an 11‐mer helical array and sequentially displace the hook cap protein (FlgD) at the distal end of the hook structure (Bulieris et al., 2017 ; Chevance & Hughes, 2008 ). It has been established that each flagellum chaperone binds to the C‐terminal helix of its substrate(s) (Altegoer et al., 2018 ; Evdokimov et al., 2003 ; Fraser et al., 1999 ; Khanra et al., 2016 ; Kim et al., 2017 ; Xing et al., 2018 ). The most well‐studied chaperone–substrate complexes are FliS:FliC (Flagellin) and FliT:FliD (HAP2 or filament cap) where detailed interactions and structures of the complexes are available (Altegoer et al., 2018 ; Evdokimov et al., 2003 ; Khanra et al., 2016 ; Kim et al., 2017 ; Xing et al., 2018 ). In contrast, no structures for the hook–filament junction chaperone FlgN bound to either of its substrates has been elucidated; only the apo‐chaperone or the substrate structures alone have been determined (Bulieris et al., 2017 ; Kinoshita et al., 2016 ).

The flgN gene was first identified in S. enterica (Kutsukake et al., 1994 ). flgNΔ mutants are weakly motile and electron micrographs reveal flagella truncated at the hook. FlgN has subsequently been identified and characterized in other bacteria including Proteus mirabilis , Bacillus subtilis , and Campylobacter jejuni , and is required for flagellum formation and optimal motility (Barrero‐Tobon & Hendrixson, 2014 ; Cairns et al., 2014 ; Gygi et al., 1997 ). FlgN binds to the hook–filament junction proteins FlgK and FlgL (Aldridge et al., 2003 ; Bennett et al., 2001 ; Fraser et al., 1999 ). FlgN also prevents premature aggregation of FlgK and FlgL in the cytoplasm prior to secretion (Aldridge et al., 2003 ). In C. jejuni , FlgN has been annotated and flgN deletion mutants have been characterized and shown to be non‐motile and have a reduced ability to infect host organisms (Barrero‐Tobon & Hendrixson, 2014 ; de Vries et al., 2017 ).

The S. enterica flagellum export chaperones FlgN and FliT exhibit several similarities, including that they both contain a C‐terminal tyrosine residue required for interactions with the export apparatus gate protein FlhA for assisting with the secretion of substrates FlgK, FlgL, and FliD (Bange et al., 2010 ; Kinoshita et al., 2013 ; Minamino, Kinoshita, Hara, et al., 2012 ; Xing et al., 2018 ). Both FlgN and FliT also bind the export apparatus component FliJ (Bange et al., 2010 ; Evans et al., 2006 ; Imada et al., 2010 ; Kinoshita et al., 2013 , 2016 ; Minamino, Kinoshita, Hara, et al., 2012 ; Xing et al., 2018 ). Recently, it was demonstrated that FliJ in an unfolded state can simultaneously bind both FlgN and FliT via distinct internal sites (Rossi et al., 2023 ). Both FlgN and FliT are required for the secretion of low abundance substrates immediately prior to filament formation. FliJ has been identified in C. jejuni (Barrero‐Tobon & Hendrixson, 2014 ; Gao et al., 2014 ) and it too has been found to be essential for motility. A likely H. pylori FliJ candidate, HP0256, is the closest homolog of C. jejuni FliJ (21% identical over 142 residues) and has been partially characterized, but its role in flagellum formation and motility is unclear (Douillard et al., 2010 ).

FliT also binds strongly to the flagellum ATPase FliI when the FliT C‐terminal region containing a conserved tyrosine residue is deleted (Khanra et al., 2016 ; Minamino, Kinoshita, Imada, & Namba, 2012 ). FliT is also known to decrease FliI ATPase activity in vitro and has been shown to bind the FliI N‐terminal helix in its substrate‐binding pocket (Imada et al., 2010 ; Khanra et al., 2016 ; Minamino, Kinoshita, Imada, & Namba, 2012 ). C‐terminally truncated S. enterica FlgN mutants lacking residues 120–140 encompassing the conserved tyrosine residue inhibit flagellum biogenesis by not permitting the passage of any substrates through the export pore, presumably by forming a stalled export apparatus complex (Stafford et al., 2007 ; Thomas et al., 2004 ).

FlgN is currently the least understood flagellum export chaperone based on the lack of structural information regarding substrate binding and export apparatus component interactions, as compared to the chaperones FliS and FliT, which are well characterized. Based on functional similarities, FlgN appears more closely related to FliT than FliS (Khanra et al., 2016 ; Kim et al., 2017 ; Kinoshita et al., 2016 ; Xing et al., 2018 ). In this study, we present the bioinformatic annotation and the biochemical characterization of HP1120, a candidate for H. pylori FlgN. Our studies draw close parallels between FlgN and FliT, such as whether they have a shared mechanism of substrate binding and, like FliT, does FlgN interact with the flagellum ATPase FliI.

2. RESULTS

2.1. Bioinformatic analysis suggests HP1120 is the FlgN chaperone

FlgN was not identified in the initial genome sequence studies of H. pylori (Alm et al., 1999 ; Tomb et al., 1997 ). Although there is at least one FlgN and one FliJ annotation on Uniprot, these annotations are most certainly automated and are at the lowest of five indicated Uniprot annotation levels. It seems plausible that the annotation is the result of sequence similarity with C. jejuni FlgN and FliJ (see below). Nevertheless, other databases exhibit a paucity of FlgN annotations for the Campylobacterota. We conducted BLAST (Altschul et al., 1990 ) searches of the H. pyl ori genome using S. enterica FlgN and orthologues from other motile bacteria as the query sequences. The best candidate protein was HP1120, having a similar size to FlgN (144 residues) and modest sequence similarity to S. enterica FlgN (14.6% identical with few introduced gaps). However, the derived BLAST E‐values lacked statistical significance. Only C. jejuni FlgN (cj1465) has been annotated and genetically characterized in the Campylobacterota (formerly epsilon proteobacteria) (Barrero‐Tobon & Hendrixson, 2014 ). C. jejuni FlgN also aligns convincingly with S. enterica FlgN (41.4% identical and BLAST E‐value 1 × 10 −27 ). We conducted further BLAST searches of Campylobacterota protein sequences using C. jejuni FlgN as the query. Statistically significant hits included HP1120 (31.9% identical, E‐value 1 × 10 −7 ). We used ClustalW (Sievers & Higgins, 2018 ) to prepare a multiple sequence alignment of FlgN sequences including unannotated FlgN candidates found in the Campylobacterota. The alignment (Figure 1 ) is in agreement with alignments produced by BLAST and consistent with the hypothesis that HP1120 is FlgN. To further validate these findings, we used gene synteny to investigate the genome location of FlgN in a variety of motile bacteria including the Campylobacterota, using the program STRING (Snel et al., 2000 ). The results indicated a high probability for FlgN being directly upstream of FlgK, and directly downstream of FlgM, which is the location of HP1120 in the H. pylori genome (Figure S1 ).

2.2. Modeling of the HP1120/FlgN tertiary structure and substrate binding

To further validate HP1120 as FlgN, and to identify and characterize the likely substrate‐binding site for H. pylori FlgN, it was desirable to have a three‐dimensional model of the protein for docking substrates and designing mutations to potentially interfere with substrate binding. The crystallographically‐derived structures for S. enterica FlgN (Kinoshita et al., 2016 ) (PDB entry 5B3D ) and Bordetella pertussis apo‐FlgN (PDB entry 3OPC ) have been determined. However, both appear to be domain‐swapped dimers, which obscures the true tertiary structure and likely substrate‐binding pocket (Kinoshita et al., 2016 ) (Figure 2 ). In addition, Kinoshita and co‐authors demonstrated that FlgN adopts two distinct conformations in solution, one extended and one compact through the conformational change of a flexible loop between helices α1 and α2. When FlgN adopts the extended conformation, FlgN tends to form a homodimer as seen in the crystallographically‐determined structure. It is also noteworthy that the extended form does not explicitly contain a hydrophobic core, at least in an isolated monomer. As a result, the extended dimeric form of FlgN cannot bind to its cognate substrates FlgK and FlgL. In contrast, when FlgN adopts the compact conformation in solution, FlgN binds to FlgK and FlgL with nanomolar affinity (Kinoshita et al., 2016 ). The structure of Pseudomonas aeruginosa apo‐FlgN (PDB entry 2FUP ) does not appear to be domain swapped, adopting a similar tertiary fold to the S. enterica FliT chaperone, and contains a well‐defined hydrophobic core (Figure 3 ; Figures S2 and S3 ) (Khanra et al., 2016 ; Kim et al., 2017 ). All three FlgN proteins of known structure are of modest sequence similarity to HP1120/FlgN but align consistently in a multiple sequence alignment and their overall secondary structural features are well conserved (Figure 1 ; Figure S2 ). One interesting feature of the domain‐swapped dimer is that helix α3 of the second monomer sits in the presumed substrate‐binding pocket of the first monomer (Kinoshita et al., 2016 ) (Figure 2 ). This observation suggests the location of the substrate‐binding pocket of FlgN closely matches the FliT substrate‐binding pocket in the FliT:FliD chaperone–substrate complex from S. enterica (Khanra et al., 2016 ; Kim et al., 2017 ; Kinoshita et al., 2016 ). Details of construction of the un‐swapped monomer structures for S. enter ica and B. pertussis FlgN, as well as construction of the H. pylori FlgN model, may be found in Section 4 . We independently used Robetta to prepare a model of H. pylori FlgN (Kim et al., 2004 ). The structurally‐derived homology model and the Robetta model for H. pylori FlgN agree well. We then compared a recently released Alphafold‐generated model of HP1120 where it is designated H. pylori FlgN in the Alphafold model structure database ( O30718 ) (Jumper et al., 2021 ). The Alphafold H. pylori FlgN model is virtually identical to the Robetta FlgN model and our manually constructed FlgN model. Overall, the H. pylori FlgN model structure agrees well with the P. aeruginosa FlgN structure and each of the modeled un‐swapped monomers for S. enterica and B. pertussis FlgN (Figure S2 ; Tables S1 and S2 ) with RMSDs of 0.70, 0.81, and 0.96 Å for 56 equivalent residues respectively (224 equivalent backbone atoms or 56 residues shared between FlgN and FliT molecules). We also overlaid the S. enterica FliT:FliD structure (Kim et al., 2017 ) onto our HP1120/FlgN model using the same residue equivalences and the RMSD ranged from 1.12 to 1.45 Å (Figure 3a ; Figure S3 ; Tables S1 and S2 ). Hence FliT and FlgN have similar overall tertiary structures and appear to have a similar substrate binding cavity located between helices α2 and α3 as was previously noted (Kinoshita et al., 2016 ).

2.3.
FliT and FlgN employ an antiparallel coiled‐coil for substrate binding

With the FliT:FliD structure facilitating the approximate placement of FliD relative to the substrate‐binding pocket in the FlgN models, we made the assumption that FliT and FlgN employ a similar mode of substrate binding and then modeled the C‐terminal segment of FlgK bound to FlgN. The structure of the S. enterica FliT:FliD complex reveals that the chaperone–substrate interaction is primarily based upon an antiparallel coiled‐coil between helix α3 of the chaperone and the C‐terminal helical segment of FliD (Table S3 ) (Kim et al., 2017 ). We used sequence and structural similarity between the C‐terminal regions of C. jejuni FlgE (Matsunami et al., 2016 ) and FlgK, to model the α‐helical C‐terminal segment of H. pylori FlgK (Figure 3 ; Figure S4 ). We found that the FlgK C‐terminal helix is significantly shorter than the C‐terminal helix of S. enterica FliD. In addition, FliD exhibits only marginal sequence similarity to FlgK. However, given that the C‐terminal helices of FliD and FlgK (and FlgE) each have an evident coiled‐coil heptad motif, and structures are available for FliD and FlgE, it was straightforward to align the C‐terminal helices of FliD with FlgK or FlgE (Figure 3 ; Figure S4 ). The resultant FlgN:FlgK model, similar to the FliT:FliD structure, forms an extensive antiparallel coiled‐coil between helix α3 of the FlgN chaperone and the C‐terminal helix of the FlgK substrate (Figure 3 ; Table S3 ). In a similar manner, we used the structure of flagellin (Yonekura et al., 2003 ) as a template for aligning the C‐terminus of FlgL and modeled its C‐terminal helix (Table S3 ; Figure S4 ). It also contains an obvious heptad motif that we presumed pairs with the heptad of helix α3 of FlgN (Figure S4 ). Our modeling of the C‐terminal helices of FlgK and FlgL are in excellent agreement with the Alphafold models constructed for these proteins (Jumper et al., 2021 ). Hence, in addition to previously published work on FlgN and FliT, these structural similarities and the H. pylori FlgN modeling are consistent with the fact that FlgN and FliT, not only bind to FliJ, but also likely share a substrate‐recognition mechanism primarily involving their third α‐helix and the C‐terminal helix of the target substrate.

2.4. H. pylori FlgN interacts with specificity and high affinity with its substrate FlgK

To strengthen our assignment of HP1120 as FlgN, we investigated whether HP1120 binds to one of FlgN's known substrates, FlgK. Full length HP1120/FlgN protein was initially problematic to purify to homogeneity, apparently undergoing proteolysis during the process (Figure S5 and results not shown). Given that a S. enterica FlgN Δ120–140 C‐terminal deletion mutant resulted in the formation of an apparent stalled export complex but still bound to export substrates (Minamino, Kinoshita, Hara, et al., 2012 ; Stafford et al., 2007 ; Thomas et al., 2004 ), we decided to clone and purify a similar H. pylori FlgN Δ126–144 C‐terminal truncation mutant lacking the last 18 residues of the polypeptide that includes the conserved tyrosine residue required for some of its export functions. The truncated H. pylori His‐FlgN Δ126–144 protein was stable and was relatively straightforward to purify (Figures 4 and 5 ; Figure S6 ). After optimization of the His‐FlgN Δ126–144 purification, we found we could readily purify full‐length FlgN using a very similar protocol (Figure S5 ), so long as we worked quickly and minimized protein handling at room temperature. To assess the interaction between FlgK and FlgN Δ126–144 , we first carried out a GST‐pulldown experiment using an Escherichia coli lysate containing GST‐FlgK and mixed it with purified His‐FlgN Δ126–144 (Figure 4a ). The results indicated that His‐FlgN Δ126–144 was captured on the Glutathione‐Sepharose resin by GST‐FlgK. Using Western blots for anti‐GST and anti‐His, we then confirmed that the two major proteins present in the eluted fractions were GST‐FlgK and His‐FlgN Δ126–144 (results not shown). We confirmed this result using purified FlgK and His‐FlgN Δ126–144 and Ni‐NTA Sepharose in a separate pulldown experiment (Figure 4b ). Again, the pulldown indicated a robust interaction between the two proteins. In a separate control pulldown experiment, we then showed that His‐FlgN Δ126–144 does not interact with the filament cap protein FliD, providing support that the FlgN:FlgK interaction is specific (Figure S8 ).

Using size exclusion chromatography and multi‐angle light scattering (SEC‐MALS), we subsequently demonstrated that H. pylori FlgK elutes as a homogeneous monomer with a theoretical molecular weight of 68.5 kDa (V e = 13.7 mL) and a MALS‐derived molecular mass of 66.7 kDa (Figure 5 and results not shown). H. pylori apo His‐FlgN Δ126–144 , with a theoretical mw of 18.0 kDa, typically elutes as a monodisperse mixture of approximately 88% dimer and 12% monomer (V e = 15.4 mL; mass 31.8 kDa) based on the MALS analysis (Figure S6 ). It should be noted that all FlgN constructs have an N‐terminal tag of 23 residues that contains a hexa‐histidine tag and a Tobacco Etch Virus protease cleavage site. In addition, it is not altogether surprising that recombinant H. pylori His‐FlgN Δ126–144 elutes as mostly a dimer as both S. enterica and B. pertussis FlgN molecules have a strong propensity to form domain‐swapped dimers and tetramers in their respective crystal lattices and in solution (Kinoshita et al., 2016 ). Size exclusion chromatography of an equilibrated 1:1 mixture of FlgK and His‐FlgN Δ126–144 resulted in a single major peak with a calculated molecular weight of 83 kDa (V e = 13.6 mL), the theoretical molecular weight of a 1:1 FlgK:FlgN Δ126–144 complex is 86.5 kDa (Figure 5 ). Hence, we concluded that the peak observed is indeed a 1:1 complex of FlgK:FlgN Δ126–144 . Based on the calculated peak elution volume alone, we would not have great confidence in the stoichiometry of the complex, as a 1:2 mixture of FlgK: FlgN Δ126–144 would be expected to have a peak elution volume only 0.1–0.2 mL less than that of a 1:1 complex, but the highly consistent monodisperse peak and derived mass from SEC‐MALS strongly indicates the complex is 1:1. However, we also observed additional free FlgN Δ126–144 eluting in later fractions in the SDS‐PAGE analysis of the peak fractions (Figure 5 ). We ran this experiment several times, but there was always some free FlgN Δ126–144 eluting after the FlgK:FlgN Δ126–144 complex. Given that the FlgK:FlgN Δ126–144 mixture was incubated at 4°C for 1 h prior to gel‐filtration chromatography, it is possible the complex had not completely equilibrated due to the competing equilibria of FlgN dimerization and this may have resulted in some excess FlgN dimer eluting in later fractions. There is also the possibility of errors in mixing stoichiometry, as the molar extinction coefficients for FlgK and FlgN Δ126–144 differ by a factor of 10. To support these findings, we carried out a microscale thermophoresis (MST) titration (Wienken et al., 2010 ) of FlgN Δ126–144 , using fluorophore‐labeled FlgK. The results (Figure 6a ) confirm that the binding is very strong in the nanomolar range, with K d = 1.8 ± 2.4 nM.

In addition, we carried out a similar SEC‐MALS experiment with FlgK and purified full‐length His‐FlgN (Figure S5 ). Full‐length His‐FlgN (mw 20.1 kDa) elutes on gel‐filtration media with V e = 13.5 mL, suggesting a large oligomeric complex (Figure S5 ). A preliminary MALS analysis of the FlgN peak (not shown) indicated that the full‐length FlgN eluted as a hexamer (observed mass of 110 kDa vs. predicted mass of 120 kDa) as opposed to a dimer or tetramer. The FlgK:His‐FlgN mixture elutes as two separate peaks on gel‐filtration media exhibiting similar stoichiometry on SDS PAGE (Figure S5B ). The first peak elutes at V e = 11.9 mL, has a mass of 190.3 kDa and contains both FlgK and FlgN. The simplest explanation is that it is a dimer of 1:1 FlgK:FlgN complexes (predicted mass 177 kDa). The second peak in the elution profile has a mass of 82.4 kDa (calculated mass of 1:1 complex is 88.5 kDa) and corresponds to the 1:1 complex plus possibly some free His‐FlgN hexamer (Figure S5C ). We then used thermophoresis to measure the interaction between purified full length His‐FlgN and FlgK, and found that K d = 3.4 nM, very similar to the K d between FlgK and His‐FlgN Δ126–144 (Table 1 ) (Figure S5E ). However, it is worth noting that there appears to be very little excess full length FlgN in the gel filtration tailing peak as compared to the mixture of FlgK and FlgN Δ126–144 . This again supports our contention that FlgK forms a 1:1 complex with FlgN.

We then constructed a number of FlgN Δ126–144 point mutations (Figure 2 ; Table S4 ; Figure S6 ) that were designed to interfere with the binding of FlgK, based on our H. pylori FlgN model, known S. enterica FlgN mutations that interfere with FlgK binding, and the likely structural similarities of a FlgN:FlgK complex with the known FliT:FliD binary complex (Figure 3 and Figure S3 ). We analyzed three of these FlgN Δ126–144 mutants in detail (Figures 7 and 8 and Figure S7 ). The first, FlgN Δ126–144 M106A is a mutant on helix α3 that faces away from the likely FlgK‐binding pocket toward helix α1 (Figure 2 and Figure S7 ) and is meant to serve as a positive control as it should not interfere with FlgK binding. The SEC‐MALS analysis indicated that the complex of FlgK with the FlgN Δ126–144 M106A mutant eluted in a very similar manner to wild‐type FlgN Δ126–144 , but with significantly less free trailing FlgN Δ126–144 M106A observed in later eluting fractions. Based on this observation coupled with the fact that the deduced mass of the FlgK:FlgN Δ126–144 M106A peak was 79.0 kDa (calculated theoretical mass 86.4 kDa or 8% error), we concluded that a 1:1 mixture of the two proteins eluted on gel filtration. This confirmed that the FlgN Δ126–144 M106A mutant did not disrupt or alter the interaction with FlgK and behaves almost identically to FlgN Δ126–144 . Thermophoresis titration analysis confirmed strong binding between the FlgN Δ126–144 M106A mutant and FlgK with the K d = 2.7 ± 1.7 nM, very close to the K d for FlgN Δ126–144 and FlgK (Table 1 ; Figure 6 ). The only reasonable explanation we can think of for there being essentially no free trailing free FlgN Δ126–144 M106A in SDS PAGE fractions of the FlgK:FlgN Δ126–144 M106A gel filtration elution profile, is that there may be more rapid dimer‐monomer equilibration for FlgN Δ126–144 M106A in solution relative to FlgN Δ126–144 . However, these observations are unlikely to have a significant impact on our interpretation of the interaction with FlgK, as it is monomeric FlgN that most certainly interacts with FlgK (Figures 3 and 5 ) and this is what we see in the elution fractions based on the MALS analysis.

Next, we analyzed the FlgN Δ126–144 I48Y mutation. Residue I48 of H. pylori FlgN sits on the inner surface of the presumed FlgK‐binding pocket on helix α2 and is likely in contact with the substrate, based on similarities with the FliT:FliD structure. Hence, we reasoned that mutation to a bulky tyrosine residue should at least partly occlude the binding of FlgK. FlgN Δ126–144 I48Y elutes as what appears to be two coalesced peaks, the first at V e = 15.1 mL, has a mass of 34.2 kDa (predicted mass of a dimer 36.0 kDa) and corresponds to a molecular dimer. A second broad shoulder peak likely corresponds to a polydisperse mixture of monomer and dimer with a monotonically decreasing MALS mass, the average mass being about 25 kDa (Figure S6 ). The 1:1 mixture of FlgK and FlgN Δ126–144 I48Y elutes as a broader peak than wild‐type FlgN Δ126–144 bound to FlgK, but with an average peak mass of 73.1 kDa. Some free FlgK and almost half of the FlgN Δ126–144 I48Y is seen to be trailing in later fractions, as observed by SDS PAGE analysis (Figure 7 ). However, the mass drops monotonically across the presumed FlgK:FlgN Δ126–144 I48Y peak, starting at about 82 kDa and finishing at about 60 kDa, indicating that there is very likely a polydisperse mixture of FlgK:FlgN Δ126–144 I48Y and free FlgK. We estimate that about 35% of the eluted FlgK is in complex with FlgN Δ126–144 I48Y. Hence, we conclude that FlgN Δ126–144 I48Y binds less strongly to FlgK with only about 35% of the mixture eluting as a 1:1 complex, based on the SEC‐MALS and SDS‐PAGE results. This result was confirmed using thermophoresis titrations with the FlgN Δ126–144 I48Y mutant and FlgK, indicating weaker than the wild‐type complex binding by about a factor of 7 (K d = 13 ± 7 nM) (Table 1 ; Figure 6 ).

Finally, we constructed a H. pylori FlgN Δ126–144 triple mutant that recapitulates a S. enterica FlgN: N92A, N95A, I103A mutant reported to severely compromise S. enterica FlgN binding to FlgK (Kinoshita et al., 2016 ). All three mutated residues are found on helix α3 of FlgN. The corresponding H. pylori FlgN Δ126–144 N100A, Y103A, S111I triple mutant (Figure 3 ) was purified and analyzed by SEC‐MALS (Figure 8 ). The FlgN Δ126‐144 triple mutant appears to be folded similarly to FlgN Δ126–144 (circular dichroism analysis of helical content, not shown) and elutes as a single peak on a S200 Increase gel filtration column (V e = 15.4 mL), with a MALS‐derived mass of 28.5 kDa indicating a monodisperse mixture of approximately 60% dimer and 40% monomer (Figure S6 ). When mixed in 1:1 stoichiometry with FlgK, most of the mixture elutes as a single monodisperse peak at V e = 13.9 mL with a mass of 65.3 KDa that is indistinguishable from FlgK alone, plus mostly trailing FlgN Δ126–144 triple mutant (Figure 8 ). SDS‐PAGE analysis, when compared with the wild‐type protein mixture elution profile, indicates the eluted peak is largely an overlapping mixture of free FlgK and free FlgN Δ126–144 N100A, Y103A, S111I, as there is very little FlgN Δ126–144 in the first three fractions of the major peak and much more trailing FlgN Δ126–144 in subsequent fractions, relative to mixtures containing the wild‐type FlgN Δ126–144 . We interpret this observation as indicating a much weaker association between FlgK and FlgN Δ126‐144 N100A, Y103A, S111I (Figures 5 and 8 ). Again, a MST titration of FlgN Δ126–144 N100A, Y103A, S111I with FlgK indicated significantly weaker binding than for FlgN Δ126–144 with the K d of 62 ± 20 nM (Table 1 ) (Figure 6 ). Hence, we conclude that the H. pylori FlgN Δ126–144 triple mutant significantly impairs FlgK binding with essentially no intact FlgK:FlgN complex eluting on gel‐filtration media.

2.5. H. pylori FlgN binds to the flagellum export ATPase FliI

The N‐terminal helix of the flagellar ATPase FliI is known to bind strongly to the FliT chaperone in S. enterica and the FliI N‐helix: FliT tertiary structure has been determined, revealing that the FliI N‐terminus binds in the same FliT‐binding pocket as the FliD substrate (Khanra et al., 2016 ). Given that FlgN appears to bind its substrate FlgK in a highly similar binding site to that of FliT (Figure 3 ), we hypothesized that H. pylori FlgN or FlgN Δ126–144 may bind to the FliI ATPase in a similar manner. It has also been reported that S. enterica FlgN C‐terminal deletion mutants copurify with membrane‐associated fractions of FliI and FliH (Stafford et al., 2007 ; Thomas et al., 2004 ). We first used pulldown experiments to demonstrate a robust interaction between H. pylori FliI and His‐FlgN Δ126–144 (Figure 9 ). Both the N‐terminal domain FliI 2–92 and full length FliI 2–438 appear to bind strongly to FlgN Δ126–144 . We followed up on these observations by carrying out MST measurements using either fluorophore‐labeled FliI 2–92 or FliI 2–438 with FlgN Δ126–144 . Both FliI 2–92 and full‐length FliI show a very clear binding event when titrated with FlgN Δ126–144 (Figure 9 ). The derived K d values are in the sub‐micromolar range, K d = 3.3 ± 0.3 × 10 −7 M for FliI 2–92 , and K d = 7.0 ± 1.5 × 10 −7 M for FliI 2–438 (Table 1 ). Hence the data indicate that FlgN, like FliT, interacts strongly with the flagellum ATPase FliI.

3. DISCUSSION

The findings in this study establish that HP1120 is the hook–filament junction protein chaperone FlgN in H. pylori and that other members of the Campylobacterota consistently contain FlgN homologs. This is in contrast with the filament capping protein chaperone FliT, which does not appear to be present in the Campylobacterota (unpublished observations) (Alm et al., 1999 ; Tomb et al., 1997 ). There is also no FliT model from Helicobacter pylori or C. jejuni in the Alphafold database of predicted protein structures (Jumper et al., 2021 ), supporting our observation that the FliT chaperone is absent in the Campylobacterota. While this raises the possibility that perhaps FlgN serves as a chaperone for the filament cap protein FliD, preliminary invitro pulldown experiments indicate that FliD does not bind strongly to FlgN (Figure S8 ).

Our work has extended and elaborated the observation that FlgN and FliT chaperones have similar tertiary structures and substrate‐binding pockets (Khanra et al., 2016 ; Kim et al., 2017 ; Kinoshita et al., 2016 ), and we provided evidence that the predominant interaction between FlgN and FlgK is very likely an antiparallel coiled‐coil interface as was observed in the FliT:FliD structure (Kim et al., 2017 ). These same C‐terminal coiled‐coil residues on FliD also contribute in part to FliD oligomerization in the filament cap structure (Al‐Otaibi et al., 2020 ). Our proposed model of FlgN:FlgK/FlgL interactions utilizes a similar coiled‐coil interface and the importance of this interface is borne out by our FlgN mutagenesis experiments. Hence, we predict that the major interaction between FlgN and its substrates FlgK and FlgL will be built upon a similar antiparallel coiled‐coil interface between helix α3 of FlgN and the coiled‐coil oligomerization residues found in the C‐terminal helices of FlgK and FlgL. Having shown that H. pylori FlgN Δ126–144 binds with nanomolar affinity to FlgK, one of its two known substrates, we initially predicted that full‐length FlgN would bind with weaker affinity to FlgK relative to FlgN Δ126–144 due to entropic effects of the C‐terminal region of FlgN likely occupying its own substrate‐binding pocket as in the structure of P. aeruginosa FlgN. However, we observed that both proteins bound FlgK with similar affinity (K d = 3.4 × 10 −9 M for full length FlgN versus K d = 1.8 × 10 −9 M for FlgN Δ126–144 ) (Table 1 ). Unlike previous mutagenesis work carried out on S. enterica FlgN (Kinoshita et al., 2016 ), which showed the near‐complete inhibition of the substrate interaction with a triple mutant FlgN protein, we determined that in H. pylori , an analogous FlgN triple mutant still had a measurable but significantly decreased affinity toward the FlgK protein. However, SEC‐MALS analysis of the same FlgN mutant in complex with FlgK suggests mostly defective binding, as only very small amounts of the FlgN Δ126–144 triple mutant were observed to co‐elute with FlgK. This difference in the findings between SEC‐MALS and thermophoresis findings plus the published S. enterica studies could be due the techniques used as we used SEC‐MALS and thermophoresis, whereas the S. enterica FlgN study used sedimentation equilibrium studies, Surface Plasmon Resonance, and pulldowns (Kinoshita et al., 2016 ). Additionally, we used a C‐terminally‐truncated FlgN protein, whereas full length S. enterica FlgN was used in the S. enterica FlgN study. However, Minamino, Kinoshita, Hara, et al. ( 2012 ) also previously demonstrated that deletion of the S. enterica FlgN C‐terminus does not affect binding to FlgK or FlgL. Our results for full length H. pylori FlgN agree with these observations, indicating that deletion of the C‐terminus of FlgN has negligible effects on the magnitude of in‐vitro binding to the FlgK substrate.

Given the apparent overall similarity between chaperones FliT and FlgN, we went on to establish that FlgN, like FliT, binds to the flagellar ATPase FliI. The FliI N‐terminal amphipathic helix was previously shown to bind FliT in the same binding pocket that accommodates the C‐terminal coiled‐coil segment of FliD (Khanra et al., 2016 ). Published evidence has also suggested an interaction between FlgN and FliI, as FlgN C‐terminal deletion mutants co‐localize with FliI and FliH in S. enterica extracts (Thomas et al., 2004 ). In addition, other studies have established that affinity‐tagged FlgN is found in membrane fractions in a S. enterica mutant carrying a catalytically defective FliI ATPase (Stafford et al., 2007 ). Hence deleting the C‐terminus of FlgN may result in a stable FlgN–FliI complex that does not permit FliI ATPase to cycle through key intermediates in preparing flagellum axial substrates for export (Thomas et al., 2004 ). Results presented here using pulldowns and thermophoresis‐based K d measurements corroborate and expand on these previous findings and demonstrate that FliI directly binds to a similar H. pylori FlgN C‐terminal deletion mutant, FlgN Δ126–144 , with a K d = 3.0 × 10 −7 M for full‐length FliI, and K d = 7.0 × 10 −7 for FliI 2–92 . The binding is significantly weaker than binding observed for FlgK, but this is not surprising given that the N‐terminal helix of FliI is much shorter than the FlgK fragment that binds to FlgN. The fact that both FliT and FlgN bind to the FliI ATPase N‐terminus suggests a common mode of interaction and mechanism of action with the export apparatus for these two flagellum export chaperones. However, studies in S. enterica on FliI and FliT suggest that interactions are likely more complex with a secondary weaker chaperone‐interacting site at the C‐terminus of FliI (see below) (Minamino, Kinoshita, Imada, & Namba, 2012 ). It is noteworthy that both FliD and the hook–filament junction proteins FlgK and FlgL are produced at low levels and only a small number of copies are secreted during flagellum formation. Perhaps the FlgN and FliT chaperones bind the ATPase to facilitate subsequent secretion of their low abundance substrates. Substrate binding by the flagellin chaperone FliS is significantly different from FlgN or FliT, and it is not likely to directly interact with the ATPase FliI (Altegoer et al., 2018 ). Published evidence supports a role for FliS facilitating partial unfolding of FliC during the secretion process, most likely in combination with the cytoplasmic domain of FlhA (Furukawa et al., 2016 ; Minamino et al., 2021 ) Promotion of filament formation by FlgK or FlgL or FliD secretion likely requires FlgN or FliT participation in a similar manner to that elucidated for FliS promoting FliC secretion as both FlgN and FliT are also known to couple with substrates and FlhA at the export gate (Bange et al., 2010 ; Kinoshita et al., 2013 ; Minamino, Kinoshita, Hara, et al., 2012 ; Xing et al., 2018 ). In addition, both FlgN and FliT require FliI and FliH for effective targeting of their cognate substrates to FlhA during the export process (Inoue et al., 2018 ). An F459A mutant of FlhA largely rescues loss of filament‐type substrate secretion observed when FliI and FliH are absent, as well as increasing the rate of substrate switching from hook‐type to filament‐type substrates (Inoue et al., 2018 ). The fact that FliI binds directly to FlgN or FliT, though, suggests a distinct mechanistic difference relative to FliS in the steps these chaperones employ in delivering export substrates to FlhA.

The details of the role of FliI ATPase in coupling hook–filament junction or filament cap proteins to secretion are currently unclear, but it is known that FliI is localized below the export gate by the presence of FliH (Bai et al., 2014 ; Minamino et al., 2009 ). Hence it seems likely that the FliI ATPase may interact with or coordinate the interaction of multiple protein partners during the substrate export process, including FliH, FliJ, FlhA, export substrates, and chaperones. For instance, it is well known that FliI interacts with FliH to form a (FliH) 2 FliI complex in vitro that is inhibitory to flagellum export in vivo (Gonzalez‐Pedrajo et al., 2002 ; Imada et al., 2016 ; Lane et al., 2006 ; Minamino & MacNab, 2000 ). Given that the N‐terminal helix of FliI, which interacts with FliH, also interacts with FlgN and FliT and occupies the substrate binding sites on these chaperones (Khanra et al., 2016 ), it suggests consecutive binding and displacement events involving FliH and FlgN or FliT to the N‐terminal helix of FliI may occur during the export process. However, FliT is also known to have a secondary interaction site with the C‐terminal region of FliI, suggesting a more complex mode of interaction for these proteins (Minamino, Kinoshita, Imada, & Namba, 2012 ). In addition, it is known that FliH forms an asymmetrical homodimer. One monomer of the dimer binds to FliI (Imada et al., 2016 ); perhaps the same FliH surface that binds FliI serves to bind the C‐terminal helical segment of export substrates when FliI has vacated binding. Upon encountering FliI (and/or FliJ) at the base of the export gate, conformational changes and/or structural rearrangements would presumably occur resulting from altered protein interactions that would facilitate ATP hydrolysis and presentation of the substrate N‐terminus to the export gate.

4. MATERIALS AND METHODS

4.1. Molecular cloning

The HP1120, FlgK, and FliD genes were amplified from genomic DNA of H. pylori strain 11637 using the polymerase chain reaction and high‐fidelity Phusion® DNA polymerase from New England Biolabs. For HP1120 and FliD, the coding sequence was inserted into the pMCSG7 vector at the multiple cloning site employing the ligation independent cloning method (LIC) using forward and reverse primers provided in Table S4 such that the recombinant protein produced was tagged with six histidine residues at the amino terminal end. The primers were designed so that the first 18 nucleotides of both forward and reverse primers were complementary to the cloning site of the pMCSG7 vector. The vector was linearized using SspI and the resultant PCR‐amplified 435 bp insert and 5.2 kb vector were subjected to the exonuclease activity of T4 DNA polymerase enzyme in the presence of dCTP and dGTP, respectively, such that an 18 nucleotide overhang was created. The HP1120 insert and the vector were mixed in a 1:1 ratio on ice followed by transforming E. coli XL1 cells using the cold shock method. The HP1120Δ 126–144 as well as FliD genes were cloned in a similar manner (Table 1 ). Point mutations within the FlgN gene were created using the QuikChange II site‐directed mutagenesis kit and primers listed in Table 1 . The FlgK gene (HP1119) was cloned into the pGEX‐6P‐3 GST‐fusion vector using the BamHI and SmaI sites in the multiple cloning site (primers provided in Table 1 ). The amplified 1.8 kb PCR product and plasmid were subjected to double digestion using BamHI and SmaI enzymes. The amplified 1.8 kb FlgK gene and 4.9 kb pGEX‐6P‐3 vector were subsequently ligated using T4 DNA ligase.

E. coli XL1 cells were transformed with the above recombinant plasmids and plated on super optimal broth agar, which incorporated Ampicillin to a final concentration of 100 μg mL −1 . The plates were incubated at 37°C overnight. Plasmids were then isolated from single transformant colonies and DNA sequencing confirmed the cloning.

4.2. Bioinformatics and structural modeling of FlgN molecules

To construct a homology‐based tertiary structure model of H. pylori FlgN, we first built un‐swapped apo‐FlgN structures for the S. enterica and B. pertussis FlgN proteins (Kinoshita et al., 2016 ). We then aligned the sequence of C. jejuni FlgN first to HP1120 and then to the sequences corresponding to the known FlgN structures in the PDB using BLAST (Altschul et al., 1990 ). This provided the basis for a multiple sequence alignment for FlgN molecules that was then expanded with a wider array of FlgN sequences, including likely FlgN candidates from the Campylobacterota, and optimized with the aid of ClustalW (Sievers & Higgins, 2018 ). To un‐swap the experimentally‐determined FlgN structures, we deleted helix α1 from monomer 1 and replaced it by helix α1 from the twofold symmetry‐related monomer 2 using Coot (Emsley & Cowtan, 2004 ). We then reconnected the chains and refined the model geometry and stereochemistry using PHENIX (Adams et al., 2011 ). In this way, we created un‐swapped monomers for both B. pertussis and S. enterica FlgN. The three un‐swapped apo‐FlgN structures were used as templates for constructing a homology model of H. pylori FlgN using Coot and Phenix (Figure 3 ) (Adams et al., 2011 ; Emsley & Cowtan, 2004 ). Models and experimentally derived structures were compared and overlaid using the programs Coot (Emsley & Cowtan, 2004 ) and Lsqkab in the CCP4 suite (Winn et al., 2011 ).

4.3.
His‐FlgNΔ 126– 144 and His‐FliD protein purification

One liter of Luria Bertani (LB) broth + Ampicillin (100 μg mL −1 ) was inoculated with an overnight E. coli p LysS seed culture harboring pMCSG7‐FlgNΔ 126–144 (or derived point mutations). Once the optical density reached A 600 = 0.6, the 1‐L culture was induced with 0.1 mM Isopropyl β‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and was incubated at 16°C for 10 hours. Then, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6000 rpm at 4°C for 30 min using a JLA 8.1 Avanti rotor and resuspended in three times the volume of lysis buffer containing 200 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, 10 mM Imidazole, 0.1 mM EDTA pH 8.0. The resuspended cells were lysed using a cell disrupter device (Constant cell disruptor system) at 25 psi followed by centrifugation at 18,000 rpm for 40 min at 4°C using an Avanti JLA 25.5 rotor. The supernatant was passed through glass wool and then injected onto a 10 mL Ni‐NTA‐Sepharose column preequilibrated with wash buffer (20 mM Tris, 0.1 M NaCl, 10 mM Imidazole pH 8.0). After washing, 15% elution buffer (20 mM Tris, 75 mM NaCl, 500 mM Imidazole pH 8.0) was used to elute non‐specifically bound E. coli proteins. Then, the column was subjected to 100% elution buffer to elute out the 6× Histidine tagged FlgNΔ 126–144 protein. The peak fractions were subjected to 15% SDS PAGE and the eluted protein fractions were pooled and dialyzed against 50 mM Tris, 75 mM NaCl, pH 8.75. The protein was then injected onto a Source Q column equilibrated with 50 mM Tris, 75 mM NaCl, pH 8.75, and eluted using a gradient wash of 50 mM Tris, 1 M NaCl, pH 8.75. The eluted peak fractions were subjected to 15% SDS PAGE to confirm the presence of pure FlgN or FlgNΔ 126‐144 protein. Further purification was carried out by gel filtration chromatography using an S200 increase column and a flow rate of 0.5 mL per min. The gel filtration buffer used was modified 1× PBS with 250 mM NaCl, pH 8.5. For His‐FliD, an almost identical procedure was used to purify the protein as for His‐ FlgNΔ 126–144. The main difference was that the pMCSG7‐FliD plasmid was used to transform E. coli Bl‐21 for subsequent protein expression. All other purification steps including Source Q anion exchange chromatography were virtually identical to those described for purification of His‐ FlgNΔ 126‐144 . In addition, the His‐tag was removed prior to any experiments using Tobacco Etch Virus (TEV) protease.

4.4.
FlgK protein purification

Two liters of LB broth + Ampicillin (100 μg mL −1 ) was inoculated with an overnight culture E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells containing FlgK‐pGEX‐6P3 plasmid. Once the OD reached A 600 = 0.6, the culture was induced with 0.1 mM IPTG and was incubated overnight at 25°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6000 rpm at 4°C for 30 min. The cells were resuspended in three times the wet weight of lysis buffer: 50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM EDTA, and 10 mM 2‐mercaptoethanol, pH 7.5. Resuspended cells were lysed using a cell disrupter at 25 psi and then centrifuged at 18000 rpm for 40 mins at 4°C. The supernatant was passed through glass wool and injected onto a 10 mL Glutathione Sepharose 4B column equilibrated with 1× PBS pH 7.5. 4.7.
Pulldown studies using glutathione sepharose

After purification of both GST‐FlgK and His‐FlgN proteins, 250 mL of each protein was mixed in a 1:1 molar ratio (25 μM final concentration) in an Eppendorf tube containing 50 μL of 1× PBS pre‐washed GST beads. The individual proteins were also added to an Eppendorf tube containing 50 μL of 1× PBS pre‐washed Glutathione‐Sepharose beads in a total volume of 600 μL to serve as positive (GST‐FlgK) and negative (His‐FlgN) controls, respectively. All samples were then diluted with 1 mL of 1× PBS buffer and then mixed gently for 30 mins at room temperature. After the incubation period was complete, all three samples were centrifuged at 4500× g at room temperature for 60 s. The supernatant was discarded and 600 μL of 1× PBS buffer with 8 mM 3‐[(3‐cholamidopropyl) dimethylammonio]‐1‐propanesulfonate (CHAPS) was added to each tube, gently mixed, and was again subjected to centrifugation. This cycle was repeated five times to wash off any non‐specifically bound protein. After the wash steps, 20 μL of 4× SDS PAGE loading buffer was added to each tube and then 10 μL from each prepared sample was subjected to 12% SDS PAGE.

4.8.
Ni‐NTA sepharose pulldowns of His‐FlgNΔ 126– 144 and FlgK proteins

To study the interaction between His FlgN Δ126‐144 with either FlgK, FliI 2–92 , or FliI 2–438 , the proteins were first purified using the above‐described protocols. To test for interaction, 250 mL of bait (His‐FlgN Δ126–144 ) and prey (FlgK or FliI) proteins were mixed in equimolar concentration (25 μM each) and were added to 50 μL of pre‐equilibrated NiNTA‐Sepharose beads and the total volume was adjusted to 600 μL. The supernatant was discarded and 600 μL of wash buffer (with 8 mM CHAPS) was added to each tube, gently mixed and then again subjected to centrifugation. This cycle was repeated five times to wash off any non‐specifically bound proteins. After the wash steps, 20 μL of 4× SDS PAGE loading buffer was added to each tube and 10 μL from each prepared sample was subjected to 12% SDS PAGE.

4.9.
SEC‐MALS analysis of the interaction of FlgK with His‐FlgNΔ 126– 144

To characterize the FlgK: His‐FlgN Δ126–144 interaction and those of FlgN Δ126–144 point mutants, the individual purified proteins were dialyzed overnight in 1× PBS, 200 mM NaCl pH 8.7 buffer followed by concentration to 850 μM. Next, FlgN Δ126–144 and FlgK proteins were mixed in a 1:1 molar ratio at a final concentration of 85 μM and a total volume of 500 μL. Individual FlgN Δ126–144 and FlgK proteins were prepared at the same concentration and injected onto the S200 Increase column under identical conditions as described above. Fractions corresponding to the control peaks as well as the mixture peaks were subjected to 15% SDS PAGE.

4.10. Thermophoresis‐based determination of equilibrium dissociation constants

MST detects the binding affinities between a fluorophore‐labeled target molecule and its unlabeled ligand in an infrared laser created temperature gradient environment (Wienken et al., 2010 ). We first covalently labeled FlgK with a suitable fluorophore, using a commercially available labeling kit according to which the epsilon amino groups of lysine residues are covalently tagged with reactive N‐hydroxyl‐succinamide (NHS) ester groups. Before the interaction experiment was carried out, the labeled FlgK protein was subjected to a pretest. In the pretest, the minimum concentration of FlgK protein that provided a useable fluorescence signal was determined. Once it was determined (20 nM for FlgK), the target and the ligand (FlgN Δ126–144 ) protein information were input to the concentration finder tool available in the Monolith software package (NanoTemper Monolith NT.115, NanoTemper Technologies). After several titrations of the ligand (starting from 20 nM) with labeled FlgK, the optimal concentration of the FlgN ligand protein was determined to be 3 μM. Once the concentration optimization step was carried out, the FlgN Δ126–144 WT protein (working stock: 3 μM) was serially diluted into 16 tubes ranging concentration from 10 −6 to 10 −10 M, and then 10 μL of the target protein (FlgK working stock: 20 nM) was added and mixed in each tube. The mixture was then carefully transferred to capillaries. After the measurements were complete, the raw data were analyzed using the NT analysis software, which evaluates the thermophoresis data and calculates the K d by fitting the data to a standard quadratic equation describing the relationship between K d and free and bound FlgK and FlgN molecules (Jarmoskaite et al., 2020 ; Wienken et al., 2010 ). In an identical fashion, titration of labeled FlgK with FlgN mutants was also carried out. A similar protocol was used to test the interaction between fluorophore‐labeled FliI 2–92 or FliI 2–438 and His‐FlgN Δ126–144 . Again, fluorophore‐labeled FliI was used at 20 nM concentration, and a suitable concentration of FlgN Δ126–144 was deduced for the titrations. It should be noted that because the concentration of the labeled molecules (e.g., FlgK) may be significantly greater than K d for the FlgK:FlgN Δ126–144 complex in several of these titrations, it is not valid to assume K d is equal to the ligand concentration when 50% fluorophore binding is achieved (Figure 9 ), as is standard practise in assessing equilibria from binding curves of macromolecular complexes, and K d must be determined from a global fit to the experimental data (Jarmoskaite et al., 2020 ).
