Cushing's disease (CD), the most common endogenous Cushing's syndrome, is caused
by activating mutations in the ubiquitin-specific protease 8 (USP8) gene. These
mutations drive adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)-secreting pituitary adenomas
and hypercortisolism. Clinical manifestations include muscle weakness,
osteopenia, cataracts and cardiovascular dysfunction. To investigate the
pathogenic mechanisms of USP8 gain of function, we generated a conditional
transgenic mouse model expressing human USP8 (referred to as hUSP8) carrying the
most prevalent activating mutation in CD adenomas (p.S718del). Systemic
expression of hUSP8S718del in mice induced diffuse corticotroph hyperplasia of
ACTH+ cells, rather than pituitary microadenomas, and did not lead to
hypercortisolemia. Despite preservation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal
axis, transgenic mice developed skeletal muscle atrophy, bone abnormalities,
corneal keratitis with cataracts and cardiac dysfunction. Notably,
myocardial-specific expression of hUSP8S718del recapitulated cardiac defects
seen in mice with ubiquitous expression, demonstrating the direct role of USP8
activation in the heart. These findings show that expression of a clinically
relevant USP8 gain-of-function mutation in mice recapitulates key features of a
USP8-associated syndrome. Our results reveal tissue-specific effects of USP8
beyond pituitary tumorigenesis and identify a direct contribution of USP8
activation to cardiac pathology.

INTRODUCTION

Cushing's disease (CD) is a rare disease with an annual incidence in Europe of 1.2–2.4 cases per million (Etxabe and Vazquez, 1994; van Haalen et al., 2015). CD originates from an adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)-secreting pituitary adenoma (Cushing, 1994), in which ACTH overproduction stimulates excessive cortisol secretion by the adrenal glands, leading to hypercortisolism. CD is the most common form of endogenous Cushing's syndrome (CS) (Castinetti et al., 2012). Patients with CS develop a wide range of symptoms resulting from systemic cortisol exposure, including central obesity, metabolic, cardiovascular and musculoskeletal disease, and increased risk of cataract development, among others (Bertagna et al., 2009; Castinetti et al., 2012; Sharon et al., 2025). They have a 3.3-fold increased risk of mortality (Ragnarsson et al., 2019), primarily due to cardiovascular complications such as hypertension, atherosclerosis, cardiac hypertrophy, cardioembolic stroke and heart failure (Colao et al., 1999; Etxabe and Vazquez, 1994; Faggiano et al., 2003; Mancini et al., 2004; Pivonello et al., 2005; Vassiliadi and Tsagarakis, 2017). Transsphenoidal surgery for pituitary tumor resection is the first-line treatment, achieving remission in 60–90% of cases (Broersen et al., 2018); besides, medical strategies for cortisol correction can alleviate and even reverse clinical comorbidities (Atkinson et al., 2005; Braun et al., 2020). However, increased cardiovascular risk persists in certain patients with CD despite successful long-term remission of hypercortisolism (Colao et al., 1999; Faggiano et al., 2003; Reincke, 2021; van Haalen et al., 2015), with a 2.5-fold increased mortality risk (Ragnarsson et al., 2019).

Recurrent somatic mutations in ubiquitin-specific protease 8 (USP8) are found in 36.5% of sporadic CD pituitary adenomas in human patients (Ma et al., 2015; Perez-Rivas et al., 2015; Reincke et al., 2015; Sbiera et al., 2019). These mutations cluster in a hotspot region within exon 14 of USP8, which encodes the highly conserved 14-3-3 binding domain (RSYSSP), and primarily affect residues p.S718 and p.P720 (96.1%) (Ma et al., 2015; Sbiera et al., 2019). These mutations prevent binding of the regulatory 14-3-3 protein, thereby enhancing the deubiquitylating activity of USP8 and functioning as activating mutations (Mizuno et al., 2007; Reincke et al., 2015). The 3-bp deletion p.S718del (USP8S718del) is the most common USP8 activating mutation, found in ∼25% of patients with CD with such mutations (Sbiera et al., 2019) and exhibits the highest deubiquitylating activity (Reincke et al., 2015). A de novo germline mutation in USP8 was identified in the same hotspot region in a pediatric patient with CD who exhibited not only common CD features but also atypical systemic symptoms beyond pituitary involvement (Cohen et al., 2019). Common symptoms of CD include the presence of ATCH-producing pituitary adenoma and hypercortisolism, overweight, hyperglycemia, hypertension, hirsutism, and lung, kidney and cardiac disease. In addition, the patient showed developmental delay, dysmorphic facial features, skeletal defects and epidermal hyperkeratosis, among other medical conditions (Cohen et al., 2019). Uncommon multiorgan defects suggest a direct role of USP8 in the pathophysiology of the syndrome, beyond pituitary involvement and hypercortisolism. We hypothesize that these defects could arise from altered cellular pathways regulated by the enhanced deubiquitylating activity of USP8 in target tissues. Current mouse models of CD either overexpress corticotropin-releasing hormone (Crh) (Dedic et al., 2012; Nishiyama et al., 2022) or rely on subcutaneous and intracranial implantation of ACTH-secreting AtT20 murine pituitary cells (Helseth et al., 1992; Kemeny et al., 2020; Leung et al., 1982; Nishiyama et al., 2022). Unfortunately, these approaches do not genetically mimic human USP8 (referred to as hUSP8) mutations, thereby restricting their utility for mechanistic studies and therapeutic development.

In this study, we generated a conditional transgenic mouse line in which expression of the hUSP8S718del mutation is controlled by Cre recombinase. This strategy allowed us to assess the tissue-specific effects of hUSP8S718del activation in epiblast-derived lineages and, independently, in the myocardium. Our model shows that expression in mice of the most common activating USP8 mutation found in humans induces systemic pathological alterations that parallel key features of the human genetic syndrome (Cohen et al., 2019).

To further characterize the phenotype at the molecular level, we assessed ACTH expression, a key diagnostic marker of CD. This approach results in constitutive transgene expression throughout the embryo while excluding extraembryonic tissues (Hayashi et al., 2002b). Whole-mount examination of embryos showed widespread GFP expression in transgenic embryonic day (E)9.5 embryos, indicating that Cre is expressed in all epiblast-derived tissues (Fig. 1B). Given that FLAG-HA-hUSP8S718del is a gain-of-function (GOF) allele, we refer to mice carrying one copy as hUSP8GOF/+ and those with two copies as hUSP8GOF/GOF.

To assess transgene expression, we performed reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) in E11.5 hearts to quantify mRNA levels. Our results confirmed expression of the transgene components, including FLAG-HA-hUSP8GOF and the reporter eGFP (Fig. 1C). Allele-specific expression analysis distinguishing human and mouse USP8 in transgenic mice showed that hUSP8 was expressed at levels up to sixfold higher than endogenous murine Usp8, consistent with transgene dose-dependent expression (Fig. 1D). The discrepancy between FLAG-HA, GFP and USP8 relative gene expression detected by RT-qPCR (Fig. 1C,D) most likely reflects technical differences in assay performance.

The USP8 14-3-3 binding domain is highly conserved between human and mouse (Reincke et al., 2015), supporting functional equivalence across species. The p.S718del mutation disrupts USP8 interaction with 14-3-3 proteins, thereby enhancing proteolytic cleavage of the full-length 130 kDa protein into a 90 kDa N-terminal fragment and a constitutively active 40 kDa C-terminal deubiquitinase (DUB) domain (Reincke et al., 2015). In our FLAG-HA-hUSP8GOF construct, the HA epitope tag is positioned at the N-terminus of hUSP8GOF (Fig. 1E, light-blue stripe), enabling specific detection by western blot analysis of the full-length ∼130 kDa protein (Fig. 1F, left, upper arrowhead) and an HA-tagged ∼90 kDa N-terminal fragment appearing as a doublet (Fig. 1F, left, lower arrowhead). We could also detect specific transgenic GFP expression (Fig. 1F, right). Western blot analysis of USP8 detected full-length USP8 protein (∼130 kDa; Fig. 1G) that was ∼2.3-fold increased in E18.5 transgenic hearts with respect to that in controls (Fig. 1G′, left). We also observed a lower, 90 kDa band corresponding to the internally processed USP8 fragment (Fig. 1G), the quantification of which showed a non-significant tendency toward increased levels (Fig. 1G′, right). These data show relatively successful expression and post-translational processing of the FLAG-HA-USP8S718del transgene (Fig. 1E).

One of the hallmarks of CD is the presence of ACTH-secreting pituitary adenomas (Cushing, 1994). To evaluate whether our model recapitulates this aspect, we examined pituitaries from adult transgenic mice. No macroscopic pituitary enlargement was detected in 8-month-old mice expressing two copies of hUSP8GOF (Fig. 1H). To further characterize the phenotype at the molecular level, we assessed ACTH expression, a key diagnostic marker of CD. Immunofluorescence analysis of pituitaries from 1-year-old hUSP8GOF/GOF;Sox2Cre mice was performed to determine whether USP8 activation leads to increased ACTH production. ACTH-positive cells were detected in both the pars intermedia and pars distalis or adenohypophysis (Fig. 1I), consistent with previous reports (Roux and Dubois, 1976). In control mice, ACTH+ cells in the adenohypophysis were typically arranged in small clusters (approximately four cells), whereas hUSP8GOF/GOF;Sox2Cre mice exhibited larger clusters (approximately nine cells) (Fig. 1I,J). Quantitatively, the proportion of ACTH+ cells in the adenohypophysis was significantly increased in transgenic mice (23.07±0.66%) compared to that in controls (12.55±2.47%; Fig. 1J). This increase occurred without a corresponding elevation in expression of the ACTH precursor proopiomelanocortin (Pomc) (Fig. 1K) or in cell proliferation (Fig. 1I,L), indicating that hUSP8GOF promotes accumulation of ACTH+ cells independently of increased Pomc transcription or proliferative expansion. These findings are consistent with enhanced ACTH stability or processing rather than transcriptional upregulation, hyperproliferation or microadenoma formation.

There is increasing evidence that USP8 regulates receptor tyrosine kinase signaling in vivo (Niendorf et al., 2007; Pau-Navalon et al., 2026). In USP8 GOF mutations associated with CD, sustained EGFR signaling leads to increased ERK phosphorylation that drives ACTH hypersecretion and tumor cell proliferation, promoting corticotroph pituirary adenomas (Liu et al., 2019; Reincke et al., 2015). In hUSP8GOF/GOF;Sox2Cre mice, the increase in ACTH+ cells did not reflect overactivation of MAPK pathway (Fig. 1M).

Elevated ACTH levels stimulate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to increased cortisol production, a hallmark of CD pathophysiology (Bertagna et al., 2009; Castinetti et al., 2012). To assess HPA axis activation in our model, we measured the free cortisol index relative to creatinine in urine samples, a standardized screening for human and canine patients with CD (Nieman et al., 2008; Schafer et al., 2023). Only at 4 months of age, hUSP8GOF/GOF;Sox2Cre mice exhibited a moderate, but significant, increase in cortisol/creatinine levels compared to those of controls (control, 0.99×10−5±0.37×10−5 nmol/l; transgenic, 1.52×10−5±0.43×10−5 nmol/l; P=0.0294) that reverted to normal levels at 9 months, indicating a mild and transient alteration of the HPA axis (Fig. 1N). Patients with CD frequently develop cardiometabolic syndrome as a consequence of sustained hypercortisolism (Pivonello et al., 2005). To determine whether this phenotype is recapitulated in our model, we analyzed plasma metabolites and enzyme levels in 4- and 9-month-old hUSP8GOF/GOF;Sox2Cre mice and found no significant differences compared to those in age-matched controls (Fig. 1O; Fig. S2D). USP8 has been implicated in tumor progression and cell migration across multiple cell types (Jiang et al., 2025; Ke et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2022). To assess potential metabolic alterations associated with increased glucose demand, we measured 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) uptake by positron emission tomography (PET) in the brain, heart and liver. FDG, a glucose analog, accumulates in tissues with elevated metabolic activity, such as tumors, inflammatory lesions, or ischemic myocardium (Pantel et al., 2024). Transgenic mice exhibited FDG uptake levels comparable to those of controls in all organs analyzed (Fig. 1P,Q). Together, these results indicate that hUSP8GOF expression does not induce overt metabolic or biochemical abnormalities associated with tumorigenesis, inflammation or ischemia in major organs. Chronic lung and kidney disease, as well as adrenal gland hyperplasia due to prolonged dysregulation of the HPA axis, are frequently observed in human patients with CD (Cohen et al., 2019; Reincke et al., 2015). To determine whether these pathologies are recapitulated in our model, we performed histopathological analyses of the lungs, kidneys and adrenal glands in hUSP8GOF/GOF;Sox2Cre mice. Tissue architecture in all examined organs was indistinguishable from that of control mice (Fig. S2A–C). Collectively, these findings indicate that germline expression of hUSP8S718del in mice is insufficient to induce the classical pathological features of CD.

A healthy ocular lens is avascular, and its inner cortex consists of densely packed and ordered fiber cells, essential for lens transparency (Teixeira, 2019). Histological analysis of lenses from 1-year-old mice revealed early subcapsular deposits, with nuclear retention in hUSP8GOF/+;Sox2Cre mice. These abnormalities were more severe in hUSP8GOF/GOF;Sox2Cre mice, extending into the inner cortex and indicating progressive cataract formation (Fig. 5A,B; Fig. S4D). Cataracts were observed in 100% of the transgenic mice analyzed (six of six), highlighting a fully penetrant lens phenotype (Fig. 5B). These findings uncover a previously unrecognized role of hUSP8GOF in ocular integrity and aging-related degeneration. Despite the absence of pituitary adenomas and MAPK signaling overactivation or increased proliferation, transgenic mice showed diffuse corticotroph hyperplasia of ACTH+ cells that was not sufficient to overstimulate activity of the HPA axis. Taking these results together, we conclude that the moderate increase in ACTH does not overstimulate the HPA axis; therefore, this transgenic mouse line does not develop CD.

Despite the normal HPA axis function, hUSP8GOF transgenic mice developed corneal opacities from juvenile stages, accompanied by signs of ongoing wound healing and increased NOTCH signaling activity in the corneal epithelium, a pathway essential for corneal maintenance and repair (Vauclair et al., 2007). Cataracts and corneal abnormalities were associated with membrane defects and mislocalized CX43, a gap junction protein implicated in lens transparency (Gong et al., 2021) and corneal integrity (Grupcheva et al., 2012; Qin et al., 2019; Zhai et al., 2014). Because USP8 directly stabilizes N1ICD (Shin et al., 2020) and regulates CX43 trafficking (Sun et al., 2018), we propose that hUSP8GOF drives persistent corneal wound healing signaling and disrupts lens remodeling, leading to complex ocular pathologies. Of note, subcapsular cataracts have been reported in patients with CD (Liu et al., 2017; Sharon et al., 2025), and CD in dogs is linked to ocular abnormalities, including corneal defects (Feldman and Nelson, 2004; Laus et al., 2002).

Aged hUSP8GOF mice developed musculoskeletal abnormalities despite exhibiting normal HPA axis activity. The muscle atrophy observed in these transgenic mice is consistent with the established role of the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) in maintaining skeletal muscle homeostasis (Bodine et al., 2001; Hunt et al., 2021), as well as with the reported increase in pERK signaling (Gallot et al., 2019; Miyake et al., 2017). In addition, USP8 has been shown to modulate WNT signaling in osteoclasts, likely through the regulation of Frizzled receptor stability via deubiquitylation (Chaugule et al., 2021; Mukai et al., 2010). Notably, a human patient carrying a germline USP8GOF mutation also developed musculoskeletal defects (Cohen et al., 2019), suggesting that the impact of USP8GOF on musculoskeletal tissues is conserved across humans and mice. Further studies will be necessary to elucidate the molecular and cellular mechanisms driving these cortisol-independent skeletal abnormalities in USP8-associated disease.

Expression of hUSP8GOF specifically in the myocardium from embryonic stages (via Tnnt2Cre) led to mild, but significant, reduction in EF, along with septal defects, like those observed in mice with ubiquitous hUSP8GOF expression. The fact that restricting transgene expression to the myocardium recapitulates the cardiac phenotype observed in hUSP8S718del ubiquitous models suggests that USP8GOF has a direct role in the myocardium. Mechanistically, USP8 is known to regulate K63-linked deubiquitylation (Alexopoulou et al., 2016; Sun et al., 2018) and autophagy (Peng et al., 2020), a tightly controlled process involved in multiple forms of cardiomyopathy (Calise and Powell, 2013; Chen et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2011). In addition, the altered localization of CX43 in the myocardium could disrupt gap junction structure and function, thereby compromising cardiac performance (Araujo et al., 2025). Notably, reduced EF has also been reported in a patient carrying a germline USP8GOF mutation (Cohen et al., 2019), leading us to speculate that myocardial expression of hUSP8GOF may contribute to cardiac comorbidities in these patients, alongside the effects of elevated cortisol levels.

To date, two germline variants of USP8 have been described in humans, both associated with multiorgan abnormalities (Cohen et al., 2019; Sakamoto et al., 2024). However, only the patient carrying a USP8GOF mutation affecting the same 14-3-3 binding domain as in our transgenic model developed CD (Cohen et al., 2019). The similarities between this patient and our mouse model point to a conserved role of USP8 in maintaining the homeostasis of cardiac, musculoskeletal and ocular tissues. Overall, this new conditional transgenic mouse line expressing the hUSP8S718del mutation highlights the importance of UPS regulation in multiorgan homeostasis and uncovers additional roles for this pathogenic USP8S718del variant.

A limitation of this study is that the hUSP8S718del;Sox2Cre model does not fully reproduce the endocrine manifestations of CD, particularly ACTH-secreting pituitary adenomas and sustained hypercortisolemia. Although transgenic mice developed diffuse corticotroph hyperplasia with moderately increased ACTH levels, HPA axis activity remained largely preserved. These findings suggest that USP8GOF alone may be insufficient to drive full pituitary tumorigenesis in mice and that additional species-specific, genetic, epigenetic or microenvironmental factors may contribute to disease development in humans. We were also unable to consistently detect the cleaved ∼90 kDa active form of hUSP8 in either control or transgenic tissues, which may reflect technical limitations of the chosen antibody. Finally, although the phenotypes observed are consistent with enhanced USP8 activity, we did not perform direct cell-based deubiquitinase assays to quantify USP8 overactivation. Future studies combining biochemical and cell-based functional approaches will be important to further define the molecular mechanisms underlying tissue-specific USP8 GOF pathology.

In vivo image quantificationCine MRI data were analyzed using Segment v1.9 R3819. Endocardial and epicardial contours were manually traced on short-axis slices to calculate ventricular end-diastolic volume (EDV), ESV, SV, EF and mass (VM) (Hernandez-Porras et al., 2014). Structural abnormalities in the LV, such as trabeculations, were assessed by an expert using the 17-segment AHA model (Cerqueira et al., 2002). The LV was divided into basal (segments 1–6), mid (7–12), apical (13–16) and apex (17). Each segment was scored (0, normal; 1, abnormal), and results were summarized in a bull's-eye plot by coronary territory. PET/CT images were analyzed using a Multimodality Workstation (Desco et al., 1999). For PET, regions of interest were drawn in the brain, heart, and liver to obtain mean and maximum standardized uptake values. CT-based skull measurements (length, width, intercanthal distance) were taken from coronal and sagittal maximum-intensity projections (MIPs). Kyphosis angles were assessed on whole-body sagittal MIPs. Right femur bone density was estimated by HU after manual segmentation in 3D Slicer.
