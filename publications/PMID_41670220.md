Loss of ALX1 gene function causes severe facial clefting and extreme
microphthalmia. Previous studies suggest that ALX1 protein function is crucial
for patterning the cranial neural crest cell (CNCC)-derived frontonasal
mesenchyme, but how ALX1 regulates eye development is not well understood. Here,
we show that Alx1 is transiently expressed in the embryonic cranial mesoderm and
that Alx1-/- mice exhibit agenesis of extraocular muscles (EOMs) without
affecting other muscles. We show that cranial mesoderm-specific Alx1
inactivation resulted in complete EOM agenesis accompanied by failure of
activation of the core myogenic regulatory network specifically in, and
increased apoptosis of, the EOM progenitor cells. Analysis of mice with
temporally induced Alx1 inactivation demonstrated that EOM myogenesis requires
Alx1 function before, but not after, formation of the EOM primordium. These data
identify ALX1 as a unique and specific upstream regulator of EOM myogenesis, and
provide new insights into pathogenic mechanisms underlying ALX1-type frontonasal
dysplasia, as well as molecular mechanisms controlling cell fate specification
in the early cranial mesoderm.

INTRODUCTIONFrontonasal dysplasia (FND), also known as median facial cleft syndrome, is a class of craniofacial malformations characterized by ocular hypertelorism, wide nasal root with hypoplastic or cleft alae nasi, and midline clefting of the nose and/or upper lip and palate (Farlie et al., 2016; Twigg et al., 2009). Whereas most cases of FND occur sporadically with unknown etiology, loss-of-function mutations in the three aristaless-like homeobox (ALX) family genes (ALX1, ALX3 and ALX4) have been associated with distinctive autosomal recessive FNDs (Farlie et al., 2016; Kariminejad et al., 2014; Kayserili et al., 2009; Pini et al., 2020; Twigg et al., 2009; Uz et al., 2010). Homozygous deletion or disruption of ALX1 caused FND3 (OMIM 613456), with bilateral facial clefting and extreme microphthalmia (Pini et al., 2020; Uz et al., 2010). Homozygous mutations in ALX3 and ALX4 have been associated with FND1 (OMIM 136760) and FND2 (OMIM 613451), respectively, characterized by milder frontonasal malformations (Kariminejad et al., 2014; Kayserili et al., 2009; Twigg et al., 2009). The ALX genes encode homeodomain-containing transcription factors and are expressed in partly overlapping patterns during craniofacial development (Beverdam et al., 2001; McGonnell et al., 2011; Meijlink et al., 1999). Gene knockout studies in mice have shown that Alx family genes play crucial roles in development and/or survival of the cranial neural crest cell (CNCC)-derived embryonic frontonasal mesenchyme (Beverdam et al., 2001; Iyyanar et al., 2022; Pini et al., 2020), consistent with disruptions and/or hypoplasia of CNCC-derived craniofacial midline structures in FND patients (Farlie et al., 2016). Most FND patients also exhibit ocular abnormalities in addition to midline frontonasal malformations, including ptosis, microphthalmia, optic nerve anomalies, and strabismus (Farlie et al., 2016; Lambert and Ramasubramanian, 2016). Several studies have reported abnormalities in ocular muscles in FND patients (Lambert and Ramasubramanian, 2016; Pavlovic and Stankovic, 2016), but the etiology underlying these ocular abnormalities is unclear. In this study, we show that ALX1 function is crucial in the early cranial mesoderm for the formation of extraocular muscle (EOM), which uncovers a previously unknown aspect of the pathogenic mechanism underlying FND and provides new insights into the gene regulatory network controlling craniofacial myogenesis.Whereas development of all skeletal muscles throughout the vertebrate embryo involves activation of a core gene regulatory network comprising the muscle regulatory factor (MRF) family of basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factors – with MYF5 and MYOD1 (also known and hereafter referred to as MYOD) acting during myogenic cell fate commitment and myogenin (hereafter referred to as MYOG) acting as an essential differentiation factor, extensive studies have clearly revealed distinct genetic programs upstream of MRFs during head and trunk myogenesis (Chang and Kioussi, 2018; Hasty et al., 1993; Kassar-Duchossoy et al., 2004; Michailovici et al., 2015; Mootoosamy and Dietrich, 2002; Nabeshima et al., 1993; Rudnicki et al., 1993; Sambasivan et al., 2009). Pax3 is a crucial regulator of MRFs during somitic myogenesis but neither expressed nor required in non-somitic mesodermal progenitors of craniofacial muscles (Dumas et al., 2024; Harel et al., 2009, 2012; Mootoosamy and Dietrich, 2002; Tajbakhsh et al., 1997; Tremblay et al., 1998). However, the transcription factors TBX1, MSC (also known as MYOR) and TCF21 (also known as capsulin), play crucial roles upstream of the MRFs in branchiomeric muscle formation but are not required for somitic myogenesis (Bothe et al., 2007, 2011; Buckingham, 2017; Dong et al., 2006; Kelly et al., 2004; Lu et al., 2002; Shih et al., 2007). During early development of the head mesoderm, Tbx1 expression is first activated in the posterior head mesoderm adjacent to the developing hindbrain and immediately anterior to the Pax3-expressing somitic mesoderm around the time of first somite formation (Bothe and Dietrich, 2006; Bothe et al., 2011; Dumas et al., 2024; Nogueira et al., 2015). At the same developmental stage, expression of Pitx2, encoding a homeodomain transcription factor, commences in the anterior head mesoderm from diencephalic to mid-myelencephalic levels (Bothe and Dietrich, 2006; Bothe et al., 2011; Nogueira et al., 2015). Expression of Msc is subsequently activated in the Pitx2-expressing domain and spreads posteriorly, while expression of Tbx1 mRNAs spreads anteriorly in the developing head mesoderm, co-labeling the pharyngeal mesoderm progenitors of the branchiomeric muscles (Bothe et al., 2011; Nogueira et al., 2015). Tbx1−/− mice exhibit hypoplastic mandibular arch-derived muscles and absence of other branchiomeric muscles, among multiple other developmental defects (Kelly et al., 2004; Sambasivan et al., 2009). TBX1 function is required for proliferation and normal activation of Myf5 and MyoD expression in the pharyngeal mesoderm cells, as Tbx1−/− embryos exhibit sporadic expression of Myf5 and MyoD in the mandibular arch mesoderm (Huynh et al., 2007; Kelly et al., 2004). MSC and TCF21 are closely related basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) proteins transiently co-expressed in the pharyngeal mesoderm, acting in a partly redundant manner in head mesoderm development, with craniofacial muscle defects only in the first pharyngeal arch muscles in the Msc−/−Tcf21−/− double mutant mice but not in either Msc−/− or Tcf21−/− single mutants (Harel et al., 2012; Lu et al., 2002; Nogueira et al., 2015). Whereas PITX2 function is important for activation of expression of Tbx1, Msc and Tcf21 in the myogenic progenitor cells within the first pharyngeal arch, Pitx2 null mouse embryos exhibit agenesis of all EOMs in addition to loss of the jaw musculature derived from the first arch pharyngeal mesoderm (Evans and Gage, 2005; Kelly et al., 2004; Lu et al., 2002; Sambasivan et al., 2009; Shih et al., 2007). The Pitx2−/− mouse embryos exhibit increased apoptosis of the EOM progenitor cells by embryonic day 10.5 (E10.5), prior to activation of the MRFs (Zacharias et al., 2011). Interestingly, Myf5/Mrf4 double mutant mouse embryos exhibit EOM agenesis due to EOM progenitor apoptosis, despite continued expression of Pitx2 in the EOM progenitor cells (Sambasivan et al., 2009). Whereas PITX2 has been shown to bind directly to the promoter region of both Myf5 and MyoD genes in an EOM cell line (Zacharias et al., 2011), expression of MyoD from the Myf5 locus rescues EOM formation in Myf5/Mrf4 double mutants, indicating that PITX2 is insufficient to activate MyoD expression in EOM progenitor cells to drive EOM myogenesis in the absence of MYF5/MRF4 (Sambasivan et al., 2009, 2011). Further studies have shown that Pitx2 function is required in the MYF5-expressing (MYF5+) myogenic progenitor cells for EOM myogenesis, as Myf5Cre;Pitx2flox/flox mice also exhibit lack of EOMs (Kuriki et al., 2024). Mice with Mesp1-Cre-mediated cranial mesoderm-specific inactivation of Twist1 – a gene widely expressed throughout mesoderm and cranial neural crest-derived craniofacial mesenchyme cell types during early embryonic development, and crucial for epithelial-mesenchymal transition and maintenance of mesenchymal properties – also exhibit EOM agenesis (Bildsoe et al., 2009, 2013; Debnath et al., 2022; Tu et al., 2025). Compared with control littermates, Pitx2 mRNA expression in the head mesoderm ventral to the developing eye is reduced in Twist1flox/flox;Mesp1-Cre mouse embryos at E10.5 (Bildsoe et al., 2013). We, therefore, performed double immunofluorescence staining for PITX2 and TWIST1 on serial sagittal sections of control and Alx1c/c;Mesp1-Cre embryos to examine if TWIST1 expression in the EOM precursor cells is affected by Alx1 inactivation. In contrast to the obvious reduction in PITX2 expression within EOM progenitor cells in Alx1c/c;Mesp1-Cre embryos compared with control littermates at 23-somite stage, no difference in the pattern or level of TWIST1 immunostaining was detected between the control and Alx1c/c;Mesp1-Cre embryos (Fig. S1).

To examine if the reduction in the PITX2+ EOM precursor cells in the Alx1c/c;Mesp1-Cre embryos was due to increased apoptosis, we performed immunofluorescence staining to detect cleaved caspase-3 (CC3) in the Alx1c/c;Rosa26mTmG/+;Mesp1-Cre embryos and control littermates from E9–E10.5. We did not detect more than five cranial mesoderm cells positively stained for CC3 on any of the serial sections from the control and Alx1c/c;Rosa26mTmG/+;Mesp1-Cre embryos up to the 23-somite stage. In the 25-somite embryos, significantly increased numbers of CC3-positive cranial mesoderm cells were detected specifically in the region positioned dorso-anterior to the mandibular arch and mostly caudal to the optic vesicle in Alx1c/c;Rosa26mTmG/+;Mesp1-Cre embryos compared with control littermates (Fig. 8A,B,G). Notably, CC3-positive cells were not detected in GFP+ mesoderm cells that populated the mandibular arch in both control and Alx1c/c; Rosa26mTmG/+;Mesp1-Cre embryos (Fig. 8A,B). By E10.5, EOM precursor cells had formed an aggregated mesodermal condensation in a dorso-caudal position relative to the developing optic cup in the control embryos and few CC3-positive cells were detected in the EOM condensation (Fig. 8C,H). In Alx1c/c;Rosa26mTmG/+;Mesp1-Cre embryos at E10.5, many CC3-postive cells were detected in GFP+ mesoderm cells in a caudal position relative to the developing optic cup (Fig. 8D,H).

Next, we analyzed whether there was any change in the proliferation of EOM precursor cells in the Alx1c/c;Rosa26mTmG/+;Mesp1-Cre embryos compared with their littermates. By using phosphorylated histone H3 (pHH3) as a marker for mitotic cells, we were able to count the number of pHH3+/GFP+ cells in the presumptive EOM precursor domain of cranial mesoderm located in a dorso-caudal position relative to the optic vesicle on each of the 8−10 serial coronal sections through the optic vesicle in the E9.5 Alx1c/c;Rosa26mTmG/+;Mesp1-Cre embryos and control littermates (Fig. 8E,F) (SS23-SS25, n=3 embryos per genotype). This analysis revealed no significant difference in proliferation of EOM precursor cells in Alx1c/c;Rosa26mTmG/+;Mesp1-Cre embryos and control littermates (Fig. 8I) prior to the loss of EOM precursor cells by apoptosis. Together, these data suggested that ALX1 function in early cranial mesoderm cells is required for the specification and survival of EOM precursor cells.

DISCUSSIONEOMs are anatomically conserved skeletal muscles that control eye movement; they are crucial for vision in vertebrates (Fritzsch, 2024; Sambasivan et al., 2011; Young, 2008). Previous studies have shown that EOMs exhibit unique structural, biochemical and functional properties compared with other skeletal muscles, including distinct myofiber types and composition, myosin isoform profile and innervation patterns (Fischer et al., 2002; Khanna et al., 2003; Titova et al., 2024; Yu Wai Man et al., 2005). These unique properties enable the EOMs to be among the fastest and most fatigue-resistant muscles in the vertebrate body (Briggs and Schachat, 2002; Fuchs and Binder, 1983; Khanna et al., 2003; Titova et al., 2024; Yu Wai Man et al., 2005). Moreover, in contrast to other skeletal muscles, EOMs are remarkably resistant to various muscular dystrophies and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis but are selectively vulnerable to certain disorders, such as congenital cranial dysinnervation disorders and Graves' ophthalmopathy (Kallestad et al., 2011; McLoon and Wirtschafter, 2003; Stuelsatz et al., 2015; Titova et al., 2024; Yu Wai Man et al., 2005). Why EOMs are resistant to many forms of muscular dystrophies remains unresolved (Kuriki et al., 2024; Sambasivan et al., 2011; Titova et al., 2024; Yu Wai Man et al., 2005). One hypothesis is that many of the unique properties of EOMs are due to the unique population of myogenic precursor cells and the unique gene regulatory network controlling EOM morphogenesis and generation of their muscle stem cell pool (Kallestad et al., 2011; Kuriki et al., 2024; Sambasivan et al., 2009, 2011). Indeed, EOMs arise from anterior-most skeletal muscle progenitors in the vertebrate embryo and the gene regulatory network that controls EOM myogenesis is distinct to those of all other muscles (Kuriki et al., 2024; Sambasivan et al., 2009, 2011). Whereas several transcription factors, including TBX1, MSC, TCF21 and PITX2, have been shown to act upstream of MRFs and are crucial for pharyngeal muscle myogenesis from the embryonic cranial mesoderm, only PITX2 is essential for EOM formation and EOM progenitor cell survival (Chang and Kioussi, 2018; Dong et al., 2006; Kelly et al., 2004; Lu et al., 2002; Michailovici et al., 2015; Sambasivan et al., 2009, 2011; Shih et al., 2007; Zacharias et al., 2011). However, whereas PITX2 has been shown to regulate EOM-specific myosin isoform profiles and multi-innervation clusters of nerve endings, its function is also required for survival of mandibular arch myogenic progenitors and formation of mandibular arch-derived facial muscles (Dong et al., 2006; Harel et al., 2012; Shih et al., 2007; Zhou et al., 2011). In our study, we have identified ALX1 as a new upstream regulator of myogenesis in EOM formation.We found that Alx1−/− mice exhibit complete EOM agenesis, while other craniofacial muscles develop normally. Alx1 is strongly expressed in the nascent cranial mesoderm but – within the developing cranial mesoderm – its area of expression is restricted to the most-rostral region by the 3-somite stage and undetectable in the cranial mesoderm by the 16-somite stage. Previous fate-mapping studies of mouse embryos at gastrulation have shown that mesodermal cell fate is spatiotemporally regulated along both the medial−lateral and anterior−posterior axis of the primitive streak region (Kinder et al., 1999; Lawson et al., 1991; Parameswaran and Tam, 1995; Tam et al., 1997). During early gastrulation, the first wave of epiblast cells that ingress through the primitive streak gives rise to the extraembryonic mesoderm, followed by ingression of epiblast cells destined for the cranial and heart mesoderm during early to mid-gastrulation stage. By mid-to-late streak stage, even cells at the anterior primitive streak are destined to posterior mesoderm derivatives (Kinder et al., 1999). Whereas retrospective clonal analysis in mouse embryos has revealed that EOM and first-arch-derived branchiomeric muscles share a common progenitor in the early cranial mesoderm (Lescroart et al., 2010), fate mapping in quail-chick chimeras indicates that the EOM progenitors are completely segregated from branchiomeric muscle progenitors by the 3-somite stage (Couly et al., 1992). The dynamic pattern of Alx1 expression in mouse embryos between E7.5 and E9 suggest that Alx1 in the early cranial mesoderm was expressed longest in EOM progenitor cells. Although Alx1 expression was downregulated prior to EOM myogenic differentiation, our generation and analyses of Alx1c/c;Mesp1-Cre and Alx1c/c;R26RCreERT/+ mice demonstrated that this early Alx1 expression in the developing cranial mesoderm is required for EOM formation. Furthermore, we found that Alx1c/c;Mesp1-Cre embryos exhibit reduced Pitx2 expression as well as a significantly reduced number of PITX2+ presumptive EOM progenitor cells at E9−E9.5 compared with their control littermates, prior to any detectable increase in apoptosis of EOM progenitor cells. These data indicate that ALX1 is required in the early cranial mesoderm for proper activation or maintenance of Pitx2 expression within EOM precursor cells. EOM progenitor cells are very sensitive to changes in Pitx2 expression levels – with even Pitx2+/− embryos exhibiting significantly increased apoptosis of EOM progenitor cells (Zacharias et al., 2011). MATERIALS AND METHODSGeneration of Alx1 conditional knockout miceAlx1 conditional knockout mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 approach as previously described (Scott and Hu, 2019). Two sgRNA targeting sequences in intron 1 (5′-GTAAGATGTGGGTGGTACT-3′) and intron 2 (5′-GTGTGGTCAGTGATGACAAG-3′) regions of the Alx1 gene, were used in combination with CAS9 protein and oliogonucleotide donor templates to insert two loxP sites to flank the exon 2 region in C57BL/6J zygotes. Initial CRISPR founder mice only transmitted correctly integrated 5′ loxP site in the intron 1 region to the G1 generation. The heterozygous G1 male mice were crossed to C57BL/6J inbred females and the resultant G2 heterozygous carriers were intercrossed to generate mice homozygous for the 5′ loxP insertion. Zygotes from homozygous intercrosses were re-targeted to insert the 3′ loxP site in intron 2, and the founder mice crossed to C57BL/6J-inbred mice to generate mice heterozygous for the Alx1 conditional knockout allele (Alx1c), carrying correctly integrated loxP sites in both intron 1 and intron 2, verified by Sanger sequencing of allele-specific PCR products. Subsequent generations of Alx1c mice were maintained in the C57BL/6J inbred background and genotyped by using PCR primers Alx1floxF1 (5′-TTCCCTCTTCCATACACGCTTATG-3′) and Alx1floxR1 (5′-CGAGACAAGTCTTGCCTTCTTAGT-3′), which generate PCR products of 271 bp from the Alx1c allele and 233 bp from the wild-type allele, respectively. For each assay, at least three pairs of mutant and control littermates at each developmental stage were used. In total, 130 pregnant dams were used in this study. All animal work procedures were performed following the recommendations in the Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals by the National Institutes of Health and approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC Protocol #2022-0047) at Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center. MATERIALS AND METHODS

Generation of Alx1 conditional knockout miceAlx1 conditional knockout mice were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 approach as previously described (Scott and Hu, 2019). Two sgRNA targeting sequences in intron 1 (5′-GTAAGATGTGGGTGGTACT-3′) and intron 2 (5′-GTGTGGTCAGTGATGACAAG-3′) regions of the Alx1 gene, were used in combination with CAS9 protein and oliogonucleotide donor templates to insert two loxP sites to flank the exon 2 region in C57BL/6J zygotes. Initial CRISPR founder mice only transmitted correctly integrated 5′ loxP site in the intron 1 region to the G1 generation. The heterozygous G1 male mice were crossed to C57BL/6J inbred females and the resultant G2 heterozygous carriers were intercrossed to generate mice homozygous for the 5′ loxP insertion. Zygotes from homozygous intercrosses were re-targeted to insert the 3′ loxP site in intron 2, and the founder mice crossed to C57BL/6J-inbred mice to generate mice heterozygous for the Alx1 conditional knockout allele (Alx1c), carrying correctly integrated loxP sites in both intron 1 and intron 2, verified by Sanger sequencing of allele-specific PCR products. Subsequent generations of Alx1c mice were maintained in the C57BL/6J inbred background and genotyped by using PCR primers Alx1floxF1 (5′-TTCCCTCTTCCATACACGCTTATG-3′) and Alx1floxR1 (5′-CGAGACAAGTCTTGCCTTCTTAGT-3′), which generate PCR products of 271 bp from the Alx1c allele and 233 bp from the wild-type allele, respectively. For each assay, at least three pairs of mutant and control littermates at each developmental stage were used. In total, 130 pregnant dams were used in this study. All animal work procedures were performed following the recommendations in the Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals by the National Institutes of Health and approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC Protocol #2022-0047) at Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center. This study conformed to the updated ARRIVE 2.0 (Animal Research Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines for preclinical studies.
