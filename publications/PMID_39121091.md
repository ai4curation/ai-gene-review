G6PC2 encodes a glucose-6-phosphatase catalytic subunit that opposes the action
of glucokinase in pancreatic islets, thereby modulating the sensitivity of
insulin and glucagon secretion to glucose. In mice, G6pc2 is expressed at
~20-fold higher levels in β-cells than in α-cells, whereas in humans G6PC2 is
expressed at only ~5-fold higher levels in β-cells. We therefore hypothesize
that G6PC2 likely influences glucagon secretion to a greater degree in humans.
With a view to generating a humanized mouse that recapitulates augmented G6PC2
expression levels in α-cells, we sought to identify the genomic regions that
confer differential mouse G6pc2 expression in α-cells versus β-cells as well as
the evolutionary changes that have altered this ratio in humans. Studies in
islet-derived cell lines suggest that the elevated G6pc2 expression in mouse
β-cells versus α-cells is mainly due to a difference in the relative activity of
the proximal G6pc2 promoter in these cell types. Similarly, the smaller
difference in G6PC2 expression between α-cells and β-cells in humans is
potentially explained by a change in relative proximal G6PC2 promoter activity.
However, we show that both glucocorticoid levels and multiple differences in the
relative activity of eight transcriptional enhancers between mice and humans
likely contribute to differential G6PC2 expression. Finally, we show that a
mouse-specific non-coding RNA, Gm13613, whose expression is controlled by G6pc2
enhancer I, does not regulate G6pc2 expression, indicating that altered
expression of Gm13613 in a humanized mouse that contains both the human promoter
and enhancers should not affect G6PC2 function.

ResultsDifferential Proximal Promoter Activity Largely Explains the Elevated Mouse G6pc2 Cell Expression in β-Cells vs α-CellsPrimer extension analyses were used to compare endogenous G6pc2 expression in the αTC-6 and βTC-3 cell lines, which are well-established experimental models for mouse pancreatic islet α-cells and β-cells, respectively. The data revealed that the primer extension products generated using αTC-6 and βTC-3 RNA were the same size (Fig. 1A, left panel), indicating that the G6pc2 transcription start site, established in βTC-3 cells in earlier studies (Ebert et al. 1999), is identical in both cell lines. G6pc2 expression was ~11 fold higher in βTC-3 cells than αTC-6 cells (Fig. 1A, right panel), similar to the ~20 fold higher G6pc2 expression in mouse islet β-cells versus α-cells (Xin et al. 2016). This result suggests that these cell lines provide a suitable model system for exploring the molecular basis for the differential expression of G6pc2 in pancreatic islet α-cells and β-cells.Transient transfection analyses showed that the proximal G6pc2 promoter from −306 to +3, relative to the transcription start site, drives ~30 fold higher fusion gene expression in βTC-3 cells than αTC-6 cells (Fig. 1B), again similar to the ~20 fold higher endogenous G6pc2 expression in mouse islet β-cells versus α-cells (Xin et al. 2016). This result suggests that differential proximal promoter activity may largely explain the differential expression of G6pc2 in β-cells versus α-cells.A 5’ deletion analysis was performed to investigate whether specific cis-acting elements drive the higher G6pc2 promoter activity in βTC-3 cells relative to αTC-6 cells. Deletion of the promoter region from −306 to −197 markedly reduced G6pc2 promoter activity in βTC-3 cells (Fig. 2A), with a further reduction observed following deletion from −197 to −97 (Fig. 2B). To compare the relative ability of these putative mouse G6pc2 enhancers to promote gene transcription in αTC-6 cells and βTC-3 cells, these regions were isolated and ligated 5’ of a G6PC2-luciferase fusion gene containing the proximal −324 to + 3 promoter sequence (Fig. 4B), either in the correct (same) (Fig. 4) or inverted (Supplemental Figure 3) orientation relative to the G6pc2 locus. Luciferase expression directed by these fusion genes was then analyzed by transient transfection. All these putative enhancers increased reporter gene expression beyond that observed using only the −324/+3 G6PC2 promoter in both cell types, though for enhancers B, E and G this effect was dependent on orientation (Fig. 4C and Supplemental Figure 3A), inconsistent with the classical definition of an enhancer (Blackwood and Kadonaga 1998; Panigrahi and O’Malley 2021). For the other enhancers, while they were active in both orientations, the relative activity conferred by the correct and inverted orientations varied with cell type (Supplemental Figure 4).

A comparison of the ratio of enhancer activity in αTC-6 cells and βTC-3 cells revealed a complex pattern, with some enhancers showing relatively higher activity in αTC-6 cells whereas others showed relatively higher activity in βTC-3 cells, depending on the orientation examined (Fig. 4D and Supplemental Figure 3B). Notably, enhancers C, G and H were more active in αTC-6 cells independent of orientation (Fig. 4D and Supplemental Figure 3B), such that they may partially offset the influence of the proximal promoter. Overall, these data suggest that, in addition to differences in proximal promoter activity (Fig. 1–3), variations in enhancer activity may also contribute to relative G6pc2 expression in α-cells and β-cells.

DiscussionOur analysis of the mouse G6pc2 and human G6PC2 proximal promoters and enhancers suggest that the influence of a single proximal promoter cis-acting element or a single cis-acting element in a specific enhancer cannot explain the differences between mouse G6pc2 and human G6PC2 expression in α-cells versus β-cells. Instead, the results are extremely complex such that the relative expression of mouse G6pc2 and human G6PC2 in α-cells and β-cells likely results from the combination of multiple differences in proximal promoter and enhancer activity. This leads to the broad conclusion that the generation of a humanized mouse expressing human G6PC2 at the levels observed in human α-cells and β-cells will require the exchange of the entire G6PC2 locus, including part of the neighboring ABCB11 gene that includes enhancers H and I. It remains unclear as to why the regulation of G6PC2 expression evolved to be so complex. It is striking that a short ~400 bp proximal promoter sequence appears sufficient to drive expression of the INS promoter in β-cells (Hay and Docherty 2006) and that this INS promoter region binds many of the same factors that also bind the G6PC2 promoter (Hutton and O’Brien 2009). We would speculate that since there are three G6PC isoforms, an ancestral G6PC gene, in contrast to the INS gene, may have been widely expressed such that the highly restricted expression of G6PC2 might have arisen due to the accumulation of proximal promoter changes and enhancers that served to restrict its expression to predominantly islet beta cells.A number of different strategies have been developed to humanize mice (Zhu, et al. 2019), most notably a recently described approach that uses a sequential, iterative genome rewriting methodology to precisely target a specific region of the mouse genome, allowing for deletion of mouse DNA and insertion of over 100 kbp of human DNA (Zhang, et al. 2023). While technically feasible, there are several potential caveats with the use of this technology to generate a G6PC2 humanized mouse. First, we have not fully defined the genomic sequences necessary to confer G6pc2 expression in mice. While previous studies have shown that the proximal G6pc2 promoter is sufficient to drive islet-specific expression of a transgene in newborn mice (Frigeri, et al. 2004), expression was not sustained in adult animals. Similarly, a ~15 kbp transgene containing enhancers A, B, C, E and F also exhibited strong islet-specific expression in newborn mice but was again unable to sustain that expression in adult animals (Wang et al. 2008). More recent studies suggest that the lack of sustained G6pc2 transgene expression in adult mouse islets might have been due to the absence of enhancers H and I (Keller et al. 2023). However, we cannot be certain that additional, distant enhancers do not exist beyond the immediate G6PC2 locus that are also important for G6PC2 expression, though an analysis of promoter-enhancer interactions in human islets found no evidence for such distant enhancers (Miguel-Escalada et al. 2019). Second, Gm13613 expression will likely be altered since mouse and human G6PC2 enhancer I, the location of the Gm13613 promoter, are only ~65% conserved (Keller et al. 2023). Unfortunately, given the number of factors that likely regulate G6PC2 expression, the veracity of this caveat will only become apparent once the humanized mouse is generated and analyzed.

One option for further modelling this work would be to specifically address the role of G6PC2 on glucagon secretion in humans. To achieve this, one could take advantage of the fact that a specific allele of rs2232326, that gives rise to a missense variant (S324P), is present at a frequency at least 10 times higher in East Asians than in other ancestry groups. The missense variant is associated with a marked reduction in G6PC2 protein expression (Overway, et al. 2021). It should therefore be possible to directly compare glucagon levels in humans with high versus low G6PC2 expression. This approach would parallel a recent study that examined the impact of a missense PAX4 variant by exploiting the much higher incidence of this variant in the East Asian population (Lau, et al. 2023). If G6PC2 does regulate glucagon secretion in humans, then the combined inhibition of G6PC2 in α-cells and β-cells is likely to have additive, rather than offsetting, benefits on FBG.
