The human calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) detects fluctuations in the
extracellular Ca2+ concentration and maintains Ca2+ homeostasis1,2. It also
mediates diverse cellular processes not associated with Ca2+ balance3-5. The
functional pleiotropy of CaSR arises in part from its ability to signal through
several G-protein subtypes6. We determined structures of CaSR in complex with G
proteins from three different subfamilies: Gq, Gi and Gs. We found that the
homodimeric CaSR of each complex couples to a single G protein through a common
mode. This involves the C-terminal helix of each Gα subunit binding to a shallow
pocket that is formed in one CaSR subunit by all three intracellular loops
(ICL1-ICL3), an extended transmembrane helix 3 and an ordered C-terminal region.
G-protein binding expands the transmembrane dimer interface, which is further
stabilized by phospholipid. The restraint imposed by the receptor dimer, in
combination with ICL2, enables G-protein activation by facilitating
conformational transition of Gα. We identified a single Gα residue that
determines Gq and Gs versus Gi selectivity. The length and flexibility of ICL2
allows CaSR to bind all three Gα subtypes, thereby conferring capacity for
promiscuous G-protein coupling.

## Full Text

The calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) is the primary detector of extracellular Ca2+ in human1,2. Most abundantly expressed in the parathyroid gland and kidney, it precisely controls extracellular Ca2+ homeostasis by negatively regulating the secretion of parathyroid hormone (PTH) and renal Ca2+ reabsorption2. Naturally occurring mutations can lead to hypocalcemia and life threatening hyperparathyroidism7. CaSR also modulates diverse cellular processes unrelated to Ca2+ balance, such as intestinal nutrient sensing4.

CaSR is a class C G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) that functions as a homodimer2. The functional pleiotropy of CaSR stems, in part, from its ability to activate multiple downstream signaling pathways2,6. Previous studies indicate that it can couple to all four G-protein subtypes in cell-dependent contexts6. In parathyroid and kidney, CaSR signals through Gq/11 and Gi/o8,9. In breast and pituitary tumors, it switches to Gs10,11. In kidney, the receptor also activates G12/1312,13. It is unknown how CaSR supports signaling through different G-protein subfamilies.

Here we report cryo-EM structures of CaSR complexed with four different G proteins from three subtypes, including Gq, Gi and Gs. Each receptor-G-protein pair was assembled in either detergent micelles or phospholipid nanodiscs to mimic the native environment of the CaSR signaling complex. These structures revealed a common CaSR-G-protein binding mode, as well as interactions that are specific to each G protein. Relative to the pre-G-protein-coupled active state (CaSRpreG), we identified conformational transitions in CaSR that enable promiscuous G-protein activation while maintaining G-protein-binding specificity.

Assembly and structure determination of CaSR-G-protein complexesWe determined the cryo-EM structures of human CaSR bound to its cognate G proteins, Gq, Gs and two members of the Gi/o subfamily, Gi1 and Gi3 (Fig. 1, Extended Data Table 1). Dominant-negative mutations were introduced into a full-length Gαi3 to stabilize its nucleotide-free form14. We also used miniGα variants of Gq (chimeric miniGαisq), Gi1 (miniGαi1) and Gs (chimeric miniGαis) to increase G-protein thermostability15. Both approaches enhanced receptor binding14,15.To initiate complex formation with each G protein, we activated CaSR with the co-agonists Ca2+ and the tryptophan analog TNCA16,17, as well as the positive allosteric modulator (PAM) NPS R-56818 (abbreviated as R-568). We purified two CaSR-G-protein complexes in detergent in the presence of stabilizing antibodies including the Gs-specific nanobody Nb3519 or the single-chain antibody fragment scFv16 against Gi20. In addition, we reconstituted three CaSR-G-protein complexes in nanodiscs with a mixture of phosphatidylcholine (POPC) and phosphatidylglycerol (POPG) lipids (Extended Data Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 1). These complexes were formed without the inclusion of Nb35 or scFv16, suggesting that the extended lipid environment provided by nanodiscs stabilizes the complexes in the absence of antibody.We assembled the CaSR-miniGisq complexes both in detergent and nanodiscs, and their cryo-EM reconstructions reached overall resolutions of 3.5 Å and 3.4 Å, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 1–5). We obtained a CaSR-miniGi1 complex in detergent and a CaSR-Gi3 complex in nanodiscs with overall resolutions of 3.6 Å and 3.8 Å, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 1–5). Finally, we solved a CaSR-miniGis complex structure in nanodiscs at an overall resolution of 3.6 Å (Extended Data Fig. 1–4).

The binding of a second G protein would require a counter rotation that could not be achieved without bending TM6 of CaSRfree, straightening TM6 of CaSRG, and releasing the first G-protein molecule.

G-protein-induced conformational changes in the receptor

Within each CaSR-G-protein complex, the heterotrimeric G protein is bound to one subunit of the homodimeric receptor (Fig. 1, Extended Data Fig. 5a,b). Each CaSR subunit consists of a large extracellular domain (ECD), a transmembrane domain (TMD) and an extended cytoplasmic tail2. The ECD contains a Venus flytrap (VFT) module that binds orthosteric ligands, followed by a cysteine-rich (CR) region and a peptide linker. The TMD is composed of seven helices (TM1–7) inter-connected by three extracellular (ECL1–3) and three intracellular (ICL1–3) loops.

In the extracellular region, all CaSR-G-protein complexes are highly similar to the CaSRpreG structures16,21 (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). First, the VFT modules of both CaSR subunits adopt a closed conformation and bind TNCA between their two lobes (LB1 and LB2). Second, the CaSR subunits associate through all individual LB1, LB2 and CR domains, a distinct feature of the active conformation. Finally, Ca2+ bridges the LB2 domain of one subunit and CR domain of the second subunit to facilitate homodimer interactions in the active receptor.

In the transmembrane region, G-protein coupling induces coordinated conformational changes in the G-protein-bound subunit (CaSRG), which propagates to the G-protein-free subunit (CaSRfree) (Fig. 2a, Extended Data Fig. 6a–d). Residues belonging to each subunit are annotated accordingly by ‘G’ or ‘free’ following their Ballesteros-Weinstein numbering designations22. The heterotrimeric G protein binds to CaSRG primarily through the C-terminal H5 helix of its Gα subunit (Fig. 2a). The G-protein-binding site on the CaSRG subunit is a shallow pocket formed by all three ICLs, an extended TM3 helix, and an ordered C-terminal H8 helix (Fig. 2a). Among these, the ICL2, ICL3 and C-terminal region have been implicated in G-protein coupling by previous mutational analyses23–26.

In the CaSRG subunit, ICL2 and ICL3 undergo dramatic transformations whereas ICL1 remains essentially unchanged relative to CaSRfree (Fig. 2b). First, ICL3 of CaSRG descends to meet the Gα H5 helix. The key G-protein-binding residue F801ICL3 travels 6.5 Å and inverts its side chain from the TMD dimer interface to the interior of the CaSRG transmembrane bundle (Fig. 2b,c). This movement establishes a hydrophobic core at the CaSRG-Gα interface. The reorganization of ICL3 also shifts TM6 toward the extracellular membrane surface by approximately 0.8 Å (Fig. 2b). The conformational changes involving ICL3 are common to all complexes.

Second, the intracellular end of TM3 is extended downward from the membrane by roughly two turns along its principal helical path into ICL2, enveloping one side of the Gα H5 helix. In the CaSRfree subunit, the entire ICL2 remains disordered as in the CaSRpreG state21 (Fig. 2b). In contrast, ICL2 becomes fully ordered in the CaSRG subunit except when bound to miniGis, where it forms a partial loop (Fig. 2d). The C-terminal stretch of ICL2 from its turning point to TM4 (P711-G720) adopts substantially different conformations in various CaSR-G-protein complexes (Fig. 2d), suggesting that this ICL2 region contributes to the promiscuous G-protein coupling of CaSR.

Third, G-protein coupling stabilizes the C-terminal H8 helix of CaSRG so that it becomes ordered within detergent-solubilized complexes of CaSR-miniGisq and CaSR-miniGi1, as well as nanodisc-reconstituted CaSR-Gi3 (Fig. 2b). CaSRG H8 flanks the side of Gα H5 helix opposite ICL2 (Fig. 2a).

G-protein binding also causes two global movements of the CaSRG TMD that reorient it with respect to the CaSRfree subunit (Extended Fig. 6e). The CaSRG subunit pivots closer to the vertical dimer axis. As a result, the CaSRG TM6 helix is shifted closer to the extracellular membrane surface. The global pivot starts gradually in the CR region and is most pronounced at the intracellular side of the TMD.

G-protein coupling also causes CaSRG to rotate and slide relative to the CaSRfree subunit (Fig. 2e). If we align CaSRfree onto one subunit of the inactive and CaSRpreG dimers, it is evident that CaSRG further advances the rotation that began with the inactive orientation and progressed to the CaSRpreG state (Fig. 2e, Extended Data Fig. 6a,c). Furthermore, the CaSRfree and CaSRG transmembrane bundles slide closer to each other, forming a tighter homodimer interface (Extended Data Fig. 6a,c).

This arrangement also alters the elements at the subunit interface between CaSRfree and CaSRG. In the inactive state, the CaSR transmembrane bundles do not make direct contact but face each other remotely through their TM5 and TM6 helices. In the CaSRpreG state, the two subunits form a TM6-mediated dimer interface, which is facilitated by a kink in TM6 of both subunits21,27–30. These helices are bent at F8216.53, whose side chains in both subunits are diverted outward to face the dimer interface21,27. Upon G-protein coupling, the CaSRG subunit comes into close proximity with CaSRfree, forming a new homodimer interface through TM6 and ECL3 of both subunits as well as TM7 of CaSRfree. The TM6 helix of CaSRfree becomes straightened and its F8216.53 is redirected toward the transmembrane core to avoid a steric clash with the approaching CaSRG (Extended Data Fig. 6d). The lack of a kink compresses the CaSRfree helix and contributes to the relative displacement between TM6 helices, as does the upward global pivot of CaSRG (Extended Data Fig. 6e). Nevertheless, the two TMDs reach the same level at the extracellular membrane boundary as a result of the different conformations adopted by subsequent ECL3 loops (Extended Data Fig. 6f,g), consistent with the TMDs being confined within the membrane bilayer.

One important question regarding CaSR activation concerns the possibility that the receptor couples to two G-protein molecules simultaneously. At first glance, an activated CaSR homodimer might have sufficient space to accommodate two Gq or Gi proteins, but not a Gs pair due to the longer hgh4 loop in Gαs (Extended Data Fig. 6h). Our structural analysis demonstrates, however, that the CaSR dimer arrangement induced by the binding of one G protein of any subtype precludes the association of a second. Specifically, G-protein coupling generates a subunit rotation that straightens TM6 in CaSRfree while preserving a kinked TM6 in CaSRG (Extended Data Fig. 6d). The binding of a second G protein would require a counter rotation that could not be achieved without bending TM6 of CaSRfree, straightening TM6 of CaSRG, and releasing the first G-protein molecule.

G-protein activation mechanismThe CaSR-G-protein complexes provide insights into the receptor-dependent mechanism of G-protein activation. The H5 helix of Gα subunit possesses an N-terminal transmission module and a C-terminal interface module31. In an inactive G protein, the H5 N-terminal module interacts with the H1 helix, maintaining its structural order to coordinate the GDP-binding pocket31. The H5 C-terminal module is disordered in the inactive state but becomes ordered upon binding to a GPCR31,32 (Fig. 3a). When bound to a receptor, the C-terminal module forms a hook and a helix that is off-register from the N-terminal module by a 60° rotation and 5 Å translation into the receptor32 (Fig. 3a). Because of their physical association, the two modules are integrated into an intact H5 helix. This integration dissociates the N-terminal module from multiple regions in Gα including its H1 helix, partially disordering H1 and destabilizing the GDP-binding pocket. The subsequent release of GDP and its replacement by GTP leads to the activation of G protein for downstream signaling.We identified two elements in CaSR that are responsible for facilitating the conformational transition of a G protein during activation: ICL2 and CaSR dimer assembly. First, during the aforementioned integration process of H5 N-terminal and C-terminal modules, the dissociation of H5 from H1 and other Gα elements requires substantial energy. Therefore, the natural tendency is for the remainder of Gα, including H1, to rotate and translate along with H5. The potential consequence of this movement is revealed by aligning the H5 helices of inactive GDP-bound and CaSR-coupled Gα subunits. Specifically, we observed a clash between the inactive GTPase domain and the ICL2 of CaSRG (Fig. 3a–c). Decoupling of H5 from H1 is necessary to avoid this steric clash.To confirm the importance of the steric hindrance provided by ICL2 in G-protein activation, we tested the functional competence of various ICL2 deletion mutants, which are expected to have reduced propensity for steric clash. We employed a bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET)-based assay to quantify the dissociation of Gαq, Gαi1 and Gαi3 subunits from Gβγ heterodimers upon CaSR coupling. We also measured CaSR-mediated Gq, Gi1 and Gs activation through inositol phosphate-one (IP1) accumulation using either Gq or chimeric Gαqi9 or Gαqs5 proteins, respectively33,34. As Gs weakly couples to CaSR13, we were only able to detect its activity in the IP1 assay, possibly due to downstream signal amplification.In both the BRET and IP1 assays, we found that shortening ICL2 by one to ten residues progressively decreased the potency and efficacy of Ca2+ when activating each G protein through CaSR (Fig. 3d, Extended Data Fig. 7a–c). Furthermore, ICL2 deletions in two different regions, one near the turning point and the other around its C-terminal end, displayed similar impairment of receptor function. This indicates that deletion length, rather than position, determines severity. In the BRET assay particularly, removing five to ten residues from ICL2 nearly abolished CaSR-dependent signaling through each G protein assessed. These data demonstrate that an intact ICL2 is critical to CaSR-mediated G-protein activation.A second element that enables G-protein activation is the restriction imposed by the CaSR dimer assembly. The rotation and upward translation of the Gα H5 helix also propels the bound CaSRG to move in the same manner (Fig. 3c). Due to its close association with CaSRfree, the movement of CaSRG is limited to the aforementioned reorientation toward CaSRfree. This explains why dimerization of CaSR is also required to prevent the GTPase domain from following the movement of H5 in Gα. This obstruction promotes H5-H1 dissociation, and in turn, the transition of Gα to an active conformation.

G-protein activation mechanism

The CaSR-G-protein complexes provide insights into the receptor-dependent mechanism of G-protein activation. The H5 helix of Gα subunit possesses an N-terminal transmission module and a C-terminal interface module31. In an inactive G protein, the H5 N-terminal module interacts with the H1 helix, maintaining its structural order to coordinate the GDP-binding pocket31. The H5 C-terminal module is disordered in the inactive state but becomes ordered upon binding to a GPCR31,32 (Fig. 3a). When bound to a receptor, the C-terminal module forms a hook and a helix that is off-register from the N-terminal module by a 60° rotation and 5 Å translation into the receptor32 (Fig. 3a). Because of their physical association, the two modules are integrated into an intact H5 helix. This integration dissociates the N-terminal module from multiple regions in Gα including its H1 helix, partially disordering H1 and destabilizing the GDP-binding pocket. The subsequent release of GDP and its replacement by GTP leads to the activation of G protein for downstream signaling.

We identified two elements in CaSR that are responsible for facilitating the conformational transition of a G protein during activation: ICL2 and CaSR dimer assembly. First, during the aforementioned integration process of H5 N-terminal and C-terminal modules, the dissociation of H5 from H1 and other Gα elements requires substantial energy. Therefore, the natural tendency is for the remainder of Gα, including H1, to rotate and translate along with H5. The potential consequence of this movement is revealed by aligning the H5 helices of inactive GDP-bound and CaSR-coupled Gα subunits. Specifically, we observed a clash between the inactive GTPase domain and the ICL2 of CaSRG (Fig. 3a–c). Decoupling of H5 from H1 is necessary to avoid this steric clash.

To confirm the importance of the steric hindrance provided by ICL2 in G-protein activation, we tested the functional competence of various ICL2 deletion mutants, which are expected to have reduced propensity for steric clash. We employed a bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET)-based assay to quantify the dissociation of Gαq, Gαi1 and Gαi3 subunits from Gβγ heterodimers upon CaSR coupling. We also measured CaSR-mediated Gq, Gi1 and Gs activation through inositol phosphate-one (IP1) accumulation using either Gq or chimeric Gαqi9 or Gαqs5 proteins, respectively33,34. As Gs weakly couples to CaSR13, we were only able to detect its activity in the IP1 assay, possibly due to downstream signal amplification.

In both the BRET and IP1 assays, we found that shortening ICL2 by one to ten residues progressively decreased the potency and efficacy of Ca2+ when activating each G protein through CaSR (Fig. 3d, Extended Data Fig. 7a–c). Furthermore, ICL2 deletions in two different regions, one near the turning point and the other around its C-terminal end, displayed similar impairment of receptor function. This indicates that deletion length, rather than position, determines severity. In the BRET assay particularly, removing five to ten residues from ICL2 nearly abolished CaSR-dependent signaling through each G protein assessed. These data demonstrate that an intact ICL2 is critical to CaSR-mediated G-protein activation.

A second element that enables G-protein activation is the restriction imposed by the CaSR dimer assembly. The rotation and upward translation of the Gα H5 helix also propels the bound CaSRG to move in the same manner (Fig. 3c). Due to its close association with CaSRfree, the movement of CaSRG is limited to the aforementioned reorientation toward CaSRfree. This explains why dimerization of CaSR is also required to prevent the GTPase domain from following the movement of H5 in Gα. This obstruction promotes H5-H1 dissociation, and in turn, the transition of Gα to an active conformation.

G-protein promiscuity and selectivity

Our structures demonstrate that ICL2 of CaSR possesses both conformational flexibility and ample length to adapt to the binding surfaces of different G-protein partners, thus allowing for promiscuous coupling to multiple G proteins. First, a comparison of the different CaSR-G-protein complexes indicates that ICL2 is the G-protein-binding element with the greatest conformational variation (Fig. 4a). Second, two critical residues along the H5 helix of Gα, G.H5.23 and G.H5.19, determine G-protein-subtype selectivity. They assume different identities in specific Gα subtypes and are accommodated by distinct conformations of ICL2.

The single amino acid at Gα position G.H5.23 determines Gq and Gs (YG.H5.23) versus Gi (CG.H5.23) selectivity. The CaSR ICL2 residue W719 assumes an inward-facing orientation to interact with CG.H5.23 of Gαi3 and miniGαi1, but an outward-facing orientation to accommodate the bulky aromatic residue YG.H5.23 of miniGαisq and miniGαis (Fig. 4e,5a).

The conformational plasticity of ICL2 arises in part from its extended length. While ICL2 deletions impaired the activation of all G proteins tested, their effects on various G proteins differ in magnitude. Specifically, we found that the impact of ICL2 deletion was greater on Gq and Gs than on Gi (Fig. 3d, Extended Data Fig. 7a,c). For example, a four-residue deletion near the turning point of ICL2 (ΔI710-S713) severely reduced CaSR-mediated IP1 production through Gαq and Gαqs5, but had little effect on Gαqi9, a chimera of Gαq and Gαi1 (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, when the Gαq residue YG.H5.23 was replaced by its counterpart CG.H5.23 in Gαi, the same four-residue deletion in ICL2 failed to disrupt activation of the Gαq-YG.H5.23C mutant. We confirmed the results on Gq using the BRET-based G-protein activation assay (Fig. 5c). We reasoned that a longer ICL2 is needed to circumvent the bulky side chain of YG.H5.23 in Gαq and Gαs. In contrast, the smaller CG.H5.23 found in Gαi presents less hindrance for ICL2 to reach and form a stable interface with Gα H5. These observations suggest that shortening ICL2 also restricts its conformational adaptability.

We corroborated the selectivity determinant role of Gα residue G.H5.23 using GABAB receptor, a class C GPCR that exclusively couples to Gi/o35. Compared with CaSR, GABAB has a shorter ICL2 that is incompatible with YG.H5.23 of Gαq and Gαs (Fig. 5d). Using both the IP1 accumulation and BRET assays, we found that the YG.H5.23C mutation in Gαq and Gαs was sufficient to enable the GABAB receptor to couple to each of these G proteins (Fig. 5e). Conversely, the CG.H5.23Y mutation in Gαi impaired canonical GABAB signaling through Gi (Fig. 5e). These data indicate that YG.H5.23 in the native forms of Gαq and Gαs prevents them from coupling to the GABAB receptor.

In addition, the Gα residue at position G.H5.19 differentiates Gq and Gi (NG.H5.19) from Gs (HG.H5.19) (Fig. 4e, Extended Data Fig. 8b). In all our CaSR-G-protein complexes, this residue contacts the turning point of ICL2 and directs the loop conformation (Fig. 4e). In CaSR-miniGis, the larger HG.H5.19 of miniGαis H5 pushes the ICL2 away, leading to partial disordering of the subsequent loop segment (714–717) (Fig. 4e).

Third, the extended length of ICL2 also provides CaSR with the flexibility to adapt to different members within a G-protein subfamily. We compared the activation of two Gi/o subtype proteins using the BRET assay. We found that Gi1 is more tolerant of ICL2 deletions than Gi3 (Fig. 3d, Extended Data Fig. 7a), consistent with our structural observation that Gαi3 features a longer and outstretched EG.H5.22, instead of a shorter and bent DG.H5.22 in Gαi1 (Fig. 4e, Extended Data Fig. 8b). In addition, ICL2 contains a short helix when coupled to Gi3 and therefore has a relatively stringent length requirement. In contrast, the loop adopts a more extended and flexible conformation in the presence of miniGi1.

Taken together, the plasticity of ICL2 provides the key mechanism of promiscuous G-protein coupling by CaSR and in turn its functional diversity, while a single Gα residue, G.H5.23, serves as a key determinant of coupling specificity.

Symmetry to asymmetry activation pathway

As a homodimer, CaSR possesses inherent two-fold symmetry. When completely inactive, the receptor displays symmetry in its ECD and TMD16,21,28–30. This manifests as an open-open ECD conformation with unoccupied ligand-binding VFTs, and two distant transmembrane bundles with opposing TM5-TM6 surfaces16,21,28–30. Synthetic NAM molecules do not disrupt this inactive conformational symmetry21.

Along the activation pathway, the ECD and TMD symmetries are altered asynchronously. Three separate intermediate states have been observed with different combinations of ECD and TMD symmetry. First, an open-closed ECD conformation is coupled with an inactive TMD arrangement27,29. The closed cleft is likely occupied by an ambient amino acid while the open cleft remains vacant27,29. Second, a closed-closed ECD is also found to pair with an inactive TMD when L-Trp is present, but Ca2+ is absent29. Third, the presence of L-Trp, Ca2+, and NAM results in closed-closed ECD with an intermediate TM6-facing arrangement27,28. In summary, CaSR intermediate states have demonstrated both asymmetric and symmetric ECD conformations, but only symmetric TMDs.

When CaSR is activated by both amino acids and Ca2+, but unbound to G protein, its ECD adopts the symmetric closed-closed conformation21,27–30. The TMD forms a symmetric dimer through a TM6-TM6 interface21. However, the dimer axes of these domains are offset by a small 4.5° angle21.

The presence of a PAM can alter receptor symmetry. The PAM etelcalcetide binds to the ECD dimer interface and stabilizes its symmetric closed-closed conformation27. All TMD-targeting PAMs preserve ECD symmetry, but they affect TMD symmetry differently21,27,28. R-568-bound CaSR exhibits two-fold symmetry in the TMDs21. Conversely, cinacalcet- and evocalcet-bound structures have vertically displaced TMDs and distinct PAM poses27,28. This asymmetry, found in the absence of G protein, is unlike that observed here when CaSR is associated with any of four different G proteins.

G-protein coupling retains the closed-closed ECD symmetry, but renders the TMD in an asymmetric conformation. The TMD asymmetry is manifested in several aspects: rearrangement of ICL2(G) and ICL3(G), ordering of H8(G), relative displacement of TM6 helices, and distinct PAM poses. G-protein-induced asymmetry in CaSR is most likely achieved by a transition from a symmetric CaSRpreG conformation.

Cryo-EM data processing

We applied a similar cryo-EM processing workflow to each complex structure, which included particle selection using the Topaz neural network-based picker41, extensive heterogeneous refinement to isolate a homogeneous class of particles for the CaSR-G-protein complex, non-uniform refinement of the overall reconstruction, and local refinement of the CaSR ECD, CaSR TMD, and G protein to compensate for the relative motion between these components (Extended Data Fig. 2,3).

Cryo-EM image processing of each CaSR-G-protein dataset was performed in cryoSPARC v3.3.242. The Patch Motion module was used to correct frame image motions and conduct dose-weighting of the raw image stacks. Patch-based contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation was determined based on the aligned micrographs. After manual inspection of the micrographs and their power spectra, a subset of the micrographs were curated for further processing.

An initial set of particles was automatically picked from a small number of micrographs using Blob Picker. After 2D classifications and 3D ab initio reconstruction, projection-based templates were created from the ab initio model and used to identify particles from the entire dataset. The particles selected based on these templates underwent 2D classifications followed by heterogeneous ab initio reconstructions, which yielded 3D models for CaSR-G-protein complex, free receptor and empty nanodiscs. The ab initio model for the CaSR-G-protein complex was subjected to heterogeneous refinement and additional 2D classifications to isolate a relatively homogeneous set of particles for the CaSR-G-protein complex. These particles were refined with the non-uniform refinement algorithm. Approximately 1,000 manually selected particles based on this set were then input into the Topaz Train routine to train a model for the Topaz neural network-based particle picker41. Based on the trained model, the Topaz Extract module was used to pick particles from all the curated micrographs.

The Topaz-picked particle set was processed through multiple rounds of heterogeneous refinement using the reconstruction obtained from the template-picked particles as the input. Additional 2D classification served to further expunge low-quality particles. The remaining particles were then subjected to non-uniform refinement. For the CaSR-miniGisq complexes in nanodiscs and in detergent, both global and local CTF refinements were also conducted to correct beam tilt.

To address the conformational flexibility exhibited by individual components of each complex, we performed local refinement on the ECD and TMDs of the CaSR homodimer as well as the heterotrimeric G protein with a soft mask covering each region. The local refinement procedures substantially improved the map quality in each region. After density modification in Phenix43, the ECD, TMD and G-protein reconstructions were aligned to the global map. A composite map was generated by combining the three local reconstructions using the maximum values from the components (vop maximum command) in UCSF Chimera44.

BRET-based G-protein activation assay

We performed bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET)-based assays to quantify the activation of heterotrimeric G protein upon CaSR coupling56. Specifically, we employed a set of TRUPATH Gα/Gβ/Gγ biosensors (gift from Dr. Jefferey DiBerto and Dr. Bryan Roth, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill) to detect the dissociation of Renilla luciferase-labeled Gα (Gα-RLuc8) and green fluorescent protein-labeled Gγ (Gγ-GFP2) subunits56. To maximize the dynamic range of BRET signals, we used the reported optimal Gβ3/Gγ9-GFP2 acceptor with each of three Gα-Rluc8 donors, Gαq-Rluc8, Gαi1-Rluc8 and Gαi3-Rluc8.

HEK293T cells (ATCC CRL-3216, authenticated by ATCC, certified mycoplasma free by ATCC) were cultured at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO2 in DMEM media (Life Technologies) supplemented with 10% FBS. The cells were co-transfected with plasmids expressing CaSR, Gα-RLuc8, Gβ3, Gγ9-GFP2 at a ratio of 5:1:5:5 using polyethyleneimine (Polysciences, Warrington, PA). The BRET ratios were calculated as the ratio of the GFP2 emission to RLuc8 emission. We normalized all data by subtracting the BRET signal recorded at 0 mM Ca2+.

Cryo-EM reconstruction analysis.

a, Locally refined density maps corresponding to the ECD (top), TMD (middle) and G-protein regions (bottom) for each of the five CaSR-G-protein complexes. Maps are colored according to local resolution. b-f, Fourier Shell Correlation (FSC) curves (purple) corrected by high-resolution noise substitution for local refinement maps of ECD (left), TMD (middle), and G protein (right) of CaSR-miniGisq in nanodiscs (b), CaSR-miniGisq in detergent (c), CaSR-Gi3 in nanodiscs (d), CaSR-miniGi1 in detergent (e) and CaSR-miniGis in nanodiscs (f). Curves reaching the FSC cut-off value of 0.143 (blue line) determine the estimated resolution for the corresponding density.

Structural models and cryo-EM density of CaSR-G-protein complexes reconstituted in nanodiscs.

Structural models of helices, loops and ligands within corresponding densities of the CaSR-miniGisq (a-d), CaSR-Gi3 (e-h) and CaSR-miniGis (i-l) complexes in nanodsics. Residue labels signify the start and end of each helix or loop. CaSRfree (a, e, i) and CaSRG (b, f, j) TMD helices are displayed in blue and cyan, respectively, along with the co-agonist TNCA (gray) and PAM R-568 (yellow) in the corresponding subunit. The C-terminal module of Gα H5 helix (yellow), POPG (green), and CHS (magenta) are presented in (c), (g) and (k). CaSRG intracellular loops (ICL1, 2, 3) are shown in cyan in (d), (h) and (l). An ordered H8 helix is shown in cyan in (h).

The density for CaSRfree TMD helices are displayed at contour levels of 0.3–0.5 (a), 0.1 (e), and 0.35 (i), while CaSRG TMD helices are contoured at 0.23–0.5 (b), 0.1 (f), and 0.35 (j). TNCA is contoured at 0.5 (a, b), 0.3 (e, f), and 0.35 (i, j), while R-568 is contoured at 0.18–0.3 (a, b), 0.01 (e, f), and 0.13–0.35 (i, j). POPG is contoured at 0.3 (c), 0.05 (g), and 0.15 (k). CHS is contoured at 0.3 (c), 0.1 (g), and 0.1 (k). The CaSRG ICLs are contoured at 0.15 in all complexes (d, h, l). The CaSRG H8 helix is contoured at 0.15 (h). The C-terminal module of Gα H5 helix is contoured at 0.5 (c), 0.3 (g), and 0.35 (k).

Structural models and cryo-EM density of detergent-solubilized CaSR-G-protein complexes.

a, b, Cryo-EM density map (left) and structural model (right) of CaSR in complex with miniGisq (a), or miniGi1 (b). Each complex is solubilized in detergent and composed of the CaSRfree (blue), CaSRG (cyan), Gα (yellow), Gβ (violet) and Gγ (crimson) subunits. The nanobody Nb35 (light green) and single-chain antibody fragment scFv16 (dark gray) were used to stabilize the heterotrimeric G protein. The bound CaSR ligands include TNCA (gray), Ca2+ (green), PO43- (orange), and R-568 (yellow). An LMNG detergent (lime) is found at the TMD dimer interface. Multiple cholesterol (CLR, pink) and CHS (magenta) molecules surround the TMDs in the CaSR-miniGisq complex. N-linked glycans (NAG, light gray) are attached to the ECD.

c-j, Structural models of helices, loops and ligands within corresponding densities of the CaSR-miniGisq (c-f) and CaSR-miniGi1 (g-j) complexes in detergent. Residue labels signify the start and end of each helix or loop. CaSRfree (c, g) and CaSRG (d, h) TMD helices are displayed in blue and cyan, respectively, along with the co-agonist TNCA (gray) and PAM R-568 (yellow) in the corresponding subunit. The C-terminal module of Gα H5 helix (yellow), LMNG (lime), CLR (pink) and CHS (magenta) are presented in (e) and (i). CaSRG intracellular loops (ICL1, 2, 3) and H8 helix are shown in cyan in (f) and (j).

The density for CaSRfree TMD helices are displayed at contour levels of 0.2–0.3 (c) and 0.15–0.35 (g), while CaSRG TMD helices are contoured at 0.1–0.3 (d) and 0.15 (h). TNCA is contoured at 0.5 (c, d) and 0.3–0.35 (g, h), while R-568 is contoured at 0.2 (c, d) and 0.1–0.15 (g, h). LMNG is contoured at 0.2 (e) and 0.15 (i). Both CHS and CLR are contoured at 0.2 (e). The CaSRG ICLs are contoured at 0.15 in all complexes (f, j). The CaSRG H8 helix is contoured at 0.25 (f) and 0.15 (j). The C-terminal module of Gα H5 helix is contoured at 0.3 (e) and 0.35 (i).

CaSR dimer arrangement pre- and post-G-protein coupling.

a, Alignment of CaSR ECD (left) and TMD (right) of PAM-bound CaSRpreG (cyan; PDB: 7SIL) and CaSR-Gi3 complex in nanodiscs (lime).

b, Alignment of CaSR ECD (left) and TMD (right) of CaSR-miniGisq (orange), CaSR-Gi3 (lime) and CaSR-miniGis (yellow) complexes in nanodiscs.

c, TMD dimers of CaSR structures along its activation pathway. Elements at the TMD dimer interface are depicted in color during evolution from the inactive state to the G-protein-bound fully active state as agonist, PAM and finally G protein are added.

d, Superposition of one CaSRpreG subunit (cyan; PDB: 7SIL) onto CaSRfree of CaSR-Gi3 complex. Side (left) and top (right) views of the alignment show the S8206.52 side chain (lime) of CaSRG has space to remain adjacent to the inward-flipped F8216.53 of CaSRfree (gray), but would clash with the outward F8216.53 of CaSRpreG (red star). This would prevent TM6 helices of both subunits from remaining kinked at this position.

e, Alignment of CaSRfree (blue) and CaSRG (cyan) VFT modules from the CaSR-Gi3 complex. Red arrows illustrate the pivot of the corresponding CaSRG TMD toward the vertical dimer axis relative to CaSRfree, resulting in a shift of the CaSRG TM6 toward the extracellular membrane.

f, The CaSR TM6-TM6 interface of CaSR-Gi3 complex in a full-on side view. The Cα atoms of TM6 residues from CaSRfree (blue) are joined to their counterparts in CaSRG (cyan) by dashed lines to show the relative displacement of CaSRG TM6 toward the extracellular membrane. This persists up until the shallow ECL3 in CaSRG allows the CaSRfree ECL3 to reach a similar elevation. Solid lines for G830ECL3 Cα and N802ICL3 Cα pairs run nearly parallel to the membrane due to their positions being at the same level between the CaSRfree and CaSRG subunits.

g, Close-up views of the CaSR ECL2 and ECL3 in the PAM R-568-bound CaSRpreG structure (PDB: 7SIL) alongside CaSR-miniGisq, CaSR-Gi3 and CaSR-miniGis complexes in nanodiscs. Orientations of L756ECL2, E757ECL2 and Y829ECL3 side chains are shown in each structure. Y829ECL3(G) mediates the interaction between ECL3 and ECL2 of CaSRG and may play a role in stabilizing specific loop conformations within this subunit.

h, The CaSR-miniGisq, CaSR-Gi3 and CaSR-miniGis complexes, each with its CaSRG TMD and bound G protein duplicated and aligned onto CaSRfree as an outline depiction. The actual G protein (colored) bound to CaSRG yields a clash with its imaginary partner through their Gαs-derived hgh4 regions in the cases of CaSR-miniGisq and CaSR-miniGis. Additional clashes with s6h5, HG and H4 occur in the case of CaSR-miniGis. The hgh4 loops in native Gαq and Gαi are 12–13 residues shorter than in Gαs. The CaSR-Gi3 complex does not show such clash, consistent with a shorter hgh4 loop.

CaSR interaction with G protein.

a, Functional analysis of CaSR ICL2 deletion mutants lacking three to ten residues near its C-terminal end. Ca2+-induced activation of Gq, Gi3 and Gi1 were measured using BRET assay.

b, Un-corrected IP1 accumulation data collected from assay buffer alone or HEK293 T/17 cells transfected with wild-type CaSR or pcDNA3.1. The vertical orange line marks 8 mM Ca2+, which is chosen as the maximum Ca2+ dose in our IP1 experiments.

c, Functional analysis of CaSR ICL2 deletion mutants lacking one to ten residues around its turning point or near its C-terminal end. Ca2+-induced IP1 accumulation through wild-type and mutant CaSR was measured in Gαq/11-knockout HEK293 cells co-transfected with Gαq, Gαqi9 or Gαqs5.

Data points represent averages ± s.e.m, with the number of independent experiments (n) indicated. Cell surface expression levels are described in the Methods (a-c).

d, Alignment of CaSRG TMDs showing relative orientations of the Gα H5 helices in all five of our CaSR-G-protein complexes. The axis of each individual H5 helix is represented by a straight rod and colored to match the corresponding CaSR-G-protein complex. Regions I and II correspond to the C-terminal and N-terminal ends of the H5 helices, respectively.

e, Regions I and II in (d), viewed from the C- and N-terminal ends of H5 helices, respectively. Using the Gαi3 H5 helix as the reference, the angle between the axes of each pair of H5 helices is marked for their N-terminal ends in region II. These angles reflect the relative orientations of H5 helix insertion into the receptor among various CaSR-G-protein complexes. The C-terminal ends of the H5 helices are closer together as they converge on the G-protein-binding pocket of CaSR.

f, g, Comparison of the depth of Gα H5 helix insertion between nanodiscs-reconstituted and detergents-solubilized complexes using the same alignment from (d). The CaSR-miniGisq in nanodiscs manifests deeper penetration of H5 helix than the same complex in detergent (f), and CaSR-Gi3 in nanodiscs similarly shows a deeper insertion of H5 when compared with CaSR-miniGi1 in detergent (g).

CaSR-G-protein interactions.

a, b, Barcode presentations of CaSR (a) and Gα (b) residues that take part in the CaSR-G-protein interfaces of our five complexes. Stars designate residues with functional significance as per our mutational analyses. In (a), filled circles indicate G-protein-binding residues of CaSR that are common to all five complexes (green) or in specific complexes (gray). Empty circles represent CaSR residues not in contact with G protein. Dotted circles mark disordered residues. In (b), G-protein residues that contact CaSR in complexes bearing miniGisq (cyan), Gi3 (green), miniGi1 (green) or miniGis (blue) are interspersed with those that lack contact with the receptor (empty).

c, d, Functional analysis of G-protein-binding residues in CaSR. Ca2+ potency (EC50) and maximal response (Emax) of wild-type and mutant receptors are compared for Gq, Gi3 and Gi1 (c) as well as Gq, Gqi9 and Gqs5 (d) using BRET-based G-protein activation and IP1 accumulation assay, respectively.

e, Analysis of Ca2+ potency (EC50) and maximal response (Emax) of wild-type CaSR-mediated IP1 accumulation through wild-type or mutant Gαq. Ca2+-stimulated IP1 accumulation was measured in Gαq/11-knockout HEK293 cells co-transfected with either wild-type or mutant Gαq.

Bar segments represent averages ± s.e.m, with the number of independent experiments (n) indicated (c-e). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was used to calculate statistical differences in EC50 and Emax between wild-type and mutant receptors. ND stands for not determined due to an incomplete response curve within the dose concentration range tested. *P<0.05; **P<0.01; ***P<0.001; ****P<0.0001; ns, not significant. Cell surface expression levels of wild-type and mutant CaSR are described in the Methods (c,d).

Receptor transmembrane dimer interface of various CaSR-G-protein complexes.

a, Structural elements at the receptor TMD dimer interface of CaSR-Gi3 (blue and cyan), CaSR-miniGisq (green) and CaSR-miniGis (yellow) complexes in nanodiscs, as well as those of CaSR-miniGisq (blue) and CaSR-miniGi1 (violet) complexes in detergent.

b, Specific TMD dimer interactions between CaSRfree (blue) and CaSRG (cyan) within each of the CaSR-G-protein complexes shown in (a). The TMD dimer interface is divided into three sections (I-III) based on the contacts involving TM6 (I), TM7 (II) and ECL3 (III) of CaSRfree. In the cases of CaSR-miniGis in nanodiscs and CaSR-miniGi1 in detergent, TM1 of CaSRfree contributes to dimer interactions in section II. An additional interaction between ECL2 of both subunits is found in the CaSR-miniGis complex and included as part of section III. F8216.53(G) is part of the dimer interface, while F8216.53(free) (yellow) is directed toward the transmembrane core.

c, d, Functional analysis of mutations at the receptor TMD dimer interface and phospholipid-binding site. Ca2+ potency (EC50) and maximal response (Emax) of wild-type and mutant receptors are compared for Gq, Gqi9 and Gqs5 (c) as well as Gq and Gi3 (d) using IP1 accumulation and BRET-based G-protein activation assay, respectively. Most of the mutants showed reduced G-protein activation when compared to the wild-type receptor.

Bar segments represent averages ± s.e.m, with the number of independent experiments (n) indicated. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was used to calculate statistical differences in EC50 and Emax between wild-type and mutant receptors. ND stands for not determined due to an incomplete response curve within the dose concentration range tested. *P<0.05; **P<0.01; ***P<0.001; ****P<0.0001; ns, not significant. Cell surface expression levels of wild-type and mutant CaSR are described in in the Methods.

Transmembrane ligands of CaSR-G-protein complexes.

f, Detailed views of the LMNG- and CHS-binding sites in CaSR-miniGisq (left) and the LMNG binding site in CaSR-miniGi1 (right). Residues from CaSRfree and CaSRG that coordinate LMNG at the TMD dimer interface are displayed for each complex. Both complexes were solubilized in detergent.

g, Superposition of the CaSRpreG (gray; PDB: 7SIL), CaSRfree and CaSRG transmembrane bundles based on their TMD helices, displaying the distinct poses of their respectively bound R-568 molecules as well as the coordinating residues F8216.53 and Y8256.57.

h, R-568-binding pockets within CaSRfree (left) and CaSRG (right) subunits of the CaSR-Gi3 complex in nanodiscs.

i, R-568-binding sites within the CaSRfree (top) and CaSRG (bottom) belonging to (from left to right) CaSR-miniGisq in nanodiscs, CaSR-miniGis in nanodiscs, CaSR-miniGisq in detergent, and CaSR-miniGi1 in detergent.
