Tick-borne encephalitis virus (TBEV) causes tick-borne encephalitis (TBE), a
severe and sometimes life-threatening disease characterized by viral invasion of
the central nervous system with symptoms of neuroinflammation1,2. As with other
orthoflaviviruses-enveloped, arthropod-borne RNA viruses-host factors required
for TBEV entry remain poorly defined. Here we used a genome-scale
CRISPR-Cas9-based screen to identify LRP8, an apolipoprotein E and reelin
receptor with high expression in the brain, as a TBEV receptor. LRP8
downregulation reduced TBEV infection in human cells, and its overexpression
enhanced infection. LRP8 bound directly to the TBEV E glycoprotein and mediated
viral attachment and internalization into cells. An LRP8-based soluble decoy
blocked infection of human cell lines and neuronal cells and protected mice from
lethal TBEV challenge. LRP8's role as a TBEV receptor has implications for TBEV
neuropathogenesis and the development of antiviral countermeasures.

There are over 10,000 annual cases of TBE in Europe and northeastern Asia, with an increasing number of cases and new endemic areas emerging in Europe3. The clinical TBE cases represent only a small fraction of all human TBEV infections4. The virus is largely transmitted to humans by the bite of infected ixodid ticks, and rarely, by the consumption of unpasteurized dairy products. Although effective TBEV vaccines have been deployed in Europe and Russia, vaccine coverage in endemic countries generally remains low, and no specific therapies are available to treat TBEV infections5-8.

TBEV is an orthoflavivirus, closely related to other arthropod-borne flaviviruses that cause viscerotropic and/or neurotropic disease, including dengue virus, Zika virus, West Nile virus, and Powassan virus9-10. Orthoflaviviruses bear a positive-strand RNA genome encoding 10 proteins, of which three - capsid (C), precursor membrane (prM), and envelope (E) protein - are incorporated into viral particles. Mature virions comprise a lipid membrane envelope covered by 90 (E/M)2 dimers arranged with icosahedral symmetry into 30 rafts of three dimers each9-10. The envelope encloses the viral C protein in complex with the genomic RNA. In addition to protecting the integrity of the viral genome, the E glycoprotein surface shell mediates viral entry into host cells, delivering the genomic RNA into the cytoplasm, which is the site of viral replication. The seven non-virion-incorporated or ‘nonstructural’ proteins mediate critical steps in viral gene expression, genome replication, host immune subversion, and virion biogenesis10.

Genetic screen for TBEV host factorsAll viruses extensively use host-encoded factors for their replication, and orthoflaviviruses are no exception. In recent years, the comprehensive interrogation of host genes through CRISPR/Cas9 genome engineering and other functional genomics approaches has yielded a deep understanding of virus-host interactions critical for infection by many important orthoflaviviruses (see refs. 11-13 for reviews). However, no such genome-wide screens have been reported for TBEV or other members of the tick-borne virus group, raising the possibility that new host factors, both specific for TBEV and broadly exploited by orthoflaviviruses, remain to be discovered. Accordingly, we used the GeCKO v2 genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 knockout library14,15 to screen human A549 cells for host genes whose loss afforded cell survival upon TBEV infection (Supplementary Information Table 1, 2). Genes enriched in the TBEV-resistant population could be grouped into multiple functional categories (Fig. 1a). Many of these groups - and individual genes belonging to them - were previously identified in genetic and proteomic screens with other orthoflaviviruses and have been validated in prior reports. These include genes involved in (i) the targeting of viral transcripts to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and their biogenesis therein (components of the ER membrane [EMC] and translocon associated-protein [TRAP] complexes); (ii) the ER-associated degradation (ERAD) pathway; (iii) lipophagy; (iv) RNA binding and replication; and (v) the biogenesis of cell-surface glycan and proteoglycan repertoires11-13. The congruence of our hits with those from other orthoflavivirus screens reinforces the conclusion that many fundamental processes in viral replication are broadly conserved across the genus.

LRP8 is selectively required by TBEVLRP8, VLDLR, the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR), and the LDLR-related protein-1 (LRP1) have been identified as entry factors and candidate receptors for divergent enveloped viruses17,18,22-30. Moreover, arthritogenic and neurotropic alphaviruses use these LDLR homologs in an overlapping manner29, consistent with the latter’s high sequence and structural similarity at the domain level. Thus, it was plausible that TBEV might also make redundant use of several LDLR homologs as entry factors. To test this hypothesis, we first used siRNAs to deplete A549 cells of VLDLR and LRP1. These interventions had little or no effect on infection by authentic viruses bearing E proteins from virulent TBEV isolates of both Far Eastern and European origin (Sofjin and 93/783, respectively) (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 2d). To more comprehensively evaluate the potential roles of LDLR-related proteins in TBEV entry, we transduced Huh-7.5.1-SpCas9 cells with lentiviruses expressing sgRNAs targeting 13 LDLR homologs, including LRP8, and measured the capacity of these cell populations to support entry by RVPs bearing TBEVNeudörfl E. Mapping minimal LRP8 sequences for TBEV entryThe canonical isoform of human LRP8 comprises an N-terminal ligand-binding domain (LBD) with seven LDLR class A (LA) repeats, two epidermal growth factor-like (EGF) domains, a six-bladed β-propeller composed of LDLR YWTD (LY) domains, a third EGF domain, a membrane-proximal Serine/Threonine-rich domain that is extensively modified with O-linked glycans, the transmembrane domain, and a cytoplasmic signaling domain (Fig. 2a). Because the LBDs of other LDLR homologs have been previously implicated in entry by multiple viruses17,26,30, we first assessed the role of LRP8’s LBD in TBEV entry using an overexpression strategy. Reasoning further that one or more membrane-distal LA repeats in the LBD are likely to be involved, we initially evaluated an LRP8-LA1-3 construct lacking repeats LA4-6 but retaining all other components of the WT protein (Fig. 2a). LRP8-LA1-3 was fully competent to enhance entry by TBEV RVPs (Fig. 2b) and infection by authentic TBEV (Fig. 2c, d) when overexpressed in A549 cells (Extended Data Fig. 4a, b), indicating that repeats LA4-6 are dispensable. Further dissection of repeats LA1-3 identified LA1-2 as the minimal LBD sequence that fully enhanced entry by both RVPs and authentic TBEV. LA1 appeared to enhance RVP entry but not authentic virus infection (Fig. 2b-d), and unlike LRP8-LA1-3 and LRP8-LA1 (Extended Data Fig. 4a, b), a construct containing LA2 alone expressed poorly and was not examined further (not shown). We conclude that the LA1-2 sequence in the LBD of LRP8 is likely directly involved in TBEV entry.

LRP8 binds TBEV E in viral particlesWe postulated that the LA1-2 sequence in LRP8 interacts with TBEV particles. To begin to evaluate this hypothesis, we expressed and purified recombinant, soluble forms of LA1 and LA1-2 fused to a human IgG1 Fc domain, together with a negative-control Fc-tagged protein bearing the first extracellular cadherin repeat of protocadherin-1 (PCDH1 EC1-Fc)31 (Extended Data Fig.4c, d), and tested their capacity to bind viral particles bearing TBEV E. First, we immobilized TBEVNeudörfl RVPs onto magnetic beads coated with a pan-flavivirus E monoclonal antibody (mAb), 4G2, and then incubated them with the soluble LRP8-derived proteins. The RVP-coated beads co-precipitated LA1-2-Fc and to a lesser degree, LA1-Fc, but not EC1-Fc (Fig. 2e). Second, RVPs immobilized on ELISA plates could capture LA1-2-Fc and LA1-Fc, but not EC1-Fc (Extended Data Fig. 5a). The enhanced binding of viral particles to LA1-2-Fc vs. LA1-Fc in both assays, together with the differential capacity of LRP8-LA1-2 and LRP8-LA1 to mediate TBEV entry (Fig. 2b-d), strongly suggests that both LA1 and LA2 contribute to functional viral engagement by LRP8. The involvement of multiple contiguous domains of the receptor in interactions with the E1/E2 glycoprotein complexes of some alphaviruses has also been observed for VLDLR32-34 and the immunoglobulin (Ig)-superfamily protein MXRA835,36. Finally, LA1-2-Fc could capture TBEV RVPs in a biolayer interferometry (BLI) assay (Fig. 2f), whereas EC1-Fc could not (Extended Data Fig. 5b). This interaction was specific for the TBEV glycoprotein E - it could be efficiently blocked by TBEV E-reactive mAbs (Fig. 2g) and we observed no binding between LA1-2-Fc and Japanese Encephalitis virus (JEV) RVPs (Extended Data Fig. 5c).

TBEV E domain III participates in LRP8 bindingTBEV E is an elongated molecule comprising three β-sheet-rich domains - a central β-barrel domain I (DI) flanked by the membrane fusion domain (domain II, DII) and the Ig-like domain III (DIII). The DIII domain is the putative receptor-binding domain for multiple orthoflaviviruses, including TBEV37,38, and is the target of the TBEV E-specific mAbs that blocked RVP:LRP8 binding in the BLI assay above (Fig. 2g). Accordingly, we postulated that LRP8 binds TBEV E through contacts with DIII. However, little or no interaction was observed between LA1-2-Fc and purified monomeric TBEV E DIII (Extended Data Fig. 5d, e). TBEV E domain III participates in LRP8 binding

TBEV E is an elongated molecule comprising three β-sheet-rich domains - a central β-barrel domain I (DI) flanked by the membrane fusion domain (domain II, DII) and the Ig-like domain III (DIII). LRP8 mediates TBEV-cell attachment and internalizationWe used the LRP8-KO and LRP8-overexpressing A549 cell lines above to investigate the step(s) in TBEV entry that require LRP8. We first incubated cells with TBEV at 4°C, washed and immunostained the cells for the TBEV E protein, and then visualized the cells by confocal microscopy (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 6a). Similar results were obtained in cells expressing LRP8-LA1-3 and LRP8-LA1-2, but not LRP8-LA1. These observations were corroborated by the detection of E by immunoblotting in cells incubated with rLGTVTBEV−93/783 at 4°C (Extended Data Fig. 6b). Overall, substantial increases in cell-associated E signal were observed at both early and late timepoints in the cell lines overexpressing forms of LRP8 containing LA1-2, but not in those lacking LA1-2 (Fig. 3b-d). Importantly, by 30 min, E staining was observed to redistribute from the cell periphery to areas throughout the cell, including the perinuclear region (Fig. 3b) and to colocalize with the early endosomal antigen-1 (EEA1) (Extended Data Fig. 6c), consistent with viral internalization into the endocytic pathway over the course of the experiment. The high concordance between the TBEV susceptibility of these cell lines (Fig. 2b-d) and their capacity to support viral attachment and internalization strongly suggests that the E protein must engage LRP8 - and its LA1-2 sequence in particular - for virions to bind, internalize into, and productively infect target cells.

LRP8 is required for TBEV pathogenesisFinally, we sought to determine the importance of LRP8 for TBEV infection and pathogenesis in vivo. The murine LRP8 ortholog is highly similar to its human counterpart in sequence (87% amino-acid sequence identity in LA1-3) (Extended Data Fig. 9a), and its ectopic expression in A549 cells boosted TBEV RVP entry (Extended Data Fig. 9b, c), indicating that a murine model is suitable to investigate the dependence of TBEV on LRP8 in vivo. Accordingly, we evaluated the protective efficacy of the LA1-2-Fc receptor decoy in mice subjected to a lethal challenge with the highly virulent strain TBEVHypr. TBEV-challenged animals in the PBS control group rapidly developed clinical signs at 7-8 days post inoculation and succumbed to infection or met the humane endpoint soon thereafter, as observed previously46 (Fig. 4c, e and Extended Data Fig. 10). Pretreatment of viral preparations with a control Fc-fusion protein, EC1-Fc (Extended Data Fig. 4), did not alter the trajectory of disease (Fig. 4c, e). By contrast, mice exposed to TBEV pre-complexed with LA1-2-Fc did not develop clinical signs and 19 of 20 animals survived to the end of the experiment (28 days) (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 10). Titration of brain tissue collected from separate groups of animals sacrificed on day 6 post-challenge corroborated these findings: mice from both PBS- and EC1-Fc-treated groups were viremic by day 6, whereas viral titers in the LA1-2-Fc group remained below the limit of detection (Fig. 4d). In situ hybridization analysis of brain tissue slices revealed extensive staining for TBEV genomic RNA in the cerebral cortex of PBS- and EC1-Fc-treated animals, but no detectable staining in their LA1-2-Fc-treated counterparts (Fig. 4g). Importantly, the LA1-2-Fc mini-receptor protein afforded similar levels of specific antiviral protection when administered to mice 6 h prior to TBEVHypr challenge (Fig. 4e, f and Extended Data Fig. 10). These findings establish a role for LRP8 recognition by TBEV in infection and disease pathogenesis in vivo.

DiscussionHerein, we have identified the LDLR-superfamily protein LRP8/ApoER2 as a critical proteinaceous receptor that directly engages the viral glycoprotein E to mediate cell entry, infection, and lethal disease by TBEV. Unlike the more generalized cell entry factors identified previously21 – including proteoglycans and TIM/TAM phosphatidylserine (PtdSer) receptors – LRP8 appears to be specifically used by TBEV; infections by other orthoflaviviruses tested in this study were not affected by LRP8 overexpression. Conversely, and in contrast to the more promiscuous use of LDLR homologs by some alphaviruses29, TBEV appears adapted to specifically use LRP8. Nonetheless, our observation that LRP8 knockout does not render cell lines fully refractory to TBEV infection suggests roles for one or more additional/alternative entry factors, such as the PtdSer receptor TIM-1, which was shown to be crucial for TBEV infection and in vivo pathogenesis47. Although more work, especially in animal models, is needed to determine the precise contributions of LRP8 and other cell entry factors, the importance of LRP8 for TBEV infection of the central nervous system in the murine model and its high levels of expression in the brain and the blood-brain barrier16,48,49 implicate it in TBEV neuroinvasiveness and/or neurotropism. The LRP8:E interaction may provide a target for the development of antiviral countermeasures.

WNV sub-genomic replicon-expressing plasmid pcDNA6.2-WNIIrep-GFP/zeo was a generous gift from T. Pierson (NIH, Bethesda, MD). For the production of RVPs, 293FT cells were transfected with DNA in a ratio of 1:3 using the WNV replicon plasmid and the respective virus C-prM-E plasmid. Media was exchanged 8 h post transfection to low-glucose DMEM (Life Technologies, Cat# 1185-084) supplemented with 5% FBS and 25 mM HEPES. After three to four days at 37°C and 5% CO2, the cell supernatant was harvested, cell debris removed and RVPs pelleted through a 30% (v/v) D-sucrose cushion using an SW28 rotor in an Optima LE-80K ultracentrifuge (both Beckman Coulter) for 4 h at 28,000 rpm and 4°C. RVPs were resuspended overnight on ice in NT buffer (10 mM Tris [pH 7.5], 135 mM NaCl), aliquoted and stored at −80°C until further use.

Confluent A549 or Huh-7.5.1 cells were inoculated with pre-titrated amounts of RVPs bearing flavivirus E proteins. Before infection, RVPs were diluted in corresponding media, and infected cells were maintained at 37°C for 24 h. In the following, cells were fixed with 4% PFA and infected cells detected using a monoclonal anti-GFP antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# G6539) and a goat anti-mouse antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Infected cells were automatically counted using a Cytation 5 cell imaging multi-mode reader (BioTek Instruments) including the onboard Gen5 software.

Genome-scale CRISPR/Cas9 knockout screensA549 CRISPR/Cas9 library cells expressing the GeCKO v2 pooled human CRISPR knockout (KO) small guide RNA (sgRNA) library were generated as described previously15. Library cells were expanded to 6-8x107 cells to ensure a minimum coverage of 500x per sgRNA, seeded in T-175 flasks and infected with TBEVSofjin in the BSL-3 facility at the Public Health Agency of Sweden. Infections were carried out using DMEM with 5% FBS and cells were observed daily for cytopathic effect.
