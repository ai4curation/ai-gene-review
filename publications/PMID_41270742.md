Microscopy offers an indispensable technique for visualizing biological
processes and for defining cytological abnormalities characteristic of disease.
However, combining microscopy with the power of pooled CRISPR screening presents
considerable technical challenges, hindering application of systematic genetic
analysis to imaging-defined phenotypes. Here, we establish a fluorescence
microscopy-based CRISPR screening platform that combines ease of implementation
with flexible analysis of live-cell or antibody-based molecular markers,
including post-translational modifications. Applying this methodology, we
systematically identify regulators of primary cilium structure and function in
human cells through targeted and genome-wide screens. We further show that
integration of screens focused on distinct ciliary phenotypes yields
multi-dimensional profiles that delineate precise gene functions. Among the
identified hits, TZMP1 (SMIM27) encodes a microprotein at the ciliary transition
zone that is required for ciliogenesis in human cells and for ciliary function
in Xenopus embryos. More broadly, our approach provides a technological and
conceptual strategy for microscopy-based functional genomics.

INTRODUCTION

Genome-scale CRISPR screening offers a powerful approach for systematic analysis of gene function. In particular, pooled screens based on growth-based selection or FACS have enabled discoveries across diverse biological processes.1–4 However, despite the unique value of fluorescence microscopy in visualizing cellular activities and disease states, current approaches for pooled CRISPR screening are not readily compatible with microscopy-defined phenotypes.

Among current challenges to pooled CRISPR screens using imaging are: 1) the need to image and analyze millions of mutant cells, and 2) the requirement for a means to link imaging phenotypes to the identity of the underlying genetic perturbation. Recently, strategies that overcome these obstacles have been developed through either on-microscope readout of sgRNA sequences/barcodes or through photoactivation-based marking of cells with a phenotype of interest (photoactivated cells are then recovered by FACS for sgRNA sequencing).5–7 Despite the power of both approaches, challenges to their wide adoption include the need for sophisticated custom instrumentation and software. Furthermore, the photoactivation approach has thus far been restricted to live cells, precluding the use of antibody-based markers to define phenotypes.

Despite these obstacles, microscopy-based screening may offer a powerful means to study diverse cellular structures, including primary cilia. Primary cilia are micron-scale compartments that organize a variety of signaling pathways.8–13 Reflecting their roles in many cell types and tissues, ciliary defects cause ciliopathies – syndromic disorders characterized by an array of symptoms, including obesity, intellectual disability, skeletal malformations, kidney cysts, heart defects, and retinal degeneration.14–17 While over 100 ciliopathy genes have been identified, there remain key gaps in our understanding of cilia.17 First, the ongoing discovery of ciliary proteins and ciliopathy genes indicates that our inventory of ciliary regulators remains incomplete. Second, key ciliary functions and processes, such as cilium disassembly and ciliary protein trafficking, have been difficult to study systematically and remain poorly understood. Lastly, a further major challenge is to elucidate how cilia vary in their functional and molecular properties across cell types and physiologic conditions. Such information will likely be key to understanding the roles cilia play in development and in syndromic disorders.

Cytological analysis of molecular markers is ideally suited to address these questions as it provides a versatile and powerful means to assess ciliary structure and function. Notably, unlike screening approaches based on cilium-dependent Hedgehog (Hh) signaling,18,19 microscopy can capture ciliary processes and properties that do not impinge directly on Hh signaling. Additionally, microscopy-based screens can be applied across ciliated cell types, including the large number that do not exhibit a robust Hh pathway output.20

Here, we develop an approach for systematic analysis of microscopy-defined phenotypes and apply this technology to study primary cilia. We combine commercially available microscope hardware and software with strategies for photoactivation-based screening in live or fixed cells, thus making the approach widely accessible and compatible with antibody staining. Through genome-wide and targeted screens, we robustly identify known and novel ciliary regulators. We further find that integration of parallel screens yields multi-dimensional functional profiles. These profiles reveal genes with context-specific ciliary functions and delineate precise gene functions. From screen hits, we identify regulators of ciliary GPCR function, establish diphthamide biosynthesis as a contributor to cilia disassembly, and discover a conserved transition zone microprotein essential for ciliogenesis.

DESIGNWe aimed to develop a microscopy-based screening platform that would enable systematic analysis of ciliary biology and that would be broadly accessible to researchers interested in studying diverse cellular processes. To this end, we pursued a pooled screening strategy based on targeted photoactivation of mutant cells displaying a desired phenotype, thus enabling their isolation by FACS (Figure 1A). By using commercially available microscope hardware and software for high-throughput imaging and analysis, we developed an automated workflow that does not require specialized expertise in optics or programming. Lastly, to provide experimental flexibility and increase the breadth of phenotypes amenable to screening, we developed two approaches: 1) imaging and photoactivation in live cells, which enables analysis of live-cell reporters and recovery of living cells, and 2) screening in fixed cells via immunolabeling of cellular markers.

RESULTSDevelopment of a microscopy-based screening platform and application to ciliary biologyAs a foundation for this platform, we first established cells expressing markers of cilia and centrioles alongside Cas9 and the photoactivatable (PA) fluorescent protein PA-mCherry (Figure 1B). Here we used RPE1 (RPE-hTERT) cells, a diploid human cell line that has been widely used to study cilia biology and in which ciliogenesis is efficiently induced by serum starvation. After establishing Cas9 expression in RPE1 cells, we introduced miRFP670-Centrin2 to label centrioles and ciliary GPCR HTR6 to label cilia. Because overexpression of ciliary proteins can cause cilia elongation,21–24 we expressed HTR6 from the weak δ-crystallin promoter and fused HTR6 to three copies of mNeonGreen (NG) to ensure bright fluorescence.21 These markers provided strong labeling of cilia and centrioles without altering cilia frequency or length (Figure 1B and Figure S1A). We further confirmed that an sgRNA targeting RAB34, a key ciliogenesis gene,25–27 caused robust loss of HTR6-labeled cilia (Figure S1B).Having established cells in which ciliary phenotypes can be readily observed and manipulated by CRISPR, we next optimized photoactivation of PA-mCherry for marking cells with ciliary phenotypes. To achieve photoactivation of many cells simultaneously, we used a high-power 405 nm LED coupled with a digital micromirror device (DMD) that generates programmable illumination patterns. Because pilot studies indicated that effective photoactivation was dependent on the expression level of PA-mCherry, we used the EF1α promoter to drive high-level expression. Under these conditions, we observed time-dependent photoactivation, with bright fluorescence achieved in <4 s (Figures 1B and S1C). To accurately identify cilia and associated properties, we trained an AI-based tool for rapid identification of cilia and centrioles. Cells were then classified as ciliated or non-ciliated, allowing photoactivation ROIs (regions of interest) to be defined (Figure 1B and Video S1). Using this approach alongside automated microscope control (targeting ~1 million cells/day), photoactivated versus unactivated cell populations could be readily distinguished by flow cytometry (On/Off separation; Figure S1D). Additionally, long versus short exposure to 405 nm light allowed differentiation of PA-mCherry-High versus PA-mCherry-Low versus unactivated cells (Hi/Low separation). This ‘Hi/Low’ modality could allow for improved delineation of phenotypes by separately marking phenotype-positive versus phenotype-negative cells7 or for two different phenotypes to be classified simultaneously (Figure S1D).We next used a proof-of-principle experiment and pilot screens to assess and optimize screening performance. For proof of principle, we used a known ciliogenesis mutant (RAB34 KO) to evaluate whether photoactivation based on ciliation state could differentially label wildtype versus mutant cell populations (Figure 1C). We prepared ciliogenesis-deficient cells that could be identified via flow cytometry by transducing our screening cell line with a RAB34 sgRNA and a co-expressed bright BFP marker. These cilium-deficient, bright-BFP cells were then mixed with wild type, dim-BFP cells. After inducing ciliogenesis via serum starvation, we subjected this cell mixture to targeted photoactivation based on ciliation state, with ciliated cells strongly photoactivated (PA-mCherry-High) and unciliated cells weakly photoactivated (PA-mCherry-Low). As expected, bright-BFP cells with sgRAB34 were strongly enriched in the PA-mCherry-Low (unciliated) population, while wildtype cells were predominantly in the PA-mCherry-High (ciliated) population (Figure 1C). Overall, flow cytometry revealed a promising ~15-fold difference in sgRAB34:WT ratio between PA-mCherry populations.We next conducted pilot screens with a custom library of 3489 sgRNAs targeting ~750 genes focused on ciliary regulators (4 sgRNAs per gene, as well as 500 non-targeting controls). After introducing this library into our screening cell line, we serum starved cells to induce ciliogenesis and carried out automated imaging, photoactivation, FACS, and sequencing of sgRNAs recovered from sorted cell populations. In the first pilot screen, genes affecting ciliation were identified by comparing sgRNA abundances in PA-mCherry-Low (non-ciliated) versus PA-mCherry-High (ciliated) cells. We first examined sgRNAs targeting genes from the IFT-B complex, a key mediator of ciliogenesis. As expected, sgRNAs targeting IFT57, IFT46, and IFT52 were strongly enriched in the non-ciliated group (PA-mCherry-Low) relative to the ciliated group (PA-mCherry-High) (Figure 1D). Comparing these photoactivated cell populations to unsorted cells, we noted that sgRNAs targeting these IFT-B genes are both enriched in the non-ciliated group (PA-mCherry-Low) and depleted from the ciliated group (PA-mCherry-High), illustrating the value of differential photoactivation of these two groups (Figure 1D). By contrast, sgRNAs targeting IFT22, an IFT-B gene that is dispensable for ciliogenesis,18,28 were not enriched or depleted in photoactivated cell populations (Figure 1D).To rigorously identify hit genes, we used the casTLE algorithm, which compares sgRNAs targeting a given gene to non-targeting controls to estimate a phenotypic effect size and a P value (Tables S1A and S1B).29 Encouragingly, RAB34 was one of the top casTLE hits (Figure 1E). To evaluate if bona fide ciliogenesis genes could be reliably distinguished from non-hits, we examined IFT-B complex genes, among which eleven are essential for ciliogenesis while three are dispensable.28,30–32 Ten out of eleven IFT-B genes needed for ciliogenesis were top hits, whereas none of the three dispensable genes were hits (Figure 1E). Similar accuracy was also seen for core versus peripheral subunits of IFT-A and among genes that encode different components of the ciliary transition zone (Figure S1E).17,18,33–38We next aimed to delineate key features of this pilot screen that would enable success at a genome-wide scale. By comparing pilot screens conducted with different experimental parameters, we found that Hi/Low and On/Off photoactivation modalities both readily identified ciliogenesis mediators but that Hi/Low photoactivation better identified hits that increase ciliation (Figure S1F). Comparing the performance with two versus four sgRNAs per gene, we noted that use of fewer sgRNAs had a mild but not severe impact on hit identification (Figure S1G). Lastly, in assessing the number of cells to be imaged, we found that reducing coverage from ~150X to ~50X excess of cells per sgRNA had a mild to moderate impact on performance (Figure S1H). We therefore concluded that two sgRNAs per gene with Hi/Low photoactivation and ~100X coverage would be suitable for a genome-wide screen.A genome-wide screen for regulators of cilium assemblyWe next conducted a genome-wide screen to identify genes that regulate formation of HTR6-labeled cilia in RPE1 cells. We used the Gattinara sgRNA library, which targets 19,993 human genes with two gRNAs per gene.39 With a Hi/Low photoactivation workflow, we imaged, photoactivated, and sorted 4.2 million cells across 18 days of imaging, corresponding to ~100-fold coverage of the sgRNA library (Figure 2A). Using casTLE to compare sgRNA abundances from cells with and without cilia labeled by HTR6-NG, we estimated phenotypic effect sizes as well as P values for each gene (Table S1C–E). Despite a ~25x increase in the number of genes analyzed, we observed strong performance for RAB34 and IFT-B genes (Figure 2B) and high concordance between pilot screen results and the genome-wide screen, supporting the robustness of our screening platform (Figure S2A). Overall, we defined 259 top hits (casTLE P < 0.02; see Methods), which span key ciliary structural features and functional classes (Figure 2C). To assess screen performance, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for the 259 hits. As expected, the top GO terms were related to cilia and centrosome function and were identified with high significance (P values from ~1x10−60 to ~1x10−30; Figure 2D; Table S2A). The screen hits also strongly overlapped with those identified in a CRISPR KO screen for cilium-dependent Hh signaling conducted in mouse NIH-3T3 fibroblasts (P < 1x10−61)18 and with genes in the SysCilia-V2 compendium of ciliary genes (P < 1x10−56)40 (Figure 2E and Table S1C–E). Furthermore, for genes with discordant outcomes between this screen and the Hh signaling screen, these differences could often be clearly rationalized and were reflected in specific GO term enrichments (Figure S2B and Table S2). For example, hits specific to the Hh signaling screen included Hh signaling components (e.g. Smo, Gli2) and genes needed for ciliary trafficking and signaling but not cilium formation (Ift25, Ift27, BBSome genes).30,31 Conversely, some genes needed for formation of RPE1 cilia labeled by HTR6-NG but not required for Hh signaling in NIH-3T3 cells were identified (Figure S2B) and further characterized below. To rigorously assess screen performance, we defined a set of genes known to be essential for ciliogenesis (88 true positives), with a set of 386 olfactory receptors serving as true negatives (Table S1D). Using these reference genes, receiver operator characteristic (ROC) and precision-recall plots demonstrated accurate identification of hits with low false positive rates (Figure S2C). Overall, these data indicate that our platform enables genome-wide screening for ciliogenesis regulators with high sensitivity and specificity.Establishing optical enrichment screening for fixed-cell immunofluorescenceWe next sought to expand the capabilities of our screening platform to include screening in fixed, immunostained cells. Such an extension would offer key advantages, including: 1) improved temporal resolution for study of dynamic processes (currently limited by ~9 h imaging sessions), 2) facile use of different antibody markers, reducing the need for cell line engineering, and 3) analysis of phenotypes that cannot be captured via live-cell markers, such as phosphorylation states or post-translational modifications of axonemal microtubules. However, fixation prevents photoactivation of PA-mCherry, precluding its use in fixed cells. We reasoned that we could overcome this limitation by using a photoactivatable small molecule fluorophore introduced by Halo labeling (Figure 3A).We chose ER-localized Halo-Sec61β for fluorophore capture as it enables intense, membrane-anchored labeling throughout cells. After transducing this construct into RPE1 Cas9-BFP cells, we isolated a cell line with strong labeling by JF549-HaloTag ligand and robust ciliation (Figures 3B and S1A). We then optimized a workflow based on the photoactivatable dye PA-JF549.41 Specifically, we identified conditions for live-cell dye labeling, gentle chemical fixation, and mild cell permeabilization42 (see Methods). We then carried out immunofluorescent staining, imaging, and photoactivation, followed by cell dissociation using mild trypsinization to preserve cell structure and PA-JF549 labeling. Under these conditions, we achieved efficient photoactivation of PA-JF549, detectable by microscopy and flow cytometry (Figures 3B and 3C). We also observed robust immunostaining with antibodies to ciliary GTPase Arl13b, polyglutamylated tubulin (GT335 antibody), and centrosomal protein Pericentrin (PCNT) (Figures 3B–D).After training AI-based algorithms to recognize of cilia labeled with Arl13b or GT335 (anti-polyglutamylated tubulin) antibodies, we evaluated the performance of our fixed-cell workflow. We prepared an sgRNA library targeting hits from our genome-wide HTR6-NG screen as well other ciliary regulators (958 total genes; four sgRNAs/gene) and introduced this library into RPE1 Cas9-BFP Halo-Sec61β cells. Cells were then serum starved, labeled with PA-JF549-HaloTag ligand, and subjected to immunostaining, imaging, photoactivation, and FACS. Genes affecting formation of Arl13b-labeled cilia or GT335-labeled cilia were identified by comparing sgRNA abundances in PA-JF549-On (ciliary marker-positive) versus PA-JF549-Off (ciliary marker-negative) cells (Figures 3D and 3E; Tables S1F and S1G).With this approach, we found that essential subunits of IFT-B were required for formation of cilia marked by Arl13b or polyglutamylated tubulin, while dispensable IFT-B subunits were not hits (Figures 3E and S3A). We also noted that many of the genome-wide HTR6-NG screen hits were also required for ciliary Arl13b staining, supporting the robustness of our screening platform (Figure S3B). Encouraged by these results, we next evaluated whether an alternative strategy for PA-JF549 labeling could bypass the need for expression of Halo-Sec61β. Specifically, we tested non-specific amino-group labeling with a PA-JF549 NHS ester. Using this approach in an Arl13b immunolabeling screen, we again observed excellent performance in identifying known ciliary genes and in distinguishing essential versus non-essential IFT-B subunits (Figure S3C and Tables S1F and S1G). Thus, our screening platform can be applied successfully in fixed cells.Comparing results from Arl13b versus GT335 screens in Halo-Sec61β cells, we observed that some genes were selectively identified as affecting ciliary Arl13b but not polyglutamylated tubulin, while others were specifically needed for axonemal polyglutamylation (Figure 3E). In several cases, the selective phenotypes seen for these genes are consistent with their known functions. For example, loss of TULP3, a protein that mediates membrane protein targeting to cilia,35,43–45 affected ciliary Arl13b but not polyglutamylation (Figure 3F). Conversely, tubulin glutamylases TTLL1 and TTLL5 were specifically hits in the GT335 screen and led to reduced GT335 staining (Figure 3G).46,47 We further noted that TTLL5 KO cells exhibited a greater reduction in ciliary staining with the GT335 antibody, which detects initiation sites of polyglutamate chains, than with the polyE antibody, which detects long polyglutamate chains (Figures 3G and S3D).48,49 By contrast, TTLL1 KO cells exhibited a greater defect in polyE staining than for GT335. Notably, these phenotypes match the known roles of TTLL5 in polyglutamate chain initiation and of TTLL1 in chain elongation.49–51 Despite these distinctions, for both TTLL1 and TTLL5 KO cells, ciliary Arl13b was not notably impacted (Figure 3G). Thus, our methodology enables identification of specific ciliary gene functions through comparison of parallel screening results.Integration of CRISPR screens enables functional classification of ciliary regulatorsGiven the functional insights enabled by comparing results from Arl13b versus poly-glutamylated tubulin screens, we anticipated that combining results across several screens could provide a multi-dimensional phenotypic portrait for known and novel ciliary genes. We therefore integrated results from the three imaging screens conducted here (HTR6-NG, Arl13b, and GT335 markers in human RPE1 cells) and a prior Hedgehog signaling screen in mouse NIH-3T3 fibroblasts (Table S1H).18 Notably, comparing phenotypes across screens revealed close correspondence with the known functions of many genes (Figure 4A). For example, IFT25 and IFT27 encode IFT-B subunits that are dispensable for ciliogenesis but needed for BBSome-mediated ciliary trafficking.30,31 Consistent with these roles, IFT25 and IFT27 exhibit a similar functional profile as genes encoding BBSome subunits and regulators (Figure 4A). Similarly, HTR6 and TTLL1 were hits selectively in screens for the corresponding HTR6-NG and GT335 markers, while key ciliogenesis gene ARL13B was a hit in all screens. Thus, insights provided by combined screen data can recapitulate known functional roles.We next asked if new functional insights could be obtained from combined screen results. Among genes with screen-specific phenotypes, TMEM218 was a strong hit in all microscopy screens conducted in human RPE1 cells but not in the Hh signaling screen conducted in mouse fibroblasts. We confirmed these data, finding that TMEM218 KO in RPE1 cells led to a severe loss of Arl13b-labeled cilia and moderate loss of polyglutamylated cilia (Figures 4B–C and S4A). Conversely, in NIH-3T3 cells, Tmem218 KO had a very mild effect on the same ciliary markers (Figures 4B–C and S4A). Additionally, the strong defects in RPE1 cells are due to loss of TMEM218, as they are rescued by TMEM218-2xMyc (Figure S4B). Given that NIH-3T3 and RPE1 cells employ similar modes of ciliogenesis, we speculated that these phenotypes may reflect a species-specific difference in TMEM218’s role. Consistent with this possibility, knockout of Tmem218 in mouse IMCD3 cells, a cell line derived from kidney epithelial cells, had only minimal effect on ciliogenesis (Figure S4C). Species-specific differences also appear present at the organismal level, as TMEM218 is a Joubert and Meckel Syndrome gene that causes severe ciliopathy in human,52,53 while Tmem218-null mice are viable and only display slowly progressing retinal and kidney defects.54Further illustrating the value of combining screen results, these data also delineated essential versus dispensable components of the ciliary transition zone, which compartmentalizes the cilium from the cell body.38,55,56 For example, TMEM231 was a strong hit in all three imaging screens, and TMEM231 KO cells exhibited a severe ciliogenesis defect (Figures 4A, D–E). By contrast, TMEM67 was a hit in most screens but not the polyglutamylated tubulin screen (Figure 4A). Consistent with these results, TMEM67 KO cells could form cilia with polyglutamylated tubulin, but these cilia often lacked HTR6-NG and Arl13b (Figures 4D–E and S4D). Thus, TMEM67 is required for the role of the transition zone in establishing the composition of the ciliary membrane but, unlike TMEM231, is dispensable for cilium formation.Characterization of ciliary regulators with diverse functional rolesAn important feature of our platform is that the throughput is sufficient for genome-wide screens that can identify new ciliary regulators. We therefore next investigated hits from our HTR6-NG screen that were not previously linked to ciliary function (Figures 5A–B). To prioritize and accelerate the functional characterization of such genes, we took advantage of our multi-dimensional screening data, which provided functional predictions for identified hits (Figure 5A). For example, four core subunits of the EMC complex were hits only in the HTR6-NG screen and not in the Arl13b and polyglutamylated tubulin screens (Figure 5A). As the EMC complex promotes biogenesis of membrane proteins including rhodopsin,57,58 we anticipated that the EMC complex may be required for HTR6 biogenesis and/or ciliary trafficking but not ciliogenesis. Indeed, the fluorescence intensity of ciliary HTR6-NG was strongly decreased in both EMC5 (MMGT1) and EMC6 KO cell lines (Figures 5C–D). Conversely, ciliary Arl13b intensity and ciliogenesis were both unchanged (Figures S5A–B). Supporting the specificity of this phenotype, loss of ciliary HTR6-NG in EMC5 KO cells was rescued by re-introduction of EMC5-2xMyc (Figure S5C). Thus, the EMC complex is a key determinant of HTR6 localization to primary cilia, and ciliary GPCR alterations may contribute to syndromes caused by EMC dysfunction.57,59Among other genes identified only in the HTR6-NG screen, METTL3 encodes an m6A methyltransferase with diverse roles, including in Polycystic Kidney Disease.60 Notably, the METTL3-associated protein VIRMA (KIAA1429)61 was also a hit in our HTR6-NG screen (Figure 5B). As expected, ciliary HTR6-NG was strongly decreased in METTL3 and VIRMA KO RPE1 cells, while the ciliation rate was unchanged (Figures 5E–F and S5D–E). A similar reduction in ciliary HTR6-NG was also seen in wildtype cells treated with the METTL3 inhibitor STM2457,62 corroborating the phenotypes observed in knockout cells (Figure S5E). Thus, our screens can identify genes that regulate HTR6 independently of ciliogenesis and reveal previously unrecognized contributions to ciliary function for m6A RNA methylation and the EMC complex.Next, DAZAP1 encodes a conserved RNA-binding protein and was hit in all three imaging screens, suggesting a role in ciliogenesis.63 Consistent with these data, DAZAP1 KO cells exhibited a significant reduction in cilia marked by Arl13b or polyglutamylated tubulin (Figures 5G–H). DAZAP1 regulates spermatogenesis in planaria,64 and mouse Dazap1 mutants exhibit sterility.65 Thus, DAZAP1 may have a conserved role as an RNA-binding protein important for formation and function of cilia.Beyond genes that promote ciliation, our screens have the potential to identify negative regulators of ciliation, a poorly characterized but important class of ciliary regulators. Among gene knockouts associated with increased ciliation in our HTR6-NG screen, DPH1 encodes an enzyme needed for post-translational diphthamide modification of eEF2.66
DPH5 also promotes diphthamide biosynthesis, and its knockout was associated with increased ciliation in the polyglutamylation screen. We therefore generated DPH1 and DPH5 KO RPE1 cells and analyzed cilia in the absence of serum starvation, a condition that disfavors ciliation. Notably, DPH1 and DPH5 KO cells exhibited strongly increased ciliation under these conditions (Figures S5F–G). In serum-starved knockout cells, a trend toward mildly increased ciliation was observed, while cilia length was unaltered (Figures S5H–I). To assess if DPH1 and DPH5 promote cilia disassembly, we analyzed the deciliation that occurs when quiescent cells are stimulated with serum mitogens. DPH1 and DPH5 KO cells both exhibited a significant defect in serum-induced cilia disassembly (Figures 5I–J). This defect is not due to impaired cell cycle re-entry from quiescence, as the portion of S phase cells (labeled by EdU) was unaffected (Figure S5J). Additionally, the increased ciliation in the presence of serum observed in DPH5 KO cells was rescued by expression of DPH5-2xMyc, supporting the specificity of the mutant phenotype (Figure S5K). Overall, these data indicate that our platform enables genome-wide screening for ciliogenesis regulators with high sensitivity and specificity.

Comparing results from Arl13b versus GT335 screens in Halo-Sec61β cells, we observed that some genes were selectively identified as affecting ciliary Arl13b but not polyglutamylated tubulin, while others were specifically needed for axonemal polyglutamylation (Figure 3E). DISCUSSIONHere we present a versatile microscopy-based functional genomic screening platform and use this technology for systematic exploration of ciliary biology. By analyzing diverse molecular markers in live and immuno-stained cells, we create a functional portrait of ciliary genes from a series of targeted and genome-wide CRISPR screens. These screens enabled identification and characterization of several previously unknown ciliary regulators, including the transition zone microprotein TZMP1. Our work thus provides a proof of concept for systematic analysis of subcellular organization through imaging-based genetic screening.The screening platform developed here provides high performance and throughput while offering key improvements to available methods for microscopy-based CRISPR screening. Although photoactivation-based approaches were previously developed for pooled screening, this methodology has required screening in live cells and engineering of fluorescently tagged marker proteins. Here, we optimize a fixed-cell screening approach that offers improved temporal resolution and compatibility with antibody staining of endogenous markers. Because antibodies are available for a range of molecular phenotypes not readily assessed in live cells, this feature greatly expands the range of cellular structures and processes that can be analyzed. Additionally, dye-conjugated antibodies can facilitate multiplex imaging to define multi-parameter phenotypes. Lastly, unlike other optical screening approaches, our methodology was implemented using commercially available microscope hardware and software. By overcoming the need for specialized expertise in optics and programming, these tools make microscopy-based screening accessible to a broad range of researchers.Due to the high performance of our screening platform, differential identification of hits between screens with alternative markers often reflects precise functional information about genes of interest rather than experimental noise. As a result, integrating screens focused on distinct ciliary phenotypes provides a functional portrait of cilia, highlighting differential contributions of transition zone proteins and an apparent species-specific requirement for TMEM218. The prevalence of context-specific ciliary regulators is further highlighted by SMIM27/TZMP1, which is required for ciliogenesis in RPE1 cells but appears absent in species such as C. elegans. In the future, expanding screens to additional cell types, species, and ciliary processes has the potential to uncover new ciliary regulators, to provide functional insights into poorly characterized ciliary genes, and to offer systems-level insights spanning the breadth of ciliary function. For example, the approach can be applied to cilia disassembly, repression of ciliogenesis, or cilia length regulation, which have been the subject of fewer large-scale studies,86 or to examine the relationship of ciliary phenotypes with other cellular features. In this respect, microscopy-based screening overcomes limitations of prior CRISPR screens that were restricted to Hh signaling readouts and to cells that respond robustly to Hh. Applications in different experimental systems, such as iPSC-derived models or primary cells, will be facilitated by PA-JF549 NHS ester labeling. As additional screening data are generated, we anticipate that cumulative integration of results will provide rich functional insights, as seen for other multi-parametric screens.87–89LimitationsWe anticipate several areas for future improvement of our screening platform. Currently, a limiting factor in our genome-wide screen was use of an sgRNA library with only two sgRNAs per gene, which is expected to reduce statistical power for detecting hits and increase the risk of false negatives. Indeed, a few expected hits (e.g. IFT122) fell below the statistical cutoff in the genome-wide screen but were successfully identified in targeted screens with four sgRNAs per gene (Table S1). Increasing screen throughput (e.g. via larger fields of view or reduced magnification objectives) would facilitate use of sgRNA libraries with more sgRNAs per gene, thereby improving performance. Incorporation of alternate modalities such as CRISPRi/a may also improve characterization of essential genes and of different types of regulators.90 An additional limitation is that On/Off and High/Low photoactivation only allow one or two phenotypes to be assessed in a given screen. With the ability to use photoactivatable dyes that may have improved photo-chemical properties compared to proteins, we anticipate that more complex encoding of phenotypes via photoactivation may soon be possible. Lastly, DMD-based photoactivation offers limited precision in the z-axis, hindering application in 3D culture models or tissue specimens. Here we present a versatile microscopy-based functional genomic screening platform and use this technology for systematic exploration of ciliary biology. By analyzing diverse molecular markers in live and immuno-stained cells, we create a functional portrait of ciliary genes from a series of targeted and genome-wide CRISPR screens. These screens enabled identification and characterization of several previously unknown ciliary regulators, including the transition zone microprotein TZMP1. Our work thus provides a proof of concept for systematic analysis of subcellular organization through imaging-based genetic screening.

The screening platform developed here provides high performance and throughput while offering key improvements to available methods for microscopy-based CRISPR screening. Although photoactivation-based approaches were previously developed for pooled screening, this methodology has required screening in live cells and engineering of fluorescently tagged marker proteins. Here, we optimize a fixed-cell screening approach that offers improved temporal resolution and compatibility with antibody staining of endogenous markers. Because antibodies are available for a range of molecular phenotypes not readily assessed in live cells, this feature greatly expands the range of cellular structures and processes that can be analyzed. Additionally, dye-conjugated antibodies can facilitate multiplex imaging to define multi-parameter phenotypes. Lastly, unlike other optical screening approaches, our methodology was implemented using commercially available microscope hardware and software. By overcoming the need for specialized expertise in optics and programming, these tools make microscopy-based screening accessible to a broad range of researchers.

Due to the high performance of our screening platform, differential identification of hits between screens with alternative markers often reflects precise functional information about genes of interest rather than experimental noise. As a result, integrating screens focused on distinct ciliary phenotypes provides a functional portrait of cilia, highlighting differential contributions of transition zone proteins and an apparent species-specific requirement for TMEM218. The prevalence of context-specific ciliary regulators is further highlighted by SMIM27/TZMP1, which is required for ciliogenesis in RPE1 cells but appears absent in species such as C. elegans. In the future, expanding screens to additional cell types, species, and ciliary processes has the potential to uncover new ciliary regulators, to provide functional insights into poorly characterized ciliary genes, and to offer systems-level insights spanning the breadth of ciliary function. For example, the approach can be applied to cilia disassembly, repression of ciliogenesis, or cilia length regulation, which have been the subject of fewer large-scale studies,86 or to examine the relationship of ciliary phenotypes with other cellular features. In this respect, microscopy-based screening overcomes limitations of prior CRISPR screens that were restricted to Hh signaling readouts and to cells that respond robustly to Hh. Applications in different experimental systems, such as iPSC-derived models or primary cells, will be facilitated by PA-JF549 NHS ester labeling. As additional screening data are generated, we anticipate that cumulative integration of results will provide rich functional insights, as seen for other multi-parametric screens.87–89

LimitationsWe anticipate several areas for future improvement of our screening platform. Currently, a limiting factor in our genome-wide screen was use of an sgRNA library with only two sgRNAs per gene, which is expected to reduce statistical power for detecting hits and increase the risk of false negatives. Indeed, a few expected hits (e.g. IFT122) fell below the statistical cutoff in the genome-wide screen but were successfully identified in targeted screens with four sgRNAs per gene (Table S1). Increasing screen throughput (e.g. via larger fields of view or reduced magnification objectives) would facilitate use of sgRNA libraries with more sgRNAs per gene, thereby improving performance. Incorporation of alternate modalities such as CRISPRi/a may also improve characterization of essential genes and of different types of regulators.90 An additional limitation is that On/Off and High/Low photoactivation only allow one or two phenotypes to be assessed in a given screen. With the ability to use photoactivatable dyes that may have improved photo-chemical properties compared to proteins, we anticipate that more complex encoding of phenotypes via photoactivation may soon be possible. Lastly, DMD-based photoactivation offers limited precision in the z-axis, hindering application in 3D culture models or tissue specimens. With holography or two-photon photoactivation, screening in such models may be achieved in future studies.

Optical coherence tomography imagingFor OCT imaging, a Thorlabs Ganymede 900 nm spectral domain imaging system was used,95 enabling an imaging depth of 1.4 mm in air and water, with an axial resolution of 2.2 μm in water. Two-dimensional cross-sectional images were captured by scanning at a frequency of 36 kHz (36,000 A-scans per second). ThorImageOCT software (version 4.4.6) was used for data acquisition. To assess cilia-driven extracellular fluid flow across the epidermis, embryos were raised to stages 23–24 and subsequently scanned with OCT to measure fluid flow speed by tracking endogenous particles within the vitelline envelope, following the approach detailed by Tang et al.103 Based on observed flow patterns, embryos were qualitatively categorized as exhibiting normal flow (similar to stage-matched controls) or abnormal flow (ranging from no particle movement to approximately half of the wildtype flow speed).104

Precision-recall and receiver-operator characteristic (ROC) plots were generated in Matlab software using signed casTLE score (positive versus negative values defined from the sign of the casTLE effect) as a classifier and using the gold standard true positive and true negative genes annotated in Table S1D. For the top 259 hit genes from the HTR6-NG screen, GO term enrichments and FDR-corrected P values were determined using the NIH DAVID functional annotation knowledgebase relative to all genes analyzed (after filtering for growth phenotypes).92 Gene sets that were shared or distinct between the HTR6-NG screen and related Hedgehog signaling screen18 and the SysCilia-v2 dataset40 were defined from among the genes common to these studies; functional enrichments for these gene sets were determined relative to all human genes using the NIH DAVID functional annotation knowledgebase. Fisher’s exact test was used to determine significance of overlap among gene sets shown in Figure 2E and to compare pitx2c and dand5 staining in Figure 7F–G. All other statistical tests were based on t-test (two-sided, unpaired, unequal variance with Welch’s correction, with the exception of comparisons of fluorescence intensity, for which two-sided paired ratio t-tests of median intensities were used).
