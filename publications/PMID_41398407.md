Break-induced replication (BIR) is a primary homologous recombination pathway
for repairing one-ended double-strand DNA breaks, including those arising from
collapsed replication forks and eroded telomeres. BIR frequently leads to loss
of heterozygosity, genetic mutations, and gross chromosomal rearrangements, all
hallmarks of cancer. Here, we conducted a genome-wide screen that allowed us to
identify and validate the involvement of 33 novel yeast genes in BIR. We report
that, while DNA damage and spindle checkpoint machineries are both required to
delay nuclear division and provide adequate time for BIR to complete, the
spindle position checkpoint is required to coordinate between nuclear division
and cytokinesis. Furthermore, we show that two nucleopore proteins play a
sequential role during BIR: Nup84 acts before DNA synthesis, while Nup188
functions later to support repair completion. Given the conservation of BIR
between yeast and humans and the role of BIR in cancer development, human
homologs of the identified BIR proteins may represent promising targets for
anti-cancer therapeutics.

Introduction

Break-induced replication (BIR) is a homologous recombination pathway that repairs one-ended DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), including those formed by telomere erosion and replication fork collapse. During BIR repair, one broken chromosome end primes DNA synthesis using the homologous donor as a template, and this repair synthesis can proceed for hundreds of kilobases (reviewed in refs. 1–4). The BIR replication machinery manifests as a “migrating bubble”, first synthesizing the leading strand, which subsequently serves as the template for lagging strand synthesis; consequently, inheritance of the newly synthesized DNA is conservative5,6. The unusual mode of DNA synthesis during BIR causes high levels of genetic instabilities such as a mutation rate that is up to 1000 times higher than that of S-phase replication as well as frequent genome rearrangements and copy number variations (CNVs)5,7–16. BIR has also been proposed as a source of genome instability in humans2. In particular, BIR can induce segmental duplications found in precancerous cells17, and, in the presence of DNA-damaging agents, BIR promotes the formation of mutation clusters similar to those observed in cancer8,18,19. BIR is also responsible for alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT) (reviewed in refs. 2,20–22) and for mitotic DNA synthesis (MiDAS)23,24, which respectively maintain telomere length in ~15% of cancers and complete replication of so-called DNA fragile sites that remain unreplicated until mitosis.

Our knowledge of BIR in human cells heavily relies on decades of studies characterizing the pathway in yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the organism in which eukaryotic BIR was initially discovered25–29. Numerous proteins that promote BIR have been identified in yeast through the candidate gene approach. For example, factors involved in BIR synthesis including Polδ-RFC-PCNA, Pif1, DNA primase-DNA polymerase α, and RPA were all identified in yeast30–33 and confirmed to be conserved in humans34,35. Pol32, which is a subunit of Polδ that is not essential for S-phase DNA replication, is required for BIR30, as is Pif1 helicase32, and these two proteins have become important biomarkers to detect BIR in various experimental systems.

The genetic requirements for the first steps of BIR are similar to other homologous repair pathways: BIR is initiated by 5′-to-3′ resection executed by the Mre11-Rad50-Xrs2 complex, then relies on Dna2-Sgs1 helicases and/or Exo1 exonuclease36–38, followed by the participation of Rad52, Rad51, Rad55, Rad57, and Rad54 to complete the homology search and displacement loop (D-loop) formation26,28,39. Additionally, Srs2 helicase/translocase plays an important role by counteracting the formation of toxic intermediates that arise from the invasion of long Rad51-bound ssDNA accumulated behind the D-loop into the BIR template40. Recent studies also revealed that BIR requires several histone modification proteins, including Hst3 and Hst4 histone deacetylases and the histone methyltransferase Dot141; however, the full complement of epigenetic enzymes involved in BIR remains unknown. Also, because BIR takes a significant amount of time to complete and usually occurs during G2/M or mitosis, the DNA damage checkpoint (DDC) machinery is required to initiate and maintain cell cycle arrest for BIR synthesis to complete prior to cell division9,28. Recently, it was discovered that nucleopore proteins Nup84 and Nup60 as well as motor protein Kinesin-14 and the Slx5-Slx8 SUMO-targeted ubiquitin ligase are required for BIR42–44, suggesting that successful completion of BIR may require relocation of the broken chromosome to the nuclear periphery.

Although the candidate gene approach has generated a notable list of genes that participate in BIR, our knowledge of the factors that contribute to successful execution and to regulation of BIR remains incomplete. To address this gap, in this study, we conducted a whole-genome screen using our new yeast BIR system and the yeast knockout (YKO) collection, which includes mutant strains with knockouts (KOs) of all non-essential genes, to identify new candidate genes required for BIR. As a result of this study, we identified and validated 33 previously unrecognized BIR driver genes encoding proteins in a variety of functional groups—including checkpoint proteins, nuclear pore structural proteins, and proteins involved in post-translational modification of nucleosomes—and subjected several of them to deeper functional analysis. We discovered that, in addition to the DNA damage checkpoint, successful completion of BIR requires coordination between nuclear division and cytokinesis mediated by the spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC) and spindle position checkpoint (SPOC), respectively.

To investigate the mechanism by which SAC mutants affect BIR, we deleted SAC genes in our allelic BIR system48, which is disomic for Chr III (Fig. 2c). In this system, galactose-induced DSBs in a truncated copy of Chr III (recipient) are repaired mainly by BIR using a full-length Chr III (donor) as the homologous template. Successful BIR repair results in copying of ~100 kb of template DNA and yields two full-length copies of Chr III. Auxotrophic markers allow determination of BIR (Leu-Ade+), while gross chromosomal rearrangements (GCRs), which also present phenotypically as Leu-Ade+, are distinguished from BIR by CHEF gel analysis (Supplementary Figs. 4–6). Phenotypes of other repair events are also distinguishable: gene conversion (GC) outcomes are Leu+Ade+; failed repair, including chromosome loss of the truncated Chr III (CL), presents as Leu-Ade-red; and half-crossovers (HCs) resulting from fusions between the recipient and donor chromosomes are characterized by Leu-Ade-white (Fig. 2c). An important feature of this strain is that it is usually viable even when repair fails. Consistent with our findings in the other systems, BIR was significantly reduced in bub1∆, bub3∆, and mad2∆ mutants (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 4 for CHEF analysis), but not in mad3∆ mutants (Supplementary Data 4). In addition, bub1∆mad2∆ double mutants showed a BIR defect similar to each single mutant (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Data 4; Supplementary Fig. 4a, h). Together, our data support that the SAC plays an important role during BIR.

To determine the specific BIR step that is defective in SAC mutants, we examined the kinetics of BIR using our droplet digital PCR (ddPCR)-based Assay for Monitoring BIR Elongation Rate (AMBER)49. AMBER showed that, in WT cells 10 h after DSB induction, DNA copy number had increased ~1.5x at the P2 position (~6 kb from the X-region of MAT locus), ~1.4x at P4 (~34 kb away), and ~1.4x at P5 (~65 kb away) (Fig. 2c, e and Supplementary Data 5, 6), in agreement with previous observations49. However, the increase in copy number at these positions was lower in bub1Δ and mad2Δ than in WT (Fig. 2f, g and see also Fig. 2h for the comparison at P2), indicative of a decrease in BIR synthesis in these mutants.

DiscussionOur study uncovered an unexpectedly large number of genes and entire networks that contribute to the success of BIR, thereby enhancing our understanding of the mechanisms driving BIR in eukaryotes.We discovered that DDC-induced cell cycle arrest alone is insufficient for BIR success; rather, successful BIR completion requires the SAC to prolong G2/M arrest. Cells transitioning from the DDC to the SAC have been previously described in the context of unrepairable DSBs65,66. Our results show that successful BIR also requires this transition, uncovering a crucial role for the DDC-to-SAC transition in the completion of DSB repair. In addition, when the DDC-to-SAC transition was studied in a system with unrepairable DSB(s), the transition occurred between 12 and 24 h after the break and likely reflected the time required for adaptation to DDC signaling65,66. In contrast, we observed the transition happening much earlier in the BIR context, at a time corresponding to the final steps of BIR and recovery from the DDC. Thus, the transition to the SAC during BIR likely serves a different purpose than that of an unrepairable DSB and possibly employs a different mechanism. For unrepairable breaks, it was established that activation of the SAC proceeds by the spreading of H2AX from the DSB to the centromere on the broken chromosome, causing kinetochore dysfunction and prolonging G2/M arrest65. However, we observed that in our allelic BIR system, an H2A-S129A mutant defective for γH2AX modification exhibited only a minor BIR defect, which was much less severe than the defect observed in SAC mutants (Supplementary Data 4). Therefore, an alternative mechanism may activate the SAC during BIR. For example, previous studies have demonstrated that SAC activation could be achieved in a Tel1- and Mec1-dependent but kinetochore-independent manner67.Our most striking finding is that the SPOC pathway is required for BIR. It was previously demonstrated that exposure to microtubule-depolymerizing drugs such as nocodazole or benomyl led to a SAC-mediated cell cycle arrest, which delayed separation of sister chromatids and spindle pole bodies (SPB), thereby triggering the SPOC and preventing mitotic exit68. We propose that the SPOC is similarly activated during BIR. However, in the absence of an intact SPOC, BIR activates the SAC, which delays sister chromatid and SPB separation, but the exit from mitosis still proceeds, resulting in cell re-budding that likely reflects a process leading to cell lethality. In support of this, we showed that deactivation of the SAC by deleting MAD2 significantly restored viability after DSB induction in bub2Δ cells. This supports the idea that “dissociation” between the SAC and SPOC leads to discoordination between nucleic division and cytokinesis, which leads to cell death.It remains unclear how activation of the SAC transduces signals to the SPOC. It was proposed that activation of Mad2 could directly affect phosphorylation of Bfa169, and then inhibits SPOC activation and mitotic exit. It is also possible that the SPB division defect following activation of the SAC may inhibit the migration of cytoplasmic microtubules, possibly involving the function of Jnm1, Arp1, and Num1, leading to SPOC activation. Also, since the viability of bub2Δmad2Δ after BIR induction was only partially restored compared to WT, it is likely that another mechanism, probably the DDC, contributes to activation of the SPOC, in addition to the SAC. This is consistent with a prior observation that an unrepairable DSB promoted direct modification of Bfa1 by Rad53, which prevented phosphorylation of Bfa1 by Cdc5, suppressing mitotic exit70. Together, we conclude that BIR is a unique repair pathway that relies on multiple checkpoints, including the DDC, SAC, and SPOC, to coordinate among DSB repair, nuclear division, and cytokinesis.We think the main reason so many checkpoints are required may be the considerable amount of time needed for BIR to complete, whereas these requirements are different for faster repair pathways, such as GC. However, the fact that G2/M is the optimal time for BIR repair could also contribute to the need to tightly coordinate BIR completion with mitosis. It is important, in this regard, to determine the role of all three checkpoints in other BIR-like processes that are also linked to G2/M phase. One such process is mitotic DNA synthesis (MiDAS), which is important for finishing incomplete replication at common fragile sites of the genome23,24. Interestingly, in different organisms, MiDAS was reported to proceed at different stages of mitosis. For example, MiDAS in yeast was reported to occur only in anaphase71, making it unlikely for its completion to depend on the SAC, though it could depend on the DDC and SPOC. In human cells, on the other hand, MiDAS was detected mostly in mitotic prophase and metaphase23; therefore, it could depend on the human functional counterparts of all three checkpoints. Another BIR-like pathway, alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT), is mainly initiated in cells arrested in G2/M (reviewed in refs. 20–22,72) in yeast and possibly in other organisms, suggesting it may also rely on tight coordination among multiple checkpoints. Together, the checkpoint control represents an important characteristic that can help in better understanding and classifying various BIR and BIR-like processes.BIR likely takes place at the nuclear periphery, like other slow DSB repair pathways. We observed that Nup84, a member of the nuclear pore outer ring complex, is required for BIR, consistent with previous studies42,43. In this study, we established that nup84Δ leads to a striking defect in the initiation of BIR synthesis. These data are in good agreement with recent data from S. pombe, where nuclear pore proteins were found to be important for the initiation of replication restart proceeding through a BIR-like mechanism73. Our study uncovered an unexpectedly large number of genes and entire networks that contribute to the success of BIR, thereby enhancing our understanding of the mechanisms driving BIR in eukaryotes.

We discovered that DDC-induced cell cycle arrest alone is insufficient for BIR success; rather, successful BIR completion requires the SAC to prolong G2/M arrest. Cells transitioning from the DDC to the SAC have been previously described in the context of unrepairable DSBs65,66. Our results show that successful BIR also requires this transition, uncovering a crucial role for the DDC-to-SAC transition in the completion of DSB repair. In addition, when the DDC-to-SAC transition was studied in a system with unrepairable DSB(s), the transition occurred between 12 and 24 h after the break and likely reflected the time required for adaptation to DDC signaling65,66. In contrast, we observed the transition happening much earlier in the BIR context, at a time corresponding to the final steps of BIR and recovery from the DDC. Thus, the transition to the SAC during BIR likely serves a different purpose than that of an unrepairable DSB and possibly employs a different mechanism. For unrepairable breaks, it was established that activation of the SAC proceeds by the spreading of H2AX from the DSB to the centromere on the broken chromosome, causing kinetochore dysfunction and prolonging G2/M arrest65. However, we observed that in our allelic BIR system, an H2A-S129A mutant defective for γH2AX modification exhibited only a minor BIR defect, which was much less severe than the defect observed in SAC mutants (Supplementary Data 4). Therefore, an alternative mechanism may activate the SAC during BIR. For example, previous studies have demonstrated that SAC activation could be achieved in a Tel1- and Mec1-dependent but kinetochore-independent manner67.

Our most striking finding is that the SPOC pathway is required for BIR. It was previously demonstrated that exposure to microtubule-depolymerizing drugs such as nocodazole or benomyl led to a SAC-mediated cell cycle arrest, which delayed separation of sister chromatids and spindle pole bodies (SPB), thereby triggering the SPOC and preventing mitotic exit68. We propose that the SPOC is similarly activated during BIR. However, in the absence of an intact SPOC, BIR activates the SAC, which delays sister chromatid and SPB separation, but the exit from mitosis still proceeds, resulting in cell re-budding that likely reflects a process leading to cell lethality. In support of this, we showed that deactivation of the SAC by deleting MAD2 significantly restored viability after DSB induction in bub2Δ cells. This supports the idea that “dissociation” between the SAC and SPOC leads to discoordination between nucleic division and cytokinesis, which leads to cell death.

It remains unclear how activation of the SAC transduces signals to the SPOC. It was proposed that activation of Mad2 could directly affect phosphorylation of Bfa169, and then inhibits SPOC activation and mitotic exit. It is also possible that the SPB division defect following activation of the SAC may inhibit the migration of cytoplasmic microtubules, possibly involving the function of Jnm1, Arp1, and Num1, leading to SPOC activation. Also, since the viability of bub2Δmad2Δ after BIR induction was only partially restored compared to WT, it is likely that another mechanism, probably the DDC, contributes to activation of the SPOC, in addition to the SAC. This is consistent with a prior observation that an unrepairable DSB promoted direct modification of Bfa1 by Rad53, which prevented phosphorylation of Bfa1 by Cdc5, suppressing mitotic exit70. Together, we conclude that BIR is a unique repair pathway that relies on multiple checkpoints, including the DDC, SAC, and SPOC, to coordinate among DSB repair, nuclear division, and cytokinesis.

We think the main reason so many checkpoints are required may be the considerable amount of time needed for BIR to complete, whereas these requirements are different for faster repair pathways, such as GC. However, the fact that G2/M is the optimal time for BIR repair could also contribute to the need to tightly coordinate BIR completion with mitosis. It is important, in this regard, to determine the role of all three checkpoints in other BIR-like processes that are also linked to G2/M phase. One such process is mitotic DNA synthesis (MiDAS), which is important for finishing incomplete replication at common fragile sites of the genome23,24. Interestingly, in different organisms, MiDAS was reported to proceed at different stages of mitosis. For example, MiDAS in yeast was reported to occur only in anaphase71, making it unlikely for its completion to depend on the SAC, though it could depend on the DDC and SPOC. In human cells, on the other hand, MiDAS was detected mostly in mitotic prophase and metaphase23; therefore, it could depend on the human functional counterparts of all three checkpoints. Another BIR-like pathway, alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT), is mainly initiated in cells arrested in G2/M (reviewed in refs. 20–22,72) in yeast and possibly in other organisms, suggesting it may also rely on tight coordination among multiple checkpoints. Together, the checkpoint control represents an important characteristic that can help in better understanding and classifying various BIR and BIR-like processes.

BIR likely takes place at the nuclear periphery, like other slow DSB repair pathways. We observed that Nup84, a member of the nuclear pore outer ring complex, is required for BIR, consistent with previous studies42,43. In this study, we established that nup84Δ leads to a striking defect in the initiation of BIR synthesis. These data are in good agreement with recent data from S. pombe, where nuclear pore proteins were found to be important for the initiation of replication restart proceeding through a BIR-like mechanism73. Our study further determines that Nup84 promotes strand invasion and is critical for the step following strand invasion, which could be D-loop stabilization or assembly of the BIR replisome. Our study also identified Nup188, which is a component of the nuclear pore inner ring55, as another BIR protein. NUP188 deletion resulted in a markedly different BIR phenotype compared to NUP84-deleted cells, wherein nup188Δ cells could efficiently begin BIR synthesis but showed significant cell lethality after DSB induction. We therefore propose that Nup188 represents a nuclear pore structure required for BIR completion. Our data also support a role for Nup188 in checkpoint regulation, but it could also act to terminate BIR synthesis or to resolve late BIR intermediates.

Our data and previous studies collectively support that complex and precise regulation of chromatin dynamics via histone-modifying enzymes is essential for all steps of BIR repair. We validated Rad6 and Bre1, which mediate histone 2B lysine 123 ubiquitination (H2BK123ub), as epigenetic regulators of BIR repair (Supplementary Data 2), consistent with previous reports74,75. One of the known functions of H2B123ub is promoting H3K79 and H3K4 methylation76,77, and our screen identified a BIR defect in cells lacking the respective H3K79 and H3K4 methylases, Dot1 and Sdc159,78, underscoring the importance of these particular histone modifications for BIR.

Our genetic data indicate that formation of final BIR outcomes is defective in the absence of the Rpd3L deacetylase complex. However, our kinetics analysis and also previous studies from ref. 79 showed that Rpd3L-deficient cells are efficient in BIR synthesis, suggesting Rpd3L may have a role in the very final steps of BIR. For example, Rpd3L could stabilize the final BIR product, possibly by assembling chromatin on the newly synthesized chromosome, and/or Rpd3L could promote recovery from BIR-associated cell cycle arrest. Interestingly, our data showed that Rpd3S, another histone deacetylase complex that shares several subunits with Rpd3L, inhibits BIR. Previous studies have documented examples of these two complexes playing opposing roles in other cellular processes80, and further studies are needed to understand how Rpd3L and Rpd3S deacetylases may counteract each other during BIR. Finally, our finding of the important role that the NuA4-SWI/SNF axis plays in BIR provides yet another piece of evidence highlighting the importance of histone dynamics for the success of BIR.

Together, genome-wide identification of BIR genes in yeast allows us to more fully define the BIR mechanism. Because BIR is suggested to contribute to cancer development and progression, genes that promote BIR may represent strong candidates to explore as targets for cancer therapies. Therefore, based on the high level of conservation between yeast and humans for known BIR proteins, future studies to evaluate validated hits from our screen in human cell line systems are warranted.

We also used a YSJ13345 which is isogenic to YSJ131 strain to conduct GC assay (see Supplementary Fig. 3a for details). AM1411 is a derivative of our BIR experimental strain, AM1003, which contains a truncated copy of Chr III that can be cut by a galactose-induced HO endonuclease at MATa and a full copy of Chr III that cannot be cut, as its Yα at MAT locus was deleted by the insertion of lys2-A47. AM1411 has the following genotype: MATa-LEU2-tel/ MAT::lys2:A4
ade1 met13 ura3 leu2-3,112/leu2 thr4 hmlΔ∷ADE1/hmlΔ∷ADE3 hmrΔ∷HPH ade3∷GAL-HO FS2Δ∷NAT/FS2 lys2Δ.

AM3422 is a new haploid BIR system, a derivative of KT556, which has the following genotype: MATa bar1Δ his7-2 trp1Δ ura3Δ leu2-3,112 ade2Δ lys2Δ cup1Δ yhr054cΔ cup2Δ V29616::CUP1 V34205::ADE2-HOcs-lys2Δ3’. This strain contains the ADE2 gene and a 5′ portion of LYS2 on Chr V between CAN1 and NPR2 genes. Importantly, there are no essential genes located centromere distal to this locus. The 3′ part of LYS2 is replaced with a modified version of the 117-bp HO endonuclease recognition cut site (see Supplementary Data 5 for the full sequence). This sequence allowed an efficient cutting (Supplementary Fig. 8), thereby promoting efficient BIR (Fig. 1b), but also allowed an optimal number of Lys+ papillae in a semi-quantitative BIR test (Fig. 1c), with no prior accumulation of Lys+. The 3′ part of the LYS2 gene (3812 bp) marked by TRP1 gene was inserted into HIS4 (position 65,934 to 68,333 bp of Chr III according to SGD). The two fragments of LYS2 (located on Chr V and Chr III) share 2339 bp of homology. Finally, the strain contains GAL::HO inserted into the ADE3 gene, while the HO gene at its native locus is deleted.

To construct the AM3874 strain used for the genome-wide screen for BIR regulators, the following modifications were made to AM3422. A NATMX cassette was inserted into the HIS4 locus 160 bp centromere distal to the lys2 fragment. The LEU2 gene at its native position in Chr III was replaced by URA3, and LEU2 was inserted into the AVT2 gene next to CAN1 to mark the 5′ part of lys2 on Chr V. Also, his7-2 was replaced by the wild type HIS7. The HIS4 gene was inserted into the native LYS2 locus on Chr II. This allowed for selection against the LYS2 allele of the YKO strains by selecting for His+. In addition, HIS3 was deleted at its native location by the delitto perfetto approach81, while MFA1 was replaced by MFA-HIS3 (similar to refs. 64,82). We introduced a fragment containing the rpl28Q38K mutation (similar to refs. 64,82) by transformation using selection for resistance to cycloheximide. Finally, URA3 inserted at LEU2 was fully deleted while the URA3 gene was inserted next to (858 bp from) the ade3::GAL::HO cassette on Chr VII.

All single-gene deletion mutants were constructed by transformation with a PCR-derived KANMX, NATMX, HPHMX, or BleoR module, each flanked by sequences homologous to the regions surrounding the open reading frame of each gene83,84. All constructs were confirmed by PCR and by phenotype. The bub1Δ, bub3Δ, mad2Δ, bub2Δ, bfa1Δ, and nup188Δ mutants were also tested by droplet digital (dd)PCR for the correct number of each chromosome, to make sure that these mutants were not aneuploids. See Supplementary Data 1 for the genotypes of all strains used in this study.

The strains used for ChIP analysis were generated by inserting a 13xMyc epitope tag, marked by KANMX at the C-terminus of the NUP84 and NUP188 coding sequences in the allelic BIR strain (Supplementary Data 1). The derivative strains were analyzed by BIR assay to confirm that the insertion of Myc tag did not affect the functions of Nup84 and Nup188 proteins (see Supplementary Data 4).

The preparation of DNA for CHEF in agarose plugs was performed as previously described in ref. 85. The running conditions for CHEF were optimized for the maximal separation of Chr V and Chr III, as follows: initial switch time of 35.43 s, final switch time of 52.33 s, running time of 39 h, and voltage of 6 Volts/cm. Following separating by CHEF, the DSB repair products were hybridized to P32-labeled probes specific to MGR1 (on the left arm of Chr III) and to GLN3 (on the right arm of Chr V) (see Supplementary Data 5 for the sequences of oligonucleotides used to create probes). The latter product was also hybridized to the GLN3 (Chr V) specific probe.

Kinetics of BIR synthesisThe kinetics of BIR synthesis was analyzed using our recently developed ddPCR-based method called Assay for Monitoring BIR Elongation Rate (AMBER)49. Yeast cells were cultured overnight in 5 ml SC-Leu medium, then transferred to YEP medium containing 2% raffinose and incubated for approximately 16 h, until the cell density reached ~5 × 10⁶ cells per ml. A 10 ml aliquot was collected for analysis of pre-DSB copy number (“0 h”), and 2% galactose was added to the remaining culture to induce DSB formation. Samples (10 ml each) were taken every 2 h for up to 10 h. Cells were resuspended in spheroplasting buffer (0.4Msorbitol, 0.4MKCl, 40 mM sodium phosphate buffer [pH 7.2], 0.5 mM MgCl2) containing 20 T zymolyase (250 µg per ml) and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min to digest the cell wall. Approximately 2.5 × 10⁵ cells were then centrifuged at 1000 × g for 2 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in 400 µl of 1× NEB rCutSmart buffer (B6004S). Next, 4 µl of 10% SDS was added, and the mixture was incubated at 60 °C for 15 min. The sample was cooled on ice, followed by addition of 50 µl 10% Triton X-100. RNase A (20 µl, 10 mg per ml) was then added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, 20 µl protease K (10 mg per ml) was added and incubated at 60 °C for 30 min. After cooling on ice for 5 min, 0.5 ml of phenol–chloroform was added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 15 min. The aqueous phase was extracted with an additional 0.5 ml of phenol–chloroform, mixed by inversion, and centrifuged. DNA was precipitated from the final aqueous phase by adding 35 µl of 3 M Sodium acetate [pH 5.2] and 0.35 ml of isopropanol, followed by incubation at room temperature for 30 min. DNA was pelleted by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 20 min, and the supernatant was removed. The pellet was washed with 0.5 ml 80% ethanol, centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 5 min, and air-dried. The dried DNA pellet was resuspended in 100 µl of molecular-grade water for subsequent analysis. Purified DNA was quantified by ddPCR using standard reaction conditions. BIR synthesis was followed by tracking the copy number increase along the track of BIR normalized to copy number at ACT1 located on Chr VI, using QX600 ddPCR software, similarly to ref. 49. See Supplementary Data 5 for the sequences of oligos used for this assay. Also, see Supplementary Data 6 for AMBER data obtained in each individual experiment.

ChIP assayYeast cells were cultured in YEP-raffinose liquid medium overnight at 30 °C to achieve a density of 1 × 107 cells/mL before adding galactose (2% final concentration) to introduce DSB. Cells were fixed in formaldehyde (1% final concentration) to cross-link DNA–protein complexes. After 10 min, the cross-linking reactions were quenched by the addition of glycine (125 mM), and the reaction continued for 5 min on a shaker. The cells were collected by centrifugation and washed first with TBS (125 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.4], 130 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 0.9 mM CaCl2, and 0.5 mM MgCl2) supplemented with glycine (125 mM), followed by a second wash in TBS alone. Cells were resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES [pH 7.5], 1 mM EDTA, 140 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mg/mL bacitracin, 1 mM benzamidine, and 1 mM PMSF). Glass beads were added to the cell suspension, followed by cell disruption in a bead beater for 6 min (1 min disruption each time with 2 min on-ice intervals). The cell lysate containing the genomic DNA was sheared to fragments with an average size of 0.5 kb by sonication (Bioruptor® Pico Sonicator B01080010). The cell debris was sedimented by centrifugation at 16,000 × g. for 20 min, and the collected supernatant was distributed into IP (Input) and Non-IP (Non-Input). For the IP samples, incubation was performed overnight with anti-c-Myc antibody (Sigma M4439) or anti-Rad51 antibody (a gift from Dr. Patrick Sung) at 4 °C, followed by the addition of Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo, 1004D) and continued incubation for 4 h. Beads were then washed twice with lysis buffer, twice with high-salt lysis buffer (lysis buffer with 500 mM NaCl), twice with wash buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8.0], 1 mM EDTA, 250 mM LiCl, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.5% Igepal CA-630), and twice with 1× TE. DNA–protein complexes were eluted by incubating beads with 100 μL of elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8.0], 10 mM EDTA, and 1% SDS) for 30 min at 65 °C. Reverse cross-linking was carried out overnight at 65 °C, followed by proteinase K treatment and phenol–chloroform purification of DNA. Purified DNA was analyzed by qPCR using the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, 4367659). Enrichment was normalized to the ACT1 locus. Accumulation of targeted proteins after DSB measured by ChIP is calculated by subtraction from before DSB.
