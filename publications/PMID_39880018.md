## Full Text

Abstract

In Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ), light and circadian clock signaling converge on PHYTOCHROME-INTERACTING FACTORS (PIFs) 4 and 5 to produce a daily rhythm of hypocotyl elongation. PIF4 and PIF5 expression is repressed at dusk by the evening complex (EC), consisting of EARLY FLOWERING3 (ELF3), ELF4, and LUX ARRHYTHMO (LUX). Here, we report that ELF3 recruits the JUMONJI (JMJ) H3K4me3 demethylases JMJ17 and JMJ18 to the PIF4 and PIF5 loci in the evening to remove their H3K4me3 marks. The association of JMJ17 and JMJ18 with the 2 genomic loci depends on the EC, and the H3K4me3 marks are enriched in the elf3 and jmj17 jmj18 mutants. Half of the globally differentially expressed genes are overlapping in elf3 and jmj17 jmj18 . Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation sequencing analysis identified 976 H3K4me3-enriched loci in elf3 . Aligning the H3K4me3-enriched loci in elf3 to genes with increased expression in elf3 and jmj17 jmj18 identified 179 and 176 target loci, respectively. Half of the loci are targeted by both ELF3 and JMJ17/JMJ18. This suggests a strong connection between the 2 JMJ proteins and EC function. Our studies reveal that an array of key genes in addition to PIF4 and PIF5 are repressed by the EC through the H3K4me3 demethylation pathway.

The evening complex requires 2 histone demethylases to repress the expression of key genes involved in light responses, the circadian clock, and photoperiodic flowering in the evening phase.

Introduction

Light, an important environmental cue, plays critical roles in plant growth and development throughout the plant lifecycle ( Liu et al. 2020 ). The perception of light signals by plants involves several groups of photoreceptors. These photoreceptors include phytochromes (phys) that perceive red (R) and far-red (FR) light, cryptochromes (crys) and phototropins that perceive ultraviolet A (UVA) and blue light, and UVR8, which perceives UVB light ( Chen et al. 2004 ; Paik and Huq 2019 ). Phytochromes are encoded by 5 different genes ( phyA to phyE ) in Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ) ( Li et al. 2015 ; Rockwell and Lagarias 2019 ).

Phytochromes are photo reversible and exist in 2 spectral forms: the inactive Pr form that absorbs red light, and the biologically active Pfr form that absorbs far-red light. Upon light excitation, the Pfr forms of phytochrome are translocated to the nucleus from the cytoplasm and interact with several phytochrome-interacting factors (PIFs), leading to PIF degradation and inhibited hypocotyl elongation ( Huq and Quail 2002 ; Shen et al. 2005 ; Chen and Chory 2011 ). PIFs belong to the basic helix-loop-helix family of transcription factors, with 8 members in Arabidopsis: PIF1, PIL1, and PIF3 to 8 ( Duek and Fankhauser 2005 ). Mutants of PIF3 , the first member identified, have short hypocotyls ( Ni et al. 1998 , 2014 ). PIF4 is a key negative regulator of light signaling and light-controlled hypocotyl elongation ( Huq and Quail 2002 ). The activities of photoreceptors are integrated with the circadian clock and deliver their signals via PIFs to optimize many plant light responses ( Nusinow et al. 2011 ).

The circadian clock in Arabidopsis contains multiple interlocking transcriptional feedback loops and synchronizes plant growth and development with environmental clues ( Huang and Nusinow 2016 ; McClung 2019 ). The morning-expressed MYB transcription factors CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED1 (CCA1) and LATE ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL (LHY) form dimers that repress the expression of PSEUDO-RESPONSE REGULATOR ( PRR ) genes, including PRR1 ( TIMING OF CAB EXPRESSION1 , TOC1 ), PRR5 , PRR7 , and PRR9 ( Schaffer et al. 1998 ; Adams et al. 2015 ). CCA1 and LHY also repress the expression of some evening-phased genes, such as GIGANTEA ( GI ), EARLY FLOWERING3 ( ELF3 ), ELF4 , and LUX ( Harmer et al. 2000 ; Kamioka et al. 2016 ). ELF3, ELF4, and LUX are components of the evening complex (EC). The 4 PRRs and the EC in turn repress the transcription of CCA1 and LHY ( Huang et al. 2012 ; Adams et al. 2015 ). In addition, morning-expressed LIGHT-REGULATED WD 1 (LWD1) activates the expression of CCA1 by recruiting TEOSINTE BRANCHED1-CYCLOIDEA-PCF 20 (TCP20) and promotes the transcription of PRRs ( Wu et al. 2016 ). REVEILLE (RVE) transcriptional activators, including RVE4, RVE6, and RVE8, induce the transcription of PRRs and EC component genes ( Hsu et al. 2013 ; Xie et al. 2014 ). GI induces the expression of CCA1 and LHY , and GI is also repressed by CCA1, LHY, the EC, and TOC1 ( Martintryon et al. 2007 ; Mizuno et al. 2014 ).

Hypocotyl elongation in Arabidopsis is fine-tuned by the circadian clock and light-signaling pathways ( Nozue et al. 2007 ; Niwa et al. 2009 ). For example, the circadian clock influences photoperiodic and thermoresponsive growth through the circadian expression of PIFs , particularly PIF4 and PIF5 ( Nusinow et al. 2011 ; Leivar and Monte 2014 ; Mizuno et al. 2014 ). CCA1 and LHY recruit SHORT HYPOCOTYL UNDER BLUE 1 to activate the expression of PIF4 and thus ensure hypocotyl elongation in the morning ( Sun et al. 2019 ). By contrast, EC represses the expression of PIF4 and PIF5 in the evening ( Nusinow et al. 2011 ). The MYB transcription factor LUX binds to the PIF4 and PIF5 promoters and suppresses their expression by nucleating the EC ( Hazen et al. 2005 ; Nusinow et al. 2011 ). ELF3 directly interacts with LUX or ELF4, whereas ELF4 does not directly interact with LUX. ELF3 and ELF4 do not contain protein domains with known functions ( Liu et al. 2001 ; Khanna et al. 2003 ). The molecular mechanism by which the EC represses PIF4 and PIF5 expression is largely unknown.

Histone methylation is a dynamic and reversible process. The methylation levels of genes are controlled by histone methyltransferases and demethylases ( Klose and Zhang 2007 ). LYSINE SPECIFIC DEMETHYLASE 1 (LSD1) is the first histone demethylase found to remove monomethyl and dimethyl groups from histone 3 at lysine 4 (H3K4). However, LSD1 demethylase family proteins are unable to remove methyl groups from trimethylated lysines ( Shi et al. 2004 ). Subsequently, Jumonji proteins were found to remove histone methyl groups from monomethylated, dimethylated, and trimethylated lysines; their demethylase activity is dependent on a Jumonji C (JmjC) domain in animals and yeast ( Tsukada et al. 2005 ; Pedersen and Helin 2010 ). The Arabidopsis genome encodes 21 JmjC domain-containing histone demethylases (JMJ11 to JMJ31), which are divided into 5 groups: KDM5/JARID1, KDM4/JMJD2, KDM3/JMJD1, JMJD6, and those containing the JmjC domain only ( Lu et al. 2008 ; Qu et al. 2012 ). Each group targets specific histone lysine residues in histone proteins ( Yamane et al. 2006 ; Lu et al. 2008 ). For example, the KDM3/JMJD1 group catalyzes H3K9me1/2 demethylation ( Yamane et al. 2006 ; Roh et al. 2013 ; Dutta et al. 2017 ), whereas the KDM5/JARID1 group is responsible for H3K4me1/2/3 demethylation ( Lu et al. 2008 ).

Rhythmic expression of core clock genes is linked to the levels of H3K56ac, H3K9/14ac, and H3K4me3 at their promoter regions in Arabidopsis ( Malapeira et al. 2012 ; Song and Noh 2012 ). Several JMJ proteins influence circadian oscillation ( Malapeira et al. 2012 ). For example, JMJ30/JMJD5 expression is repressed by the central oscillators CCA1 and LHY ( Lu et al. 2011 ). The H3K36me3 demethylase JMJ31 promotes CCA1 and LHY expression, whereas CCA1 and LHY bind the upstream region of the H3K36me3 demethylase gene JMJ30 and repress its expression. JMJ29 is recruited by ELF3 and shapes rhythmic changes in H3K4me3 histone marks at the CCA1 and PRR9 loci ( Lee and Seo 2021 ). CCA1 and PRR9 expression in jmj29-1 has an early phase shift after 64 h for CCA1 and 90 h for PRR9 under continuous light. Whether JMJ29 affects the expression of PIF4 or PIF5 remains unknown.

The WD40 repeat protein HIGH EXPRESSION OF OSMOTICALLY RESPONSIVE GENE 15 (HOS15) forms a complex with EC components and HISTONE DEACETYLASE 9 (HDA9) to repress the expression of GI ( Park et al. 2019 ). GI expression is elevated by several folds in hox15-2 and hda9-1 without a change in its rhythmicity. PIF4 and PIF5 expression is not substantially altered in hos15-2 or hda9-1 mutants. HDA9 also interacts with ELF3 and associates with the TOC1 promoter to suppress TOC1 expression during the night ( Lee et al. 2019 ). TOC1 and other HDA9 target genes display a notable period shortening or early rhythmic phase in hda9-1 but not arrhythmic expression, as often observed in elf3-8 or lux-6. ELF3 also directly interacts with a protein from the SWI2/SNF2-RELATED (SWR1) complex to control the deposition of H2A.Z-nucleosomes at EC target genes such as PSEUDO-RESPONSE REGULATOR7 ( PRR7 ) and PRR9 ( Tong et al. 2020 ). PIF4 and PIF5 are upregulated in arp6-1 .

In this study, we demonstrate that ELF3 recruits JMJ17 and JMJ18 to demethylate the H3K4me3 marks at the PIF4 and PIF5 loci and decreases their expression. Our findings shed light on the role of the EC component ELF3 in repressing the expression of PIF4 and PIF5 in the evening. We also identified a list of other genes targeted by ELF3 and JMJ17/18 through large-scale CUT&Tag and RNA-seq analysis.

Results

JMJ17 and JMJ18 interact with ELF3 or ELF4

To identify possible interactors of ELF3, we conducted a yeast three-hybrid (Y3H) screen against a normalized Arabidopsis Mate and Plate library. We used ELF3 as a bait fused with the GAL4 DNA-binding domain (GBD) and ELF4 as a bridge protein in the pBridge vector in case ELF3 requires ELF4 as its interaction partner ( Supplementary Fig. S1A ). We identified JMJ18, a member of the evolutionarily conserved JARID1 family, as an interactor of ELF3 ( Supplementary Fig. S1B ). To examine whether this interaction required ELF4 as a bridge protein, we verified these interactions using yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays. JARID1 family members are capable of demethylating H3K4 methylation marks in yeast, animals, and Arabidopsis ( Liu et al. 2010 ). Arabidopsis contains 6 JARID1 family members: JMJ14 (At4g20400), JMJ15 (At2g34880), JMJ16 (At1g08620), JMJ17 (At1g63490), JMJ18 (At1g30810), and JMJ19 (At2g38950).

It is possible that the 5 other JMJ proteins (in addition to JMJ18) also interact directly with ELF3 or ELF4. To examine this issue, we performed Y2H assays using JMJs fused with GBD and ELF3 and ELF4 fused with the GAL4 activation domain (GAD). Co-transformation of GBD:JMJs with GAD did not support yeast growth on selection medium. JMJ14, JMJ15, JMJ16, and JMJ19 did not interact with either ELF3 or ELF4 in Y2H assays ( Supplementary Fig. S2A ). GBD:JMJ18 only interacted with GAD:ELF3 but not with GAD:ELF4 ( Fig. 1, A and B ; Supplementary Fig. S2B ). Y2H assays also detected a strong interaction of GBD:JMJ17 with GAD:ELF3 and GAD:ELF4 ( Fig. 1, A and B ; Supplementary Fig. S2B ). We further examined the interaction of JMJ18 with ELF4 by bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays, and the result was negative ( Supplementary Fig. S2C ). LUX, another component of the EC, did not interact with either JMJ17 or JMJ18 ( Supplementary Fig. S2B ).

Figure 1.

To map the interaction domain of JMJ17 and JMJ18 with ELF3 or ELF4, we split JMJ17 into the N-terminus (aa 1 to 422) and C-terminus (aa 423 to 1208) and JMJ18 into the N-terminus (aa 1 to 572) and C-terminus (aa 573 to 819) and performed a Y2H assay. The JMJ17-N and JMJ18-N termini contained the JmjN, JmjC, and Pfam zinc-finger domains but lacked the 2 other domains ( Supplementary Fig. S3A ). ELF3 only interacted with full-length JMJ17 and JMJ18 but not with their N- or C-termini ( Supplementary Fig. S3B ). ELF4 also interacted only with full-length JMJ17, but not with its N- or C-terminus ( Supplementary Figs. S2B and S3C ). LUX did not interact with either full-length or truncated JMJ17 or JMJ18 ( Supplementary Figs. S2B and S3C ). Therefore, full-length JMJ17 and JMJ18 are required for their interaction with either ELF3 or ELF4.

We fused full-length JMJ17 or JMJ18 to YFP N and ELF3 or ELF4 to YFP C and performed BiFC assays. All fusion genes were driven by the CaMV 35S promoter. We transiently co-expressed the fusion constructs in onion epidermis cells and observed YFP signals ( Fig. 1C ). Cells that co-expressed JMJ17:YFP N and YFP C , YFP N and ELF3:YFP C , or ELF4:YFP C did not produce fluorescent signals ( Fig. 1C ). Consistent with the Y2H results, JMJ17:YFP N and JMJ18:YFP N interacted with ELF3:YFP C in the BiFC assays. JMJ17:YFP N fusion protein also interacted with ELF4:YFP C .

We then performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays using protein extracts prepared from transgenic Arabidopsis seedlings carrying pJMJ17::JMJ17:GFP or pJMJ18::JMJ18:GFP and pELF3::ELF3:MYC or pELF4::ELF4:Flag . We immunoprecipitated the proteins using anti-GFP antibodies, separated them by SDS-PAGE, and analyzed them by immunoblotting with anti-MYC or anti-FLAG antibodies. JMJ17:GFP co-immunoprecipitated ELF3:MYC or ELF4:FLAG, whereas JMJ18:GFP co-immunoprecipitated ELF3:MYC ( Fig. 1, D to F ; Supplementary Fig. S4 ).

JMJ17 and JMJ18 associate with the PIF4 and PIF5 promoters in vivo

During early evening to late night, PIF4 and PIF5 expression is repressed by the EC proteins ELF3, ELF4, and LUX ( Nusinow et al. 2011 ). To investigate whether the EC components ELF3 and ELF4 directly recruit JMJ17 and JMJ18 to the PIF4 and PIF5 loci in a temporal manner, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments with plants that carry the JMJ17:GFP or JMJ18:GFP transgene driven by their native promoters using anti-GFP antibodies followed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis. We designed 4 pairs of primers to detect different regions in the PIF4 promoter and 3 pairs of primers to detect different regions in the PIF5 promoter ( Fig. 2, A and B ). All seedlings were grown under a 12-h light and 12-h dark (12L:12D) cycle and harvested at Zeitgeber time 2 (ZT2) and ZT14. JMJ17 associated with the 4C and 4D fragments in the PIF4 promoter and the 5B fragment in the PIF5 promoter at ZT14 ( Fig. 2, C and F ). JMJ18 also bound to the 4C and 4D regions in the PIF4 promoter but is associated with a broad region from 5A to 5C in the PIF5 promoter ( Fig. 2, I and J ). The promoter regions associated with JMJ17 and JMJ18 were close to the LUX binding site (LBS) in the PIF4 promoter but overlapped with the LBS in the PIF5 promoter ( Nusinow et al. 2011 ). Although LUX binds to the 4B region in the PIF4 promoter, LUX may nucleate a protein complex, including JMJ17 and JMJ18, that may occupy the neighboring regions, such as 4C. By contrast, we are unable to detect the association of JMJ17 and JMJ18 with the PIF4 and PIF5 promoters at ZT2 ( Supplementary Fig. S5 ).

Figure 2.

We then explored whether the association of JMJ17 or JMJ18 with the PIF4 and PIF5 promoters at ZT14 requires ELF3, ELF4, or LUX. We introduced the JMJ17:GFP and JMJ18:GFP transgenes into the elf3 , elf4 , or lux mutant background. JMJ17 and JMJ18 are not target genes of the EC, since the expression of the 2 genes was not altered in elf3 compared with Col-0 (see Supplementary Table S8 ). The expression levels of the JMJ17:GFP transgene in elf3 , elf4 or lux were comparable to that in the Col-0 background ( Supplementary Fig. S6A, to 6D ). The expression level of the JMJ18:GFP transgene in the elf3 and lux background was also similar to that in the Col-0 background ( Supplementary Fig. S6E ). The levels of their encoded proteins were also comparable in Col-0 and the mutants ( Supplementary Fig. S6F and 6G ). However, JMJ17 and JMJ18 were unable to associate with the 4C and 4D fragments in the PIF4 promoter or the 5B fragment in the PIF5 promoter in elf3 ( Fig. 2, D to I and J ). Notably, less JMJ17:GFP was recruited to the PIF4 promoter in elf4 than Col-0, but its recruitment to the PIF5 promoter was completely abolished in elf4 ( Fig. 2, E and H ). The PIF4 and PIF5 promoters were also not occupied by JMJ17:GFP or JMJ18:GFP in lux at ZT14 ( Supplementary Fig. S7 ). These results suggest that the association of JMJ17 and JMJ18 with the PIF4 or PIF5 promoter largely depends on the EC.

H3K4me3 modifications at the PIF4 and PIF5 loci in elf3 or jmj17 jmj18

JMJ17 and JMJ18 are KDM5/JARID1 group JmjC domain-containing histone demethylases ( Lu et al. 2008 ; Chen et al. 2011 ); members of this group also actively demethylate H3K4me1/2/3 in Arabidopsis ( Yang et al. 2012 ; Huang et al. 2019 ; Liu et al. 2019 ). JMJ17 and JMJ18 function as H3K4-specific demethylases via conserved iron-binding amino acids in vitro and in vivo ( Yang et al. 2012 ; Huang et al. 2019 ; Islam et al. 2021 ). We reasoned that JMJ17 and JMJ18 are likely recruited by ELF3 to the PIF4 and PIF5 promoters ( Fig. 2 ). We obtained 1 JMJ17 T-DNA insertion mutant line, SALK_037362 ( jmj17-2 ), and 2 JMJ18 T-DNA insertion mutant lines, SALK_011042 ( jmj18-7 ) and SALK_109242 ( jmj18-8 ), from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR). The jmj17-2 mutant contains a T-DNA insertion in the 19th exon of JMJ17 ( Supplementary Fig. S8A ). JMJ17 transcripts were completely absent in jmj17-2 , as revealed by RT-PCR analysis. RT-qPCR also revealed a significant reduction in JMJ17 expression in this mutant ( Supplementary Fig. S8A ). Both jmj18-7 and jmj18-8 contain T-DNA insertions in the promoter region of this gene ( Supplementary Fig. S8B ) and have approximately one-third the JMJ18 transcript levels as detected by RT-PCR and RT-qPCR analysis ( Supplementary Fig. S7B ). We therefore generated a new jmj18 allele using CRISPR–Cas9 editing known as jmj18-cr ( Supplementary Fig. S8C ). The jmj18-cr mutant showed long hypocotyls compared with Col-0 when grown under red light ( Supplementary Fig. S8, D and E ). We constructed the jmj17 jmj18 double by crossing jmj17-2 to jmj18-cr .

We examined the levels of H3K4me3 marks at the PIF4 and PIF5 loci in Col-0 and elf3 at ZT2 and ZT14. We selected similar genomic regions in the PIF4 and PIF5 promoters for ChIP followed by quantitative PCR (ChIP RT-qPCR) ( Fig. 3, A and B ). The EC functions predominantly in the dark; Arabidopsis plants showed more pronounced growth defects under shorter photoperiods when the EC was disrupted ( Song and Noh 2012 ). Our previous experiments were designed based on the observation that Arabidopsis is a long-day plant. A photoperiod of 12-h light/12-h dark might be close to natural and physiological conditions. However, such studies may not fully capture the phenotypic changes in the jmj17 jmj18 mutants. In addition, the EC plays a major role in thermogenesis through PIF4 ( Box et al. 2015 ). PIF4 directly targets genes involved in the heat response, and EC components modulate PIF4 activity. Therefore, to achieve a comprehensive understanding of EC function, we performed ChIP experiments against the 4 fragments at the PIF4 promoter at 20 and 27 °C under an 8-h light/16-h dark photoperiod. At ZT10, a similar level of H4K4me3 enrichment was observed for the 4C and 4D regions in jmj17 jmj18 at the 2 different temperatures; these results are comparable to those obtained under a 12-h light/12-h dark photoperiod ( Supplementary Fig. S9 ; Fig. 3 ). However, we detected better H4K4me3 enrichment at the 4B region in jmj17 jmj18 at 20 °C. We therefore performed all subsequent experiments at 20 °C. In Col-0, H3K4me3 marks were enriched at the 4C region of the PIF4 promoter and the 5C region of the PIF5 promoter at ZT2 but were diminished at ZT14 ( Fig. 3, D and F ). At ZT2, the H3K4me3 marks in elf3 were extended to the 4B and 4D regions of the PIF4 promoter and to the 5B region of the PIF5 promoter ( Fig. 3, C and E ). At ZT14, the H3K4me3 marks in elf3 were enriched and detected across the entire PIF4 and PIF5 promoter regions ( Fig. 3, D and F ).

Figure 3.

We further investigated whether the level of H3K4me3 was also affected in jmj17 jmj18 at the PIF4 and PIF5 loci by performing ChIP RT-qPCR. At ZT2, a similar enrichment pattern of H3K4me3 marks to that in elf3 was observed for both the PIF4 and PIF5 loci in the jmj17 jmj18 double mutant ( Fig. 3, C and E ). At ZT14, substantial levels of H3K4me3 marks were detected from the 4B to 4D regions in the PIF4 promoter and the 5A to 5C regions in the PIF5 promoter in the jmj17 jmj18 double mutant, like that in elf3 ( Fig. 3, D and F ). Overexpressing JMJ17 or JMJ18 reduced the enrichment of the H3K4me3 marks at the PIF4 and PIF5 loci at ZT14 compared with Col-0 ( Fig. 3, D and F to H ). When overexpressed in elf3 , neither JMJ17 nor JMJ18 was able to remove the H3K4me3 marks ( Supplementary Fig. S10 ). Therefore, the association of JMJ17/JMJ18 with the 2 PIF loci and the removal of H3K4me3 marks by JMJ17/JMJ18 are likely dependent on the EC at dusk.

We examined whether JMJ17 and JMJ18 are expressed in a circadian pattern like that of the 3 EC component genes. We entrained seedlings under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle for 7 days, transferred them to continuous light conditions ( Supplementary Fig. S11, A and B ), and collected samples at ZT0 and every 3 h thereafter for 60 h. JMJ17 and JMJ18 transcript levels weakly oscillated, with a peak at dusk. We also examined their expression patterns under a diurnal condition of 12-h light/12-h dark ( Supplementary Fig. S11, C to G ). We observed a similar expression peak in the early evening to late light for JIM17 , JMJ18 , and the 3 EC component genes, ELF3 , ELF4, and LUX . However, the magnitude of the oscillations for JMJ17 and JMJ18 was relatively small. We examined the accumulation of JMJ17:GFP and JMJ18:GFP under diurnal conditions or continuous light ( Supplementary Fig. S12, A and B ). We barely detected JMJ17:GFP and JMJ18:GFP protein oscillation, in contrast to that for ELF3, ELF4, and LUX ( Nusinow et al. 2011 ).

JMJ17 and JMJ18 repress PIF4 and PIF5 expression in the evening

We analyzed the diurnal expression of PIF4 and PIF5 in Col-0, jmj17 , jmj18, and jmj17 jmj18 seedlings grown under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle for 7 days ( Fig. 4 ). Samples were collected at ZT0 and every 3 h thereafter for 60 h. In general, the expression of PIF4 and PIF5 in jmj17 , jmj18, or jmj17 jmj18 was still rhythmic, and the diurnal pattern was not altered. Therefore, chromatin modifications by JMJ17 and JMJ18 at the target sites may only ensure proper amplitude but not entirely rhythmicity. PIF4 expression increased 2- to 3-fold in the jmj17 and jmj18 single mutants ( Fig. 4, A and C ) but 3- to 4-fold in the jmj17 jmj18 double mutant ( Fig. 4, E and G ), suggesting that JMJ17 and JMJ18 might have redundant functions. In an earlier study, PIF4 expression was arrhythmic in elf3 , and the increase in PIF4 expression in elf3-8 or in h2a.z was 2- to 3-fold in the evening ( Tong et al. 2020 ). HDA9-mediated chromatin remodeling for histone H3 in hox15-2 or hda9-1 only increases GI expression a few fold but does not alter its rhythmicity ( Park et al. 2019 ). The changes in PIF5 expression were ∼2-fold in jmj17 , jmj18 , and jmj17 jmj18 in the evening ( Fig. 4, B, D, F, and H ).

Figure 4.

The expression of PIF4 and PIF5 also significantly increased at ZT2 and remained higher during the day in jmj17 and jmj18 compared with Col ( Fig. 4, E and F ). ELF3, ELF4, and LUX accumulate to their highest levels in the evening, but they still accumulate at considerable levels at ZT2 ( Nusinow et al. 2011 ). Although less EC is available during the day, the recruitment of the EC to the PIF4 and PIF5 loci is not completely abrogated during this time. The absence of the demethylases JMJ17 and JMJ18 at the PIF4 and PIF5 loci may lead to an increase in PIF4 and PIF5 expression in the morning. To compare the expression of PIF4 and PIF5 in jmj17 jmj18 vs. elf3 , we performed side-by-side analysis of the 2 mutants for 24 h ( Fig. 4 G and H ). This also served to assess the magnitude of changes in PIF4 and PIF5 expression in jmj17 jmj18 at ZT2 and ZT15. Compared with Col-0, the increases in PIF4 expression were 2.3-fold in jmj17 jmj18 and 4.9-fold in elf3 at ZT15 and 3.1-fold in jmj17 jmj18 and 5.9-fold in elf3 at ZT18 ( Fig. 4G ). The increases in PIF5 expression compared with Col-0 were 2.8-fold in jmj17 jmj18 and 4.1-fold in elf3 at ZT15 and 1.7-fold in jmj17 jmj18 and 2.5-fold in elf3 at ZT18 ( Fig. 4H ). By contrast, the difference in their expression between jmj17 jmj18 and Col-0 was 1.3- to 1.5-fold throughout the day. Therefore, it is important to measure both absolute increases and fold-changes in gene expression to obtain a broader perspective.

H3K4me3 marks were significantly enriched in elf3 at both the PIF4 and PIF5 loci at ZT2 ( Fig. 3, C and E ). However, the expression of PIF4 and PIF5 was significantly lower at ZT2 ( Fig. 4, G and H ). This contradicts the finding that EC is a repressor of PIF4 and PIF5 expression. The reason for this discrepancy remains unknown. Perhaps this difference could be attributed to the role of the EC as a central oscillator. In elf3 , the expression of PIF4 and PIF5 is arrhythmic and most likely, the expression of PIF4 and PIF5 might return to a level between the peak and trough. In addition, the weaker phenotype of jmj17 jmj18 compared with elf3 might be due to the involvement of one or more unidentified JMJs. JMJ29 was shown to interact with ELF3 to repress CCA1 and PRR9 ( Lee and Seo 2021 ). CCA1 and PRR9 expression in jmj29-1 had an early phase shift: after 64 h for CCA1 and 90 h for PRR9 under continuous light. We created the jmj29-cr1 mutant with a small deletion in JMJ29 and the jmj29-cr2 mutant with a base insertion in the jmj17 jmj18 background through CRISPR–Cas9 editing ( Supplementary Fig. S13, A and B ). The expression of either PIF4 or PIF5 was not altered in jmj29-cr1 at ZT17 compared with Col-0, as revealed by RT-qPCR ( Supplementary Fig. S13, C and D ). The expression of PIF4 and PIF5 in the jmj17 jmj18 jmj29-cr2 triple mutant was also comparable to that in the jmj17 jmj 18 double mutant ( Supplementary Fig. S13, C and D ).

Genetic interaction between JMJ17 / JMJ18 and PIF4 / PIF5 or EC genes

We examined the genetic interaction between JMJ17 / JMJ18 and PIF4 / PIF5 by measuring hypocotyl elongation in Col-0, jmj17-2, jmj18-cr , jmj17 jmj18 , pif4 pif5 and jmj17 jmj18 pif4 pif5 under 12-h white light/12-h dark conditions. The jmj17-2 and jmj18-cr mutants showed long hypocotyls compared with Col-0, whereas the jmj17 jmj18 double mutant exhibited slightly longer hypocotyls than the single mutants ( Fig. 5A ). By contrast, pif4 pif5 had short hypocotyls under diurnal white light ( Fig. 5B ). We genotyped the pif4 pif5 double mutant using T-DNA- and gene-specific primers ( Supplementary Fig. S14, A and B ). The jmj17 jmj18 pif4 pif5 quadruple mutant exhibited short hypocotyls comparable to those of the pif4 pif5 double mutant. Therefore, the loss of PIF4 and PIF5 was epistatic to the loss of JMJ17 and JMJ18. JMJ17 and JMJ18 function upstream of PIF4 and PIF5 in this signaling branch.

We then studied the epistasis of JMJ17 and JMJ18 with EC genes. We introduced 35S::JMJ17:GFP into the Col-0 to elf3 , elf4 and lux backgrounds via transformation and examined JMJ17 expression levels in Col-0 and these various lines ( Supplementary Fig. S14, C to F ). We introduced pJMJ18::JMJ18:GFP into Col-0 and crossed the resulting transgenic line with elf3 or lux . JMJ18 driven by its own promoter was also expressed at a high level in the transgenic plants compared with the endogenous gene ( Supplementary Fig. S6E ). Similar expression levels were observed for the JMJ17 and JMJ18 transgenes in Col-0 and the elf3 , elf4 and lux mutants. For 35S::JMJ17:GFP , at least 10 transgenic lines were created and at least 5 independent lines were examined. The JMJ17 or JMJ18 overexpression lines had considerably shorter hypocotyls than Col-0 under diurnal white light ( Fig. 5, C and G ). The elf3 , elf4, and lux mutants had long hypocotyls ( Zagotta et al. 1996 ; Khanna et al. 2003 ; Hazen et al. 2005 ). By contrast, plants overexpressing JMJ17 or JMJ18 in the elf3 , elf4 or lux background produced hypocotyls like those of elf3, elf4, or lux under diurnal white light ( Fig. 5, D to F and H to I ). Therefore, the functions of JMJ17 and JMJ18 in hypocotyl elongation are dependent on the EC.

Genome-wide CUT&Tag and RNA-seq analysis of elf3 and jmj17 jmj18

We investigated the global profiles of the H3K4me3 marks likely erased by JMJ17 and JMJ18 H3K4me3 demethylases. We performed Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) sequencing analysis of Col-0 and elf3 at ZT14 using IgG or anti-H3K4me3 antibodies. We did not perform CUT&Tag analysis of jmj17 and jmj18 . The regions targeted by JMJ17 and JMJ18 might be broad in addition to the loci targeted by the EC for circadian gene expression. For example, the KDM5 group JmjC domain H3K4 demethylases play important roles in plant development ( Huang et al. 2019 ; Liu et al. 2019 ; Islam et al. 2021 ; Wang et al. 2021 ). JMJ17 is involved in cotyledon greening during seedling establishment and plays a crucial role in the responses of Arabidopsis to dehydration stress and abscisic acid ( Huang et al. 2019 ; Islam et al. 2021 ). JMJ18 reduces the level of H3K4 methylation in the FLOWERING LOCUS C ( FLC ) chromatin region and represses the expression of FLC to stimulate flowering ( Yang et al. 2012 ).

We studied the methylation status of the PIF4 and PIF5 promoter regions close to the EC binding sites ( Fig. 3 ). Although modification starts in the promoter region, methylation or demethylation should be extended to the gene body in most cases. For example, enrichment of H3K4me3 marks was mostly detected in the coding regions from transcription start sites (TSS) to transcription termination sites (TTS) ( Fig. 6A ). The CUT&Tag profiles of all biological replicates for Col-0 and elf3 in the presence of IgG or anti-H3K4me3 antibodies are shown in Supplementary Fig. S15 . In Col-0, the enriched H3K4me3 marks were defined by comparing the total counts using anti-H3K4me3 antibodies to that of IgG above a threshold of at least 2-fold and P < 0.05 ( Supplementary Table S2 ). This analysis revealed 17,052 H3K4me3-enriched loci in Col-0, as calculated from 3 biological replicates. We examined changes in H3K4me3 counts in elf3 via a comparison to Col-0 locus by locus ( Supplementary Table S3 ). A total of 976 loci had much more highly enriched H3K4me3 marks in elf3 than Col-0 at ZT14, with a change greater than 1.50-fold and P < 0.05 ( Supplementary Table S4 ). We then set the threshold to 1.5-fold instead of 2.0-fold to detect more candidate loci aligning with genes with increased expression in elf3 . Approximately 438 loci had decreased levels of H3K4me3 marks in elf3 at ZT14, with fold change > 2.0 and P < 0.05 ( Supplementary Table S5 ). We increased the threshold from 1.5 to 2.0 when comparing the reduced levels of H3K4me3 marks in elf3 compared with Col-0. Why many loci had reduced H3K4me3 marks in elf3 remains unclear. As discussed above, perhaps other JMJs are involved in this process in addition to JMJ17 and JMJ18. The unidentified JMJ H3K4me3 demethylases might be activated in some manner to compensate for the loss of JMJ17 and JMJ18 in a locus-specific manner.

Discussion

PIF4 and PIF5 play negative roles in plant photomorphogenesis and play crucial roles in light-regulated hypocotyl growth ( Huq and Quail 2002 ; Nusinow et al. 2011 ). PIF4 and PIF5 have an expression peak in the morning, and their encoded proteins also accumulate to high levels in the morning ( Huq and Quail 2002 ; Chen and Chory 2011 ). The transcription of PIF4 and PIF5 is repressed by the EC during the early evening ( Nusinow et al. 2011 ). Here we propose a model explaining how EC represses the expression of PIF4 and PIF5 in Arabidopsis ( Fig. 8 ). JMJ17 and JMJ18 are recruited to the PIF4 and PIF5 loci by ELF3 in the EC. The EC-JMJ17/JMJ18 complex is then able to erase the H3K4me3 marks at the PIF4 and PIF5 loci. The expression of PIF4 and PIF5 is therefore repressed in the dark, and the hypocotyl growth of seedlings is released.

Figure 8.

We split full-length JMJ17 and JMJ18 into the JMJ17-N, JMJ17-C, JMJ18-N, and JMJ18-C to test their interactions with ELF3 or ELF4. JMJ17-N contains JmjN, ARID, PHD, JmjC, and zf-C5HC2 domains, whereas JMJ17-C contains PLU-1 and PHD domains ( Supplementary Fig. S3A ). JMJ18-N contains JmjN, JmjC, and zf-C5HC2 domains, whereas JMJ18-C contains FYRN and FYRC domains. The JmjN and JmjC domains are needed for the enzyme activity of histone demethylases ( Klose et al. 2006 ). The zf-C5HC2 domain is involved in DNA binding in mammals and yeast ( Akhtar et al. 2010 ; Liang et al. 2013 ). The ARID domain is also a DNA-binding module ( Tu et al. 2008 ). The PHD is a reader domain that recognizes various histone modifications and is usually paired with other reader modules, such as PHD-PHD, PHD-Bromo, and PHD-Chromo domains ( Wang and Patel 2011 ). The PLU-1 domain of JMJ17 is required for ABA responses ( Wang et al. 2021 ). The FYRN/C domains interact with other transcription factors and associate with their targets ( Liu et al. 2019 ). Strikingly, ELF3 or ELF4 of the EC only interacted with the full-length JMJ17 and JMJ18 but not with the truncated versions of JMJ17 and JMJ18 ( Supplementary Fig. S3, B and C ).

In the EC, LUX recognizes its genomic targets and recruits ELF3 as a scaffold protein to bring ELF4 to the complex ( Nusinow et al. 2011 ). Although LUX has a homologous gene ( NOX ), LUX might play a major role in this process ( Helfer et al. 2011 ). ELF4 promotes the nuclear localization of the entire EC including ELF3 and LUX ( Helfer et al. 2011 ; Herrero et al. 2012 ). ELF4 often interacts with other proteins in an ELF3-dependent manner, reinforcing the idea that ELF3 acts as a hub to link circadian expression and light-signaling networks ( Huang et al. 2016 ). Here we showed that JMJ17 interacts with both ELF3 and ELF4, whereas JMJ18 only interacts with ELF3 ( Fig. 1A ; Supplementary Fig. S2C ). The interaction of JMJ17 with ELF3 does not depend on ELF4. JMJ17 and JMJ18 were unable to associate with the PIF4 and PIF5 promoters in either elf3 or lux ( Fig. 2 ; Supplementary Fig. S7 ). JMJ17 still associated with the PIF4 promoter, but at a reduced magnitude, in elf4 at ZT14 compared with Col-0 ( Fig. 2, C and E ). Together, these results point to the formation of a potential protein complex, which should be explored in the future.

Huang et al. (2016) demonstrated that multiple protein–protein interactions within a complex can have synergistic effects on gene expression. ELF4 interacts with the prion-like domain of ELF3 near its intermediate domain ( Herrero et al. 2012 ; Jung et al. 2020 ). The N-terminal portion of ELF3 facilitated its interaction with phyB (in either the red or far-red light-absorbing form) in Y2H assays ( Liu et al. 2001 ). The C-terminal region of ELF3 directly interacts with TOC1 ( Huang et al. 2016 ). Therefore, JMJ17 or JMJ18 might interact with a specific region of ELF3. Identifying this region remains an outstanding issue. Given the finding that JMJ18 interacts with ELF3 in a protein complex, this region might be less critical for the direct interaction of JMJ18 with ELF4. ELF4 recruits ELF3 into foci ( Nusinow et al. 2011 ). JMJ17 and JMJ18 might also associate with these foci involving ELF3 and ELF4 in the nucleus. The potential co-localization of JMJ17 and JMJ18 with ELF3 and ELF4 foci could be studied through immunofluorescence or imaging techniques. In addition, JMJ17 and JMJ18 may communicate with other chromatin remodeling factors such as histone acetyltransferases or deacetylases for H3K9. Analyzing this issue could provide a more comprehensive understanding of dynamic chromatin remodeling and gene expression.

Based on the finding that ELF3 interacts with JMJ17 and JMJ18, we performed CUT&Tag with anti-H3K4me3 antibodies in Col-0 and elf3 and RNA-seq of Col-0, elf3 and jmj17 jmj18 . Almost half of the genes with either increased or decreased expression in elf3 were also found in jmj17 jmj18 ( Fig. 6C ). We aligned the enriched H3K4me3 peaks in elf3 with the upregulated or downregulated genes in either elf3 or jmj17 jmj18 ( Fig. 6D ). Approximately half of the targets were found in both elf3 and jmj17 jmj18 . In conclusion, JMJ17 and JMJ18 might play major roles in regulating the expression of many target genes of the EC such as PIF4 and PIF5 . Other target loci of EC encode key players in photosynthesis, light signaling, the circadian clock, and phytohormone signaling ( Ezer et al. 2017 ). In addition to PIF4 and PIF5 , PIL1 , and PIF7 are also on the list of genes repressed by the EC and JMJ17/JMJ18. Other components of light signaling or photoperiodic flowering in the BBX family are also on this list, such as BBX1 (CO), BBX6 (COL5), BBX16, BBX18, BBX25 (STH), BBX28, and BBX31. This list also includes the CDF family genes CDF1 and CDF2 . CDFs are involved in flowering and abiotic stress responses and promote hypocotyl elongation ( Martín et al. 2018 ). PIF4 and CDF2 are temporally and spatially co-expressed to form a complex to promote hypocotyl elongation ( Gao et al. 2022 ). Another target gene, PKS4 , encodes an inhibitor of hypocotyl growth ( Schepens et al. 2008 ). MYB61 acts as a crucial nexus to carbon and nitrogen metabolism in rice ( Gao et al. 2020 ). RGL1 is a repressor of the gibberellin signaling pathway whose overexpression reverses plant senescence ( Chen et al. 2017 ).

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

All Arabidopsis thaliana plants use in this study are in Columbia-0 (Col-0) background except the elf4 mutant, which is in the Wassilewskija (Ws) background. The mutant lines elf3 (CS3793), elf4 (CS9879), lux (SALK_022315), pif4 (CS66043) and pif5 (SALK_087012) were described previously ( Hicks et al. 2001 ; Huq and Quail 2002 ; Zhang et al. 2015 ; Tian et al. 2021 ). jmj17-2 , jmj18-7 and jmj18-8 seeds were obtained from ABRC ( https://abrc.osu.edu/ ). Double and quadruple mutants of pif4 with other pif or jmj mutants were generated by crossing pif4 and genotyped by PCR using the primers listed in Supplementary Table S14 . All plants were grown under a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle at 22 °C in a growth room. For circadian expression analysis, Arabidopsis seedlings were entrained under 12-h light/12-h dark cycles, transferred to continuous light, and grown for various durations as described in the figure legends. To analyze hypocotyl elongation, seedlings were treated with white light for 2 h after a 3-day vernalization at 4 °C and grown under a 12-h light (30 μmol m −2 s −1 white light)/12-h dark cycle for 7 days. The hypocotyls were photographed and hypocotyl lengths were measured using ImageJ software ( http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij ).

Plasmid construction and plant transformation

All primers used to generate various constructs are listed in Supplementary Table S14 , and all constructs were produced using the Gateway system. For pJMJ17::JMJ17:YFP , 2 DNA fragments were amplified by the JMJ17-gF1/gR1 and JMJ17-gF2/gR2 primer sets to amplify the 1721bp JMJ17 promoter and 7285 bp JMJ17 genomic coding sequence (CDS). The pCAMBIA1302-YFP vector was linearized by digestion with EcoR Ⅰ and Sal Ⅰ. The final construct was assembled using a Vazyme ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit. For the pJMJ18::JMJ18:GFP and pJMJ18::JMJ18:MYC constructs, the JMJ18 genomic DNA sequence containing ∼2 Kb of the JMJ18 native promoter sequence was PCR-amplified, cloned into the TA vector, and recombined into the pMDC107 or pMDC303 vector. The full-length CDS of JMJ17 was PCR-amplified, cloned into the pCR8/GE/TOPO TA cloning vector, and recombined into the pEarleyGate 103 vector to generate the 35S::JMJ17:GFP construct. The ELF3 genomic fragment containing ∼2 Kb of the sequence upstream of the ELF3 start codon was PCR-amplified from Arabidopsis Col-0 genomic DNA. The fragment was cloned into the TA vector and recombined into the pEarleyGate 303 vector to generate the pELF3::ELF3:MYC construct. All stable transgenic plants were generated in the Col-0 background by the floral dip method with Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 ( Clough and Bent 1998 ). To overexpress JMJ17 and JMJ18 in the elf3 , elf4 , and lux mutant backgrounds, the JMJ17 and JMJ18 transgenes were introduced into the elf3 , elf4 , or lux background via genetic cross.

CRISPR–Cas9 editing of JMJ18 and JMJ29

For CRISPR–Cas9 knockout, 2 sgRNA oligos against either JMJ18 or JMJ29 were annealed ( Supplementary Figs. S8 and S13 ). The gRNAs were evaluated for target specificity using the potential off-target finder ( http://www.rgenome.net/cas-offinder/ ) and by genome-wide prediction of plant CRISPR/Cas9 targets ( http://www.genome.arizona.edu/crispr/CRISPRsearch.html ). The gRNAs did not have off-target sites according to this analysis. The sgRNA oligos were cloned into the pHEE401E vector ( Wang et al. 2015 ). The vectors were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 and introduced into Arabidopsis by the floral dip method. Kanamycin was used for selection in E. coli and hygromycin in plants.

To detect editing of JMJ18 , the genomic region was PCR-amplified with Cas9J18JD-F1 and Cas9J18JD-R1 primers and digested with the restriction enzyme BspTI. To detect editing of the C-terminus of JMJ29 in jmj29-cr1 , the genomic region was PCR-amplified with Cas9J29JD-F1 and Cas9J29JD-R1 primers and digested with the restriction enzyme BspTI. To detect editing of the N-terminus of JMJ29 in jmj29-cr2 , the genomic region was PCR-amplified with Cas9J29JD-F2 and Cas9J29JD-R2 primers and digested with the restriction enzyme DpnII. Genomic fragments in homozygous edited lines were PCR-amplified and sequenced for lesions. All primers are listed in Supplementary Table S14 .

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from seedlings grown under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle for 7 days and sampled at ZT14 using an Omini Plant RNA Kit (CWBIO) or GeneJET RNA mini kit (Thermo). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from the RNA with oligo(dT) primer using a SuperScript III reverse transcriptase kit (Invitrogen). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix Low ROX Premixed (Vazyme) in an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR machine (Thermo). The real-time PCR thermal cycling program was 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 56 °C for 10 s and 72 °C for 30 s, with a melting curve detected at 95 °C for 5 s, 65 °C for 1 min, 98 °C for 1 min and 40 °C for 10 s. Gene expression levels in each sample were normalized to that of the internal control UBIQUITIN 10 ( UBQ10 ) and are shown relative to the expression levels in the wild type. RT-qPCR was performed with 3 biological replicates and 3 technical replicates. The gene-specific primers used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S14 .

Yeast two-hybrid assays

Yeast two-hybrid assays and ONPG assays were performed using the Matchmaker Gold Yeast Two-Hybrid System (Takara) according to the manufacturer's instructions. JMJ14 , JMJ15 , JMJ16 , JMJ17 , JMJ18 , JMJ19 , JMJ17-N , JMJ17-C , JMJ18-N , JMJ18-C , ELF3 , ELF4 , and LUX cDNAs were subcloned into the GBD vector pDEST-GBKT7 or the GAD vector pDEST-GADT7. All plasmids were co-transformed into yeast strain AH109 using the lithium acetate transformation method. Yeast cells were selected on SD/-Trp/-Leu medium, and protein–protein interactions were detected in yeast cells grown on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade medium at 30 °C or in liquid medium for the α-galactosidase activity assay.

Yeast three-hybrid assays

The pBridge vector contains 2 distinct multiple cloning sites to allow for the expression of a GBD fusion protein and another protein. ELF3 fused with the GBD was introduced into one cloning site and ELF4 was introduced into a different cloning site in the pBridge vector. The resulting construct was used to screen a normalized Arabidopsis Mate and Plate library (Takara) for interacting partners. Yeast cells were selected on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-Met (SD/-T-L-M) medium. Subsequent Y3H interactions were screened on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-Met/-His/-Ade (SD/-T-L-M-H-A) medium at 30 °C for 3 days as described in Methods in Molecular Biology (MIMB, volume 1363).

BiFC assays

Full-length JMJ17 , JMJ18 , ELF3 , and ELF4 cDNAs were PCR-amplified and recombined into pSPYCE-35S or pSPYNE-35S vector ( Walter et al. 2004 ). For BiFC assays, the epidermal layers of the scaly leaves of onion were peeled with a medical scalpel and tweezers and infiltrated with A. tumefaciens harboring various constructs. The onion epidermal layers were incubated for 20 min. After removing excess Agrobacterium solution with sterilized filter paper, the onion epidermal cells were incubated for 2 to 3 days in the dark at 28 °C. Fluorescence images were acquired under YFP optics using an Olympus BX51 microscope with an Olympus DP26 CCD camera (Waltham).

Co-IP assays

Transgenic seedlings harboring 35S::JMJ17:GFP , pJMJ18::JMJ18:GFP , pELF3::ELF3:MYC and pELF4::ELF4:Flag were used for the Co-IP assays. Total proteins were extracted from the samples with IP buffer (10% glycerol, 25 m m Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 1 m m EDTA, 150 m m NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, 2 m m β-mercaptoethanol, 1 m m PMSF, and 1× protease inhibitors). The protein extracts were centrifuged at 17,000 g for 30 min. The bait and prey proteins were then mixed and incubated with anti-GFP rabbit polyclonal antibody (Thermo Fisher) for 2 h at 4 °C. After incubation, the beads were washed 3 times with IP buffer. The beads were pelleted, boiled in 20 μL 2 × SDS buffer, and the proteins separated by 8% or 12% SDS-PAGE and detected by immunoblotting using monoclonal anti-GFP (TransGen Biotech), anti-MYC (Thermo Fisher), or anti-Flag (Thermo Fisher) antibodies at 1:1,000 dilution.

ChIP RT-qPCR assay

Arabidopsis seedlings for the ChIP assay were grown under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle for 7 days, transferred to continuous light, and harvested at ZT2 or ZT14. The fresh plant materials were fixed and cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 20 min, and crosslinking was stopped by adding 2 m glycine as described previously ( Sun et al. 2019 ). The samples were ground to a powder in liquid nitrogen. The chromatin complexes were isolated from the samples with nuclear lysis buffer and sonicated to generate fragments from 300 to 1000 bp. The sonicated chromatin complex was then immunoprecipitated by anti-GFP (Thermo Fisher), anti-MYC (Thermo Fisher) or anti-H3K4me3 (Active Motif, 39,159) antibodies for 8 h. After the reversal of the crosslinking and treatment with proteinase K, immunoprecipitated DNA fragments were analyzed by RT-qPCR using primers specific for various loci. Fold enrichment for specific chromatin fragments was normalized to the enrichment level of the Arabidopsis UBQ10 gene and the control with immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies. Each biological replicate was represented by at least 3 technical replicates. All primers used for ChIP RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S14 .

RNA-Seq library preparation, sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was isolated from Col-0, elf3 , or jmj17 jmj18 seedlings at ZT17 using an RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) and treated with RNase-free DNase. The RNA-seq libraries were constructed from at least 3 biological replicates for each genotype. The libraries were sequenced in paired-end 150 bp mode on an Illumina HiSeq 2,500 sequencing platform.

The low-quality reads ( q < 20) and adapters were trimmed using cutadapt ( Martin 2011 ) with the setting “- q 20 - Q 20 –minimum-length = 50”. For each sample, an average of approximately 21,929,262 trimmed reads per RNA-seq sample were mapped to the TAIR10 reference genome using the Hitsat2 aligner ( Kim et al. 2015 ) in “–no-unal” regular mode. Samtools was utilized to filter alignments with low-quality mapping (MAPQ < 30), sort the SAM alignment files, and convert the files to BAM files ( Li et al. 2009 ). The BAM files were subsequently used to generate read counts for each locus using htseq-count ( Anders et al. 2015 ) with the parameters “-f bam -s no -t gene -r pos -i Dbxref”. The EdgeR package was then used to determine the differential expression values between Col-0 and elf3 or jmj17 jmj18 ( Robinson et al. 2010 ). The list of genes with significant increases or decreases in expression was generated based on at least a 2-fold change in expression ( P < 0.05). GO analysis of the functional categories was performed with AgriGO v2.0 ( Tian et al. 2017 ).

CUT&Tag experiments

For CUT&Tag analysis of the H3K4me3 marks, seedlings were grown under a 12-h white light/12-h dark cycle for 7 days and sampled at ZT14. Nuclei were isolated from 2 g seedlings in 30 mL nucleus isolation buffer as previously reported ( Saleh et al. 2008 ), and the subsequent steps were performed as previously described with minor modifications ( Ouyang et al. 2021 ). CUTANA pAG-Tn5 was used for the CUT&Tag experiments (EpiCypher) and anti-H3K4me3 antibodies (Active Motif, 39,159). The CUT&Tag sequencing library was prepared using 3 to 6 μL of tagmented DNA purified in a 25 μL PCR for 15 cycles with i5 and i7 primers and Q5 high fidelity DNA polymerase (NEB). AMPure XP beads were used to purify the PCR-enriched DNA libraries. The CUT&Tag libraries were pooled and sequenced in paired-end 150 bp mode on an Illumina HiSeq 2,500 sequencing platform.

CUT&Tag data analysis

Raw paired-end reads in the CUT&Tag sequencing libraries were trimmed with Trimgalore ( https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore ) to remove low-quality bases ( q < 20) and adapters. Trimmed reads at least 32 bp long were aligned to the TAIR10 genome using bowtie2 in “–local –very-sensitive” mode ( Langmead and Salzberg 2012 ). The reads that were not uniquely mapped and PCR duplicates were removed with Samtools ( Li et al. 2009 ) and Picard ( http://picard.sourceforge.net/ ). The average number of reads for each of the 4 +IgG libraries was 3,912,127, and the average number of reads for each of the 6 +anti-H3K4me3 libraries was 12,740,625. The reads were selected in SAM files. Bedtools ( Quinlan and Hall 2010 ) was used to transfer and normalize (RPGC method) the Sam files to bedGraph files (bin size 10 bp) for next-step analysis. DeepTools was used to generate the heatmap ( Ramírez et al. 2014 ). The Integrative Genomics Viewer genome browser was used to visualize the various regions with enriched coverage ( Thorvaldsdottir et al. 2013 ).

The peak regions defined from the start to the end by other peak calling programs are variable for different biological replicates. Different peak counts for the levels of H3K4me3 enrichment were thus obtained for the different biological replicates in either Col-0 or elf3 . The enriched regions for anti-H3K4me3 marks generally occurred from the TSS to the TTS for most loci ( Fig. 6A ). Therefore, each individual locus was counted by summing the covered reads from the TSS to the TTS in the bedGraph files. In Col-0, the enriched H3K4me3 marks were defined as the total counts with anti-H3K4me3 antibodies to that of IgG above a threshold of 2-fold and P < 0.05 ( Supplementary Table S2 ). Changes in the H3K4me3 counts in elf3 were determined by subtracting the counts from their IgG controls and comparing the net counts in Col-0 versus elf3 from locus to locus ( Supplementary Table S3 ).

CUT&Tag-qPCR

PCR-enriched DNA libraries were purified with AMPure XP beads and dissolved in 30 μL of ddH 2 O. One μL was used for qPCR analysis with a 5′ primer derived from the i5 adaptor and 3′ gene-specific primers listed in Supplementary Table S14 ( Zhang et al. 2024 ).
