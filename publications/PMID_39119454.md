## Full Text

Abstract

Introduction

Diencephalic-mesencephalic junction dysplasia syndrome is a rare neurogenetic disorder reported to be caused by variants in several genes. Phenotypic presentation is characterized by clinical findings including developmental delay, hypotonia, spasticity, and dyskinetic movements in combination with distinctive imaging features on brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Methods

Whole exome sequencing was conducted to unveil the molecular etiology of patients presenting with neurological manifestations from two unrelated families.

Results

To the best of our knowledge, here we report the third family affected with diencephalic-mesencephalic junction dysplasia caused by a novel variant in GSX2 and two siblings with a PCDH12 variant exhibiting a less severe phenotype. The siblings with a PCDH12 variant were positioned at the milder end of the phenotypic spectrum. Although both exhibited a clinical phenotype resembling cerebral palsy, one showed partial fusion of the hypothalamus and mesencephalon, whereas MRI was unremarkable in the other. Biallelic GSX2 variants have been implicated in basal ganglia agenesis, and similarly, our patients had basal ganglia hypoplasia along with hypothalamic-mesencephalic fusion.

Conclusion

Identifying variants associated with the syndrome in different genes will contribute to genotype-phenotype correlation.

Keywords: Diencephalic-mesencephalic junction dysplasia, PCDH12 , GSX2 , Brain malformation, Whole exome sequencing

Diencephalic-mesencephalic junction dysplasia (DMJD) is a rare brain malformation characterized by the “butterfly sign” seen on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) that refers to enlargement of the dorsoventral axis of the midbrain commonly associated with a deep midline cleft of the midbrain continuous with the third ventricle, which represents abnormal cleavage between the diencephalon and mesencephalon [ 1 ]. Later on, the phenotypic spectra of DMJD have been expanded [ 2 , 3 ]. DMJD is associated with a variety of brain malformation.

Recent investigations on the molecular basis of embryonic brain development have revealed a number of molecules that are involved in pattern formation of the basic brain structures during the 4th and 5th weeks of gestation, comprising the prosencephalon (forebrain) divided into telencephalon (cortices, white matters, and basal nuclei) and diencephalon (thalamus, hypothalamus, and epithalamus), mesencephalon (midbrain), and rhombencephalon (hindbrain) [ 4 , 5 ]. The ganglionic eminence arising from the ventral telencephalon is an important transitory fetal brain structure that generates the basal ganglia, thalami, olfactory bulb, and cortical interneurons [ 4 – 6 ]. It is well known that Fgf8 that is expressed in the isthmus organizer at the boundary between the midbrain and hindbrain is a master regulator of the signaling cascade for early brain development, which drives expression of the engrailed (En) and Paired box (Pax) transcription factors and plays an essential role in signal transduction processes required for DMJ formation [ 1 , 7 , 8 ].

On molecular grounds, DMJD is currently divided into two types: type 1 (DMJD1) due to biallelic PCDH12 pathogenic variants and type 2 (DMJD2) due to biallelic GSX2 pathogenic variants. To date, 19 affected families with PCDH12 -related DMJD (DMJD type 1) have been reported, while only 2 affected families with GSX2 -related DMJD (type 2) have been reported [ 9 – 16 ]. PCDH12 encodes a cell adhesion molecule termed protocadherin-12 that is a member of the nonclustered protocadherin family of calcium-dependent cell adhesion proteins. In experiments on human cerebral organoid cultures, loss of protocadherin-12 expression results in proliferation defects, accelerated neuronal differentiation, impaired neuronal migration, and aberrant lamination [ 17 ]. GSX2 encodes a transcription factor that belongs to a group of homeobox genes. GSX2 is highly expressed in the hindbrain and lateral ganglionic eminence of the forebrain, whose normal structures are missing in Gsh-2 mutant mice [ 18 ]. It is also postulated that L1CAM encoding a cell adhesion molecule and VRK1 encoding a serine/threonine kinase are responsible for rare cases with DMJD [ 3 ]. These cell adhesion molecules, transcription factor, and signal transduction kinase play a pivotal role in brain embryogenesis in conjunction with the FGF8 -signaling cascade [ 1 , 7 , 8 ].

GSX2 -mutated cells have been shown to exhibit increased expression levels of PAX6 , which plays a role in neuronal migration and differentiation [ 9 ]. PAX6 expression is essential for the normal formation of the DMJ with other several genes regulated by FGF8 [ 8 ]. Besides, it has been proposed that the overexpression of PAX6 may lead to a reduction in cortical cell production [ 19 ]. L1CAM is a neuronal cell adhesion molecule that enhances neurite outgrowth through the direct fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1) interaction [ 20 ]. FGFR1 is expressed in the midbrain–hindbrain junction, suggesting that FGFR1 could serve as the receptor for FGF8 -mediated signaling in this region [ 21 ]. The loss of PCDH12 expression has been demonstrated to result in premature neuronal differentiation and defective migration, attributed to the disruption of its interaction with cytoskeleton regulators [ 22 ]. However, further research is needed to elucidate the mechanisms through which the expression and interaction of these genes lead to the formation of DMJD. Herein, we report on an additional family with PCDH12 -related DMJD and the third family with GSX2 -related DMJD, providing further insights into the genotypic information of DMJD.

Case Report

Family 1 Patient 1: The proband was born after a full-term pregnancy with a birth weight of 3,510 g to a consanguineous couple. She was the third child of first-cousin parents. Their second live-born child deceased at the age of 4 months due to respiratory distress and swallowing difficulties. Our patient had been suffering from feeding difficulties, requiring tube feeding, and shortly after birth, she was hospitalized for a month due to respiratory distress. A cine esophagram was performed owing to her ongoing feeding problems and revealed aspiration with no opening of the upper esophageal sphincter. She underwent direct laryngoscopy in view of her difficulty breathing which was noted to be normal. On echocardiography, a secundum atrial septal defect was noted. A cranial ultrasonography showed ventriculomegaly, while abdominal ultrasonography was normal. A brain MRI revealed mesencephalic malformation and diffuse symmetrical T2 hyperintensities in the subcortical white matter of the bilateral cerebral hemispheres.

On clinical admission at the age of 3.5 months, she had a body length of 60 cm (−0.42 SDS), a weight of 5,300 g (−1.05 SDS), and a head circumference of 40.5 cm (−0.01 SDS). She had axial hypotonia without dysmorphic features. She continued to have pneumonia and respiratory distress episodes necessitating ventilatory support, and she was hospitalized several times. Hypotonia was accompanied by peripheral hypertonicity in both upper and lower extremities and severe episodic dsytonic posturing and opisthotonus from the age of 12 months. Also, it was noted that the patient could not achieve any developmental milestones.

Laboratory tests for screening inborn errors of metabolism, including plasma and urine amino acids, organic acids, very long chain fatty acids, lysosomal enzyme assays, and transferrin isoelectric focusing, were all normal. No clinical diagnosis could be established, and whole exome sequencing (WES) was conducted, which revealed a homozygous variant in GSX2 . Subsequently, upon re-evaluation of the brain MRI, hypoplasia of the basal ganglia, non-separation of the hypothalamus-thalamus-mesencephalon, hypoplastic brainstem, and optic nerves were noticed. The brain MRI scans of the patient’s deceased sibling (Patient 2) were evaluated, revealing hypoplasia of the basal ganglia, non-separation of the hypothalamus and mesencephalon, enlarged interthalamic adhesion, and brainstem hypoplasia, thus suggesting that they were affected by the same disorder. Brain MRI findings of the patient and her sister (Patient 2) are presented in Figures 1 and 2 . She was admitted with sepsis, acute renal failure, and hypovolemic shock; thus, peritoneal dialysis was applied. During the course of this last admission, she required intubation and underwent tracheostomy in view of worsening respiratory condition at the age of 16 months. Dystonia was drug-resistant and efforts failed to control intermittent dystonic posturing ranging from opisthotonus to status dystonicus with irritability and agitation. Involuntary muscle contractions in the patient escalated to a severity causing intermittent apnea. While clonazepam treatment was discontinued due to its tendency to increase secretions, improvement in dystonia was observed with combined therapy, including diazepam, baclofen, and gabapentin. She was discharged with a mechanical home ventilator and deceased at the age of 2 years due to respiratory failure.

Family 2 Patient 3: A 2-year-old boy presented with an initial diagnosis of cerebral palsy. He was born as the third live-born child of a consanguineous couple, with a birth weight of 2,400 g at the 36th gestational week. The patient had been monitored for 2 days in the neonatal intensive care unit due to jaundice. He achieved unsupported sitting at 8 months of age, independent walking at 18 months, and limited speech and language development with only a few single words. He was on levothyroxine treatment for hypothyroidism and was operated on due to inguinal hernia. He had no obvious dysmorphic findings on physical examination and had right hemiparesis. On his last admission, he had a length of 95 cm (−0.66 SDS), a weight of 14 kg (−0.56 SDS), and a head circumference of 47 cm (−2.06 SDS). His brain MRI was unremarkable. He has global developmental delays affecting all five areas of development. He shows objects but does not name them when asked, cannot construct sentences with 2–3 words, and struggles with using tense suffixes or personal pronouns appropriately. He is unable to jump with both feet off the ground, string beads, eat with a spoon without spilling, and lacks the ability to imitate a straight line. He walks with the right foot slightly dragging on the ground and exhibits a preference for the left hand, indicating right hemiparesis.

Methods and Results

Genomic DNA was obtained from the peripheral blood of the patients and their parents using the salt precipitation method after informed consents were obtained from the families. Microarray analyzes were unremarkable in both families. WES was conducted by using Ion AmpliSeq Exome RDY kit and Ion Proton sequencer in Patient 1 and TWIST Comprehensive Exome Kit and MGI DNB SEQ G400 in Patient 3. Targeted sequencing was performed on Patient 4, who was the sibling of Patient 3 with similar complaints.

WES revealed a homozygous variant c.747G>C (p.Trp249Cys) in the GSX2 ( NM_133267.3 ) in Patient 1, which was not previously reported. The variant was further confirmed using Sanger sequencing, and her healthy parents were found to be heterozygous carriers of this variant. The detected variant was classified as a variant of uncertain significance according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines (PP3, PM2) [ 23 ] and was predicted to be deleterious by in silico prediction tools with a PolyPhen-2 score of 1.0, CADD score of 33, and MetaRNN score of 0.987. The variant has not been reported in reference population databases, such as gnomAD, ExAC, and 1000 G, and has a high evolutionary conservation score (PhyloP100way score: 10.003).

A homozygous nonsense variant NM_016580.4 : c.451C>T (p.Arg151Ter) in the PCDH12 was identified in Patients 3 and 4. Segregation analysis showed that both parents were heterozygous carriers for this variant. The detected variant was previously reported as pathogenic in the ClinVar database and was classified as pathogenic according to the ACMG guidelines (PVS1, PP5, PM2) [ 23 ].

Discussion

DMJD was first described by Zaki et al. [ 1 ] in 2012, who reported six cases from three Egyptian families with a novel central nervous system malformation. They named the condition “diencephalic-mesencephalic junction dysplasia,” inherited as an autosomal recessive trait [ 1 ]. Subsequently, Severino et al. [ 2 ] provided detailed information on two distinctive patterns of this malformation, termed types A and B, based on neuroimaging findings.

Type A pattern of DMJ (DMJD-A) is characterized by complete fusion between the hypothalamus and midbrain, presenting as an abnormal contour of the midbrain with or without a ventral cleft on axial MRI images [ 2 ]. DMJD-A is associated with other congenital brain abnormalities, including ventricular enlargement, nodular heterotopias, schizencephaly, callosal anomalies, basal ganglia abnormalities, elongated midbrain, kinked bulbo-medullary junction, hypoplasia of the hindbrain, and notably perithalamic and periventricular calcifications [ 2 ]. In addition, optic nerve hypoplasia, hypoplastic olfactory bulbs, and disrupted corticospinal tract projections with premature termination at the level of the pons have been described as well [ 2 ].

The clinical features of DMJD-A include spastic tetraparesis, axial hypotonia, severe developmental delay, microcephaly, seizure, and sometimes dyskinetic movements [ 2 ]. As a result of hypothalamic dysfunction, affected individuals may demonstrate vasomotor instability, fluctuations in body temperature, and sleep disturbances [ 2 ].

Type B pattern of DMJ (DMJD-B) is characterized by incomplete thalamic-mesencephalic cleavage with parenchymal bands between the interthalamic adhesion and the superior surface of the midbrain on sagittal MRI images [ 2 ]. Other brain abnormalities include hypothalamic adhesion, moderate enlargement of the lateral ventricles, nodular heterotopias, small midbrain, mild pontine, and vermian hypoplasia, as well as brain calcifications [ 2 ]. Clinical findings in affected individuals such as spastic hemiparesis, hypotonia, developmental delay, seizure, and microcephaly overlap with but tend to be less severe phenotype than those with DMJD-A [ 2 ]. In addition, there are reports that DMJD-B presents with mild cognitive impairment and movement disorder in adulthood [ 24 , 25 ]. Given the concurrence of brain calcifications and complex brain malformation in both DMJD-A and DMJD-B, the similarities and differences in clinical and imaging findings between this entity and TORCHS infection were brought to attention.

The correlation between the neuroimaging classification (DMJD-A and DMJD-B) and molecular classification ( PCDH12 -related DMJD and GSX2 -related DMJD) remains elusive. In the present study, the phenotype of the Family 2 with PCDH12 -related DMJD was consistent with DMJD-B; yet, neuroimaging abnormalities were not discernible in one sib (Patient 3). On the other hand, the phenotype of the Family 1 with GSX2 -related DMJD was compatible with DMJD-A. Extrapyramidal symptoms, including episodic dystonia and severe opisthotonos posturing, were notable in this family. Hypoplasia of the basal ganglia was a neuroimaging finding that attracted attention.

In 14 patients from 8 unrelated families with typical clinical presentation and MRI characteristics of DMJD, Guemez-Gamboa et al. [ 13 ] identified homozygous mutations in PCDH12 . In their study, genetic studies were first conducted in three families previously reported by Zaki et al. [ 1 ], and disease-causing variants in the PCDH12 were identified in two families, whereas no variant was detected in one family in whom extensive enlargement of lateral ventricles was present, suggesting a genotype-phenotype correlation [ 13 ]. Also, they determined loss of PCDH12 expression and reduced neurite length in neural precursor cells derived from the fibroblasts of affected individuals [ 13 ]. It has also been reported that PCDH12 variants should be considered in the differential diagnosis for congenital microcephaly and neurodevelopmental delay associated with intrauterine infections [ 12 ]. De Mori et al. [ 9 ] identified biallelic loss of function variants in GSX2 from 2 unrelated patients with DMJD-A, originally reported by Severino et al. [ 2 ]. Both patients displayed an identical phenotype, including hypothalamic-mesencephalic fusion with an abnormal contour of the midbrain, bilateral putaminal and pallidal agenesis with enlarged dysmorphic caudate nuclei, thalamic hypoplasia, periventricular nodular heterotopia, hypoplasia of the olfactory bulbs, and abnormal course of the corticospinal tracts at the level of the pons on brain MRI [ 2 , 9 ]. Moreover, among eight additional individuals with DMJD who were not associated with basal ganglia agenesis, no GSX2 variant was documented, indicating a possible genotype-phenotype correlation [ 9 ]. In a manner supportive of this assertion, basal ganglion hypoplasia was present in our patients as well. It is tentatively assumed that GSX2 -related DMJD is DMJD-A associated with malformation of the basal ganglia. Recently, Lawrence et al. [ 3 ] suggested a third pattern associated with DMJD, referred to as type C. DMJD-C is characterized by complete or near-complete continuity between the thalamus and the midbrain, commonly associated with fetal hydrocephalus [ 3 ]. Their study was based on fetal MRI findings of 33 pregnancies, including most commonly hydrocephalus and also other brain findings (aqueduct stenosis, corpus callosal abnormalities, cortical malformations, nodular heterotopia, schizencephaly, cerebellar hypoplasia, and kinked brainstem) [ 3 ]. They found 9 cases with DMJD-C. It has been reported that fetuses with DMJD-C postnatally survived and exhibited clinical manifestations associated with DMJD, such as global developmental delay, hypotonia, spasticity, and seizures [ 3 ]. In the same study, genetic analysis identified biallelic L1CAM variants in 4 patients with DMJD and compound heterozygous variants in VRK1 in 1 patient with DMJD-C [ 3 ].

There have been reports that imply the relationship between L1CAM and DMJD. Severino et al. [ 26 ] reported concurrence DMJD and fetal hydrocephalus in male fetuses whose manifestation in the second trimester raised a suspicion of X-linked hydrocephalus; yet, genetic testing was not available in this study. In the other study on fetuses diagnosed with L1 syndrome (a wide variety of X-linked inherited neurodevelopmental disorders due to mutations in L1CAM , such as X-linked hydrocephalus, MASA syndrome, X-linked complicated hereditary spastic paraplegia type 1, etc.), DMJD was found in a subset of affected individuals [ 27 ]. In our patients, there was no evidence of prenatal hydrocephalus documented during pregnancy.

In summary, to the best of our knowledge, we have presented the third family with DMJD caused by a GSX2 variant who displayed severe clinical manifestations and 2 additional patients with a milder phenotype resulting from a PCDH12 variant. A comparative analysis of the clinical and neuroimaging findings between the patients in this study and those with PCDH12 and GSX2 variants reported in the literature is presented in Table 1 . While DMJD was initially categorized into A, B, and C classifications, these distinctions were not consistently maintained in subsequent reported cases with identified molecular etiologies. As a result, a clear genotype-phenotype correlation could not be established; even individuals without brain malformations were reported, as was the case in 1 of our patients [ 10 , 11 ].

Precise molecular diagnosis allows families to be informed about prognosis and provides genetic counseling. This implies that WES can be used as a convenient diagnostic tool in malformations of cortical development where genetic etiology is heterogeneous. Of note, even in the absence of a malformation of cortical development, as presented in our Patient 3, highlights the value of genomic testing in “cerebral palsy-mimics” despite intrafamilial variability. Although the present report may contribute to broadening the genomic knowledge of the syndrome, further studies are needed to elucidate the cellular molecular etiology and the genotype-phenotype correlations in DMJD and overlap with cerebral palsy spectrum disorders.
