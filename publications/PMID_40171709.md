# Septin complexes: Ahead of the curve.

Individual cells have robust repair systems to survive cell cortex damage caused
by mechanical and chemical stresses, allowing them to maintain the integrity of
tissues and organs. The contraction of an actomyosin ring at the wound edge is a
major mechanism for physically closing the cell wound. In contrast to
polymerization and bundling of actin filaments, little is known about how linear
actin filaments are bent to be integrated into the actin ring structure
encircling the wound edge. We recently found that the five Drosophila Septins
function simultaneously in the regulation of actomyosin ring assembly,
contraction, and disassembly during cell wound repair. These Septins form two
distinct complexes-Sep1-Sep2-Pnut and Sep4-Sep5-Pnut-composed of different
subunits from the same groups. Strikingly, these two distinct Septin complexes
have different degrees of F-actin bending activities that are consistent with
their spatial recruitment: different degrees of curved actin filaments are
required for the robust formation of different regions of the actomyosin ring.
In addition, we found that the two Septin complexes are regulated by different
molecular pathways as a loss of Anillin only affects Sep1-Sep2-Pnut complex
recruitment. These findings open new directions for how individual Septin
subunits form complexes and function differentially in cellular and
developmental processes.

## Full Text

Cells frequently encounter physiological and environmental stresses that lead to the rupture of their cortex (plasma membrane and underlying cortical cytoskeleton). To prevent further damage or even death, the cell must rapidly implement a robust and efficient wound repair system to close the ruptured cell cortex and restore normal function (Sonnemann and Bement, 2012; Cooper and McNeil, 2015; Andrews and Corrotte, 2018; Nakamura et al., 2018). A major mechanism in this cell cortex repair is the formation and contraction of an actomyosin ring at the wound edge, which generates the physical force needed to pull the cortical cytoskeleton and overlying plasma membrane inward (Ammendolia et al., 2021; Ebstrup et al., 2021; Hui et al., 2022). Much of the research to date has focused on identifying molecules involved in regulating the polymerization and bundling of actin filaments at the wound edge (Hui et al., 2022; Hui et al., 2023). However, the recruited actin filaments must be bent in order to assemble into the actomyosin ring circumscribing the wound edge. Using the Drosophila cell wound repair model, our recent study demonstrated that two distinct Septin complexes have different degrees of actin filament bending activities and contribute to actomyosin ring assembly and disassembly during cell wound repair (Stjepic et al., 2024).

Septins are a family of conserved GTP-binding proteins, form hetero-oligomeric complexes that assemble into higher order structures such as filaments, rings, gauzes, or collars, and function in many cellular processes, including cytokinesis, cell polarity, epithelial wound healing, and membrane remodeling (Weirich et al., 2008; Founounou et al., 2013; Dolat and Spiliotis, 2016; Zander et al., 2016; Menon and Gaestel, 2017; Shindo et al., 2018; Spiliotis and Nakos, 2021; Wang et al., 2021). Deficiency or mis-regulation of Septins has been implicated in neurodegenerative diseases and cancers (Gilden and Krummel, 2010; Beise and Trimble, 2011; Dolat et al., 2014; Menon and Gaestel, 2017). While the number of Septin proteins varies among organisms, ranging from 13 in humans to 2 in C. elegans, individual Septins are subdivided into four groups (Sept2, Sept3, Sept6, and Sept7) based on amino acid sequence homology (Kinoshita, 2003; Cao et al., 2007). Septins typically form hetero-hexamers (Sept2- Sept6- Sept7- Sept7- Sept6- Sept2) or hetero-octamers (Sept2- Sept6- Sept7- Sept3- Sept3- Sept7- Sept6- Sept2) in a context-dependent manner (Sirajuddin et al., 2007; Weirich et al., 2008; McMurray and Thorner, 2019; Mendonca et al., 2019; Mendonca et al., 2021). Members of each Septin group are thought to be somewhat interchangeable and occupy the same position within the Septin complex (Kinoshita, 2003; Spiliotis and McMurray, 2020). Indeed, members of the same Septin group frequently exhibit functional redundancy in cellular and developmental processes (Peng et al., 2002; Ono et al., 2005; Fujishima et al., 2007; Tsang et al., 2008; O’Neill and Clark, 2013; Kaplan et al., 2017). Septin complexes localize to the cell cortex, where they bind to the plasma membrane, F-actin, and microtubules to function as a scaffold for other proteins, provide an anchor between membrane and cytoskeleton, and/or serve as diffusion barriers (Beise and Trimble, 2011; Mostowy and Cossart, 2012; Bridges and Gladfelter, 2015; Khan et al., 2015; Spiliotis and Nakos, 2021; Woods and Gladfelter, 2021; Benoit et al., 2023). One interesting feature of Septin function is that the Sept2- Sept6-Sept7 complex is associated with curved membranes and can bend actin filaments (Mavrakis et al., 2014; Bridges and Gladfelter, 2015; Bridges et al., 2016; Cannon et al., 2017).

Drosophila has 5 Septins that are grouped into three groups: Sep2 and Sep5 (Sept6), Sep1 and Sep4 (Sept2), and Pnut (Sept7) (Adam et al., 2000). To date, studies in Drosophila have focused on one Septin complex: Sep1-Sep2-Pnut (Field et al., 1996; Adam et al., 2000; Akhmetova et al., 2018; de Freitas Fernandes et al., 2023; Gabbert et al., 2023). Interestingly, all five Drosophila Septins are recruited to the cell wound edge. Unexpectedly, members of the same Septin class do not share similar recruitment patterns to the wound edge: Sep1 and Sep2 are recruited inside the actin ring, whereas Sep4 and Sep5 recruitment overlaps with the actin ring (Figure 1a). Strikingly, knockdowns of members within the same Septin class also do not exhibit similar phenotypes. In particular, the actomyosin ring is disassembled before completing wound closure in Sep1 and Sep2 knockdowns, whereas disassembly of the actomyosin ring takes a much longer time in Sep4 and Sep5 knockdowns. Thus, in the context of Drosophila cell wound repair, Septins form two distinct complexes—Sep1-Sep2-Pnut and Sep4-Sep5-Pnut—to differentially regulate actin dynamics.

A previous study showed that the Drosophila Sep1-Sep2-Pnut complex interacts with F-actin and promotes its bundling and bending using in vitro assays (Mavrakis et al., 2014). We found that the Sep4-Sep5-Pnut complex also promotes F-actin bundling and generates curved actin filaments. In contrast, individual Septins or mixed conditions (Sep1+Pnut and Sep1+Sep2+Sep4) can bundle actin filaments, but they do not generate curved actin filaments. Thus, the organization of these canonical hetero-hexamer Septin complexes contributes to their ability to bend F-actin.

While the different spatial recruitments of the two distinct Drosophila Septin complexes to wounds could account for why two distinct complexes are needed simultaneously for optimal cell wound repair, we found that these two complexes do not bend actin filaments at the same degree. Strikingly, the Sep1-Sep2-Pnut complex promotes higher curvature of actin filaments than the Sep4-Sep5-Pnut complex. This is consistent with the actin ring needing to be more tightly bent at the innermost part of the wound edge (Sep1-Sep2-Pnut) and to a lesser degree moving outward from the wound edge (Sep4-Sep5-Pnut) (Figure 1b). A cryo-EM study of the Sept2-Sept6-Sept7-Sept7- Sept6- Sept2 hetero-hexamer showed that the interface of Sept7-Sept7 tends to bend, and is likely to be involved in recognizing membrane curvature and/or bending membrane (Mendonca et al., 2021). However, in the context of Drosophila cell wound repair, Sept7(Pnut) is present in both complexes, indicating that the different F-actin bending activities must come from other features of the complex. Further cryo-EM studies are needed to elucidate the mechanism by which different Septin complexes confer their different F-actin bending activities.

The recruitment of Septins to F-actin is thought to require an adaptor protein that binds both Septins and F-actin. We found that Anillin, a known crosslinker for membrane, F-actin, and Septins, regulates the recruitment/function of the Sep1-Sep2-Pnut complex during cell wound repair, but does not affect the recruitment/function of Sep4-Sep5-Pnut complex, suggesting that another, as yet unidentified, adaptor protein must be providing this role (Figure 1c). Identifying what molecules regulate the Sep4-Sep5-Pnut complex is needed to understand how the two distinct Septin complexes are coordinately regulated during cell wound repair.

Taken together, our study demonstrated that the bending of actin filaments by two distinct Septin complexes regulates the assembly and disassembly of the actomyosin ring during cell wound repair, which provides new insight into how actin filaments integrate into the ring structure during cell wound repair. This study opens new directions for investigating the functions and regulation of distinct Septin complexes composed of different subunits in various membrane and cytoskeleton organizations during cellular and developmental processes.
