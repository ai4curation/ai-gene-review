Abstract

While glucocorticoids (GCs) are known to be present in the zebrafish embryo, little is known about their physiological roles at this stage. We hypothesised that GCs play key roles in stress response, hatching and swim activity during early development. To test this, whole embryo cortisol (WEC) and corticosteroid-related genes were measured in embryos from 6 to 120 h post fertilisation (hpf) by enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). Stress response was assessed by change in WEC following stirring, hypoxia or brief electrical impulses applied to the bathing water. The impact of pharmacological and molecular GC manipulation on the stress response, spontaneous hatching and swim activity at different stages of development was also assessed. WEC levels demonstrated a biphasic pattern during development with a decrease from 0 to 36 hpf followed by a progressive increase towards 120 hpf. This was accompanied by a significant and sustained increase in the expression of genes encoding cyp11b1 (GC biosynthesis), hsd11b2 (GC metabolism) and gr (GC receptor) from 48 to 120 hpf. Metyrapone (Met), an inhibitor of 11β-hydroxylase (encoded by cyp11b1 ), and cyp11b1 morpholino (Mo) knockdown significantly reduced basal and stress-induced WEC levels at 72 and 120 hpf but not at 24 hpf. Spontaneous hatching and swim activity were significantly affected by manipulation of GC action from approximately 48 hpf onwards. We have identified a number of key roles of GCs in zebrafish embryos contributing to adaptive physiological responses under adverse conditions. The ability to alter GC action in the zebrafish embryo also highlights its potential value for GC research.

Key points

Glucocorticoids are known to be present in the developing zebrafish embryo but little is known about their physiological role at this early stage.

The zebrafish embryo demonstrates a functional glucocorticoid system from around 48 h post fertilisation.

This system and the stress response is amenable to pharmacological and genetic manipulation in a manner predicted by mammalian physiology.

Glucocorticoids play a key developmental role in hatching, swimming and stress response.

The zebrafish embryo is a relevant model for the study of glucocorticoid physiology.

Introduction

In mammalian adults glucocorticoids (GCs) act via specific receptors to influence many physiological and pathophysiological processes. In addition, GCs play important and diverse regulatory roles in development and growth of fetal tissue, providing nutritional and maturational signals ( Fowden & Forhead, 2004 ). Fetal exposure to excess GCs at key time points results in alterations in blood pressure and glucose metabolism in the adult rat in a process that has been referred to as fetal reprogramming ( Stocker et al. 2005 ). The underlying mechanisms responsible for this phenomenon remain unclear. The zebrafish is emerging as a useful model for GC research ( Alsop & Vijayan, 2008 ; Schaff et al. 2008 ; Pikulkaew et al. 2011 ). As an experimental model it has a number of advantages over higher vertebrates; eggs and embryos can be generated in large numbers on a daily basis ( Lawrence, 2007 ) and are fertilised externally, circumventing many of the problems associated with in utero studies in mammals, embryonic lethality can be studied and defined as embryos are transparent, ( Glickman & Yelon, 2002 ) and the rapid processes of organ development and tissue maturation ( Lieschke & Currie, 2007 ) can be observed easily using standard microscopy techniques. Pharmacological and environmental manipulation of the embryo can be readily performed by altering conditions in the bathing water ( Zon & Peterson, 2005 ). In addition, at relatively low cost, stable antisense oligonucleotides called morpholinos (Mo; Summerton, 1999 ) permit genetic techniques to temporarily knock down specific gene function.

The zebrafish and human corticosteroid systems have many similarities at a molecular and cellular level with the main circulating GC in both of these species being cortisol ( Veldman & Lin, 2008 ). For example, the GC synthesis pathway in zebrafish is virtually identical to that found in mammals ( Schaaf et al. 2009 ) with evidence of a functional homologue to the mammalian hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, defined as the hypothalamic–pituitary–interrenal (HPI) axis ( Schoonheim et al. 2010 ). The biochemical, anatomical, organisational and mechanistic control of GC biosynthesis is very similar in teleost fish and mammals ( Steenbergen et al. 2011 ).

The GC receptor (GR) is also remarkably similar in fish and mammals ( Schoonheim et al. 2010 ). The nucleotide sequence of the zebrafish GR gene ( gr ) is approximately 60% homologous to the human isoform ( GR ) with the DNA and ligand binding domains showing a high degree of similarity ( Schaaf et al. 2009 ). The zebrafish also has a splice variant, grβ, which shows similarity to the human GRβ isoform. Recent studies have suggested that GR is important for zebrafish embryogenesis with Mo knockdown experiments demonstrating marked early developmental defects in mesoderm ( Pikulkaew et al. 2011 ), muscle somitogenesis ( Nesan et al. 2012 ) and increased mortality ( Hillegass et al. 2007 , 2008 ).

While these findings support the utility of the zebrafish as a model for GC research, a number of issues remain unanswered in relation to the GC system in zebrafish embryos. These include: (1) the developmental roles played by GCs during the first 120 h of embryogenesis, (2) the developmental time point when the HPI axis become active and (3) whether the physiological responses of the GC system can be manipulated by pharmacological and molecular approaches in a manner predicted by our understanding of mammalian physiology. In order to address these questions, we have carefully characterised the components of the GC system during the first 120 h of development and assessed the developmental and physiological consequences of pharmacological, molecular and environmental interventions during early embryogenesis.

Methods

Ethical approval

All experiments were carried out in accordance with the accepted standards of humane animal care under the regulation of the Animal (Scientific Procedures) Act UK 1986 and were approved by the University of Edinburgh animal ethics committee.

Zebrafish care and husbandry

Adult fish were housed in 10 l tanks and maintained under standard conditions − 14 h:10 h light:dark photoperiod cycle at ambient temperature of 28.5°C (as suggested by Lawrence, 2007 ). Embryos were bred from adult zebrafish of the wild-type line.

Containers of marbles were placed in tanks prior to onset of the light cycle to encourage spawning, enabling egg collection from onset of light period. Collected eggs were stored in ‘systems water’ (6 g salt; Tropic Marin, Wartenberg, Germany) in 20 l of in-house deionised H 2 O) and 0.5 mg l −1 of the antiseptic methylthioninium chloride (Methylene Blue). All fertilised age-appropriate eggs were collected at the two- to eight-cell stage ∼1 h post fertilisation (hpf) and housed in 10 cm Petri dishes at 28.5°C with systems water replenishment every 24 h.

Whole embryo cortisol (WEC) level determination

Embryos were killed (4.2% MS222) over ice and rinsed well in systems water to remove any residual drug or Methylene Blue. Ten embryos were pooled per time point and homogenised in 1 ml of ice cold systems water, made up to 5 ml with methanol and stored at 4°C for a minimum of 48 h. Samples were then briefly sonicated to reconstitute the solution and then centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. A 2 ml aliquot of the resulting supernatant was evaporated to dryness under nitrogen, and then reconstituted in 0.5 ml of ELISA assay buffer.

Total cortisol was measured using a sensitive and specific in-house ELISA method as previously validated and applied extensively within our laboratory for buffer-reconstituted urine, saliva and cell culture in vitro samples ( Al-Dujaili et al. 2009 , 2011 ; Moore et al. 2011 ). Briefly, assay sensitivity is 0.025 ng ml −1 (0.06 nmol). Assay validation also involved an extraction step with recoveries ranging from 94.8% to 106.7% for cortisol levels between 2.6–140.8 ng ml −1 . Cross-reactivities of the anti-sheep cortisol antibody were 0.68% for cortisone, 1.12% for deoxycortisol, 0.4% for testosterone,0.01% for progesterone and other interfering steroids <0.01%. Intra- and inter-assay precision values were 3.2% and 5.7%, respectively.

Briefly a 96-well plate was coated overnight at 4°C with coating buffer containing 200 ng ml −1 Cortisol–CMO–BSA conjugate: plates were then washed and blocked at 37°C for 2 h. Cortisol standards (0–1000 ng ml −1 ) and samples were added to wells, along with antibody solution (1:20,000 sheep anti-cortisol antibody) and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Samples were then washed and treated with enzyme solution (1:15,000 horseradish peroxidase–donkey-anti-sheep IgG; Sigma Chemical Company, Poole, UK) for 1 h. After washing, peroxidase substrate solution (0.33 mg ml −1 TMB and 30% H 2 O 2 in 0.2 m acetate citrate buffer) was added. The reaction was stopped after 15 min incubation at room temperature by the addition of 1 m H 2 SO 4 . Absorbance at 450 nm was measured in an MRX Microplate reader (Dynatech Laboratories, Billinghurst, West Sussex, UK).

Gene expression by qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from pools of 10 embryos using the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Manchester, Greater Manchester, UK) according to manufacturer's instructions. Genomic DNA was removed using DNA-free kit (Applied Biosystems, Paisley, Renfrewshire, UK). RNA quantity was determined by measuring absorbance at 260 nm using a Nano-drop spectrophotometer ND-1000 (Fisher Scientific, Loughborough, Leicestershire, UK). RNA integrity was verified by a 260/280 nm absorbance ratio of ∼2 and agarose gel electrophoresis.

Total RNA was reverse transcribed using the High capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems), according to manufacturer's guidelines. qRT-PCR was performed to quantify the expression of gr , cyp11b1 and hsd11b2 mRNA (primer sequences given in Table 1 ) using the LightCycler 480 system and universal probes library (UPL) probes (Roche Diagnostics Ltd, Burgess Hill, West Sussex, UK). Standard operating conditions were 95°C for 5 min, 50 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 30 s and 72°C for 1 s, followed by 40°C for 30 s. For each experiment housekeeping genes elongation factor 1 alpha ( ef1α ) and 18S rRNA ( 18S ) were quantified concurrently with genes of interest using the LightCycler software provided by the manufacturer. Results were calculated using the maximum second derivative method, whereby the entire amplification curve is considered rather than just the threshold point.

Table 1.

Targeted knockdown of the cyp11b1 gene

Mo gene knockdown technology ( Summerton, 1999 ) was targeted towards the cyp11b1 gene which encodes the enzyme 11β-hydroxylase. Three Mo sequences were purchased from GeneTools (Philomath, OR, USA): a start codon Mo targeted to the translation site start codon (Atg-Mo), a splice site specific Mo (ss-Mo used here only to show a reduction in mRNA) and a 5 bp mispair Mo (mm-Mo) which served as a control ( Table 2 ).

Table 2.

Morpholino oligonucleotide sequences

Mo sequences were modified with a 3′-fluorescein tag allowing confirmation of successful integration of within the developing embryo. Mo sequences were microinjected at the two-cell stage using a standard protocol ( Nusslein-Volhard & Dahm, 2002 ), with a IM-300 Narishige programmable micro-injector. The volume and concentration of Mo injected were calculated from bolus radius using the equation 4/3πr 3 . Mo concentrations were titrated to produce a consistent knockdown of cyp11b1 mRNA (ss-Mo) without causing non-specific developmental defects. Injection of 1 ng nl −1 of Mo specific for cyp11b1 ( cyp11b1 -Mo) resulted in a 30% reduction in cyp11b mRNA expression ( Fig. 2 D ).

Pharmacological manipulation of cortisol

Embryos were exposed to pharmacological agents by addition to the bathing water. Detailed dose-ranging studies were carried out for each drug to establish a concentration that did not result in significant developmental abnormalities. This was systematically assessed by scoring individual embryos for various morphological abnormalities including pericardial oedema, body axis deformities (tail defects such as coiling), red blood cell pooling, slow heart rate and cranial deformities. The final bathing concentrations of drugs were as follows: 10 μ m 11-deoxycorticol (Doxy), 10 μ m metyrapone (Met), 10 μ m mifepristone (RU486) and 100 μ m dexamethasone (Dex). Stock solutions were prepared in ethanol; vehicle controls were prepared with equivalent amounts of ethanol (≤1%). Embryos were incubated in systems water containing drugs from the two-cell stage (∼1 hpf) to 120 hpf in Petri dishes at a population density of 1 embryo (0.5 ml) −1 . Drug– and vehicle–systems water solutions were replaced daily.

In separate experiments Dex (0.5 ng per embryo) was injected at the two-cell stage using a technique similar to that used for injection of Mo. This treatment specifically assessed the effect of corticosteroid manipulation on hatching rate and chorion absorption. Control embryos were injected with vehicle.

Determination of developmental time of onset of the stress response

WEC production was assessed at 24, 72 and 120 hpf in cohorts of approximately 10 embryos ( n = 3) following exposure to one of a number of different stressors as follows. (1) Brief stirring of embryos – 10 embryos were placed in 10 ml of systems water in a 30 ml Petri dish on a standard laboratory orbital shaker (SSL1-Stuart, Bibby Scientific Ltd, Stone, Staffordshire, UK) and agitated at an intensity of 120 rpm for 30 s (brief kinetic stress) to produce a mild vortex effect. (2) Prolonged stirring of embryos as above, for 1 h producing a sustained vortex effect. Similar stress experiments have been used by others ( Alsop & Vijayan, 2008 ; Fuzzen et al. 2011 ). (3) Hypoxia stress with embryos in a 15 ml Petri dish within a hypoxic chamber (Coy Laboratory Products, MI, USA; 1% oxygen, 5% carbon dioxide, 94% nitrogen, relative humidity of 45%) for 3 h. (4) Electrical stimulation with direct current pulse (DCP; 2 mA, 6 V, 10 ms) applied by silver electrodes to the bathing water of a Petri dish containing up to 10 embryos at 20 min intervals for 2 h. As demonstrated by others, electrical stimulation did not appear to have any lasting adverse effects on the embryos ( Steenbergen et al. 2011 ). WEC was determined prior to stress and 1 h following stress.

Unless stated otherwise all embryos were sampled at the same time of day (between 14.00 h and 15.00 h) under standard light:dark cycle conditions to control for the possible influence of diurnal circadian cortisol production ( Dekens et al. 2003 ; Dickmeis, 2009 ; Lohr & Hammerschmidt, 2011 ).

Determination of hatching, swim bladder inflation and swim activity

For embryos that had been exposed to drug in the bathing water or had been treated with cyp11b1 -Mo a number of normal developmental features were assessed. Chorion self-hatch rate was assessed by pooling embryos into groups of 30 embryos, housing them under a standard housing temperature of 28.5°C ( Kimmel et al. 1995 ) and counting the number embryos within each group which had hatched over a 24 h period. Swim bladder inflation was determined by imaging embryos of 120 hpf under a dissection microscope and scoring whether the swim bladder was present or absent.

Embryos that had been continuously incubated in Dex, RU486 or Met were assessed at 120 hpf for locomotor (swim) activity using open-field observation. Briefly, a single embryo was placed in a 5 cm Petri dish with three concentric rings, defining inner, mid- and outer regions (as described by Levin & Cerutti, 2009 ). Each embryo was introduced into the mid-region of a marked dish containing 3 ml of fresh systems water. Embryo movements were recorded from above using a digital CCTV camera (Baxall, AD group, UK). Data were analysed by commercially available software (Limelight Ltd, UK) to determine total distance travelled, percentage of time spent in each region of the Petri dish and average speed of movement for each embryo ( n = 12 embryos per group).

Statistical analysis

Data were processed and statistical analysis was carried out with GraphPad Prism Version 5 (Graph Pad Software). Unpaired Student's t tests, repeated measures ANOVA for multiple comparisons, and area under the curve analysis were performed as appropriate. P < 0.05 is considered significant. All data displayed (unless stated otherwise) are means ± SEM of ≥3 cohort groups.

Results

Temporal changes in GC system components during early development of the zebrafish embryo ( Fig. 1 )

Figure 1. Temporal changes in GC system components during early development of the zebrafish embryo.

During normal development, WEC decreased significantly between 8 and 36 hpf (from 2.20 ± 0.29 ng per embryo to 0.40 ± 0.42, P = 0.01). This was followed by a steady and progressive increase in WEC levels between 48 hpf and 120 hpf (from 0.63 ± 015 ng per embryo to 3.39 ± 0.44, P = 0.05; Fig 1 A ).

cyp11b1 mRNA expression was initially lowest at 24 hpf, rising at 48 hpf, and then falling before rising again from 72 to 120 hpf. hsd11b2 (the gene encoding 11βHSD2) mRNA levels were lowest at 24 hpf and increased significantly from 48 hpf, reaching a plateau ( P < 0.01) by 72 hpf; this was maintained for the remainder of the time course. gr mRNA expression had increased significantly, by threefold, at 48 hpf and continued to rise, reaching a sustained plateau at 72 hpf ( P < 0.001 Fig. 1 B–D ).

Pharmacological and genetic manipulation of 11β-hydroxylase activity ( Fig. 2 )

Following Dex exposure from the two-cell stage, WEC was reduced by ∼25% at 120 hpf (2.28 ± 0.1 ng per embryo vs . 3.08 ± 0.1, P = 0.01). However, WEC was not reduced at 72 hpf (Dex 0.44 ± 0.09 ng per embryo vs . control 0.59 ± 0.05, P = 0.24). Similarly, RU486 treatment reduced basal WEC at 120 hpf (RU486 1.82 ± 0.20 ng per embryo vs . control 3.08 ± 0.10, P = 0.008) but not at 72 hpf (0.41 ± 0.10 ng per embryo vs . 0.59 ± 0.05, P = 0.19; Fig 2 A ).

Doxy-incubated embryos had greater basal WEC levels compared to controls at 120 hpf (5.43 ± 0.01 ng per embryo vs . 3.68 ± 0.02, P = 0.05; Fig. 2 B ). Continuous Met incubation reduced WEC levels in a concentration-dependent manner at 120 hpf compared to controls with the highest concentration (10 μ m ), resulting in a 65.5% reduction in WEC levels ( Fig. 2 C ). It was noted that Met incubation at 24 and 72 hpf did not show a similar reduction in WEC, and results were comparable to controls (control 0.66 ± 0.10 ng per embryo vs . Met 0.78 ± 0.40 at 24 hpf, P = 0.19; control 0.77 ± 0.06 ng per embryo vs . 1.21 ± 0.50 at 72 hpf, P = 0.42; Fig. 2 E ).

Mo - treated embryos showed a 30% reduction in cyp11b1 mRNA at 72 hpf. The cyp11b1 -Mo treatment also resulted in a significant (∼45%) reduction in WEC levels at 120 hpf (control 2.96 ± 0.26 ng per embryo vs . cyp11b1 -Mo 1.67 ± 0.36, P = 0.016) but not at 24 hpf (0.66 ± 0.10 ng per embryo vs . 1.03 ± 0.20, P = 0.29) or 72 hpf (0.77 ± 0.06 ng per embryo vs . 1.13 ± 0.30 P = 0.36; Fig. 2 D and E ).

Effects of stress on whole embryo cortisol (WEC) levels ( Fig. 3 )

Figure 3. Effects of stress on whole embryo cortisol levels.

Various forms of stress, including kinetic agitation (brief and prolonged), hypoxia and DCP, resulted in a significant increase in WEC levels, with prolonged kinetic agitation and DCP resulting in the greatest increases in WEC compared to unstressed controls (stress 6.712 ± 0.013 ng per embryo vs . control 0.709 ± 0.160, P < 0.01).

A clear change in stress responsiveness was observed during development whereby at 24 hpf embryos showed no change in WEC levels with prolonged kinetic agitation but clear 3- to 5-fold increases at 72 and 120 hpf (72 hpf vs . basal: 5.477 ± 0.73 vs . 0.7 ± 0.04, P = 0.002; 120 hpf vs . basal: 5.738 ± 0.80 vs . 2.950 ± 0.16, P = 0.02). The cortisol response to this stressor was abrogated by Met and in cyp11b1 -Mo embryos at both 72 hpf (Met 0.932 ± 0.136, P = 0.004; cyp11b1 -Mo 0.991 ± 0.136, P = 0.003) and 120 hpf (Met 0.88 ± 0.08, P = 0.004; cyp11b1 -Mo 1.126 ± 0.07, P = 0.02) ( Fig. 3 B ).

Effects of GC manipulation on spontaneous hatching ( Fig. 4 )

Figure 4. Effects of glucocorticoid manipulation on spontaneous hatching.

The impact of endogenous cortisol on hatching was assessed in embryos incubated in Met (10 μ m ) or injected with cyp11b1 -Mo. No significant difference in hatch rate was observed for either of these treatments at 48 hpf (Met 24.3 ± 1.8% hatched vs . control 17.4 ± 2.2%, P = 0.07; cyp11b-Mo 28.3 ± 2.8% vs . control 17.4 ± 2.2%, P = 0.06). In contrast, at 72 hpf both of these treatments reduced hatch rate (Met 71 ± 9.0% hatched vs . control 98.3 ± 1.52%, P < 0.0001; cyp11b1 -Mo 74 ± 5.9% vs . control 98.3 ± 1.52%, P < 0.0001). At 96 and 120 hpf ( Fig. 4 A ), no differences in hatch rate were observed for either of these groups compared to controls.

Dex incubation (100 μ m ) increased the proportion of hatched embryos at 48 hpf compared to controls (44 ± 2.0% hatched vs . 17 ± 2.2%, P = 0.003). Injected Dex (0.5 ng nl −1 ) resulted in a significantly greater proportion of embryos hatching by 48 hpf (97 ± 0.8% hatched vs . control 17 ± 2.2%, P < 0.0001; Fig. 4 B ). However, RU486 reduced hatch rate compared controls at 48 hpf (RU486 2 ± 0.8% hatched vs . controls 17.4 ± 2.2%, P = 0.03) and 72 hpf (65 ± 5.3% hatched vs . controls 98.3 ± 1.52%, P = 0.047). At 96 and 120 hpf, a significantly greater number of RU486-treated embryos remained unhatched compared to controls (96 hpf RU486 19 ± 5.3% unhatched vs . controls 0.4 ± 0.3%, P < 0.001; 120 hpf RU486 9.33 ± 0.6% unhatched vs . controls 0.4 ± 0.2%, P < 0.001; Fig. 4 C )

Effects of GC manipulation on swim activity ( Fig. 5 )

Figure 5. Effects of glucocorticoid manipulation on swim activity.

Embryos incubated in Dex spent significantly more time in the outer region of the Petri-dish, (avoidance region) compared to controls during open-field assessment (Dex 60.72 ± 7.70% vs . control 33.86 ± 7.10%, P = 0.02). Conversely, embryos incubated with RU486 or Met spent less time in the outer region compared to controls (RU486 27.7 ± 18.0%, Met 14.2 ± 7.51, P = 0.059; control 33.86 ± 7.10%; Fig. 5 A ). Dex incubation had no significant effect on total distance travelled (Dex 17.2 ± 0.8 mm min −1 vs . control 15.7 ± 0.9, P = 0.24) or average swim speed (Dex 0.11 ± 0.02 mm ms −1 vs . control 0.13 ± 0.01, P = 0.45). However, total distance travelled with both Met and RU486 treatments was reduced (Met 11.8 ± 0.5 mm min −1 , RU486 10.70 ± 0.3; controls: 15.67 ± 0.9, P < 0.01). Average swim speeds were also reduced (Met 0.030 ± 0.0 mm ms −1 ; RU486 0.02 ± 0.01; control 0.13 ± 0.01, P < 0.01; Fig. 5 B and C ).

Many of the embryos that had been incubated with RU486 had no detectable swim bladder at 120 hpf (RU486 7.33 ± 1.25% had inflated swim bladder vs . controls 99.39 ± 0.19%, P < 0.001). No other treatment affected swim bladder inflation ( Fig. 5 D ).

Discussion

This study has shown clearly that key components of the GC system are present and functional in the zebrafish embryo during early development. Furthermore, we have shown that the GC system is endogenously active from around 48 hpf and thereafter plays an important role in hatching, responsiveness to stress and in swim activity. We have shown for the first time that a stress response, defined by a rise in cortisol following stimulation, is present from 72 hpf. Stress responses were not seen at earlier time points – although genes involved in GC biosynthesis and GC hormone signalling are expressed at 48 h.

GC system components are present at earliest stages – larval versus maternal origin

In zebrafish a number of GC components are known to be transferred from the mother in the yolk sac. These are initially depleted during embryogenesis ( Alsop & Vijayan, 2008 ). This was confirmed in our study as a biphasic pattern of WEC levels during the first 120 hpf. The fall and rise in cortisol and GC-related transcripts showed a clear inflection point at or near to 48 hpf. This suggests that cortisol detected before this time point was of maternal origin and subsequent increases represent de novo synthesis by the embryo itself. This analysis was confirmed by showing that from 48 hpf cortisol levels were amenable to pharmacological manipulation of 11β-hydroxylase, the final enzyme in the pathway of GC biosynthesis. Treatment with 11β-hydroxylase substrate (Doxy) increased cortisol levels, whereas inhibition of 11β-hydroxylase activity with Met or Mo knockdown of cyp11b1 , the gene encoding 11β-hydroxylase, both reduced WEC levels. Even a modest reduction in mRNA levels resulted in a significant decrease in WEC levels at 120 hpf compared to controls, suggesting that this enzyme limits cortisol production at this stage of development.

Physiological role of GC in embryo-functional stress response

We have confirmed previous observations that at 120 hpf zebrafish respond to a variety of stressors with an increase in WEC ( Alsop & Vijayan, 2008 ; Pikulkaew et al. 2010 ; Steenbergen et al. 2011 ). Hypoxia and prolonged stirring both increased WEC twofold. Varying the intensity of the stressor altered the range or response: a shorter period of stirring caused only a mild increase whereas electrical stimulation caused a 2.5-fold increase. Differences in the degree of stimulation may explain why others failed to show significant cortisol changes in larval fish. The ontogeny of the response was investigated by comparing stress-induced WEC at different stages of development following the same stressor protocol, prolonged kinetic agitation. No response was seen at 24 hpf, consistent with the idea that cortisol at this stage is of residual maternal origin rather than synthesised by the embryos de novo .

A marked stress response was seen at 72 hpf at a time when cyp11b1 is also expressed. Taken together these data suggest that once the steroidogenic pathway is complete, endogenous cortisol production is stress responsive. Indeed, it is significant that inhibition of 11β-hydroxylase or Mo knockdown of cyp11b1 both abrogated the stress response at 72 and 120 hpf. Interestingly, at 72 hpf 11β-hydroxylase blockade lowered WEC levels in stressed embryos to control levels in unstressed embryos, but at 120 hpf levels were reduced even further. This implies that some cortisol in unstressed embryos at 72 hpf may still be maternal and therefore not susceptible to 11β-hydroxylase inhibition.

The mature HPI axis is subject to negative feedback control by GCs via corticosteroid receptors ( Alderman et al. 2012 ). In the present study this aspect of control was tested by treating fish with the synthetic GC Dex. As previously shown, basal WEC levels increase dramatically between 72 and 120 hpf, a rise of almost 500%. Following prolonged incubation in Dex this rise in WEC was reduced at 120 hpf, indicating firstly that cortisol synthesis is under the tonic control of ACTH and secondly that gr mRNA, which we have observed to be expressed at this time, is translated into functional GR protein. WEC levels in 72 hpf fish were unaffected by Dex incubation, suggesting again that residual maternal cortisol is still important at this point. A suppression of WEC was also observed in RU486 embryos. However, these data, taken along with delayed hatching and impaired swim activity, are consistent with developmental delay.

hsd11b2 mRNA is present at a significant level from 48 hpf onwards. The enzyme encoded by this gene catalyses the local inactivation of cortisol to cortisone, a conserved homeostatic regulatory response found in mammals and other species ( Draper & Stewart, 2005 ). In mammals, cortisol metabolism by the enzyme 11βHSD2 can be regarded as a method of protecting tissues from excess GC exposure (e.g. the fetus from maternal cortisol) by preventing cortisol binding to MR (allowing lower levels of aldosterone to bind to MR) and as a source of 11-dehydro derivatives of GCs that can be used as substrate for the enzyme 11βHSD1 to regenerate active hormone in GC-sensitive tissues (e.g. liver). However, as zebrafish do not synthesise aldosterone and do not reactivate GCs via 11βHSD1, it seems likely that 11βHSD2 function is to modulate cortisol access to its receptors, including both MR and GR, since cortisol may well be the principle endogenous ligand for both. As well as being the means of controlling hormone activity in a tissue-specific manner, 11βHSD2 has also been shown to be a key component, along with a second enzyme, 20β-HSD, in the disposal of cortisol ( Tokarz et al. 2012 , 2013 ). It is significant that larval expression of these steroid-catabolising enzymes has been shown to be regulated by cortisol. These observations, together with our own evidence of developmental changes in cyp11b1 mRNA expression and enzyme activity, indicate that for homeostatic regulation of cortisol levels biosynthesis and catabolism are both important in controlling the physiological processes of development.

It is apparent in rodent and human studies, where there is a greater understanding of the physiological roles of GCs, that chronic overexposure to GCs (whether during development or in the adult) can result in multiple and wide-ranging adverse effects (as reviewed by Almawi et al. 2002 ; Girod & Brotman, 2004 ; Walker, 2007 ; McDonough et al. 2008 ; Feldman et al. 2013 ). These studies highlight the importance of homeostatic regulation through the processes of biosynthesis and catabolism.

Furthermore it has been shown elsewhere that the expression of these catabolism genes is up-regulated in response to cortisol at 120 hpf ( Tokarz et al. 2013 ), not only emphasising the functionality of these regulatory genes but also highlighting the potential physiological potency of cortisol in the developing embryo and thus the importance of homeostasis.

Physiological role of GC in the developing zebrafish – spontaneous hatching and swimming

GCs have been shown in rodents and humans to be important for tissue maturation ( Seckl, 2004 ); in fact this phenomenon is displayed well through lung maturation and surfactant production by premature offspring. This association between GCs and embryogenesis also appears to be preserved in the zebrafish, coinciding with important developmental stages which include chorion hatching and subsequent free swimming.

We have shown that incubation of embryos in Dex causes precocious spontaneous hatching compared to controls. Arguably, this could be a non-specific physical effect caused by softening of the chorion ( Kim et al. 2006 ). However, embryos directly injected with Dex also show advanced hatching, indicating that it is initiated from within the embryo itself by a GR-mediated process. This conclusion is supported by the observed delay in hatching in embryos treated with the GR antagonist RU486. A suggested mechanism for these responses is that GR promotes hatching gland maturation and subsequent production of proteolytic enzymes that induce softening of the chorion ( Hagenmaier, 1974 ). It is notable that this hatching response is not dependent on GC synthesis by the embryo (Met has no significant affect) and may be as a result of maternal deposit.

One limitation of RU486 is that it also shows progesterone receptor antagonism in mammalian models. To date only one paper has suggested a possible association with progesterone receptor antagonism and delayed hatching, when RU486 was combined with a magnetic field stress ( Skauli et al. 2000 ). However, this could be unrelated to progesterone receptor pathways since many physical stresses such as magnetic stress may perturb embryonic development ( Spitsbergen & Kent, 2003 ).

GC agonists and antagonists also affected swimming activity. RU486 treatment reduced swim speed and swim distances compared with controls or Dex-treated embryos. However, this behaviour was also seen with Met treatments, indicating a role for embryonic rather than maternal GCs. It has previously been shown that swim movements ( Muller & van Leeuwen, 2004 ) increase as fish grow and change shape during early development, particularly between 72 and 120 hpf. This allows a more active exploration of their environment – mainly to forage for food ( Brustein et al. 2003 ; Thirumalai & Cline, 2008 ). Our findings confirm these reports and show that inhibition of GC synthesis and its actions impacts on normal swim activity. This could be a consequence of delayed development of the skeletomotor system, although detailed phenotype scoring of all treated and control embryos in our studies failed to detect any significant structural differences other than a lack of swim bladder inflation in the majority of RU486-treated embryos. Swim bladder inflation usually occurs at 72 hpf and assists buoyancy. It has previously been shown that ‘swim-up’ to the surface behaviour is critical in the larval zebrafish for swim bladder inflation ( Lindsey et al. 2010 ). Our data suggest this is the case as we observed a reduced swim activity and poor swim bladder inflation in our RU486-treated embryos. In addition to its GR antagonism, RU486 can act as a progesterone antagonist and we cannot exclude the possibility that these findings relate, at least in part, to inhibition of progesterone action. However, the swim bladder has been suggested as a corollary of early mammalian lung development ( Winata et al. 2009 ) which is well known to be modified by GC administration.

Increases in time spent in the centre of the Petri dish were seen in embryos treated with Met or RU486 which could also likely to be related to the impact of GCs on development and maturation. Swimming activity in fish is linked to defined behavioural characteristics such as escape and avoidance. Zebrafish larvae have been shown to be thigmotactic from 96 hpf, i.e. they spend longer in the periphery of a tank or open-field area, than in the centre; an evolutionary response to avoid predation ( Colwill & Creton, 2011 ). Again these data further suggest an important developmental role for GCs in adaptive responses linked to survival and feeding.

Conclusion

This work has established a number of key aspects of GC hormonal control during early development in the zebrafish embryo. The corticosteroid biosynthesis pathway becomes physiologically active and relevant from 48 hpf, with negative and positive HPI axis control from 72 hpf. Zebrafish responses to GCs are demonstrable before 48 hpf, implying a functional role for GCs of maternal origin. Adaptive responses involving the action of GCs on hatching, swimming and stress response are likely to influence embryo survival in the zebrafish natural environment. Furthermore the manipulation of GC system by pharmacological and genetic techniques supports the suitability of the zebrafish embryo as a model system to study physiological and pathophysiological consequences of corticosteroid excess and deficiency.

Translational perspective

Glucocorticoids are widely used in the treatment of mothers at risk of pre-term delivery in order to promote maturation of the fetal lung and this has been shown to reduce infant morbidity and mortality. Concerns remain that this early exposure of the fetus to glucocorticoids could result in reprogramming of cellular and molecular mechanisms that could in turn lead to increased susceptibility to disease later in life. Current animal models are not ideally suited to assess the developmental impact of both excess and low levels of glucocorticoid during critical developmental time points. In contrast, the zebrafish embryo allows ready access to this developmental window. Using pharmacological and genetic manipulation we have demonstrated that increased and decreased activity of glucocorticoids can have significant effects on hatching, swim activity and stress response. These data are important in establishing the zebrafish as a potentially valuable model for glucocorticoid research and in addition confirms the impact of altered glucocorticoids on early development.
