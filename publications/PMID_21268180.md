In vitro studies have suggested that the Cables1 gene regulates epithelial cell
proliferation, whereas other studies suggest a role in promoting neural
differentiation. In efforts to clarify the functions of Cables1 in vivo, we
conducted gain- and loss-of-function studies targeting its ortholog (cables1) in
the zebrafish embryo. Similar to rodents, zebrafish cables1 mRNA expression is
detected most robustly in embryonic neural tissues. Antisense knockdown of
cables1 leads to increased numbers of apoptotic cells, particularly in brain
tissue, in addition to a distinct behavioral phenotype, characterized by
hyperactivity in response to stimulation. Apoptosis and the behavioral
abnormality could be rescued by co-expression of a morpholino-resistant cables1
construct. Suppression of p53 expression in cables1 morphants partially rescued
both apoptosis and the behavioral phenotype, suggesting that the phenotype of
cables1 morphants is due in part to p53-dependent apoptosis. Alterations in the
expression patterns of several neural transcription factors were observed in
cables1 morphants during early neurulation, suggesting that cables1 is required
for early neural differentiation. Ectopic overexpression of cables1 strongly
disrupted embryonic morphogenesis, while overexpression of a cables1 mutant
lacking the C-terminal cyclin box had little effect, suggesting functional
importance of the cyclin box. Lastly, marked reductions in p35, but not Cdk5,
were observed in cables1 morphants. Collectively, these data suggest that
cables1 is important for neural differentiation during embryogenesis, in a
mechanism that likely involves interactions with the Cdk5/p35 kinase pathway.

INTRODUCTION

The CDK5 and ABL1 Enzyme Substrate 1 (Cables1/ik3-1) gene, a member of the cyclin superfamily, was identified in two independent screens for genes that interact with the cyclin-dependent kinase (Cdk) family of serine/threonine protein kinases (Matsuoka et al., 2000; Zukerberg et al., 2000). In a yeast-two hybrid screening assay, Cables1 protein interacted strongly with Cdk5 (a non-cell cycle-associated kinase required for neural differentiation), while weaker interactions were also observed with cell cycle-associated kinases Cdk2 and Cdk3. While informative, these in vitro studies have yet to provide definitive insights into the functions of the Cables1 gene in vivo, particularly during early development.

Although Cables1 knockout mice are viable, early (non-congenic) strains were notably subfertile, with evidence of fetal resorption in pregnant dams (Zukerberg et al., 2004). These observations suggest that embryonic development is compromised in Cables1 knockouts. Interpretation of these data remains difficult, however, due to the potential redundancy of a related gene (Cables2) in mice. In light of multiple studies reporting a loss of Cables1 expression in selected tumor types (Wu et al., 2001; Dong et al., 2003; Tan et al., 2003; Zukerberg et al., 2004; DeBernardo et al., 2005; Zhang et al., 2005; Kirley et al., 2005a; Park do et al., 2007) there remains a strong rationale to clarify the in vivo functions of the Cables1 gene.

To broaden our understanding of Cables1 functions in vivo, we have performed gain- and loss-of-function studies of the zebrafish Cables1 ortholog (cables1; NM_ 001105665.1). Importantly, the lack of a functional paralog in this species permitted a direct investigation of cables1 functions in vivo. The data from these studies support the notion that cables1 is critically important for neural development in the zebrafish embryo. The developmental impact of cables1 suppression appears substantially more dramatic than that observed in murine or avian models (Zukerberg et al., 2000; Rhee et al., 2007). In the zebrafish, knockdown of cables1 results in disruptions in the expression of multiple neural transcription factors, increased apoptosis in embryonic neural tissues, and subsequent behavioral defects characterized by hyperactivity in response to stimulation. In addition, we present evidence suggesting that the embryonic functions of cables1 likely involve interactions with the Cdk5/p35 kinase pathway. Collectively, these data from the zebrafish model provide in vivo evidence supporting an essential functional role for cables1 during embryonic neural differentiation.

DISCUSSIONUsing the zebrafish as an experimental model, this study provides molecular, cellular, biochemical, and behavioral evidence that suggest an important functional role for cables1 in embryonic neural development. Knockdown of cables1 in zebrafish embryos led to increased apoptosis in embryonic neural tissues, enhanced neural excitability in response to stimulation, significant reductions in p35 protein levels, and alterations in the expression of neural transcription factors during early neurulation.It is important to note that not all transcription factors examined were affected equally. We observed consistent decreases in emx3, eng2a, and gsc expression in cables1 morphants, whereas otx2 expression was consistently increased. However, no changes were detected in the expression patterns of pax2a, or the axial mesoderm marker ntl. These observations suggest an underlying deregulation of neural differentiation pathways, rather than simply global shifts in gene expression. The down-regulated expression of some transcription factors also cannot be explained by increased p53-dependent cell death during differentiation, as these observations were made in embryos in which p53-dependent apoptosis had been suppressed. Taken together, these data suggest that cables1 is functionally important during neural tube differentiation, such that a lack of cables1 expression results in neural tube patterning defects. These findings are in agreement with in vitro studies in the chick model, where Cables1 was shown to link the Slit/Robo complex to the N-cadherin pathway, leading to β-catenin phosphorylation, nuclear translocation and increased transcription of genes involved in CNS patterning (Rhee et al., 2007).The behavioral response to a loss of cables1 is interesting, although the underlying mechanism for this is not entirely clear. The rescue experiments suggest that this phenotype is linked partly to an increase in p53-dependent cell death, but the precise mechanism underlying the behavioral phenotype remains unclear. Notably, hyper-excitability is not observed in all cases of excess cell death in the embryonic CNS. For example, knockdown of an insulin-like growth factor receptor (Igf1rb) led to widespread neural apoptosis in the zebrafish embryo, but a distinct loss of motor activity (Schlueter et al., 2006). It is noteworthy that the aberrant behavior was only partially rescued by suppressing p53 expression, whereas a complete rescue was achieved by co-expressing full-length cables1. In mammals, Cables1 has been reported to influence both p53-dependent and p73-dependent cell death pathways, functions that were attributed to distinct functional domains within the Cables1 protein (Tsuji et al., 2002). Further studies are required to determine whether or not the zebrafish ortholog of Cables1 also contains discrete domains with distinct functional properties.The significant reduction in p35 protein levels in cables1 morphant embryos is consistent with previous in vitro data suggesting that cables1 regulates neural differentiation via interactions with the Cdk5/p35 kinase pathway (Zukerberg et al., 2000). Unlike related cyclin-dependent kinases, Cdk5 is not activated by cyclins and does not function in cell cycle regulation. Rather, its kinase activity is confined to postmitotic (differentiating) neurons, via interactions with p35 and p39, that exhibit neural-specific expression (Ohshima et al., 1996; Ko et al., 2001). p35 is a highly labile protein that is rapidly degraded by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway (Patrick et al., 1998; Kerokoski et al., 2002; Saito et al., 2003), and this degradation is dependent upon phosphorylation by Cdk5 (Kerokoski et al., 2002; Saito et al., 2003). The marked reduction of p35 protein levels in cables1 morphants is therefore suggestive that cables1 may function to stabilize p35, similar to its reported function in stabilizing p63 (Wang et al., 2010).The results of overexpressing a C-terminal truncation mutant lacking the cyclin box suggest that the cyclin box confers potent biological activity to cables1. This observation is consistent with the model involving interactions between cables1 and Cdk5/p35, as the cyclin box is the domain predicted to interact with the Cdk5/p35 complex. While deletion of the cyclin box substantially reduced the activity of cables1 overexpressed in vivo, this could potentially be explained by precocious degradation (e.g., nonsense-mediated decay) of the ΔCB mRNA (Wen and Brogna, 2008). However, our data are consistent with in vitro studies showing that the cyclin box is essential to mediate important functions of cables1, including p53-dependent cell death (Tsuji et al., 2002).Lastly, the lack of a significant difference in the number of phosphorylated histone H3-positive cells among cables1 morphants and controls suggests that during early embryogenesis, cables1 does not directly regulate cell proliferation. This differs from what has been reported in other biological contexts. For example, CABLES1 over-expression in human endometrial cells markedly slowed the rate of cell proliferation (Zukerberg et al., 2004), while embryonic fibroblast from Cables1−/− mice proliferated more rapidly than wild-type cells (Kirley et al., 2005b). It is unclear whether these differences reflect species-specific variations in gene function, or reflect the different experimental or biological contexts in which the genes were studied.In summary, this study provides in vivo evidence that cables1 is critically important for neural development in the zebrafish embryo, and suggests that the observed defects may be due in part to its interactions with the Cdk5/p35 kinase pathway during neural differentiation. Future studies using conditional gene ablation approaches are required to determine if cables1 adopts other biological functions after the embryonic period. The mounting evidence of a role for this gene in early development and possibly tumorigenesis (Dong et al., 2003; Zukerberg et al., 2004; Zhang et al., 2005; Kirley et al., 2005a,b; Lee et al., 2007; Park do et al., 2007; Sakamoto et al., 2008) suggests that much remains to be learned about its functions in humans and other vertebrates.

A commercially available MO targeting p53 (p53MO) has been previously described (Robu et al., 2007).

Synthetic mRNA Overexpression

Three expression constructs were made to overexpress variants of cables1 in zebrafish embryos. pCS2 + cabORF corresponded to the complete open reading frame (525 amino acids) of zebrafish cables1. pCS2 + cabRESC also encoded the complete open reading frame, but excluded the morpholino (cabMO) target region. pCS2 +ΔCB encoded a truncated variant of cables1; this was generated by RT-PCR using primers that introduced a stop codon (TAA) at amino acid residue 407, thereby encoding a 406 amino acid protein (ΔCB) lacking the C-terminal cyclin box.

Each fragment was amplified by RT-PCR using total RNA purified from adult brain tissue as template. The PCR products were subcloned into pCRII-TOPO (Invitrogen), sequenced to confirm accuracy, digested with EcoRI and ligated into pCS2+. After identifying clones containing the appropriate inserts, they were sequenced to ensure accuracy and used for synthetic mRNA synthesis, as described (Turner and Weintraub, 1994).

Synthetic mRNA was generated using the SP6 mESSAGE mACHINE (Sang et al., 2008), precipitated in LiCl, washed extensively in nuclease-free 70% ethanol and resuspended at the desired concentration in sterile 1× Danieau microinjection buffer.

Embryo MicroinjectionApproximately 1 nl of each solution (morpholino, synthetic mRNA) was microinjected into 1-cell stage zebrafish embryos, as previously described (Sang et al., 2008). Microinjection volumes were calibrated using the oil drop method (Yuan and Sun, 2009). For rescue experiments, synthetic mRNA (cabRESC) was simultaneously co-injected with cabMO (2 ng/embryo). Synthetic mRNA encoding GFP (100 pg/embryo) was injected into a subset of embryos to confirm the quality of in vitro-derived synthetic mRNA.

TUNEL AnalysesTUNEL analyses for DNA fragmentation were performed on paraformaldehyde-fixed whole embryos, as previously described (Schlueter et al., 2007). For quantification purposes, a mean TUNEL index was generated by counting the number TUNEL-positive cells in five random-selected, non-overlapping fields of view per embryo. This method of quantification was replicated in each of five embryos per treatment group. The observer was blinded with respect to the identity of each embryo during quantification.

ImmunohistochemistryImmunohistochemical staining was performed on paraformaldehyde-fixed embryos after dehydration and storage (−30°C) in methanol. Embryos were rehydrated by serial washes in phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween (PBST), incubated in blocking buffer (PBST containing 2% serum, 1% DMSO, 1% BSA), and incubated in primary antibody solution overnight at 4°C. Embryos were then were washed extensively in PBST, and incubated overnight (4°C) in biotinylated secondary antibody diluted (1:500) in blocking buffer. After washing in PBST, embryos were incubated in Vectastain ABC reagents for 30 min at RT, washed in PBST and incubated in DAB solution (DAKO) to complete the color reaction.

Western Immunoblot AnalysesA total of 20 embryos from each sample group were suspended in Ca2+/Mg2+-free Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS), deyolked by passage through a narrow-bore pipette, and washed briefly in fresh HBSS. The carcasses were then reconstituted in SDS sample buffer (5 μl/embryo) and homogenized for 1 min in a Mini-Beadbeater-8 (Biospec Products, Inc., Bartlesville, OK), using 1.0 mm glass beads. The homogenates were then heated for 15 min at 75°C, centrifuged for 1 min at 14,000g, and aliquots of the soluble fraction (20 μl homogenate/lane) were electrophoretically separated in 4–12% Bis–Tris polyacrylamide gels, with MOPS running buffer. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membrane, washed briefly in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST), and blocked for a minimum of 2 hr at room temperature (RT) in blocking buffer (10% non-fat dry milk in TBST). Primary antibody was diluted (1:1,000) in blocking buffer and applied overnight (4°C) with gentle agitation. After extensive washes in TBST, membranes were incubated in HRP-conjugated secondary antibody solution (diluted 1:5,000 in blocking buffer) for 1 hr at RT, and target proteins were detected using the Enhanced Chemiluminescence system (ECL, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI). Proteins were visualized by exposure to X-ray film and quantified by densitometry on a FluorChem ChemiImager 5500.

Photomotor Response (PMR)PMR measurements were conducted in a 96-well plate format, using 8–10 embryos per well, similar to methods previously described (Kokel et al., 2010). PMR measurements were performed on embryos at 40–42 hpf. Reported PMR values correspond to the 3rd quartile of the Motion Index, for a measurement interval lasting 3 sec after the light pulse. Mean PMR data represent the average of three replicate measurements, with each replicate derived from a separate group of 8–10 embryos.

Data AnalysesData are presented as mean values ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Mean values for TUNEL, PMR, and densitometry data were statistically compared by analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the Student–Newman–Keuls multiple comparison test. An unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare mean values for phosphorylated histone H3 staining. Differences between mean values were considered statistically significant if P <0.05.
