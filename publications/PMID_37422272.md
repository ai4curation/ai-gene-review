BACKGROUND: Phospholipase C (PLC) γ1 is a critical enzyme regulating nuclear
factor-κB (NF-κB), extracellular signal-related kinase, mitogen-activated
protein kinase, and nuclear factor of activated T cells signaling pathways, yet
germline PLCG1 mutation in human disease has not been reported.
OBJECTIVE: We aimed to investigate the molecular pathogenesis of a PLCG1
activating variant in a patient with immune dysregulation.
METHODS: Whole exome sequencing was used to identify the patient's pathogenic
variants. Bulk RNA sequencing, single-cell RNA sequencing, quantitative PCR,
cytometry by time of flight, immunoblotting, flow cytometry, luciferase assay,
IP-One ELISA, calcium flux assay, and cytokine measurements in patient PBMCs and
T cells and COS-7 and Jurkat cell lines were used to define inflammatory
signatures and assess the impact of the PLCG1 variant on protein function and
immune signaling.
RESULTS: We identified a novel and de novo heterozygous PLCG1 variant, p.S1021F,
in a patient presenting with early-onset immune dysregulation disease. We
demonstrated that the S1021F variant is a gain-of-function variant, leading to
increased inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate production, intracellular Ca2+ release,
and increased phosphorylation of extracellular signal-related kinase, p65, and
p38. The transcriptome and protein expression at the single-cell level revealed
exacerbated inflammatory responses in the patient's T cells and monocytes. The
PLCG1 activating variant resulted in enhanced NF-κB and type II interferon
pathways in T cells, and hyperactivated NF-κB and type I interferon pathways in
monocytes. Treatment with either PLCγ1 inhibitor or Janus kinase inhibitor
reversed the upregulated gene expression profile in vitro.
CONCLUSIONS: Our study highlights the critical role of PLCγ1 in maintaining
immune homeostasis. We illustrate immune dysregulation as a consequence of PLCγ1
activation and provide insight into therapeutic targeting of PLCγ1.

Besides immune cells, PLCγ1 is also expressed in various organs and tissues, including skin, brain, kidney, gastrointestinal tract, and muscles [26]. Considering the ubiquitous expression of PLCγ1, it is highly likely that the activating PLCG1 variant may affect other organs, therefore, additional manifestations beyond the immune dysregulation we observed in our patient are possible. Based on this novel association, we will refer to this new disorder as APLS (Activating PLCG1 variant-associated Syndrome). Our findings suggest that APLS should be added to the growing list of monogenic disorders of immune dysregulation.

PLCγ1 is abundantly expressed in T cells. Besides immune cells, PLCγ1 is also expressed in various organs and tissues, including skin, brain, kidney, gastrointestinal tract, and muscles [26]. Considering the ubiquitous expression of PLCγ1, it is highly likely that the activating PLCG1 variant may affect other organs, therefore, additional manifestations beyond the immune dysregulation we observed in our patient are possible. Infiltration of activated immune cells in tissues may also contribute to the variable clinical manifestations. Understanding the full clinical spectrum of this disorder requires identifying additional patients with germline-activating PLCG1 variant.

Although PLCγ1 and PLCγ2 share similar signalling pathways, the expression patterns of the two molecules are different. Compared with PLCγ2, which is mainly expressed in B cells and myeloid cells, PLCγ1 is universally expressed and most abundantly expressed in T cells and NK cells. Therefore, gain-of-function mutations in PLCG2 and PLCG1 are likely to result in distinct clinical phenotypes. Activating PLCG2 mutations cause the autoinflammatory disease APLAID (autoinflammation and PLCγ2-associated antibody deficiency and immune dysregulation), which is notable for the absence of class-switched memory B cells [27]. In contrast, as PLCG1 is primarily expressed in T cells, our patient with the gain-of-function variant in PLCG1 manifested with a combination of autoimmune and autoinflammatory phenotypes without noticeable numeric B cell defect. However, considering PLCγ2 and PLCγ1 exert similar functions as phospholipases, activate similar downstream pathways, and share high expression of G-CSF that may drive neutrophil production to propagate the inflammatory process [28], more connections between APLS and APLAID may be identified in the future.

Based on the upregulated expression of IFN-γ receptor on the patient’s plasma cells, we suspect that the autoimmune manifestations, including the presence of autoantibodies, might be the consequence of germinal center development caused by constitutive IFN-γ signal[29–31]. We also found that IFN-γ receptors expression was upregulated on CD16+ monocytes, moDCs and LDGs. In CD16+ monocytes, increased IFN-γ signaling could stimulate the antiviral immunity and enhance the inflammatory response. As for moDCs, elevated expression of IFN-γ receptors could change the differentiation trajectory of monocytes under enhanced IFN-γ signalling, which may further contribute to the patient’s immune activation [32]. LDGs were reported to play a proinflammatory role in pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases [33]. Upregulated IFN-γ receptors on LDGs implicate a potential role of T cells regulating LDGs by IFN-γ signal in development of autoimmunity.

Together, our results suggest that T cells and monocytes as two key cell populations in the pathogenesis of the immune dysregulation disease caused by the PLCG1 variant. The PLCG1 activating variant first activates T cells through the NF-κB pathway and upregulated IFN-γ responses, which then triggers monocytes and leads to its subsequent inflammatory responses including increased NF-κB and type I IFN signalling. The NF-κB signalling and type I IFN signalling in monocytes exacerbate the inflammatory responses and may contribute to the development of autoimmunity. The patient with the PLCG1 S1021F activating variant has a strong IFN signature. Our in vitro experiments showed that constitutive activation of the IFN pathway due to PLCG1 gain-of-function variant can be blocked with JAK inhibitors.

Somatic PLCG1 variants have been identified in cancer, mainly in cutaneous T-cell lymphomas. Although those somatic PLCG1 variants are frequently found in T-cell lymphomas, they are not considered driver mutations, and somatic variants are found in many other genes. On the other hand, somatic variants in NLRP3, NLRC4, NOD2, and UBA1 have been identified in patients with late-onset inflammatory diseases [34–37]. Whether somatic gain-of-function PLCG1 variants are associated with immune dysregulation in adulthood warrants further investigation.

Heterozygous loss-of-function PLCG1 variants are common in public databases, indicating haploinsufficiency of PLCγ1 is unlikely to be pathogenic. However, homozygous loss-of-function PLCG1 variants are absent in public databases. Plcg1-deficient mice cease growing at early to mid-gestation [38, 39]. Intestinal tissue-specific Plcg1 knock-out mice showed earlier death with a propensity to develop colorectal tumors [40]. Together, these data suggest that individuals with biallelic loss-of-function PLCG1 variants resulting in PLCγ1 deficiency may also be affected. However, the phenotypes of PLCγ1 deficiency are expected to be distinct from the phenotypes of patients with PLCG1 gain-of-function variant.

In summary, our study identifies the first germline pathogenic variant in PLCG1 resulting in constitutive PLCγ1 activation and upregulation of downstream inflammatory pathways. Our study highlights that PLCγ1 plays critical roles in the maintenance of immune homeostasis, and PLCγ1 is a key upstream driver in the pathogenesis of immune dysregulation disease.
