The synthesis of glycerolipid is essential to prevent the toxic effects of
excess fatty acids. Glycerolipids are generated by the sequential attachment of
acyl-CoA to glycerol by endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-localized acyltransferases,
ultimately yielding non-toxic triacylglycerol that can be stored in lipid
droplets. The key acyltransferase GPAT4 associates with the CHP1 protein, but
the regulation of GPAT4 remains poorly understood. Here, we have used genetic,
biochemical, and imaging techniques to identify TMEM120A as GPAT4-activating
protein. We show that ER-localized TMEM120A and CHP1 synergistically activate
GPAT4 and promote the incorporation of medium and long chain acyl-CoA into
glycerolipid. C. elegans mutants of TMEM120A or CHP1 ortholog are susceptible to
high-fat diet induced sterility, in part due to their deficiency in lipid
droplet expansion. In mammalian cells, fatty acid supplementation and
myristoylated CHP1 enhance the association of TMEM120A with GPAT4. Together, our
results reveal an unexpected mechanism that alleviates lipotoxicity.

Introduction

Excess fatty acids are toxic to cells1. To prevent lipotoxicity, excess fatty acids are incorporated into neutral lipids, such as triacylglycerol (TAG), via the glycerol-3-phosphate (G-3-P) or the monoacylglycerol pathway2. The nontoxic TAG can then be stored in lipid droplets (LDs), which are conserved organelles for fat storage3–7. Central to the G-3-P pathway is the sequential addition of fatty acyl-coenzyme-A (acyl-CoA) molecules to the glycerol backbone by acyltransferases, with the G-3-P acyltransferase, GPAT4, as the rate-limiting enzyme8. However, how the G-3-P pathway is regulated in response to excess fatty acids is poorly understood.

In the G-3-P pathway, purified acyltransferases show a clear preference to acyl-CoA with specific chain lengths9,10. Nevertheless, various lipidomic analyses revealed there to be a spectrum of acyl chains in TAG from animal cells11–13, suggesting a cryptic mechanism that enhances the ability of acyltransferases to accept less favorable substrates. Here, we report TMEM120A as a key enhancer of substrate plasticity.

C. elegans has been used extensively as a model organism to link mechanisms of LD regulation to whole animal physiology14. From a genetic screen for mutants with altered LD size, we identified the C. elegans ortholog of TMEM120A (alternatively known as NET29 or TACAN), which is a member of a conserved family of transmembrane proteins. TMEM120A was first reported in a proteomic study of the nuclear envelope15. A subsequent functional analysis indicated that TMEM120A and its paralog TMEM120B are required for the differentiation of 3T3-L1 pre-adipocytes into mature adipocytes16. More recently, TMEM120A was reported to act at the cell surface as an ion channel that is sensitive to mechanical cues17. However, multiple independent studies did not support this conclusion18–21. Instead, structural analyses indicated that TMEM120A forms a symmetrical homodimer and each protomer binds specifically to a coenzyme-A (CoASH) molecule19–21. Although TMEM120A shares structural similarity to the fatty acid elongase, ELOVL722, results from the MacKinnon lab show that TMEM120A does not promote fatty acid elongation19. Therefore, the precise role of TMEM120A in lipid metabolism is still largely unknown.

The lipid-modified, myristoylated form of calcineurin B homologous protein (CHP) 1 is the only known GPAT4 activator thus far23. Interestingly, CHP1 appears to play additional roles in regulating the maturation and trafficking of Na+/H+ exchangers (NHEs) to the cell surface24. Therefore, it remains unclear how CHP1 can engage different clients to modulate their functions in diverse biological pathways.

In this paper, we report the identification of mutants that are defective in the sole C. elegans ortholog of TMEM120A and CHP1. We present new evidence, based on genetic, biochemical, imaging, and lipid analysis, that C. elegans TMEM-120/TMEM120A acts at the ER, in the same pathway as CHPF-1/CHP1, to promote the incorporation of medium and long chain acyl-CoA into glycerolipid. Furthermore, excess fatty acids promote the interaction between GPAT4, CHP1 and TMEM120A, which leads to the synergistic activation of GPAT4. Thus, our study reveals an unexpected convergence of regulatory mechanisms at the rate-limiting step of glycerolipid synthesis.

ResultsTMEM-120 is required for LD expansion in C. elegansIn response to demand for energy or membrane components, fatty acids that are stored in the form of TAG can be released from LDs and subsequently processed by β-oxidation pathways to yield acetyl-CoA25. We have previously shown that LDs undergo abnormal expansion in C. elegans daf-22/thiolase mutants, owing to a block in the peroxisomal β-oxidation pathway26. These animals accumulate more TAG through the concerted action of ACS-22/acyl-CoA synthetase and DGAT-2/diacylglycerol acyltransferase27, presumably to prevent the inappropriate release of fatty acids that cannot be further processed. Accordingly, a loss of ACS-22 or DGAT-2 function attenuates LD expansion in daf-22 mutant animals. In a genetic screen for additional daf-22 suppressors, we identified a complementation group consisting of two recessive alleles, hj49 and hj50. Molecular cloning revealed lesions in a gene annotated as M01G5.3, hereafter named tmem-120, which encodes a predicted transmembrane protein that is homologous to mammalian TMEM120A and TMEM120B16 (Fig. S1A). The hj50 nonsense allele (Glutamine 290 to amber) caused ~85% reduction of tmem-120 mRNA level (Fig. S1A, B), possibly due to nonsense mediated decay. Therefore, it is a strong loss-of-function allele. The hj49 missense allele caused the substitution of a conserved glycine to glutamate (G195E) (Fig. S1A). Based on the structure of TMEM120A, this conserved glycine residue is located in a flexible linker immediately upstream of a coenzyme A (CoASH) binding site19–21. Therefore, its replacement with glutamate may alter the conformation of the linker and, in turn, affect the orientation of the residues that constitute the CoASH binding site (including W193 of human TMEM120A and W197 of C. elegans TMEM-120). Since the phenotypes of mutant animals carrying hj49 and hj50 were indistinguishable (Fig. S1C–E), we concluded that the G195E substitution severely compromised TMEM-120 function. TMEM-120 is required for LD expansion in C. elegans

In response to demand for energy or membrane components, fatty acids that are stored in the form of TAG can be released from LDs and subsequently processed by β-oxidation pathways to yield acetyl-CoA25. We have previously shown that LDs undergo abnormal expansion in C. elegans daf-22/thiolase mutants, owing to a block in the peroxisomal β-oxidation pathway26. These animals accumulate more TAG through the concerted action of ACS-22/acyl-CoA synthetase and DGAT-2/diacylglycerol acyltransferase27, presumably to prevent the inappropriate release of fatty acids that cannot be further processed. Accordingly, a loss of ACS-22 or DGAT-2 function attenuates LD expansion in daf-22 mutant animals. In a genetic screen for additional daf-22 suppressors, we identified a complementation group consisting of two recessive alleles, hj49 and hj50. Molecular cloning revealed lesions in a gene annotated as M01G5.3, hereafter named tmem-120, which encodes a predicted transmembrane protein that is homologous to mammalian TMEM120A and TMEM120B16 (Fig. S1A). The hj50 nonsense allele (Glutamine 290 to amber) caused ~85% reduction of tmem-120 mRNA level (Fig. S1A, B), possibly due to nonsense mediated decay. Therefore, it is a strong loss-of-function allele. The hj49 missense allele caused the substitution of a conserved glycine to glutamate (G195E) (Fig. S1A). Based on the structure of TMEM120A, this conserved glycine residue is located in a flexible linker immediately upstream of a coenzyme A (CoASH) binding site19–21. Therefore, its replacement with glutamate may alter the conformation of the linker and, in turn, affect the orientation of the residues that constitute the CoASH binding site (including W193 of human TMEM120A and W197 of C. elegans TMEM-120). Since the phenotypes of mutant animals carrying hj49 and hj50 were indistinguishable (Fig. S1C–E), we concluded that the G195E substitution severely compromised TMEM-120 function. In subsequent experiments, we used animals carrying the hj50 nonsense allele for phenotypic analysis.

To define more precisely the block imposed by TMEM-120 deficiency on neutral lipid synthesis, we compared the lipid profiles of wild type and tmem-120 mutant animals, which were raised on a control diet or HFD, by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Principal component analysis (PCA) of all detectable lipid species (including but not limited to TAG, PC, PE, DAG, PI, and PS) in the glycerolipid synthesis pathways (Fig. 3C) suggested a strong difference in the overall lipid profiles of wild type and tmem-120 mutant animals, in the absence or presence of HFD (Fig. S4A). As expected, we found that tmem-120 mutants had almost 30% less TAG than wild type animals (Fig. 3D). If TMEM-120 acted exclusively with DGATs, then it is plausible that excess fatty acids might be diverted to phospholipid synthesis, according to the canonical glycerolipid synthesis pathway (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, such a compensatory increase in PC and PE levels was not observed in tmem-120 mutants under HFD. Instead, the DAG, PC, and PE levels were all lower in the tmem-120 mutants than in the wild type animals (Fig. 3E–G), suggesting that TMEM-120 acts at an early step of glycerolipid synthesis. Our results indicate that TMEM-120 deficiency causes a broad impairment in the incorporation of excess fatty acids into glycerolipid.

To explore the role of TMEM-120 in promoting the incorporation of de novo synthesized fatty acids, we performed intestine-specific RNAi against fasn-1/fatty acid synthase, a key enzyme for lipogenesis37, using previously established method38. Attenuating fatty acid synthesis significantly reduced the LD size in wild type animals (Fig. 3H, I). In contrast, there was no further reduction in LD size when fasn-1 was knocked down in tmem-120 mutants, suggesting that FASN-1 and TMEM-120 act in the same pathway. We concluded that TMEM-120 contributes to the incorporation of de novo synthesized fatty acids into glycerolipid.

Enhanced susceptibility of tmem-120 mutants to HFDThe incorporation of excess dietary fatty acids into glycerolipid is a crucial protective mechanism against lipotoxicity. Hence, the increase in dietary fatty acids resulting from an HFD was tolerated by wild type animals, with only a minor impact on their growth rate and brood size (Fig. 3J, K). In contrast, when tmem-120 mutants were fed an HFD, we observed a severe delay in their growth and sterility. The correlation between membrane fluidity, growth rate and fertility in C. elegans was previously established39. Therefore, we entertained the hypothesis that the altered acyl chain composition of glycerolipid in tmem-120 mutant animals might affect their membrane fluidity and, in this way, contribute to their susceptibility to dietary fatty acids. We determined the fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) of a membrane-associated prenylated green fluorescent protein (GFP-CAAX) as an indicator of membrane fluidity (Fig. S3A, B)39. We detected an increase in recovery half-time and a decrease in diffusion coefficient, indicating a reduction in membrane fluidity, which was specific to the tmem-120 mutants raised under HFD (Fig. S3C, D). Our results correlate with the enhanced susceptibility of tmem-120 mutant animals to excess fatty acids and further reinforce the notion that TMEM-120 plays a protective role against lipotoxicity.

CHPF-1/CHP1 is required for LD expansion in C. elegans

From the genetic screens that led to the discovery of tmem-120, we recovered 4 loss-of-function alleles of chpf-1, which encodes the C. elegans ortholog of CHP1 (Fig. S6A). The glycine residue at position 2 is the conserved site for myristoylation. The recovery of the G2R mutant, therefore, underscored the importance of myristoylation to CHPF-1 function. According to the CHP1 structure and the AlphaFold3 prediction of the CHPF-1 structure50, glutamate and phenylalanine at positions 13 and 35, respectively, reside in α-helices, whereas glycine at position 44 resides in a flexible region (Fig. S6B). Therefore, it is conceivable that missense mutations in these conserved residues may impair the structural integrity of CHPF-1.

We used chpf-1(hj47) as the reference allele, since it was predicted to disrupt the myristoylation and hence the function of CHPF-1. We showed that the loss of CHPF-1 function reduced the size and blocked the expansion of LDs in wild type and daf-22 mutant animals (Fig. 9A, B). However, importantly, there was no difference in LD size in daf-22; chpf-1 and daf-22; chpf-1; tmem120 mutant animals, suggesting that chpf-1 and tmem-120 acted in the same genetic pathway. Based on our lipid analysis (Figs. 4 and S4), we propose that TMEM-120/TMEM120A assists in the presentation of medium to long chain acyl-CoA to GPAT4, regardless of their degree of unsaturation.

GPAT4, and its paralog GPAT3, catalyze the first step of glycerolipid synthesis from glycerol-3-phosphate (G-3-P) in distinct tissues in mammals9. Besides acting in the ER, where most GPAT activity is detected, GPAT4 has also been shown to re-localize to LDs53. Our results place TMEM120A at one of the early steps of glycerolipid synthesis, thus explaining the reduction of TAG, DAG, PC and PE in tmem-120 mutant animals (Fig. 3D–G), especially when they are under HFD.

The tmem-120 and chpf-1 loss-of-function mutant animals share remarkably similar phenotypes, including their deficiency in promoting LD expansion, and HFD sensitivity (Fig. 9). Nevertheless, CHP1 and its paralogs interact with a broader range of proteins than TMEM120A57. Therefore, further analysis of chpf-1 mutant animals may reveal additional physiological functions of CHPF-1/CHP1.

In conclusion, our functional studies of the GPAT4-CHP1-TMEM120A complex reveal a hitherto unknown mechanism that overcomes the intrinsic substrate preference of acyltransferases, thus enabling the glycerolipid synthesis pathways to accept a range of fatty acids with comparable efficiency. This can be achieved by an increase in the local concentration of medium and long chain fatty acyl-CoA and their subsequent presentation to GPAT4 by TMEM120A. Pharmacological modulation of the TMEM120 family of proteins should therefore have a direct impact on fatty acid absorption and utilization.

The animals were lysed in an ice-cold ultrasonic bath with cycles of 2 min sonication followed by 30 s rest, repeated for a total of 7 min. Phase separation was induced by the addition of 210 µl water, and the samples were sonicated for a further 15 min, keeping the MTBE:methanol:H2O ratio to 10:3:2.5 (vol/vol/vol). They were then centrifuged at 16,100 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, after which the upper organic phase was transferred into a glass vial. MTBE (325 µl) was added to the lower phase, and the sample was centrifuged once again at 16,100 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to re-extract the lipids. The upper organic phase was combined with those from previously collected samples and then dried under a stream of nitrogen at room temperature. A principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using ClustVis65. Before quantitative analysis, lipid species with variations > 10-fold between the technical replicates were manually excluded. Then, the mean value of the technical replicates of each lipid species was calculated for each biological sample. Only lipids detected in all three biological samples were retained for further analysis.For the targeted analysis of sphingomyelin, the dried extracts were dissolved and injected for analysis as previously described66 with three independent biological samples and one technical injection per sample. The intensity was normalized to the protein content using the BCA assay. Subsequently, 800 μL MTBE was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 30 s. Then, 240 μL pre-cooled methanol was added, and the mixture was vortexed again. The mixture was subjected to ultrasonic extraction in an ice-water bath for 20 min and then allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min. After UHPLC separation, the samples were analyzed with a Q Exactive series mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Lipid analysis

Two lipidomic approaches were employed, namely relative and absolute lipidomics. For both experimental strategies, synchronized L1 larvae were used: ~3000 larvae per sample for relative lipidomics and ~6000 larvae per sample for absolute lipidomics. Animals were cultured on 10-cm NGM plates ± 2% glucose such that 20× concentrated overnight cultures of E. coli OP50 were used for seeding the plates. Animals were harvested at the L4 stage as follows: With a normal diet, 43 h after L1 for WT animals and 46 h after L1 for tmem-120(hj50) animals. With a diet supplemented with 2% glucose, 50 h after L1 for WT animals and 59 h after L1 for tmem-120(hj50) animals. All the animals were washed with detergent-free PBS at least three times and then transferred into organic solvent resistant Eppendorf tubes, after which they were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

For relative lipidomic analysis, lipid extraction was conducted using methyl-tert-butyl ether (MTBE) as described62,63, with some modifications. In brief, the samples were thawed on ice, and 250 μl methanol (precooled to −20 °C; RDH, for HPLC) and 875 µl MTBE (VWR, for HPLC) were added. The animals were lysed in an ice-cold ultrasonic bath with cycles of 2 min sonication followed by 30 s rest, repeated for a total of 7 min. Phase separation was induced by the addition of 210 µl water, and the samples were sonicated for a further 15 min, keeping the MTBE:methanol:H2O ratio to 10:3:2.5 (vol/vol/vol). They were then centrifuged at 16,100 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, after which the upper organic phase was transferred into a glass vial. MTBE (325 µl) was added to the lower phase, and the sample was centrifuged once again at 16,100 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to re-extract the lipids. The upper organic phase was combined with those from previously collected samples and then dried under a stream of nitrogen at room temperature. For the untargeted analysis of lipid profiles, the dried extracts were dissolved in acetonitrile (RCI Labscan, for HPLC), isopropanol (RNH) and water at a 65:30:5 (vol/vol/vol) ratio and stored at −80 °C.

Lipid analysis was performed as previously described29. A 100-µl aliquot of extract was analyzed using the Bruker Elute UPLC system with three independent biological samples and two technical injections per sample. The mass spectrometry data were analyzed using MetaboScape (ver5.0; Bruker), annotated with the following spectral libraries: MSDIAL-Tandem Mass Spectral Atlas-VS68-pos and MSDIAL-Tandem Mass 736 Spectral Atlas-VS68-neg. The intensity was normalized using the probabilistic quotient normalization method64, and datasets were exported to Excel for further processing. A principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using ClustVis65. Before quantitative analysis, lipid species with variations > 10-fold between the technical replicates were manually excluded. Then, the mean value of the technical replicates of each lipid species was calculated for each biological sample. Only lipids detected in all three biological samples were retained for further analysis.

For the targeted analysis of sphingomyelin, the dried extracts were dissolved and injected for analysis as previously described66 with three independent biological samples and one technical injection per sample. The intensity was normalized to the protein content using the BCA assay. The data were exported to Excel for further processing.

For the absolute lipidomic analysis, samples were thawed on ice, mixed with 200 μL water and 20 μL internal lipid standard mixture (SPLASH™ LIPIDOMIX™, Avanti, 330707), followed by vortexing. Subsequently, 800 μL MTBE was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 30 s. Then, 240 μL pre-cooled methanol was added, and the mixture was vortexed again. The mixture was subjected to ultrasonic extraction in an ice-water bath for 20 min and then allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min. Centrifugation was performed at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 10 °C, after which the upper organic phase was collected and dried under a stream of nitrogen. For mass spectrometry analysis, this residue was reconstituted with 200 μL 90% isopropanol/acetonitrile solution, vortexed thoroughly, and 90 μL of the reconstituted solution was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 10 °C. The supernatant was then collected for analysis by LC-MS. Pooled quality control (QC) samples were prepared by combining equal amounts (i.e., 10 μL) of each extraction mixture. The samples were separated using a UHPLC Nexera LC-30A ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (SHIMADZU) equipped with a C18 column (Waters, ACQUITY UPLC CSH C18, 1.7 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm column). The column temperature was maintained at 45 °C, with a flow rate of 300 μL/min. The mobile phase consisted of (A) acetonitrile-water (6:4, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid and 0.1 mM ammonium formate, and (B) acetonitrile-isopropanol (1:9, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid and 0.1 mM ammonium formate. The gradient elution program was conducted as follows: 0–2 min, (B) maintained at 30%; 2–25 min, (B) linearly increased from 30 to 100%; 25–35 min, (B) returned to and held at 30%. Throughout the analysis, samples were stored in an autosampler at 10 °C. To minimize the impact of instrument signal fluctuations, the samples were analyzed consecutively in a random order.

Mass spectrometric detection was performed in both electrospray ionization (ESI) positive and negative ion modes. After UHPLC separation, the samples were analyzed with a Q Exactive series mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). The ESI source parameters were as follows: heater temperature, 300 °C; sheath gas flow rate, 45 arbitrary units (AU); auxiliary gas flow rate, 15 AU; sweep gas flow rate, 1 AU; spray voltage 3.0 kV; capillary temperature, 350 °C; S-Lens RF level, 50%; and MS1 scan range, 200–1800 m/z.

The mass-to-charge ratios of the lipid molecules and fragments were acquired by the following method: after each full scan (MS1), ten MS2 fragmentation spectra (HCD) were recorded. For MS1 and MS2, the resolution was set to 70,000 at m/z 200 and 17,500 at m/z 200, respectively. The LipidSearch software was used for peak identification, peak extraction, and lipid identification (including MS/MS-based identification) of the lipid molecules and internal standard lipids. The main parameters were as follows: precursor tolerance, 5 ppm; product tolerance, 5 ppm; and product ion threshold, 5%.

The GPAT4 enzymatic assay was performed in a total volume of 100 µL containing 75 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 4 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 4 mM NaF, 50 µg/mL fatty acid-free BSA, 80 µM glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) (Macklin, G881258), and 20 µM NBD-palmitoyl-CoA (Avanti, 810705).
