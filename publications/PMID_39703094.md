Progressive loss of motor neurons is the hallmark of the neurodegenerative
disease amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), but the underlying disease
mechanisms remain incompletely understood. In this study, we investigate the
effects of C21ORF2 mutations, a gene recently linked to ALS, and find that
primary cilia are dysfunctional. Human patient-derived mutant C21ORF2 motor
neurons have a reduced ciliary frequency and length. We report that C21ORF2 is
located at the basal body of the primary cilium, and mutations associated with
ALS alter this localization. Furthermore, we show that a reduction of C21ORF2
levels in cell lines and motor neurons is sufficient to cause fewer primary
cilia and reduced cilial length. This ciliary dysfunction leads to defective
downstream sonic hedgehog signalling and reduces the expression of cellular
retinoic acid binding protein 1 (CRABP1), a protein involved in motor neuron
maintenance and survival. In a compartmentalized co-culture system of motor
neurons and muscle cells, these ciliary defects were associated with a reduced
ability of neuromuscular junction formation. Interestingly, these cilia defects
are seemingly not restricted to C21ORF2 ALS, as we also observed perturbed
primary cilia in cultured motor neurons and post-mortem motor cortex from
patients with the most common genetic subtype of ALS caused by repeat expansions
in the C9ORF72 gene. Finally, overexpression of C21ORF2 in mutant C21ORF2 motor
neurons rescued the ciliary frequency and length, CRAPBP1 expression and
neuromuscular junction formation, confirming the importance of primary cilia for
motor neuron function. These results point towards primary cilia dysfunction
contributing to motor neuron degeneration in ALS and open new avenues for
further research and interventions for this as yet untreatable disease.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a complex neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the death of motor neurons (MN) in the spinal cord, brainstem and motor cortex, leading to progressive muscle weakness.1,2 The site at which the first symptoms arise is often focal, but the disease spreads to other body regions and ultimately leads to death from respiratory failure.3 The average survival time for a patient with ALS is only 2–5 years after symptom onset, and treatment options are limited for most patients; there are currently four US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drugs (riluzole, edaravone, relyvrio and tofersen).4-7 There is a strong genetic contribution to ALS aetiology.8 Currently, mutations in more than 40 genes have been associated with ALS, explaining a large proportion of patients with familial ALS9,10 and also 5%–10% of cases without a positive family history.11,12 ALS is a heterogeneous disease, from a genetic, pathophysiological and clinical point of view. While many of the genes associated with ALS cluster in RNA biology, proteostasis and axon architecture/transport, no comprehensive disease mechanism has been found.13 Recent advances in the genetics of ALS have paved the way for new studies into the disease mechanisms of MN degeneration.

One of the newly discovered ALS genes is chromosome 21 open reading frame 2 (C21ORF2), also known as cilia and flagella associated protein 410 (CFAP410).14  C21ORF2 was identified as a susceptibility gene for ALS and burden analyses on whole-genome sequencing data found rare heterozygous loss-of-function mutations and missense mutations to be associated with ALS. Over 75% of the associated variants are predicted to be detrimental by altering the expression levels or protein structure, based on bioinformatic modelling approaches.15 The biological function of C21ORF2 remains poorly characterized, yet the highly conserved leucine-rich-repeat domain (LLR), the ubiquitous expression and the autosomal recessive mutations found in known ciliopathies like axial spondylometaphyseal dysplasia, Jeune syndrome and retinal dystrophy underscore its functional importance.16-19 Moreover, reduced expression of C21ORF2 has been reported in brain of people with Down syndrome, indicating the potential role it may play in neurons.20 Many functional roles have been attributed to C21ORF2, but studies of these have suggested cellular processes are scarce.

C21ORF2 has been implicated in actin cytoskeleton organization and DNA repair via interacting with NIMA-related kinase 1 (NEK1), another ALS gene of which rare variants have been observed in ALS patients.21-23 The clinical presentation of C21ORF2 patients does not appear to deviate from the typical ALS phenotype described in the broader patient population in terms of age of onset, site of onset and survival.14  C21ORF2 has also been proposed to regulate primary cilia, and a siRNA-based functional genomics screen demonstrated that C21ORF2 is a possible interactor of the ciliome that localizes to primary cilia structures in the photoreceptor cells of human, pig and mouse retinas.24,25 The role of primary cilia has been studied mainly in the context of development. However, in recent years, the importance of cilia function in neuronal ageing and health has emerged. Primary cilia harbour various G protein-coupled receptors and thus play a crucial role in signal transduction of various stimuli. Furthermore, primary cilia are pivotal in Hedgehog (Hh) signalling transduction.26 Thus far, ciliary defects have remained largely unexplored in the context of ALS. One study reported reduced primary cilia in primary cell culture and lumbar spinal cord of superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) mice.27 One unique case study reported siblings with homozygous variants in C21ORF2, who developed retinitis pigmentosa, a ciliopathy, prior to ALS.28

In this study, we investigated the consequences of ALS-associated mutations in C21ORF2 in cell lines and induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived MNs. The mutations altered primary cilia morphology and affected MN function and viability. Altered cilia morphology was not limited to this subtype of ALS, as it was found in the most common familial subtype of ALS caused by C9ORF72 mutations, suggesting that ciliary dysfunction may have broader implications in ALS.

C21ORF2 is essential for ciliogenesisAs a first step to understanding the cellular function of C21ORF2, we performed a gene ontology analysis on reported interactors of C21ORF2 (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table 1). The top hit resulting from this analysis was cilium assembly, a pathway largely unexplored in ALS. Other significant ontologies (P < 0.05) were largely related to either ciliogenesis or microtubule stability (Fig. 1B). To confirm these in silico findings, we performed immunocytochemistry (ICC) on human neuroblastoma cells (SH-SY5Y) to examine the localization of C21ORF2 (Fig. 1C and E). As described previously, we used serum-starvation as a method to induce primary cilium formation on SH-SY5Y cells, which we labelled with Arl13B (Supplementary Fig. 1A).24 C21ORF2 co-localizes with γ-tubulin, a centrosomal marker, localized to the basal body region of primary cilia. We hypothesized that the heterozygous nonsense and missense mutations found in ALS could perturb C21ORF2’s role in primary cilia assembly and maintenance. To test this hypothesis, we designed three siRNAs targeting C21ORF2 and quantified primary ciliary length and frequency. All three of the tested siRNAs could reduce protein levels significantly, with C21ORF2-II being the most potent. We continued to use C21ORF2-II in all of our following experiments (Supplementary Fig. 1B and C). Compared to scrambled-control treated cells, C21ORF2-depleted cells displayed significantly reduced ciliary frequency and length (Fig. 1D–F). This observation was confirmed using different cilia markers (Supplementary Fig. 1A). C21ORF2 has homologues in nearly all genome-sequenced vertebrates (Supplementary Fig. 2A). In addition, a C21ORF2 orthologue is also found in Drosophila melanogaster (CG15208). We used the GAL4-UAS system to reduce the levels of CG15208 in vivo via the use of an RNAi line expressed under the control of a ubiquitously expressed driver (tubulin-gal4) (Supplementary Fig. 2B). After validating the knockdown levels using qPCR, we performed a phenotypic screen (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Flies with reduced levels of the C21ORF2 orthologue CG15208 died shortly after eclosion because they immediately became stuck in the food (Supplementary Fig. 2D). CG15208 knockdown flies showed no morphological changes; however, a severely uncoordinated phenotype was observed. We additionally observed a large effect on the climbing ability of CG15208 knockdown flies (Supplementary Fig. 2E). Both of these phenotypes are also observed in flies with defective mechanosensory transduction due to cilia dysfunction.29 Mechanosensory neurons, located in type I sensory organs, are the only neurons in Drosophila equipped with specially modified cilia that transduce stimuli. Loss of cilia in the mechanosensory neurons results in morphologically normal but uncoordinated flies.30-32 We used immunohistochemistry to visualize the mechanosensory neurons in the 2nd antennal segment (also called Johnston’s organ). Ciliated structures were observed in normal control flies. In flies with reduced levels of the C21ORF2 Drosophila orthologue CG15208, these ciliated structures were no longer detectable (Supplementary Fig. 2G and H), while the overall neuronal distribution in the mechanosensory organ appeared normal. Additional evidence of defective cilia in vivo was found by examining a second ciliated structure. Most cells in the fruit fly have no cilia; we therefore focused on sperm cells and tested fertility.33 Male flies with reduced levels of CG15208 were crossed with control females, and the progeny ratio was calculated based on the number of eggs in the tube. No progeny were observed when control females were mated with CG15208-depleted males. Control flies, on the other hand, had an egg-to-progeny ratio of almost 90% (Supplementary Fig. 2F). Based on our in vitro and in vivo findings, we conclude that loss-of-function of C21ORF2 is sufficient to induce problems with ciliogenesis. This stimulated us to explore whether C21ORF2 mutations found in ALS could potentially disrupt cilium homeostasis.
C21ORF2 ALS variants cause ciliary defectsTo assess the functional role of C21ORF2 in ALS, we generated constructs expressing C21ORF2 tagged with a C-terminal turbo-GFP (tGFP) and introduced missense mutations and one loss-of-function mutation associated with ALS (V58L, Y68*, R106C, R172W, A255T).14 Mutations were chosen based on their allele frequency34 and predicted Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) score,35 a tool that estimates the deleteriousness of genetic variants (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Three of the variants chosen were located in the highly conserved LRR domain of C21ORF2. Substitutions of amino acids in these LRRs can change the structural framework required for molecular interactions.36 In an initial step, we investigated if the ALS-associated missense mutations could act via gain-of-function or in a dominant-negative manner. We therefore quantified ciliary length in cells expressing the different C21ORF2 constructs. Overexpression of wild-type C21ORF2 slightly increased ciliary length, whereas overexpression of mutant C21ORF2 did not affect ciliary length compared to controls cells (Supplementary Fig. 3C). To ensure these differences were not explained by destabilization of the mutant protein, we assessed protein levels using immunoblotting and observed no differences between the conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Next, we wondered if ALS associated variants might lead to non-functional C21ORF2 and therefore result in abnormal primary cilia in cells not expressing endogenous C21ORF2. To test this hypothesis, we overexpressed the generated constructs in SH-SY5Y cells with reduced levels of C21ORF2 and quantified ciliary frequency and length (Fig. 2A–C and Supplementary Fig. 3B). Whilst overexpression of wild-type C21ORF2 rescues C21ORF2 knockdown (indicated on Fig. 2B and C as ‘no’), overexpression of LRR mutated constructs (indicated on Fig. 1B and C as ‘58’, ‘68’, ‘106’) could not rescue the reduced ciliary frequency nor the reduced cilia length. Furthermore, overexpression of variants (R172W and A255T) located in the conserved C-terminal domain and predicted to be deleterious mutations15 did not rescue the reduced cilia frequency and length. We hypothesized that these missense mutations may affect recruitment to the basal body. Other significant ontologies (P < 0.05) were largely related to either ciliogenesis or microtubule stability (Fig. 1B). To confirm these in silico findings, we performed immunocytochemistry (ICC) on human neuroblastoma cells (SH-SY5Y) to examine the localization of C21ORF2 (Fig. 1C and E). As described previously, we used serum-starvation as a method to induce primary cilium formation on SH-SY5Y cells, which we labelled with Arl13B (Supplementary Fig. 1A).24 C21ORF2 co-localizes with γ-tubulin, a centrosomal marker, localized to the basal body region of primary cilia. We hypothesized that the heterozygous nonsense and missense mutations found in ALS could perturb C21ORF2’s role in primary cilia assembly and maintenance. To test this hypothesis, we designed three siRNAs targeting C21ORF2 and quantified primary ciliary length and frequency. All three of the tested siRNAs could reduce protein levels significantly, with C21ORF2-II being the most potent. We continued to use C21ORF2-II in all of our following experiments (Supplementary Fig. 1B and C). Compared to scrambled-control treated cells, C21ORF2-depleted cells displayed significantly reduced ciliary frequency and length (Fig. 1D–F). This observation was confirmed using different cilia markers (Supplementary Fig. 1A). The findings show the pivotal role of C21ORF2 for primary cilia assembly and maintenance and suggest that C21ORF2 ALS variants could potentially disrupt cilium homeostasis.

Reduced C21ORF2 levels disturb cilia functioning in vivo
C21ORF2 has homologues in nearly all genome-sequenced vertebrates (Supplementary Fig. 2A). In addition, a C21ORF2 orthologue is also found in Drosophila melanogaster (CG15208). We used the GAL4-UAS system to reduce the levels of CG15208 in vivo via the use of an RNAi line expressed under the control of a ubiquitously expressed driver (tubulin-gal4) (Supplementary Fig. 2B). After validating the knockdown levels using qPCR, we performed a phenotypic screen (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Flies with reduced levels of the C21ORF2 orthologue CG15208 died shortly after eclosion because they immediately became stuck in the food (Supplementary Fig. 2D). CG15208 knockdown flies showed no morphological changes; however, a severely uncoordinated phenotype was observed. We additionally observed a large effect on the climbing ability of CG15208 knockdown flies (Supplementary Fig. 2E). Both of these phenotypes are also observed in flies with defective mechanosensory transduction due to cilia dysfunction.29 Mechanosensory neurons, located in type I sensory organs, are the only neurons in Drosophila equipped with specially modified cilia that transduce stimuli. Loss of cilia in the mechanosensory neurons results in morphologically normal but uncoordinated flies.30-32 We used immunohistochemistry to visualize the mechanosensory neurons in the 2nd antennal segment (also called Johnston’s organ). Ciliated structures were observed in normal control flies. In flies with reduced levels of the C21ORF2 Drosophila orthologue CG15208, these ciliated structures were no longer detectable (Supplementary Fig. 2G and H), while the overall neuronal distribution in the mechanosensory organ appeared normal. Additional evidence of defective cilia in vivo was found by examining a second ciliated structure. Most cells in the fruit fly have no cilia; we therefore focused on sperm cells and tested fertility.33 Male flies with reduced levels of CG15208 were crossed with control females, and the progeny ratio was calculated based on the number of eggs in the tube. No progeny were observed when control females were mated with CG15208-depleted males. Control flies, on the other hand, had an egg-to-progeny ratio of almost 90% (Supplementary Fig. 2F). Based on our in vitro and in vivo findings, we conclude that loss-of-function of C21ORF2 is sufficient to induce problems with ciliogenesis. This stimulated us to explore whether C21ORF2 mutations found in ALS could potentially disrupt cilium homeostasis.

C21ORF2 is required for intraflagellar transportA unique and highly regulated mechanism of primary cilia is intraflagellar transport (IFT). Proper biogenesis of cilia requires this specialized trafficking system for the movement of proteins into and out of the organelle.38 Moreover, defects in IFT have been linked to several human diseases.39 To test if the observed morphological changes (shorter and less frequent cilia) disturbed IFT, we focused on two proteins: IFT88 and IFT140. IFT88 and IFT140 are subunits of larger protein complexes (IFT-B and IFT-A) that are shown to regulate anterograde and retrograde IFT within the cilium, respectively.40-42 IFT88 normally exits from the cilium and localizes to the basal body once the primary cilium is formed (Fig. 2E). In cells with reduced levels of C21ORF2, IFT88 accumulates primarily at the tip of the cilium and forms a ‘bulb’ phenotype (Fig. 2E–G). Overexpression of wild-type C21ORF2 reverts this aberrant accumulation, unlike overexpression of the ALS mutant C21ORF2 constructs (Fig. 2E–G). We observed a similar effect when we looked at retrograde transport, using IFT140, and at an essential cargo protein, Bardet-Biedl Syndrome 4 (BBS4),43,44 which is normally rapidly exported from the cilium after assembly (Fig. 2H–J). As such, we conclude that C21ORF2 is required for proper IFT functioning and that mutant C21ORF2 is not able to rescue the defective anterograde and retrograde transport.

Overexpression of C21ORF2 rescues ciliary phenotype and loss of neuromuscular junctions

To study the relationship between C21ORF2 loss-of-function, cilia alterations and MN dysfunction, we overexpressed wild-type spot labelled C21ORF2 in sMNs derived from C21ORF2 ALS patients and controls. We used a doxycycline-inducible lentiviral approach with a RFP transduction sensor, which we transduced at Day 14 in the differentiation protocol (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. 7A–C). We quantified the presence of lentiviral wild-type C21ORF2 via the spot tag on western blot and observed the wild-type C21ORF2 spot at the primary cilium in sMNs (Fig. 7B and C). Next, we quantified ciliary length and frequency in RFP-positive sMNs and compared them with non-transduced C21ORF2 ALS and control sMNs. Overexpression of wild-type C21ORF2 was able to rescue ciliary length and increase ciliary frequency in sMNs derived from C21ORF2 ALS patients (Fig. 7E and F). In addition, we quantified the protein levels of CRABP1 after C21ORF2 overexpression and found them to be restored (Supplementary Fig. 8D and E). Finally, we measured NMJ formation in microfluidic devices after wild-type C21ORF2 overexpression in C21ORF2 mutant and control sMNs. Overexpression of wild-type C21ORF2 also rescued NMJ formation of mutant C21ORF2 sMNs (Fig. 7G and H). Together, these results further confirm the link between C21ORF2, primary cilia function and MN health.

DiscussionDifferent possible disease mechanisms have been linked to MN dysfunction and cell death in ALS, but none can fully explain the molecular biology of ALS. Recent large next-generation genetic studies have resulted in a surge in the number of ALS-associated genes, refining our understanding of ALS disease mechanisms. Here, we investigated the role of a new ALS-associated gene, C21ORF2. Our findings confirm a pivotal role for C21ORF2 in ciliogenesis and show the detrimental effects of mutated C21ORF2 on this crucial signalling organelle. Nonsense and missense mutations in C21ORF2 associated with ALS act as loss-of-function mutations by reducing C21ORF2 levels or interfering with C21ORF2’s interaction and localization with the basal body, respectively. MNs derived from ALS patients with C21ORF2 missense mutations display similar ciliary defects as observed after knockdown of C21ORF2 in control MNs. The functional consequences of defective cilia on MNs and, potentially, other cell types in ALS are still unknown and need further investigation. An obvious downstream effect of perturbed cilia function is inhibition of the Shh signalling pathway, which is controlled by primary cilia. We showed that Shh signalling is abolished in MNs of ALS patients with C21ORF2 mutations, suggesting that this pathway is not only important in MN differentiation but also MN maintenance and function. A potential role for this established developmental pathway in age-related neurodegeneration is emerging. Dysregulation of the Shh pathway has been reported in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease,49,66,67 Parkinson’s disease,68,69 Huntington’s disease63 and ALS,64-66 demonstrating the importance of a fully functioning Shh pathway, not only during development but also during ageing. By using sMNs and post-mortem motor cortex samples of patients with a repeat expansion in the C9ORF72 gene, we would like to bring forward the idea that primary cilia dysfunction could have broader implications in ALS. It is currently unclear how C9ORF72 repeat expansions cause this cilia defect. A recent study has described the C9ORF72 protein as a negative regulator of ciliogenesis.70 However, we have not observed reduced C9ORF72 protein abundance in mutant iPSC-derived MNs, nor ALS-associated phenotypes, upon C9ORF72 knockout in iPSC-derived MNs.57,71 This implicates a C9ORF72 mutation-associated gain-of-function mechanism in the cilia defects we observed. Notably, we have not observed any effect on C21ORF2 protein levels in C9ORF72 mutant cells. Future work will be required to determine whether C21ORF2 localizes correctly at the basal body in C9ORF72 mutant neurons or whether dysfunction of other distinct cilia proteins occurs in C9ORF72 mutation carriers.Finally, we propose a mechanistic link between cilia malfunctions and MN degeneration by means of the aberrant expression CRABP1. Downregulation of CRABP1 expression in sMNs is common to various forms of MN diseases.63 Furthermore, an epigenetic study suggests that CRABP1 expression levels decrease with age, which fits the clinical observation that ALS incidence increases with age.72,73 Secondly, Crabp1 knockout mice revealed a direct link between CRABP1 and neural Agrin levels, indicating that CRABP1 signalling in sMNs could be an important physiological regulator of healthy NMJ maintenance. Moreover, Crabp1 knockout mice developed an adult-onset ALS phenotype that could be rescued by elevating the levels of CRABP1. In our C21ORF2 ALS sMN model, we showed that CRABP1 protein levels are reduced and that there were fewer NMJs compared to controls using a co-culture system of sMNs and myotubes. We also aged C21ORF2 sMNs until Day 60 and observed MN degeneration, a key hallmark of ALS pathobiology. Mechanistically, we hypothesize that the morphologically abnormal primary cilia seen in different ALS models are not able to transduce essential signalling factors required for MN homeostasis, which in turn leads to the loss of NMJs and age-dependent MN degeneration. Furthermore, we show that overexpression of wild-type C21ORF2 could restore CRABP1 levels and rescue the ciliary phenotype and NMJ phenotype in sMNs derived from C21ORF2 ALS patients. This first line of evidence sheds light on the involvement of C21ORF2 ALS-associated mutations in ALS pathology, indicating a potential therapeutic avenue involving the restoration of C21ORF2 levels or modulation of SHH signalling in MNs. Some important questions and concerns remain. Is this cilia phenotype present only in C9ORF72 and C21ORF2 patients or also in other forms of ALS? When does the primary cilia defect emerge in ALS pathobiology, and what is the exact modus operandi? How exactly is C21ORF2 carrying out its role in ciliogenesis? We provide evidence for its role in the basal body due to C21ORF2’s location and interactors, but more extensive studies are needed to unravel the mechanisms of cilia dysfunction. Of note, primary cilia defects have been linked with pathways such as autophagy and NF-κB regulated inflammatory responses, mechanisms that are both linked with ALS, suggesting that defective primary cilia could aggravate these already perturbed signalling pathways.74-77 While further research is needed, we provide evidence that the impairment of this overlooked organelle in ALS contributes to the complex pathobiology of this disease. In conclusion, our results reveal a novel link between defective primary cilia and ALS pathology, with potential implications for intervention.
