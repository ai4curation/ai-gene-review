Histone ubiquitination is a crucial post-translational modification (PTM)
regulating chromatin function, yet many histone ubiquitination sites and the
enzymes that control them remain poorly understood. Here, we identify SMARCA3, a
SWI/SNF-related protein frequently downregulated in colorectal cancer (CRC), as
an E3 ubiquitin ligase that targets histone H3 at lysine 23 (H3K23). We
demonstrate that SMARCA3 histone ubiquitination activity is stimulated by the
repressive H3K9me3 mark. Loss of SMARCA3 reduces both H3K23Ub and H3K9me3,
increasing chromatin accessibility at promoters and enhancers enriched for
pioneer transcription factor motifs. This chromatin "rewiring" alters the
transcriptional landscape, driving upregulation of cancer-promoting genes. We
validate this mechanism in CRC cell lines and patient-derived organoids, where
SMARCA3 loss reduces H3K23Ub and H3K9me3. In xenograft mouse models,
overexpression of wild-type SMARCA3, but not a RING domain mutant, suppresses
tumor growth. Together, our findings define SMARCA3 as a key chromatin regulator
contributing to CRC pathogenesis through epigenetic mechanisms.

INTRODUCTION

The spatial organization of chromatin governs essential DNA-templated processes, including replication, transcription, and the DNA damage response (DDR)1–4. Chromatin dynamics are regulated by diverse covalent modifications to its fundamental components, DNA and histones. Despite these established roles, over 30 ubiquitination sites have been identified on histones which remain largely functionally uncharacterized9.

To explore additional E3 ligases involved in ubiquitinate histones, we focused on the SWI/SNF-related protein SMARCA3 (also known as helicase-like transcription factor, HLTF). SMARCA3, the human homolog of the yeast RING finger E3 ligase Rad5, catalyzes non-proteasomal K63-linked polyubiquitination of proliferating-cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) in cooperation with the UBE2N/UBE2V2 E2 conjugating heterodimer during post-replication repair18–20. Beyond this canonical function, SMARCA3 has been implicated in replication-independent cellular events in both proliferating and non-proliferating cells including neurons21–23. Notably, SMARCA3 expression is silenced via promoter hypermethylation in approximately 40% of colorectal cancers (CRC)24–26, although the mechanism underlying its tumor suppressor function remains unclear. Given its E3 ligase and DNA helicase activities, along with its strong chromatin association, we hypothesized that SMARCA3 may also ubiquitinate histones, thereby contributing to the regulation of epigenetic states.

Employing a biochemically reconstituted system, we found that SMARCA3 is active with several E2s beyond its known partner UBE2N/UBE2V2 (Figure 1A, Figure S1C). Further testing of individual K18R, K23R, and unrelated K36R/K37R mutants confirmed that K23 is the predominant site of SMARCA3-mediated ubiquitination on H3 (Figure S1D, E). High-resolution mass spectrometry corroborated these findings, identifying H3K23 and K18 as ubiquitination sites, with K23 being the dominant target (Figure 1D & Figure S1D). Loss of SMARCA3 led to a marked reduction in H3K9me3 levels (Figure 2A), while levels of H3K9me2 or H3K9me1 remained unchanged. Importantly, this effect was rescued by reintroducing WT SMARCA3 in the KO cells. We found that endogenous SMARCA3 interacts with SUV39H1, but not SETDB1 (Figure 2C & Figure S2B). Based on this interaction, we tested whether SUV39H1 overexpression could rescue SMARCA3-dependent reduction in H3K9me3 levels. While prior studies demonstrated that UHRF1-mediated ubiquitination at H3K14 and, to a lesser extent, H3K18 can allosterically enhance SUV39H1 activity35, we did not observe enhanced SUV39H1 activity on H3K23Ub-modified NCPs (Figure S2C). To test this, we performed ATAC-seq analysis on WT and SMARCA3 KO cells. SMARCA3 KO cells indicated a marked increase in accessibility across broad genomic regions (Figure 3A, B). These newly accessible sites spanned the genome and include promoter, intronic, and distal intergenic regions (Figure 3C). Based on ATAC-seq signal intensity we identified four clusters of increased accessibility regions (Figure 3D). Clusters 1–3, which were primarily centered around promoters, were the most accessible in WT cells and showed further increased accessibility in SMARCA3 KO cells (Figure 3E). These clusters also displayed enriched H3K27ac signal flanking the regions of increased accessibility, with acetylation levels correlating with the degree of chromatin accessibility (Figure S3C). In contrast, cluster 4 comprised regions enriched for intronic and distal intergenic areas, which displayed reduced accessibility in WT cells but became more accessible in SMARCA3 KO cells (Figure 3E). Notably, these cluster 4 regions were enriched for H3K9me3 in WT cells, indicating that they transitioned from a repressive, heterochromatic state to an open accessible state in the SMARCA3 KO cells (Figure S3D). Motif enrichment analysis of these regions revealed a significant overrepresentation of Forkhead and Zinc Finger transcription factor binding sites (Figure 3F). Interestingly, NFY is a transcription factor that can function in both activation and repression of gene expression, in part by regulating chromatin accessibility at enhancer regions, while Hoxa11 is essential for cell differentiation and tissue maintenance45,46. These findings suggest that SMARCA3-dependent changes in chromatin accessibility may facilitate the binding of these factors, thereby contributing to the observed transcriptional reprogramming. Consistent with our findings at promoters, active enhancers that were already accessible in WT cells exhibited increased accessibility in SMARCA3 KO cells (Figure 4F, bottom; Figure 4G, right). Similarly, inactive enhancers representing genomic regions that were previously less accessible and marked with H3K9me3 in WT cells (Figure S3E) also displayed increased accessibility in the SMARCA3 KO cells (Figure 4F, top; Figure 4E, left). Motif enrichment analysis of these enhancers revealed a significant overrepresentation of specific transcription factor binding motifs, including the previously identified NFY motif. Consistent with accessible binding sites identified at distal intergenic regions (Figure 3F), CTCF/BORIS binding sites were significantly enriched at enhancers with increased accessibility in SMARCA3 KO cells. Further testing of individual K18R, K23R, and unrelated K36R/K37R mutants confirmed that K23 is the predominant site of SMARCA3-mediated ubiquitination on H3 (Figure S1D, E). High-resolution mass spectrometry corroborated these findings, identifying H3K23 and K18 as ubiquitination sites, with K23 being the dominant target (Figure 1D & Figure S1D). This pattern contrasts with UHRF1, which ubiquitinates H3 at lysines 14, 18, and 23, with a preference for K1816.

The canonical function of SMARCA3 at stalled replication forks relies on prior monoubiquitination of PCNA by the Rad6-Rad18 complex29,30. SMARCA3 recognizes monoubiquitinated PCNA and catalyzes the addition of non-proteasomal K63-linked polyubiquitin chains. Based on this mechanism, we hypothesized that SMARCA3 might similarly elongate ubiquitin chains on histone H3. To test this, we generated a thioether-linked mimic of monoubiquitinated H3 at lysine 23 (H3K23Ub) to serve as a primed substrate for chain extension (Figure S1F–I)31. This new semi-synthetic H3K23Ub was assembled into NCPs and subjected to similar in vitro ubiquitination assays. In the presence of UBE2N/UBE2V2, but not UBE2E1, SMARCA3 efficiently extended H3K23Ub into polyubiquitin chains (Figure 1E). As UBE2N/UBE2V2 catalyzes K63-linked chains, we confirmed that the resulting polyubiquitination on H3K23 was indeed K63-linked (Figure 1F). This activity was specific to SMARCA3, as neither UHRF1 nor the RING-containing histone acetyltransferase PCAF could elongate H3K23Ub (Figure 1G). Together, these findings establish SMARCA3 as a histone H3 E3 ligase that monoubiquitinates K23 in complex with UBE2E1 or UBE2D2 and extends this modification into K63-linked polyubiquitin chains via UBE2N/UBE2V2.

Chromatin accessibility increases in SMARCA3 KO cellsGiven the established role for H3K9me3 in maintaining chromatin compaction and transcriptional silencing40,41, we hypothesized that SMARCA3-dependent downregulation of this mark may lead to alterations in chromatin structure. To test this, we performed ATAC-seq analysis on WT and SMARCA3 KO cells. SMARCA3 KO cells indicated a marked increase in accessibility across broad genomic regions (Figure 3A, B). These newly accessible sites spanned the genome and include promoter, intronic, and distal intergenic regions (Figure 3C). Based on ATAC-seq signal intensity we identified four clusters of increased accessibility regions (Figure 3D). Clusters 1–3, which were primarily centered around promoters, were the most accessible in WT cells and showed further increased accessibility in SMARCA3 KO cells (Figure 3E). These clusters also displayed enriched H3K27ac signal flanking the regions of increased accessibility, with acetylation levels correlating with the degree of chromatin accessibility (Figure S3C). In contrast, cluster 4 comprised regions enriched for intronic and distal intergenic areas, which displayed reduced accessibility in WT cells but became more accessible in SMARCA3 KO cells (Figure 3E). Notably, these cluster 4 regions were enriched for H3K9me3 in WT cells, indicating that they transitioned from a repressive, heterochromatic state to an open accessible state in the SMARCA3 KO cells (Figure S3D). Given that distal intergenic regions often harbor cis-regulatory elements, we next asked whether chromatin decompaction in SMARCA3 KO cells facilitates accessibility to transcription factor binding motifs. Motif enrichment analysis of these regions revealed a significant overrepresentation of Forkhead and Zinc Finger transcription factor binding sites (Figure 3F). Forkhead transcription factors, such as Foxa3 and Foxo3, regulate metabolism, stress response, and longevity, while Zinc finger proteins, such as KLF1 and CTCF, are critical for erythropoiesis and chromatin architecture regulation, respectively42–44. Together, these findings suggest that loss of SMARCA3 leads to H3K9me3 depletion impacting distal regulatory elements, resulting in increased accessibility at cis-regulatory elements for transcription factor binding and altered transcriptional programs.

Chromatin architecture influences the transcriptional program in SMARCA3 knockout cellsTo evaluate the impact of these changes in chromatin accessibility on gene expression, we performed RNA-seq analysis on WT and SMARCA3 KO cells and identified significant differences between the groups, with a total of 583 genes displaying altered expression (Figure 4A, B). Motif enrichment analysis of the promoters of upregulated genes revealed significant overrepresentation of NFY and Hoxa11 transcription factor binding motifs (Figure 4C). Interestingly, NFY is a transcription factor that can function in both activation and repression of gene expression, in part by regulating chromatin accessibility at enhancer regions, while Hoxa11 is essential for cell differentiation and tissue maintenance45,46. These findings suggest that SMARCA3-dependent changes in chromatin accessibility may facilitate the binding of these factors, thereby contributing to the observed transcriptional reprogramming. Indeed, alignment of upregulated genes with ATAC-seq data indicate increased chromatin accessibility in SMARCA3 KO cells (Figure 4D, upper right quadrant; Figure 4E). To determine whether this correlation extends to enhancer elements, we curated a set of putative “active” and “inactive” enhancers in HEK293T cells using the FANTOM5 enhancer database47. We validated our enhancers classification using established histone PTMs H3K27ac and H3K4me3 for active enhancers, and H3K4me1 for inactive or “poised” enhancers (Figure S3E)48–50. We next assessed whether these enhancers overlapped with regions of increased chromatin accessibility in SMARCA3 KO cells. Consistent with our findings at promoters, active enhancers that were already accessible in WT cells exhibited increased accessibility in SMARCA3 KO cells (Figure 4F, bottom; Figure 4G, right). Similarly, inactive enhancers representing genomic regions that were previously less accessible and marked with H3K9me3 in WT cells (Figure S3E) also displayed increased accessibility in the SMARCA3 KO cells (Figure 4F, top; Figure 4E, left). Motif enrichment analysis of these enhancers revealed a significant overrepresentation of specific transcription factor binding motifs, including the previously identified NFY motif. Consistent with accessible binding sites identified at distal intergenic regions (Figure 3F), CTCF/BORIS binding sites were significantly enriched at enhancers with increased accessibility in SMARCA3 KO cells. As these factors are known regulators of chromatin looping51, our data further support the notion that SMARCA3 may potentially impact chromatin structure through its interaction with H3K9me3 (Figure 4H). Collectively, these findings suggest that SMARCA3 regulates chromatin architecture by modulating chromatin accessibility, ultimately reprogramming the cellular transcriptional landscape.

DISCUSSIONHistone ubiquitination is a critical yet understudied post-translational modification that regulates diverse cellular processes including transcription and DNA damage repair9, underscoring the importance of characterizing histone E3 ubiquitin ligases. SMARCA3 is particularly compelling in the context of colorectal cancer (CRC), as its silencing via promoter hypermethylation occurs in approximately 40% of cases24–26. Our findings uncover a previously unrecognized role for SMARCA3 in maintaining chromatin integrity through its regulation of histone modifications. We identified SMARCA3 as an E3 ligase that specifically targets histone H3 at lysine 23, expanding its functional repertoire beyond DNA replication and repair. Loss of SMARCA3 leads to a marked reduction in both H3K23Ub and H3K9me3, accompanied by widespread changes in chromatin accessibility. At the transcriptional level, SMARCA3 deficiency results in significant gene expression changes, particularly among genes implicated in CRC progression, such as FABP6, suggesting a mechanistic link between SMARCA3 loss and tumorigenesis. Histone ubiquitination is a critical yet understudied post-translational modification that regulates diverse cellular processes including transcription and DNA damage repair9, underscoring the importance of characterizing histone E3 ubiquitin ligases. SMARCA3 is particularly compelling in the context of colorectal cancer (CRC), as its silencing via promoter hypermethylation occurs in approximately 40% of cases24–26. Our findings uncover a previously unrecognized role for SMARCA3 in maintaining chromatin integrity through its regulation of histone modifications. We identified SMARCA3 as an E3 ligase that specifically targets histone H3 at lysine 23, expanding its functional repertoire beyond DNA replication and repair. Loss of SMARCA3 leads to a marked reduction in both H3K23Ub and H3K9me3, accompanied by widespread changes in chromatin accessibility. At the transcriptional level, SMARCA3 deficiency results in significant gene expression changes, particularly among genes implicated in CRC progression, such as FABP6, suggesting a mechanistic link between SMARCA3 loss and tumorigenesis. Given our in vivo findings that SMARCA3 functions as a tumor suppressor in CRC through its RING domain, we propose that its histone ubiquitination activity is central to its tumor-suppressive role.

At a mechanistic level, our data reveal a unique functional interplay between histone H3 ubiquitination and methylation mediated by SMARCA3. Specifically, SMARCA3 interacts with SUV39H1 and UHRF1, key regulators of H3K9me3, to maintain chromatin integrity. Colocalization studies, immunoprecipitation assays, and the loss of H3K9me3 in SMARCA3 KO cells collectively suggest that SMARCA3 recruitment to H3K9me3-marked chromatin helps stabilize this repressive histone modification. Notably, SMARCA3’s ubiquitination activity is enhanced in the presence of H3K9me3-marked nucleosomes, a feature shared with UHRF1, whose activity is likewise stimulated by this modification17,33. While UHRF1-mediated H3 ubiquitination activity is critical for DNMT1 recruitment, recent studies have shown that additional E3 ligases can function in parallel to support this mechanism61. UHRF1-mediated H3 ubiquitination also directly stimulates SUV39H1 methyltransferase activity as a function of proximity to H3K934. In contrast, our findings show that H3K23Ub does not enhance SUV39H1 activity, highlighting a mechanistic distinction between SMARCA3 and UHRF1 in regulating H3K9me3. Together, these observations support a model in which SMARCA3 and UHRF1 have a division of labor in the crosstalk between H3 ubiquitination and methylation. We propose that SMARCA3 is recruited, either on its own or in complex with SUV39H1 and UHRF1, to H3K9me3-marked chromatin, where it is activated to ubiquitinate H3K23, thereby stabilizing H3K9me3 (Figure 5G). Additionally, given that SMARCA3 contains an N-terminal DNA recognition domain, it may also be targeted to specific loci through sequence-specific DNA interactions62–65. Interestingly, both UHRF1 and SMARCA3 are implicated in preserving chromatin integrity during replication. UHRF1 facilitates DNMT1 recruitment to hemi-methylated DNA during S phase17,66, while SMARCA3 localizes to replication forks and promotes fork reversal in response to DNA damage18,63,64. Moreover, SMARCA3 specifically catalyzes K63-linked polyubiquitin chains (Figure 1F)67, distinct from UHRF1, which is limited to monoubiquitin14,17,33,66. K63-linked ubiquitination on histones serves as a key genomic stress signal, orchestrating the recruitment of repair factors and chromatin remodelers68. Thus, we propose that SMARCA3-mediated elongation of H3K23Ub may initiate downstream repair pathways, contributing to genome stability and integrity.

While our study establishes SMARCA3’s involvement in regulating H3K23Ub and H3K9me3, important questions remain regarding its precise recruitment to chromatin and coordination with SUV39H1. Our in vitro assays indicate that H3K9me3 can stimulate SMARCA3’s ubiquitination activity, although the precise underlying mechanism remains unclear. Furthermore, while overexpression of SUV39H1 restores H3K9me3 levels in SMARCA3 KO cells, it is uncertain whether this restoration occurs at the correct genomic loci, as SMARCA3 loss may disrupt proper SUV39H1 localization. We hypothesize that SMARCA3 might indirectly influence SUV39H1 activity by maintaining a chromatin landscape permissive to its recruitment, as suggested by SMARCA3 enrichment at H3K9me3 in cells. Additionally, the interaction between SMARCA3 and SUV39H1 may stabilize SUV39H1’s chromatin association or modulate its function through a non-enzymatic mechanism. Interestingly, recent findings show that SMARCA3 loss results in decreased LSD1 binding to chromatin69, which may further compromise H3K9me3 maintenance and overall chromatin stability. Elucidating how SMARCA3’s structural domains contribute to these phenotypes, as well as its role in genomic integrity and suppressing CRC, will be essential for fully understanding the broader implications of SMARCA3-containing interactions.

In the context of cancer, the tumor-suppressive role of SMARCA3 is strongly supported by our data demonstrating that SMARCA3 overexpression attenuates tumor growth in a partially RING-dependent manner. Although mutations in SMARCA3 are rare in CRC, its gene, HLTF , is frequently silenced through promoter hypermethylation, occurring in about 40% of cases24–26 and associated with poor prognosis70–75. Loss of SMARCA3 function has also been shown to promote genomic instability, likely contributing to the progression of benign adenomas into malignant carcinomas70. Mechanistically, our data show that SMARCA3 knockout leads to increased chromatin accessibility across promoter, intronic, and intergenic regions, reflecting widespread chromatin reorganization. Motif enrichment analysis of accessible intergenic regions revealed significant enrichment for Forkhead and Zinc Finger transcription factor binding sites. These pioneering transcription factors may facilitate oncogenic transcriptional programs in the absence of SMARCA3. Transcriptomic analysis further corroborated this notion, revealing upregulation of genes accosted with CRC progression, including those involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, epithelial-mesenchymal transition and Wnt signaling. Interestingly, despite the global chromatin decompaction observed in SMARCA3 KO cells, many genes were downregulated, suggesting that the transcriptional outcome of SMARCA3 loss are shaped by a more complex and nuanced regulatory network.

Restoring SMARCA3 function in CRC presents a promising therapeutic opportunity. SMARCA3 plays a crucial role in safeguarding genomic stability through multiple mechanisms including PCNA ubiquitination18,67, replication fork reversal62,76,77, nucleotide excision repair78, and G4 quadraplex resolution69. Loss of SMARCA3 leads to widespread chromatin decompaction, which in turn activates enhancers near oncogenes contributing to their aberrant expression. Importantly, hypermethylation of the HLTF promoter has been detected in the serum of CRC patients, highlighting its potential utility as a biomarker for disease progression72. Given that SMARCA3 overexpression rescues H3K9 methylation and suppresses tumor growth, epigenetic therapies targeting HLTF promoter hypermethylation could restore its tumor-suppressive functions. An alternative therapeutic approach may involve stabilizing the epigenetic marks regulated by SMARCA3, such as H3K9me3 and H3K23Ub. For example, pharmacological activation of SUV39H1 or the use of ubiquitin mimetic compounds could help compensate for SMARCA3 deficiency. Furthermore, SMARCA3-deficient cells exhibit heightened sensitivity to replication stress, suggesting a therapeutic window for treatments like PARP inhibitors, especially when combined with chromatin-stabilizing compounds to selectively target tumor cells. Finally, a deeper understanding of SMARCA3’s interactions with other chromatin regulators could uncover additional vulnerabilities and inform the development of innovative combination therapies in CRC.

Limitations of the studyDespite uncovering a unique role for SMARCA3 in regulating H3K23 ubiquitination and its functional connection to H3K9me3, our study has several important limitations. Chief among them is the inability to demonstrate a direct causal link between H3K23Ub and H3K9me3 in vivo. While our in vitro data show that H3K9me3 nucleosomes enhance SMARCA3 E3 ligase activity, H3K23Ub-modified nucleosomes do not stimulate SUV39H1 methyltransferase activity, suggesting an asymmetric and potentially indirect crosstalk. Testing this relationship in vivo remains challenging due to the absence of precise tools to site-specifically install or manipulate H3K23Ub. Furthermore, we were unable to fully disentangle SMARCA3’s epigenetic function from its established role in replication stress response and DNA damage repair. Although our findings support an epigenetic mechanism for its tumor suppressor activity in colorectal cancer, the contribution of its canonical DNA repair functions—and potential interplay between the two pathways—remains to be defined.

Generation of SMARCA3 knockout cells using CRISPR/Cas9The following guide RNA sequence targeting SMARCA3 was selected using the optimized CRISPR design tool (http://crispr.mit.edu): ACATCTTGGAATTCAAAACG. A plasmid composed of the guide RNA sequence cloned into pSpCas9(BB)-2A-Puro (PX459) V2.0 was obtained commercially (GenScript). HEK293T or HCT116 cells were transfected with the plasmid and grown for 72 hours to allow for Cas9/gRNA expression and gene editing to occur. Cells were then sub cultured into new plates and selected with puromycin (2–5 μg/mL final concentration) for five days. After puromycin selection, cells were expanded with non-selection media until reaching 80–90% confluence and single clones were isolated by limiting dilution. Clones were analyzed by western blot for SMARCA3 expression and putative KOs were confirmed by genomic sequencing.

Briefly, cells were resuspended in hypotonic lysis buffer (10 mM tris, 1 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, c0mplete protease inhibitors (Millipore Sigma), pH 7.5) and rotated end-over-end for 1 hour at 4 °C to promote hypotonic lysis. Histone Propionylation and DigestionAcid extracted histones were derivatized with propionic anhydride and digested as previously described80. Briefly, 50 μg of histones were resuspended in 30 μL 50 mM NH4HCO3 pH 8.0 and combined with 15 μL of a 1:3 (v/v) propionic anhydride:isopropanol (PA:IPA) mixture. The pH was rapidly readjusted to 8.0 using NH4OH, and the reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 15 minutes. An additional 22 uL of the PA:IPA mixture was added to each sample, the pH rapidly readjusted to 8.0 using NH4OH, and the reaction was incubated at 37 °C for another 15 minutes. The samples were lyophilized and propionylation was repeated to ensure complete capping of lysine residues. Propionylated histones were resuspended in 50 mM NH4HCO3 pH 8.0 to a concentration of 1 μg/μL or higher and trypsin was added in a 1:20 (w/w) ratio. Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 6 hours and quenched by lowering the pH to 3.0 using acetic acid. The newly liberated N-termini were propionylated as described above, upon which peptides were isolated and desalted using C18 reverse-phase pipet tips. Peptides were eluted in 100% acetonitrile, lyophilized, and resuspended in buffer A (0.1% formic acid) for mass spectral analysis.

Histone Propionylation and Digestion

Acid extracted histones were derivatized with propionic anhydride and digested as previously described80. Briefly, 50 μg of histones were resuspended in 30 μL 50 mM NH4HCO3 pH 8.0 and combined with 15 μL of a 1:3 (v/v) propionic anhydride:isopropanol (PA:IPA) mixture. The pH was rapidly readjusted to 8.0 using NH4OH, and the reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 15 minutes. An additional 22 uL of the PA:IPA mixture was added to each sample, the pH rapidly readjusted to 8.0 using NH4OH, and the reaction was incubated at 37 °C for another 15 minutes. The samples were lyophilized and propionylation was repeated to ensure complete capping of lysine residues. Propionylated histones were resuspended in 50 mM NH4HCO3 pH 8.0 to a concentration of 1 μg/μL or higher and trypsin was added in a 1:20 (w/w) ratio. Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 6 hours and quenched by lowering the pH to 3.0 using acetic acid. The newly liberated N-termini were propionylated as described above, upon which peptides were isolated and desalted using C18 reverse-phase pipet tips. Peptides were eluted in 100% acetonitrile, lyophilized, and resuspended in buffer A (0.1% formic acid) for mass spectral analysis.

Mass SpectrometryDigested histones were analyzed by mass spectrometry (MS) coupled online with nano-liquid chromatography (nanoLC). For histone peptide analysis, nano-liquid chromatography was coupled online with tandem mass spectrometry (nanoLC-MS/MS) as previously described81. Briefly, nanoLC was configured with a 75 μm ID x 25 cm Reprosil-Pur C18-AQ (3 μm; Dr. Maisch GmbH, Germany) nano-column packed in-house using a Dionex RSLC Ultimate 3000 (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). The HPLC gradient was as follows: 2% to 28% solvent B (A = 0.1% formic acid; B = 80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) over 45 minutes, from 28% to 80% solvent B in 5 minutes, 80% B for 10 minutes at a flowrate of 300 nL/min. The Dionex system was coupled online to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA). Data were acquired using a data-independent acquisition (DIA) method as described82, consisting of a full scan MS spectrum (m/z 300−1100) performed at 120,000 resolution (m/Δm for the ion at 200 m/z), followed by 16 MS/MS with windows of 50 m/z using HCD fragmentation and 7,500 resolution both in the orbitrap analyzer. For the analysis of PTMs, peptide abundance was normalized to total peptides. Briefly, DIA data obtained from the nanoLC-MS/MS runs were searched using EpiProfile 2.083. The peptide relative ratio was calculated using the total area under the extracted ion chromatograms of all peptides with the same amino acid sequence (including all of its modified forms) as 100%. For isobaric peptides, the relative ratio of two isobaric forms was estimated by averaging the ratio for each fragment ion with different mass between the two species.
