Erythropoiesis, the process of red blood cell production, is highly dependent on
iron uptake by transferrin and its receptor, transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1).
However, the mechanisms governing the proper recycling of TfR1 in relation to
cellular iron demands remain elusive. Here, we identify human TMEM187, a Golgi
transmembrane protein of unknown function, as a novel negative regulator of
erythropoiesis. Lack of TMEM187 in cell models initiates erythropoiesis without
the normal induction protocol and accelerates iron uptake. Following the
induction protocol, TMEM187 ablation leads to premature erythroid maturation,
resulting in early phosphatidylserine ectopia and cell membrane fragility,
hallmarks of cellular senescence that render the cells susceptible to macrophage
recognition and phagocytosis. In zebrafish embryos, tmem187 deletion leads to
enhanced early erythropoiesis, although this phenotype is later compensated,
whereas hematopoietic stem cell expression of human TMEM187 in mice, which lack
a homologous gene endogenously, results in compromised erythropoiesis and
moderate anemia. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that TMEM187 interacts with
RAB11A to restrain endosomal recycling by interfering with RAB11A-GRAB
association, involved with endosomal vesicle back to the plasma membrane, that
activates RAB11A. Consequently, TMEM187 modulates TfR1 recycling to the cell
membrane to fine-tune iron uptake efficiency for erythropoiesis. Our findings
reveal a novel modulatory pathway in which TMEM187 plays a crucial role in
regulating erythroid differentiation, maturation, and senescence, providing a
previously unexplored perspective on TMEM187's physiological function.

Introduction

Red blood cells (RBCs) are the predominant type of blood cells, with ∼5 million mature RBCs per microliter of blood in adults and an average life span of about 120 days.1 To maintain a constant level of RBCs in circulation, the bone marrow (BM) must produce nearly 200 billion erythrocytes daily, necessitating the use of over 2 × 1015 iron ions per second in adults.2 This process consumes >16 mg of the body's daily iron supply (20-25 mg),3 making erythropoiesis the most iron-intensive physiological process. Therefore, efficient iron uptake is critical to ensure that this process is functional.

The interaction between transferrin (Tf) and transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) is a significant step in iron uptake by erythrocytes.4 Tf, loaded with 2 Fe3+, first binds to TfR1, abundantly expressed on the erythrocyte surface. The Tf–Fe/TfR1 complex is then internalized by endocytosis, after which iron dissociates from Tf in early acidic endosomes.5 After reducing Fe3+ to Fe2+ and releasing it from the endosome into the cytoplasm, endosomal vesicles containing the apo-Tf/TfR1 complex are sorted and returned to the cell membrane for recycling.6,7 TfR1 also localizes autophagosomes for degradation through the autophagy–lysosomal pathway.8 It is imperative during erythropoiesis to tightly regulate TfR1-containing endosomal vesicles during differentiation to facilitate rapid recycling back to the cell surface in a functional state. However, the fine-tuning of efficient TfR1 recycling remains unclear.

The endomembrane system is an intricate and dynamic network. In most instances, macromolecular cargo can be transported between organelles by vesicle trafficking.9 The Golgi apparatus, a central organelle within the endomembrane system, consists of numerous flat vesicle-like structures that facilitate the recognition, capture, and sorting of vesicles to guarantee the accurate delivery of cargoes.10,11 This process enhances transport efficiency and helps maintain homeostasis of the endomembrane system. Prior studies have demonstrated the participation of various Golgi proteins in the TfR1 recycling pathway,12, 13, 14 indicating that the Golgi apparatus plays a significant role in the process of erythropoiesis.

TMEM187 encodes a 261-amino-acid, 6-transmembrane protein located on chromosome Xq28. The precise function of TMEM187 remains unknown. We initially became interested in TMEM187 because of its involvement in genome-wide association studies in systemic lupus erythematosus,15 but then serendipitously observed that culturing mutant cells showed a darker red cell pellet, which led us to investigate the potential relationship between TMEM187 and erythroid differentiation. We found that TMEM187, primarily localized to the trans-Golgi network, interacts with RAB11A, one of the members of the Rab (small guanine triphosphatases [GTPases]) protein family, and modulates the endosomal vesicle release from the Golgi. This process controls the rate of TfR1 recycling and iron uptake. Combining results from in vitro erythroid-like cells and in vivo studies in zebrafish and humanized mice, we identified TMEM187 as a novel negative regulator of erythropoiesis that stabilized erythroid differentiation, maturation, and senescence.

Erythrocyte maturation is accompanied by organelle removal and enucleation, hallmarks of mammalian erythrocyte maturation.26 During the terminal process, erythrocytes must eliminate enormous amounts of nuclear proteins and drastically compress the nucleus.27 Therefore, we measured nuclear size and found that the nuclear volume of KO cells was ∼30% smaller than that of WT cells after induction (Figure 2G-H), indicating that TMEM187 deficiency accelerates hemoglobin synthesis and erythrocyte maturation into a more advanced stage in K562 cells.
