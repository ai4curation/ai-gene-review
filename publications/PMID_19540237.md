# The yeast Cdc8 exhibits both deoxythymidine monophosphate and diphosphate kinase activities.

Author information:
(1)Institute of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology, National Yang-Ming
University, Taipei, Taiwan.

The existence of multifunctional enzymes in the nucleotide biosynthesis pathways
is believed to be one of the important mechanisms to facilitate the synthesis
and the efficient supply of deoxyribonucleotides for DNA replication. Here, we
used the bacterially expressed yeast thymidylate kinase (encoded by the CDC8
gene) to demonstrate that the purified Cdc8 protein possessed
thymidylate-specific nucleoside diphosphate kinase activity in addition to
thymidylate kinase activity. The yeast endogenous nucleoside diphosphate kinase
is encoded by YNK1, which appears to be non-essential. Our results suggest that
Cdc8 has dual enzyme activities and could duplicate the function of Ynk1 in
thymidylate synthesis. We also discuss the importance of the coordinated
expression of CDC8 during the cell cycle progression in yeast.

Introduction

Thymidylate kinase (EC 2.7.4.9.) is an enzyme, which phosphorylates dTMP to dTDP in the presence of ATP and magnesium. This enzyme is important in cellular DNA synthesis because the synthesis of dTTP, either via the de novo pathway or through the exogenous supply of thymidine, requires the activity of this enzyme [1]. In the budding yeast, thymidylate kinase is encoded by the CDC8 gene and is required for nuclear and mitochondrial DNA replication [2–5]. Previous study has shown that expression of yeast CDC8 peaks at G1/S transition during the cell cycle, and coordinates with expression of other DNA synthesis genes, e.g. CDC9 and CDC21, which encode DNA ligase and thymidylate synthase, respectively [6]. A single amino acid change at the residue 75 of the Cdc8 protein from glutamate to lysine creates a temperature-sensitive mutant allele of cdc8-1 that is defective in the TMPK activity [7,8]. Temperature-sensitive cdc8-1 mutants arrest cell division at the Gl/S boundary of the cell cycle at the non-permissive temperature [9,10]. Furthermore, the mutant is hypomorphic and is partially defective in meiotic and mitochondrial functions even when grown at the permissive temperature [11,12]. It has been suggested that Cdc8 not only provides precursors for DNA synthesis but also has a role in regulating the process of DNA replication [2,3,7,8].

Nucleoside diphosphate kinase (NDPK; EC 2.7.4.6), which acts at the next step after Cdc8 in the thymidylate synthesis pathway, is an enzyme that catalyzes the synthesis of various nucleoside triphosphates except ATP [13]. In the budding yeast, YNK1 gene encodes the only species of NDPK [14]. Earlier studies indicated that the yeast cell only carries one single YNK1 gene [14]. Surprisingly, disruption of YNK1 locus in a haploid strain of yeast causes no obvious growth defect, even though the NDPK activity in the YNK1 null mutant has reduced to around 10% of the wild-type cells when assayed in vitro [14]. A similar observation was also made in the ndk1 deletion mutant, a homolog of the budding yeast YNK1 in the fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe [15]. One plausible explanation is that other yeast gene products may possess NDPK activity and sustain the requirement of NDPK for cell growth. However, the identity of these genes that duplicate NDPK activity remains unknown.

To learn more about the role of Cdc8 in regulating DNA replication, we produced both recombinant GST-tagged wild type and mutant Cdc8 proteins in bacteria and studied their function in vitro. Unexpectedly, the purified wild-type Cdc8 protein was found to possess the NDPK activity in addition to its TMPK activity. The dual enzymatic Cdc8 may partially substitute for Ynk1 in sustaining the NDPK activity. We also discuss the importance of the coordinated expression of CDC8 during the cell cycle progression.

Results and discussionExpression and analysis of the recombinant Cdc8 proteinsTo study the function of the Cdc8 protein in vitro, we produced and purified the wild type and mutant Cdc8 recombinant proteins in bacteria. The estimated molecular mass of Cdc8 protein is 24.6 kDa [16]. Both the purified wild type and mutant GST-Cdc8 proteins after being eluted from GSH-beads appeared as a major band with a molecular mass of 52 kDa when analyzed on SDS-PAGE (Fig 1A), which is expected as the molecular mass of GST is 26 kDa. Purified proteins were then subjected to an analysis of thymidylate kinase activity by using [3H]-dTMP as the substrate. As shown in Figure 1B, the thymidylate kinase activity was readily detected in the reaction containing the wild-type Cdc8 protein, but not in the mutant protein. The activity found in the mutant Cdc8 was less than 0.5% of the wild type protein, even though up to 3 µg of the purified protein was used in the reaction. This is consistent with a previous result in which the crude extract prepared from both the wild type CDC8 and mutant cdc8-1 cells were compared for the TMPK activity [3]. Unexpectedly, the wild type Cdc8 protein produced not only dTDP but also dTTP in the assay (Fig. 1B). The production of dTTP was about a quarter of the amount obtained for dTDP in the assay. This result was unexpected because only the purified Cdc8 was applied in the reaction, and it prompted us to investigate whether Cdc8 contained nucleoside diphosphate kinase activity in addition to TMPK.Purification of Cdc8 on Mono Q anion-exchange chromatographyTo rule out the possibility that the expressed GST-Cdc8 could be contaminated by bacterial NDPK in the extract, two approaches were undertaken. First of all, the GSH-sepharose-purified GST-Cdc8 proteins were subjected to further purification by Mono Q anion-exchange column on FPLC (Fig. 2A). The peak of GST-Cdc8 (fractions # 9 to #13) was eluted from the column at ~100 mM NaCl. The protein collected from the peak fractions of chromatography contained only one single band when visualized on SDS-PAGE (Figure 2B). The Coomassie Blue-stained gel did not reveal any visible protein band with the molecular mass around 16.5 kDa, which is the estimated Mw of the E. coli NDPK [18], despite the fact that 3~5 µg of total purified protein from each fraction was analyzed on the gel (Fig. 2B). Again, the NDPK activity was readily detected from the most pure fractions of GST-Cdc8 (Fig. 3 and see below).Secondly, NDPKs are highly conserved in protein sequences among the proteins identified in various organisms [18]. The E. coli ndk gene encodes the bacterial NDPK which shares ~40% identity with the yeast YNK1 [14]. Polyclonal anti-Ynk1 antiserum was raised in mice against the purified Ynk1 protein and used to test if the antibody could recognize the bacterial Ndk protein. We cloned the E. coli ndk gene on a yeast expression vector and expressed it in a yeast strain in which the endogenous YNK1 gene was deleted. The yeast crude extract prepared from ndk-expressing or control yeast cells were immunoblotted and found that bacterial Ndk was efficiently recognized by anti-Ynk1 antibody (Fig. 2C, lane 2 and Lane 3). In contrast, when up to 3 µg of purified GST-Cdc8 was analyzed on the same immunoblot, not a trace of bacterial NDPK was observed (Fig. 2C, lane 1). These results suggest that purified GST-Cdc8 was not associated with any recognizable amount of bacterial NDPK, and that the yeast Cdc8 is a genuine dual enzymatic protein containing both TMPK and NDPK activities.Characterization of the TMPK and NDPK activities in Cdc8We next characterized the nucleotide kinase activities of recombinant Cdc8 in vitro. The Mono Q-purified GST-Cdc8 was used throughout the experiment. The production of dTDP and dTTP in the reaction with GST-Cdc8 were observed both in a protein dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 3A and 3B). The reaction rates appeared to be linear with increasing protein additions for the production of dTDP and dTTP (Fig. 3A). Strikingly, we noticed that the accumulation of dTTP only became apparent when the enzyme was added above 200 ng per reaction during the dose-dependent assay. It is possible that higher amounts of Cdc8 are required to activate the NDPK activity. In addition, the rate of dTTP accumulation increased linearly over time, which correlated entirely with the decrease of dTDP, suggests that a single enzyme is involved in the reaction (Fig. 3B). Because the production of dTTP depends on the formation of dTDP in the reaction, we calculated the TMPK activity based on the combination of the production of dTDP and dTTP. Therefore, the specific activity of Cdc8 for the production of dTDP (as for TMPK) was estimated at 2.9 nmole/min/mg of protein, whereas the activity for dTTP (as for NDPK) was at 1.6 nmole/min/mg of protein at 37 °C. The TMPK activity is similar to that found previously for the purified yeast Cdc8 enyzme (1.2 to 1.6 nmole/min/mg) [2].To determine the substrate specificity of Cdc8, we employed various tritium-labeled nucleoside monophosphates as substrates in the nucleotide kinase assay. As shown in Figure 4, Cdc8 was able to phosphorylate dTMP and, to a lesser extent, UMP as substrates. The activity of UMP was estimated about one-third of the activity of dTMP. None of the other nucleotides tested (AMP, GMP, or CMP) was a substrate of Cdc8 (Fig. 4). As a control, the mutant cdc8 protein was not able to phosphorylate any of the nucleotides tested (data not shown). The result suggests that Cdc8 is rather specific to the thymidylate substrate and with a much weaker activity toward uridylate.Direct phosphorylation of dTDP by Cdc8To further confirm that Cdc8 contains the NDPK activity, a direct phosphorylation of dTDP was tested. In this experiment, we adopted a strategy to obtain γ-32P-dTDP as the substrate so that the production of dTTP could be visualized by autoradiography. In the preparatory reaction, γ-32P-ATP was used as the phosphate donor in the presence of dTMP and low concentration of GST-Cdc8 (50 ng) as the enzyme. Under this condition, the primary product in the reaction was the radioactive dTDP as described above (Fig. 3A), which was scraped off from the TLC plate and extracted. The extracted γ-32P-dTDP was then used as the substrate in the direct phosphorylation reaction (Fig. 5, lane 1). As shown in the Figure 5, lane 3 and lane 4, the production of dTTP was detected in a dose-dependent manner when the wild-type Cdc8 was used as the enzyme. In contrast, up to 1 µg of the purified cdc8 mutant protein was not able to phosphorylate dTDP (Fig. 5, lane 2).The failure of mutant protein to phosphorylate dTDP further argues against the possibility that the preparation of GST-Cdc8 proteins was contaminated with bacterial NDPK, which would have been associated with both wild type and mutant GST-Cdc8 equally during the protein purification. It is noticeable that the efficiency of direct phosphorylation of dTDP by Cdc8 was poorer than when dTMP was used as the substrate. In the direct dTDP phosphorylation, only about 10% of dTDP were converted to dTTP as 1 µg of the wild-type GST-Cdc8 was used (Fig. 5, lane 4). However, nearly 40% of dTDP were successfully converted to dTTP when dTMP was used as the initial substrate with the same reaction conditions (Fig. 3). The reduced efficiency of Cdc8 in producing dTTP or UDP may explain why it was not found in studies of the purified yeast enzyme in which only 20–40ng of enzyme was used per reaction [21].We speculate that there might be a channeling mechanism existing in the Cdc8 catalytic function. In that model the enzyme preferably catalyzes the reaction from the monophosphate substrate and subsequently channels the primary products to the adjacent diphosphate kinase site. The second catalytic site would only be fully activated once the diphosphate substrates are accumulated and channeled from the primary site under a regulatory manner. The hypothesis of the existence of a less-efficient NDPK site in Cdc8 is, to some degree, consistent with our observation that the NDPK activity of Cdc8 required the presence of higher amount of protein, which could compensate for the less active enzyme sites. Furthermore, the notion that a higher amount of Cdc8 is required to achieve the second NDPK activity in vitro may not deviate much from the endogenous situation in vivo. One early cell cycle study of CDC8 has indicated that the level of CDC8 expression could extend to as many as 30-fold at the G1/S period during the cell cycle [6]. Given the small volume of yeast cell, the dramatic increase of the local concentration of Cdc8 upon the cell cycle stimulation could fulfill the requirement of protein content effectively and help to supply needed levels of dTTP. This theory awaits further investigation in the future.Earlier work has indicated that yeast cells only carry one single YNK1 gene encoding the endogenous NDPK [14]. Disruption of YNK1 locus in a haploid strain of yeast appears to have no obvious growth defect [14]. We demonstrated here that the recombinant Cdc8 protein exhibits substantial NDPK activity in vitro. It is likely that Cdc8 is only partially substituting for Ynk1 in fulfilling the NDPK function in vivo because Cdc8 has narrow substrate specificity. Other gene products such as other purine and pyrimidine monophosphate kinases in yeast may possess NDPK activity as well. Other known examples of nucleotide kinases exhibiting additional NDPK activity have been found in bacteria. For example, adenylate kinases from both E. coli and Mycobacterium tuberculosis were found to contain the NDPK activity, although the bacterial enzymes seem to possess broad substrate specificity and are able to complement the endogenous NDPK completely [22,23].The proteins involved in the pathway of thymidylate synthesis including TMPK have been favorite targets for many therapeutic drugs in the fields of cancer and other infectious diseases [24,25]. Most of these drugs take effect based on the application of various thymidine analogs, which can be converted into analogous DNA precursors through the thymidylate synthetic pathway and lead to cytotoxicity after being incorporated into host cells [24–26]. The effect of the protein structure and catalytic ability of TMPK with respect to the action of these drugs have been extensively investigated [27–29]. We have previously cloned the human TMPK gene and shown that the human protein shares ~45% identity in amino acid sequences and almost identical structure with the yeast Cdc8 [8]. Given the unique and important function of TMPK in the nucleotide metabolism, both of the human and yeast enzymes are likely to share similar catalytic characteristics. Our finding indicates that Cdc8 possessing additional NDPK activity could have an impact on the research of these chemotherapeutic agents in the future.

Both the purified wild type and mutant GST-Cdc8 proteins after being eluted from GSH-beads appeared as a major band with a molecular mass of 52 kDa when analyzed on SDS-PAGE (Fig 1A), which is expected as the molecular mass of GST is 26 kDa. Purified proteins were then subjected to an analysis of thymidylate kinase activity by using [3H]-dTMP as the substrate. As shown in Figure 1B, the thymidylate kinase activity was readily detected in the reaction containing the wild-type Cdc8 protein, but not in the mutant protein. The activity found in the mutant Cdc8 was less than 0.5% of the wild type protein, even though up to 3 µg of the purified protein was used in the reaction. This is consistent with a previous result in which the crude extract prepared from both the wild type CDC8 and mutant cdc8-1 cells were compared for the TMPK activity [3]. Unexpectedly, the wild type Cdc8 protein produced not only dTDP but also dTTP in the assay (Fig. 1B). The production of dTTP was about a quarter of the amount obtained for dTDP in the assay. This result was unexpected because only the purified Cdc8 was applied in the reaction, and it prompted us to investigate whether Cdc8 contained nucleoside diphosphate kinase activity in addition to TMPK.

Purification of Cdc8 on Mono Q anion-exchange chromatographyTo rule out the possibility that the expressed GST-Cdc8 could be contaminated by bacterial NDPK in the extract, two approaches were undertaken. First of all, the GSH-sepharose-purified GST-Cdc8 proteins were subjected to further purification by Mono Q anion-exchange column on FPLC (Fig. 2A). The peak of GST-Cdc8 (fractions # 9 to #13) was eluted from the column at ~100 mM NaCl. The protein collected from the peak fractions of chromatography contained only one single band when visualized on SDS-PAGE (Figure 2B). The Coomassie Blue-stained gel did not reveal any visible protein band with the molecular mass around 16.5 kDa, which is the estimated Mw of the E. coli NDPK [18], despite the fact that 3~5 µg of total purified protein from each fraction was analyzed on the gel (Fig. 2B). Again, the NDPK activity was readily detected from the most pure fractions of GST-Cdc8 (Fig. 3 and see below).Secondly, NDPKs are highly conserved in protein sequences among the proteins identified in various organisms [18]. The E. coli ndk gene encodes the bacterial NDPK which shares ~40% identity with the yeast YNK1 [14]. Polyclonal anti-Ynk1 antiserum was raised in mice against the purified Ynk1 protein and used to test if the antibody could recognize the bacterial Ndk protein. We cloned the E. coli ndk gene on a yeast expression vector and expressed it in a yeast strain in which the endogenous YNK1 gene was deleted. The yeast crude extract prepared from ndk-expressing or control yeast cells were immunoblotted and found that bacterial Ndk was efficiently recognized by anti-Ynk1 antibody (Fig. 2C, lane 2 and Lane 3). In contrast, when up to 3 µg of purified GST-Cdc8 was analyzed on the same immunoblot, not a trace of bacterial NDPK was observed (Fig. 2C, lane 1). These results suggest that purified GST-Cdc8 was not associated with any recognizable amount of bacterial NDPK, and that the yeast Cdc8 is a genuine dual enzymatic protein containing both TMPK and NDPK activities.

Characterization of the TMPK and NDPK activities in Cdc8We next characterized the nucleotide kinase activities of recombinant Cdc8 in vitro. The Mono Q-purified GST-Cdc8 was used throughout the experiment. The production of dTDP and dTTP in the reaction with GST-Cdc8 were observed both in a protein dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 3A and 3B). The reaction rates appeared to be linear with increasing protein additions for the production of dTDP and dTTP (Fig. 3A). Strikingly, we noticed that the accumulation of dTTP only became apparent when the enzyme was added above 200 ng per reaction during the dose-dependent assay. It is possible that higher amounts of Cdc8 are required to activate the NDPK activity. In addition, the rate of dTTP accumulation increased linearly over time, which correlated entirely with the decrease of dTDP, suggests that a single enzyme is involved in the reaction (Fig. 3B). Because the production of dTTP depends on the formation of dTDP in the reaction, we calculated the TMPK activity based on the combination of the production of dTDP and dTTP. Therefore, the specific activity of Cdc8 for the production of dTDP (as for TMPK) was estimated at 2.9 nmole/min/mg of protein, whereas the activity for dTTP (as for NDPK) was at 1.6 nmole/min/mg of protein at 37 °C. As shown in Figure 4, Cdc8 was able to phosphorylate dTMP and, to a lesser extent, UMP as substrates. The activity of UMP was estimated about one-third of the activity of dTMP. None of the other nucleotides tested (AMP, GMP, or CMP) was a substrate of Cdc8 (Fig. 4). As a control, the mutant cdc8 protein was not able to phosphorylate any of the nucleotides tested (data not shown). The result suggests that Cdc8 is rather specific to the thymidylate substrate and with a much weaker activity toward uridylate.

The extracted γ-32P-dTDP was then used as the substrate in the direct phosphorylation reaction (Fig. 5, lane 1). In the direct dTDP phosphorylation, only about 10% of dTDP were converted to dTTP as 1 µg of the wild-type GST-Cdc8 was used (Fig. 5, lane 4). The second catalytic site would only be fully activated once the diphosphate substrates are accumulated and channeled from the primary site under a regulatory manner. The hypothesis of the existence of a less-efficient NDPK site in Cdc8 is, to some degree, consistent with our observation that the NDPK activity of Cdc8 required the presence of higher amount of protein, which could compensate for the less active enzyme sites. Furthermore, the notion that a higher amount of Cdc8 is required to achieve the second NDPK activity in vitro may not deviate much from the endogenous situation in vivo.
