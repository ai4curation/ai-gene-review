The fly eye is composed of hundreds of ommatidia, each containing a stereotyped
pattern of photoreceptors and assorted support cells. Over the past 50 years,
molecular genetic studies have illuminated how the fates of many of these cells
are specified. As a result, the field has become a classic paradigm for
cell-fate specification during animal development. The initial focus of the
studies was the sevenless (sev) mutant, in which the cell normally destined to
become the R7 photoreceptor instead develops into a lens-secreting support cell.
This phenotype served as the entry point into the patterning mechanisms
underlying ommatidial fate specification. sev was found to encode a receptor
tyrosine kinase (RTK) expressed on the plasma membrane of the R7 precursor. Its
ligand-Bride of Sevenless (Boss)-was then identified as a plasma membrane
protein expressed on a neighboring cell, the presumptive R8 photoreceptor. Here,
I present a personal perspective on the studies of Sev, from its discovery to
the present day, beginning with the characterization of its loss-of-function
phenotype and culminating in the highly detailed description of its biological
role and the molecular mechanism by which it transduces the R8-to-R7 Boss
signal. These studies have revealed surprisingly complex and novel properties of
this seemingly simple signaling mechanism, including (i) the unexpected
circuitry by which Sev signaling is integrated with that of the Drosophila EGF
receptor and Notch to specify 3 distinct cell types (R7 and R1/6 photoreceptors
and the lens-secreting cone cells) and (ii) the likely discovery of a new
molecular mechanism of RTK activation in which transendocytosis of Boss from the
presumptive R8 into the R7 precursor directs Sev to a select endocytic
compartment, where it is activated by a conformation state induced by low pH.
Further, I address evolutionary studies of Sev and Boss, which indicate that
their roles as ligand and receptor are restricted to flies, and pose the
intriguing question of whether R7 photoreceptors outside Diptera are specified
by similar signaling logic but different molecular machinery.

Other studies examined the patterning of adult tissues as it occurred in their progenitor epithelia, addressing more classical aspects of developmental biology. One of these, the fly eye, blossomed into a model system for how intercellular signals direct cell-fate specification.

I chose to work on the eye after reading Ready et al. (1976), a seminal paper that described the following salient features of the fly eye and its development. First, the fly eye is compound, composed of hundreds of ommatidia; this was a data-amplifying feature for studies of ommatidial formation, as scores could be examined in a single preparation. Second, in each ommatidium, photoreceptors and support cells are arrayed in a crystal-like manner, allowing individual cells to be readily identified. We could examine a specific cell position within a developing ommatidium and determine which cell type occupies that position under different experimental conditions. Third, during the late larval stage in the eye imaginal disc, a pattern-initiating wave flowed from posterior to anterior, leaving in its wake a smoothly graded temporal series of developing ommatidia. In single-disc preparations, ommatidia could be assessed at progressive developmental stages. Fourth, mosaic analysis could be performed in adult eyes. A mutant gene could be linked to the absence of a pigment marker, and mixtures of pigmented and unpigmented cells could then be evaluated to determine which cells required the gene's function for the ommatidium to develop correctly.

Cell-autonomy tests proved very powerful in breaking open the molecular genetics underlying pattern formation. Initially, they could be performed only in adult eyes using the pigment-marking method, but later technological advances enabled their use in eye disc tissues as well. Mosaic analysis could determine whether the gene function was required in cells that exhibit the aberrant behavior, indicating a cell-autonomous effect consistent with an error in the receipt or interpretation of positional signals. In contrast, if the gene function were required in cells other than those that manifest the phenotype, this would indicate an error in the production or release of positional signals.

By the early 1980s, it was evident that positional information determined the fates of ommatidial cells and that cell–cell signaling likely played a major role in this process. A number of mutants that disrupted ommatidial development were already available, one of which, sevenless (sev), showed the precise absence of the R7 photoreceptor from each ommatidium of the eye. It proved to be the tool we needed to break open the cell–cell signaling that controls ommatidial fate specification. It was found to encode a plasma membrane receptor required in the R7 precursor to direct the cell to its appropriate developmental fate. Initially, it appeared that Sev mediated a simple signal that specified the R7 fate, but later work revealed that it was part of a multicell signaling program with cross-communicating transduction pathways in the R7 precursor.

This Perspective documents how sev was used to unlock the cell–cell signaling active in the developing ommatidium and how, as that work progressed, its many unusual features emerged. The first part describes how molecular genetic experiments defined the precise role of Sev in R7 specification. Later sections describe its structure and evolution and discuss the roles it may play in other insects and, more widely, in other animals.

Models of fate specification in the developing fly eyeThe absence of R7 from the adult sev ommatidia begged the question of the developmental defect that caused it. At the time (the late 1970s), there were competing models for cell specification during ommatidial development. The Benzer lab favored positional information as the primary determinant of cell types, with naive cells receiving fate-specifying signals from the cells they contacted (Ready et al. 1976). A contrary view held that separate lineage mechanisms generated photoreceptor and support cell precursors and that these prespecified cells then assembled into ommatidia (Hofbauer and Campos-Ortega 1976). From this latter perspective, sev was viewed as a mutant lacking the R7 lineage, leaving no prespecified R7 photoreceptors to occupy R7 positions (Campos-Ortega et al. 1979). Thereafter, the role of lineage in ommatidial cell terminal fate assignments was convincingly negated by analysis of small, infrequent clones (Lawrence and Green 1979). By default, positional information became the assumed operative agency directing ommatidial cell fates. The questions then became: what positional information specifies R7, and what goes wrong in the sev mutation?

Identification of Boss—the Sev ligand

In screens for sev-like mutations, the bride of sevenless (boss) gene was identified (Reinke and Zipursky 1988). boss was a healthy, fertile mutant with a single overt phenotype: the absence of the R7 photoreceptor—the exact same phenotype of sev. Mosaic analysis defined boss as a non–cell-autonomous mutation: R7 cells could form in the absence of the boss gene product. Rather, boss was required in the R8 precursor for the specification of R7 (Reinke and Zipursky 1988). Excitement was palpable again; did the boss encode the ligand for Sev? Again, the answer was yes.

Boss encodes an integral plasma membrane proteinMolecular characterization of boss revealed it to encode a protein with 7 TMs, a large (∼500-residue) ECD and a small ICD, structurally similar to a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) (Hart et al. 1990; Cagan et al. 1992). Boss antibodies showed it expressed on the R8 plasma membrane and strikingly, also within MVBs of the R7 precursor, indicating that it was transendocytosed thither from the R8 plasma membrane (Krämer et al. 1991; Cagan et al. 1992) (Fig. 3). This translocation was Sev dependent and suggested that as Sev was internalized into the R7 precursor, the Boss to which it was bound was carried along: first into small internal compartments and later accumulating in MVBs (Fig. 4a–c). This suggested that Boss and Sev might engage with each other directly. Corroboration came from tissue culture experiments in which cells expressing Boss bound to others expressing Sev. Tyrosine phosphorylation of RTK ICDs is the molecular signature of their activation, and the binding of Sev and Boss-expressing cells induced Sev ICD phosphorylation (Krämer et al. 1991). Thus, not only did Boss and Sev appear to physically interact, but the interaction correlated with Sev's activation. This, together with the mosaic data, provided compelling evidence that Boss, expressed on the surface of R8, acted as the ligand for Sev on the plasma membrane of the R7 precursor. Furthermore, an activated form of Sev specified R7s in a boss mutant, indicating that Boss is dispensable once Sev is activated (Basler et al. 1991). Boss expression is also observed later in all photoreceptors, beginning at 48 h after pupa formation, but since the boss mutant has no eye phenotype other than the loss of R7, its role there is unclear (Krämer et al. 1991).The identification of Boss and Sev as ligand and receptor was a significant achievement; the binding of 2 plasma membrane proteins on adjacent cells incarnated the relay of positional information from one cell to another.

When the R7 precursor joins the cluster, it already expresses the transcription factor Tramtrack (Ttk), which represses the photoreceptor fate. The role of Sev is to remove Ttk, and it achieves this by triggering a series of protein interactions at the plasma membrane that culminate in the phosphorylation and activation of MAPK, which translocates to the nucleus. There, activated MAPK promotes transcription of the phyllopod (phyl) gene, which encodes an adaptor protein that brings an ubiquitin ligase complex to Ttk. This degrades Ttk, and the R7 precursor is specified as a photoreceptor.

At the time, a view emerged that this explained R7 specification and that the problem had been solved. But this was far from the case; it failed to explain many key questions. If removal of Ttk allowed the R7 precursor to become a photoreceptor, what directed it to be an R7 rather than an R1/6 type? What happened in the R1/6 photoreceptors? Did they express Ttk, and if they did, what removed it to allow their specification as photoreceptors? The answers to these questions awaited an understanding of the roles of the Drosophila EGF receptor (DER) and Notch signaling pathways in ommatidial cell-fate specification.

The Drosophila EGF receptorDER is an RTK that is active at multiple stages and in many tissues during fly development (Schejter and Shilo 1989). In the developing eye, it is ubiquitously expressed. As work on Sev progressed, a role for DER in general photoreceptor development became apparent, and it appeared that Sev and DER activate the same MAPK signaling pathways (Freeman 1996). This was corroborated by a chimerical receptor in which the ICD of Sev was replaced by that of DER, which fully rescued R7 specification in the sev mutant (Tomlinson et al. 2019). (The reciprocal experiment to determine whether the Sev ICD can substitute for the DER ICD has not yet been performed.) Although they activate the same MAPK pathway, activated Sev promotes hyperactivation of the pathway, whereas activated DER induces a significantly milder level of transduction. These activation levels correlate with their expression levels; Sev is expressed at high levels, whereas DER is found at much lower levels. When DER was expressed at Sev levels, it hyperactivated the pathway (Tomlinson et al. 2019). The importance of these different attributes will be addressed later.There are many DER ligands, but in the developing ommatidia, Spitz (Spi) (Schweitzer et al. 1995) alone appears to supply this function (Tio and Moses 1997). It is ubiquitously expressed in the developing retina, but a cleavage event promoted by Rhomboid-class proteases is required for its activation (Urban et al. 2002). Accordingly, the Rhomboid expression patterns determine which cells release an active DER ligand. Spi is a diffusible peptide that activates DER first in neighboring cells and then spreads to more distant cells over time. This contrasts with Boss, which, as an integral membrane protein, activates Sev only in its immediate neighbors. As is detailed later, this distinction plays a critical role in R7 specification.

The Notch transduction pathwayNotch (N) is a plasma membrane receptor (Wharton et al. 1985; Kidd et al. 1986) that plays critical roles in a wide range of cell-fate decisions during fly development. It is ubiquitously expressed and activated by transmembrane ligands from neighboring cells. Delta is the ligand in the developing ommatidia (Parks et al. 1995), and when it binds N in its neighbors, it induces a cleavage that releases the N ICD from the plasma membrane. N plays at least 3 roles in R7 specification (Tomlinson et al. 2011), and when its function is lost or activated, all 3 roles are correspondingly modulated. This made identification of these roles challenging. Yet, the combination of effective fly genetics and the developing eye's facility for cell-fate analysis allowed the clear separation of the 3 functions. They are described in the next section.

The insulin-related receptor is also pH sensitive; alkalinity is required for its activation (Scapin et al. 2018). But this is supplied by the extracellular fluid in which the protein's ECD resides, and since low pH is typically found only in intracellular compartments, we infer that Sev is directed to an intracellular compartment for activation.

The model of Sev activation

The following model accommodates the many features of Boss and Sev described so far and provides a coherent account of how Sev activation is achieved in the R7 precursor:

Sev is expressed at high levels on the plasma membranes of many cells, including the R7 precursor. The ambient high pH is not conducive to oligomerization, and the Sev α/b resting structure (Fig. 6b) does not dimerize and remains inert.

When Sev is not bound to Boss, it is internalized by standard endocytosis, which does not direct it to the low-pH compartment, and Sev remains in the resting, inert state.

When Sevenless in the R7 plasma membrane binds to Boss on R8, a protein bridge is formed between the 2 cells, which prevents standard endocytosis from internalizing the Sev/Boss complex into the R7 precursor. Instead, a phagocytic-like process substitutes, dragging Boss and its associated plasma membrane into the Sev-expressing cell. This specialized internalization route directs the Boss/Sev complex to a low-pH compartment, where Boss dissociates and the Sev α/β structure dimerizes, thereby bringing its 2 intracellular kinase domains together, promoting mutual phosphorylation and activation.

The Sev-expressing cell requires the presence of the transcription factor Lz to allow the phagocytosis of the Boss/Sev complex. In its absence, the Sev–Boss protein bridge remains “frozen” at the R7/8 interface, and Sev stays inactive. The Lz target genes that regulate this process remain unknown.

Sev and Boss localize to the apical plasma membraneA prominent feature of Sev and Boss is that they are found on the apical plasma membranes of their expressing cells, above (apical to) the adherens junctions (Banerjee et al. 1987; Tomlinson et al. 1987; Krämer et al. 1991). The Boss/Sev protein interactions thus occur between the cells’ apical microvilli. The reason for the apical localization of Boss and Sev is unknown, but it may relate to the phagocytosis-like mechanism that imports the Boss/Sev complex into the R7 precursor and underlies Sev activation. In this regard, we expressed human ROS1 (the Sev ortholog) under sev transcriptional control (it showed no evident phenotype) and observed that it localizes to basolateral membranes (not shown). It may be that ROS1 is normally expressed apically, but, as a human protein, it lacks the apical targeting signals required in the fly. Alternatively, it may represent a major difference between Sev and ROS1—the former localizes to the apical plasma membrane, whereas the latter is targeted basolaterally. Sev and Boss localize to the apical plasma membrane

A prominent feature of Sev and Boss is that they are found on the apical plasma membranes of their expressing cells, above (apical to) the adherens junctions (Banerjee et al. 1987; Tomlinson et al. 1987; Krämer et al. 1991). The Boss/Sev protein interactions thus occur between the cells’ apical microvilli. The reason for the apical localization of Boss and Sev is unknown, but it may relate to the phagocytosis-like mechanism that imports the Boss/Sev complex into the R7 precursor and underlies Sev activation. In this regard, we expressed human ROS1 (the Sev ortholog) under sev transcriptional control (it showed no evident phenotype) and observed that it localizes to basolateral membranes (not shown). Sev has an internally displaced signal peptideWhen Sev was first characterized, 2 hydrophobic regions were identified as potential TM domains. One was the canonical TM domain that separated the ICD from the ECD. The other was close (∼60 aa) to the N-terminus (Fig. 6a). This suggested that Sev was a unique RTK, with its N- and C-terminal domains in the cytosol and a large extracellular loop extending between the 2 TM domains (Simon et al. 1989). Later, however, the N-terminal hydrophobic domain was shown to be an internally displaced signal peptide, and Sev was restored to the canonical single TM-domain status (Fig. 6a) (Tomlinson et al. 2019). The internally displaced Sev signal peptide is conserved in Drosophilids and some other Dipterans, but has not been detected in other animals. Interestingly, the Drosophila insulin receptor has a similarly displaced signal sequence (Fernandez et al. 1995), but its function here or in Sev remains unknown.

Boss is both the Sev ligand and a GPCRWhen Boss was first identified, it appeared to be a GPCR-type protein with 7 TMs. It was assumed to be an archaic, defunct GPCR that had been co-opted to provide its N-terminus as the Sev ligand. Later, Boss was shown to act as a bona fide GPCR, regulating the nutritional state of the hemolymph in the fat body (Kohyama-Koganeya et al. 2008). It belongs to the metabotropic glutamate-like, class C, GPCR family, which typically has a large ligand-binding N-terminal ECD organized into a bilobed venus fly trap (VFT) structure, which closes around and constrains the ligand. The Boss N-terminus is a VFT-like bilobed structure (Cerutti et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025), but is sufficiently divergent to suggest a different mode of ligand binding. There are 2 such binding proteins to consider: Sev and the ligand (possibly glucose), which relays the nutritional state of the hemolymph. How, then, did evolution maintain the Boss VFT domain that could bind to 2 distinct ligands? One possibility is that each ligand binds to different regions of the VFT structure. Indeed, Sev binds to the membrane-proximal lobe, leaving the membrane-distal domain available for interactions with other ligands. This possibility awaits identification and the binding features of the canonical ligand.In standard GPCRs, the VFT domain connects to the first TM domain via a flexible linker that characteristically contains many cysteine residues. This domain is absent in Boss (Cerutti et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025), and the VFT structure connects directly to the first TM domain. Gabriele Cerutti has observed that when Sev binds to Boss, it is thus likely pressed against R8, which may induce an outward bulging of its plasma membrane and initiate phagocytosis by the R7 precursor.

Across the Diptera, Sev and Boss homologs are evident, but no experiments have been conducted to assess their roles in R7 specification (Bao and Friedrich 2008). The ligand for ROS1 is a secreted proteinROS1 in vertebrates has been best studied in rodents, where it is expressed in many tissues, including epithelial cells of the seminiferous tubules. As sperm pass through the tubules, proteases secreted by epithelial cells direct their maturation to full motility. A similar phenotype occurs in the absence of Nell2, a protein secreted into the seminiferous fluid that binds ROS1 (Kiyozumi et al. 2020). These data argue that NELL2 is a ligand for ROS1 and, as a secreted protein, contrasts with the membrane-bound ligand for Sev, Boss. The earlier descriptions of Sev activation highlight the critical role of Boss's membrane-bound nature. Yet if Nell2 is the ROS1 ligand, as a secreted protein, it will not support the activation mode inferred for Sev. Herein lies a mystery. Structural similarities suggest that Sev and Ros1 have conserved molecular functions, but their ligand profiles argue otherwise.

The Boss/Sev signaling module appears unique to DipteraAs work on the development of the Drosophila ommatidium unfolded, a naive view emerged that the patterning uncovered would be common to all insects. In terms of R7 specification, the divergent features of the Sev and ROS1 ligands have undermined this assumption, as have the expanded roles of ROS1 in butterfly and beetle photoreceptor specification (see above). We now suspect that the Boss/Sev signaling module is unique to Diptera, and that the extent to which other patterning features of the fly ommatidium are conserved throughout insects is therefore also called into question. The dipteran lineage diverged from other insects ∼200 million years ago, and if the roles of Boss/Sev are indeed unique to R7 specification in flies, then we infer a change in ommatidial patterning that occurred at the point of divergence. The role of Sev in Drosophila is clearly defined; it hyperactivates the RTK pathway in the R7 precursor to overcome a blockade on phyl transcription imposed by N activity. Does the absence of the Boss/Sev module in other insects suggest that the N blockade does not occur in their R7 precursors? We do not know, but there is nothing evidently different between the ommatidia of Diptera and those of other insects that cries out for a unique R7 specification mechanism.

This contrasts with the many other proteins involved in eye cell-fate specification. ConclusionWhen sev was first identified, we knew only that it lacked an R7 photoreceptor. We now have a broad understanding of cell-fate specification in the fly eye, which has, in turn, become a paradigm for patterning within a developing epithelium. We understand how the geometries of cell contacts play critical roles in pattern formation and how different types of positional information are supplied by diffusing vs membrane-bound ligands. We have learned that an apparently simple fate decision (the specification of R7) may be intrinsically complicated. Initially, Sev activation alone appeared necessary for R7 specification. Then it became clear that N was also active in the process. Thereafter, dissection of the 2 pathways revealed their complex, ramifying interactions that ultimately resolve into 2 distinct fate directives: (i) become a photoreceptor and (ii) become an R7 type. Within these interactions, N activity both opposes and promotes the Sev pathway. This was counterintuitive and completely unexpected and was only detected because of the power of fly genetics and the facility of the eye for these studies. It stands as a salutary warning for cell-fate studies in other tissues and organisms.As the roles played by Sev in R7 specification progressively became clear, its many unusual features were revealed.
