Platelet production, or thrombopoiesis, is a critical process involving the
differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells into megakaryocytes, which release
platelets into circulation. This study employed a comprehensive screening
approach through a piggyback knockdown strategy targeting 394 protein-encoding
genes expressed explicitly in young thrombocytes. This approach led us to
identify eight candidate genes associated with thrombopoiesis, including spi1b,
a transcription factor that potentially regulates thrombocyte development. The
sequencing of spi1b mutant progeny harboring a termination codon after Arg254
within the conserved ETS transcription factor domain confirmed the lethality of
homozygous mutations, highlighting the essential role of Spi1b in embryonic
development. Comparative analysis revealed homology between zebrafish Spi1b and
human SPI1, suggesting evolutionary conservation of thrombopoiesis regulatory
mechanisms. Additionally, analysis of spi1b knockdown zebrafish and the mutant
demonstrated increased bleeding, further emphasizing the importance of spi1b in
maintaining hemostasis. Our study provides novel insights into the regulatory
networks governing thrombopoiesis and identifies Spi1b as a critical regulator
of young thrombocyte development in zebrafish. Further investigations into the
functional roles of identified genes in thrombocyte biology may elucidate
mechanisms underlying thrombopoiesis and inform therapeutic strategies for
bleeding disorders.

Introduction

Mammalian platelets are small anucleate cell fragments released into the bloodstream from megakaryocytes (MKs) by thrombopoiesis. Inside the bone marrow, MKs originate from the differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and mature into large, polyploid cells1. Platelets are mainly involved in primary hemostasis and thrombus formation. At the injury site, activated platelets aggregate to form a plug, resulting in primary hemostasis. Platelet abnormalities, including thrombocytopenia characterized by a low platelet count, can lead to bleeding disorders2,3. The regulation of thrombopoiesis involves multiple transcription factors (TFs), critical mechanisms for platelet production. These processes are tightly regulated, and any disruption in these mechanisms impairs platelet production and release. Several studies have shown that large numbers of platelet production and release from MKs depend on lineage-specific TFs, including GATA-1, NF-E2, FLI-1, and FOG-14. However, we do not know whether there are more TFs and other factors involved in MKs differentiation and platelet production4,5.

Zebrafish thrombocytes are functionally similar to mammalian platelets on one side. On the other hand, several TFs that regulate the development and maturation of MK are also present in these thrombocytes6,7. Various platelet receptors, such as PARs, GPIIb/IIIa, and GPIb, have been identified in zebrafish thrombocytes. Additionally, thrombocytes respond to platelet agonists like collagen, ristocetin, and arachidonic acid, consistent with vertebrate platelets8,9. Like humans with young and mature platelets, we identified two thrombocyte populations in zebrafish using DiI-C18 (DiI) labeling and Fli-1 GFP fish10,11. Notably, zebrafish young thrombocytes have shown to be more active and have more receptor content than mature thrombocytes11. Also, they are the first to appear in blood circulation and respond to arterial injury11. We also showed a loss of GATA1 expression and a gain of Fli-1 expression in mature thrombocytes10. In addition to Fli-1, Nfe2 has been shown to regulate thrombopoiesis12. Our study identified six Hox genes acting as positive regulators, with Hoxc11b identified as a negative regulator, and its expression is also lost in mature thrombocytes13.

In our previous study, single-cell lipid analysis revealed differences in the phosphatidylethanolamine to phosphatidylcholine ratio between young and mature thrombocytes14. To investigate thrombocyte maturation, we conducted single-cell RNA sequencing on young and mature zebrafish thrombocytes, uncovering distinct gene expression patterns and identifying several genes shared with megakaryocytes6. This suggests thrombocytes have traits of both megakaryocytes and platelets, making them a useful model for studying platelet or megakaryocyte maturation. Zebrafish, with about 70% genetic similarity to humans, are a valuable model for studying transcription factors and factors involved in MK development15. We recently identified 397 genes exclusively expressed in young thrombocytes6.

In this paper, in a primary screen, we knocked down 394 protein-encoded genes uniquely expressed in young thrombocytes identified by single-cell RNA-seq data, which are lost during thrombocyte maturation in zebrafish. Subsequently, we conducted a secondary screening using individual piggyback knockdown to identify the most promising candidate genes. We hypothesized that knocking down uniquely expressed genes in young thrombocytes would impact the differentiation and development of young thrombocytes that may affect maturation. We found that among the 394 genes, knocking down si: ch73-359m17.9, si: dkey-24c2.9, si: dkey-24p1.1, spi1b, spi1a, uncx4.1, upf3a, and wars1 genes resulted in a significant reduction in young thrombocytes in zebrafish. Notably, spi1b, spi1a, and uncx4.1 were the only transcription factor genes identified among these genes. These results provide the basis for investigating their role in thrombocyte development in the future.

Zebrafish cultivation and breeding

Adult wild-type (WT) zebrafish were purchased from Ekk-Will Tropical Fish Farm, Gibsonton, FL, while GloFish were sourced from Spectrum Brands (Blacksburg, VA). The adult spi1b-sa16105 mutant progeny generated from crossing heterozygotes were obtained from the Zebrafish International Resource Center (ZIRC). The zebrafish were kept in a recirculating system of deionized water enriched with Instant Ocean, maintaining a pH of 7.6 and a temperature of 28 °C. They were subjected to a 14:10 h light-dark cycle and provided live brine shrimp and flakes as their diet.

For breeding purposes, adult males and females were introduced into a breeding tank and kept apart overnight with a divider. The following day, the divider was removed once the lights were on to allow the fish to mate and lay eggs. Fertilized eggs were then collected and transferred into clean plastic cups filled with E3 medium (5 mM NaCl, 0.33 mM CaCl2, 0.17 mM KCl, and 0.33 mM MgSO4, pH 7.2, supplemented with 0.1% methylene blue) and incubated at 28 °C. The hatched larvae were utilized for subsequent experiments.

DNA extraction from zebrafish adults and larvaeFor genomic DNA isolation, either the zebrafish tail clipped using scissors or an individual larva was placed into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube containing 50 µl of DNA extraction buffer (10 mM Tris pH 8.5, 0.01% gelatin, 50 mM KCl, 0.45% Tween20, 0.45% NP-40, and 5 mM EDTA). Subsequently, 0.5 µl of proteinase K (20 mg/mL) from NEB, Ipswich, MA, was added to the mixture, which was then incubated overnight at 55 °C. Following this incubation, the samples were transferred into a 96 °C water bath for 10 min to deactivate the proteinase K. The samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm at room temperature, and the supernatant containing the genomic DNA was carefully transferred to a new 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube. These samples were then stored at −20 °C for subsequent mutant screening and genotyping purposes.

Piggyback knockdownZebrafish cDNA sequences for 394 genes uniquely expressed in young thrombocytes compared to mature thrombocytes were obtained from the Ensembl Genome Browser. Using Primer 3 software, a specific antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) sequence for each gene comprising 25 nucleotides was selected within the coding sequence. These primers were then organized into 132 sets. For every set of 3 mRNAs, 3 ASOs were designed (Table 1). At the 3’-ends of these ASOs, oligonucleotide sequences 5′-TATAAAT-3′, 5′-TAACTGA-3′, and 5′-TAAGAGG-3′ that are complementary to the Vivo Morpholino (VMO), 5′-CCTCTTACCTCAGTTACAATTTATA-3′, were respectively added for a simultaneous 3-gene knockdown. For a single gene knockdown, 5′-TATAAATTGTAACTG-3′ was added to each ASO at their 3’-end. For ruling out off-target effects, a separate set of new ASOs was used with the addition of 5′-TATAAATTGTAACTG-3′ to each ASO at their 3’-end (Table 2). The oligonucleotides were purchased from Fisher Scientific, Grand Island. NY, in 96-well array format or as individual tubes and VMO was obtained from Gene Tools LLC, headquartered in Philomath, OR.

Piggyback knockdown

Zebrafish cDNA sequences for 394 genes uniquely expressed in young thrombocytes compared to mature thrombocytes were obtained from the Ensembl Genome Browser. Using Primer 3 software, a specific antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) sequence for each gene comprising 25 nucleotides was selected within the coding sequence. These primers were then organized into 132 sets. For every set of 3 mRNAs, 3 ASOs were designed (Table 1). At the 3’-ends of these ASOs, oligonucleotide sequences 5′-TATAAAT-3′, 5′-TAACTGA-3′, and 5′-TAAGAGG-3′ that are complementary to the Vivo Morpholino (VMO), 5′-CCTCTTACCTCAGTTACAATTTATA-3′, were respectively added for a simultaneous 3-gene knockdown. For a single gene knockdown, 5′-TATAAATTGTAACTG-3′ was added to each ASO at their 3’-end. For ruling out off-target effects, a separate set of new ASOs was used with the addition of 5′-TATAAATTGTAACTG-3′ to each ASO at their 3’-end (Table 2). The oligonucleotides were purchased from Fisher Scientific, Grand Island. NY, in 96-well array format or as individual tubes and VMO was obtained from Gene Tools LLC, headquartered in Philomath, OR.

ResultsIn our earlier study, we discovered 397 genes that are specifically expressed in young thrombocytes of zebrafish, identified through RNAseq analysis using 10x genomic sequencing (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA753153; A comparative analysis of the transcriptional profiles of the young and mature zebrafish thrombocytes). Out of these 397 genes, 3 were RNA coding genes, and the rest 394 genes were protein coding genes6. To screen and identify genes in thrombopoiesis, especially those affecting the production of young thrombocytes, we employed a piggyback approach to knock down these 394 genes, aiming to classify potential genes involved in the maturation of zebrafish thrombocytes. To accomplish this goal, we prepared VMO-ASO hybrids containing ASOs for three genes for a set. Thus, we prepared for the primary screening, 132 sets to perform 3-gene knockdowns. Subsequently, we intravenously injected each hybrid set of primers into six fish, while 1x PBS was used to inject a daily control. 48 h post-injection, blood was collected from these fish, and DiI was added to the whole blood sample to label young thrombocytes selectively. The sample was then placed on a flow cytometer, and the DiI-labeled thrombocytes were counted and their percentage calculated. The initial knockdown screening revealed that out of the 132 sets, 11 sets showed a decrease in the percentage of DiI-labeled cells, whereas 3 sets yielded an increase in these thrombocytes compared to the daily controls (Fig. 1).

In our earlier study, we discovered 397 genes that are specifically expressed in young thrombocytes of zebrafish, identified through RNAseq analysis using 10x genomic sequencing (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA753153; A comparative analysis of the transcriptional profiles of the young and mature zebrafish thrombocytes). Out of these 397 genes, 3 were RNA coding genes, and the rest 394 genes were protein coding genes6. To screen and identify genes in thrombopoiesis, especially those affecting the production of young thrombocytes, we employed a piggyback approach to knock down these 394 genes, aiming to classify potential genes involved in the maturation of zebrafish thrombocytes. To accomplish this goal, we prepared VMO-ASO hybrids containing ASOs for three genes for a set. Thus, we prepared for the primary screening, 132 sets to perform 3-gene knockdowns. Subsequently, we intravenously injected each hybrid set of primers into six fish, while 1x PBS was used to inject a daily control. 48 h post-injection, blood was collected from these fish, and DiI was added to the whole blood sample to label young thrombocytes selectively. The sample was then placed on a flow cytometer, and the DiI-labeled thrombocytes were counted and their percentage calculated. The initial knockdown screening revealed that out of the 132 sets, 11 sets showed a decrease in the percentage of DiI-labeled cells, whereas 3 sets yielded an increase in these thrombocytes compared to the daily controls (Fig. 1).

Since the knockdown of eight genes affected young thrombocytes, using the same data from flow cytometry, we analyzed total thrombocyte percentage followed by calculating the mature thrombocyte percentages. We found the mature thrombocyte percentages did not change in knockdown samples compared to controls (Fig. 2C). To confirm these results and to rule out any off-target effects, we have designed a different set of ASOs (Table 2) and repeated these knockdowns on the eight genes. We found similar results as observed with the secondary knockdowns (Fig. 2D). These results confirmed the secondary screen and addressed potential off-target effects due to the previous set of primers.

The genes we found have the following diverse functions: si: ch73-359m17.9, a gene homologous to mucin C.1 of Danio aeasculapii, si: dkey-24c2.9, a gene encoding Vopp1, si: dkey-24p1.1, a CD22 like protein, spi1b & spi1a, hematopoietic transcription factors, uncx4.1, a homeobox transcription factor, upf3a, a factor involved in nonsense-mediated mRNA decay and wars1, a tryptophanyl tRNA synthetase. Among these eight genes, we chose spi1b for further analysis because previous reports demonstrated that spi1 knockout mice died at day 18 of embryonic development and had a normal number of megakaryocytes in contrast to our finding of decreased thrombocyte numbers in the knockdown experiments19.

To check whether the knockdown of spi1b affects the phenotype, we performed a gill bleeding assay and compared the percentage of bleeding using red pixel intensity measurements. The results indicated that spi1b knockdown fish exhibited more bleeding compared to the control group, with a significant increase in total red intensity observed in spi1b knockdown fish compared to the control (Fig. 3A and C).

Although the phenotype showed a reduction in thrombocyte numbers and more gill bleeding in the spi1b knockdown sample, we wanted to confirm whether the knockdowns occurred in the fish. Therefore, we knocked down spi1b and performed real-time qRT-PCR on the RNA from the liver and spleen because thrombocytes are present in these highly vascularized organs. The results indicated a significant reduction in gene expression fold change in VMO-ASO-injected fish with spi1b, confirming the success of the knockdown (Fig. 3B).

In another experiment to further confirm the effect of Spi1b on thrombocytes, we used GloFish, which has young RFP + thrombocytes14. We knocked down spi1b in GloFish and analyzed the thrombocyte percentages described above. The results showed a significant decrease in the percentage of RFP + thrombocytes in the knockdown GloFish, consistent with the DiI findings (Fig. 3D and F). The mature thrombocyte percentages (Fig. 3E and F) also coincided with the results in Fig. 2. To rule out bleeding due to defects in vascular integrity, we also examined the tail vessels of the controls and spi1b knockdown zebrafish. We found no noticeable vascular changes, as seen by morphology (Fig. 3G).

DiscussionThis study identifies eight genes critical for thrombocyte development using a piggyback 3-gene knockdown strategy. This method involves a 25-nucleotide VMO with a cell-permeable group attached to morpholino, which carries or piggybacks ASOs designed to base pair with both target mRNAs and VMO sequences, into cells. Once inside the cells, these ASOs form RNA-DNA hybrids that are degraded by RNaseH, leading to mRNA degradation20,21. This RNA degradation-based knockdown in adult zebrafish is a novel approach developed in our laboratory and precedes CRISPR-based RNA knockdowns, which are not available to knockdown RNA in adults22.The primary screening method with this piggyback strategy efficiently identifies key gene trios for thrombocyte development, improving throughput. Although the method may occasionally miss some genes due to potential interactions between activators and repressors, such cases are rare. Overall, the benefits of rapid screening outweigh the occasional omissions. The secondary screening assesses individual gene knockdowns within positive sets from primary screening to isolate a single key gene from each set. Combined effects observed in primary screening might not always be visible in single-gene knockdowns. False positives are filtered out during this phase to validate the significance of identified genes.Previous gene cohort screenings have shown favorable results13,16,23,24. Similarly, this study also identified new genes involved in thrombocyte development. Notably, only 1.7% of the genes screened were positive, compared to 5–10% in prior research23,24. In this study, the results indicate a higher number of false positives in primary screening, which showed as negatives in secondary screening. One possible explanation is that due to difficulties with adult intravenous injections that are not trivial, it is possible to miss the target injections in the secondary screening. Interestingly, it is surprising to note that positives that yielded greater thrombocyte numbers in the primaries yielded opposite results in the secondaries. This is probably due to the combinatorial action of lack of three factors which may give different results individually. These are difficult to predict and are rare. Even with the above caveats, this study identified novel genes and testified to the screening approach.In this study, we used DiI labeling to identify young thrombocytes and calculated mature thrombocyte percentages by subtracting young thrombocytes from the total count8. Alternative methods, like GloFish or GloFli fish, could also be considered for primary and secondary screening14.Among the eight identified genes, three are transcription factors, one is a mucin gene, one is tryptophanyl synthetase, one is Vopp1, one is a CD22-like protein, and one is involved in nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD). The NMD factor may globally regulate multiple factors and impact thrombopoiesis, while tryptophanyl synthetase could affect thrombocyte development by influencing tryptophan incorporation into proteins.Interestingly, only hereditary motor neuropathy (HMND9) is linked to tryptophanyl synthetase deficiency25,26. It is unclear if HMND9 affects platelet counts, though Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease is associated with thrombocytopenia27. Thus, an investigation of HMND9’s impact on platelets is needed. Additionally, the role of CD22 in thrombocyte development, including its expression in young thrombocytes and loss during maturation, requires validation in mammals. Interestingly, although the knockdown of si: dkey-24p1.1 reduced young thrombocytes, it did not result in a bleeding phenotype. Thus, it remains to be investigated whether thrombocytes produced after the knockdown have enhanced thrombocyte activity, compensating for the reduction in numbers. Likewise, the wars1 knockdown phenotype may also result in hyperactive mature thrombocytes that needs further investigation.Vopp1’s role in boosting NFκB activity suggests its involvement in thrombocyte development28,29. The mucin1-like protein, linked to kidney disease and NFκB signaling, indicates it may also play a role in thrombocyte production30,31. Additionally, the discovery of a homeobox transcription factor adds to the repertoire of hox genes implicated in thrombocyte development13.Interestingly, we have shown both Spi1a and Spi1b influence young thrombocyte numbers. Spi1b was chosen in this study as an initial step to confirm its role in thrombocyte development by mutant studies. Also, Spi1 knockout mice studies showed normal megakaryocyte numbers, prompting further investigation using zebrafish19.We believe mature thrombocytes arise from young ones through maturation in circulation because we have shown the loss of GATA-1 and the gain of Fli-1 in circulating mature thrombocytes and also by the loss and gain of expression of many genes by single-cell RNA sequencing6,10. In the spi1b knockdown and mutants, young thrombocytes are selectively reduced, possibly due to their accelerated maturation. Since the average half-lives of young thrombocytes (one day) is approximately one-fourth of that of mature thrombocytes (4.5 days) (unpublished results), these differences may obscure changes in mature cell numbers in 48 h post knockdown analysis. Alternatively, the young and mature thrombocytes may arise from bipotential thrombocyte precursor cells or from entirely different populations of progenitor cells. Future studies are needed to resolve this. In this context, it must be noted that in our studies on microRNA knockdowns, we found let-7b and mir-223 knockdowns resulted in reduced spi1a and spi1b transcripts. This study identifies eight genes critical for thrombocyte development using a piggyback 3-gene knockdown strategy. This method involves a 25-nucleotide VMO with a cell-permeable group attached to morpholino, which carries or piggybacks ASOs designed to base pair with both target mRNAs and VMO sequences, into cells. Once inside the cells, these ASOs form RNA-DNA hybrids that are degraded by RNaseH, leading to mRNA degradation20,21. This RNA degradation-based knockdown in adult zebrafish is a novel approach developed in our laboratory and precedes CRISPR-based RNA knockdowns, which are not available to knockdown RNA in adults22.

The primary screening method with this piggyback strategy efficiently identifies key gene trios for thrombocyte development, improving throughput. Although the method may occasionally miss some genes due to potential interactions between activators and repressors, such cases are rare. Overall, the benefits of rapid screening outweigh the occasional omissions. The secondary screening assesses individual gene knockdowns within positive sets from primary screening to isolate a single key gene from each set. Combined effects observed in primary screening might not always be visible in single-gene knockdowns. False positives are filtered out during this phase to validate the significance of identified genes.

Previous gene cohort screenings have shown favorable results13,16,23,24. Similarly, this study also identified new genes involved in thrombocyte development. Notably, only 1.7% of the genes screened were positive, compared to 5–10% in prior research23,24. In this study, the results indicate a higher number of false positives in primary screening, which showed as negatives in secondary screening. One possible explanation is that due to difficulties with adult intravenous injections that are not trivial, it is possible to miss the target injections in the secondary screening. Interestingly, it is surprising to note that positives that yielded greater thrombocyte numbers in the primaries yielded opposite results in the secondaries. This is probably due to the combinatorial action of lack of three factors which may give different results individually. These are difficult to predict and are rare. Even with the above caveats, this study identified novel genes and testified to the screening approach.

In this study, we used DiI labeling to identify young thrombocytes and calculated mature thrombocyte percentages by subtracting young thrombocytes from the total count8. Alternative methods, like GloFish or GloFli fish, could also be considered for primary and secondary screening14.

Among the eight identified genes, three are transcription factors, one is a mucin gene, one is tryptophanyl synthetase, one is Vopp1, one is a CD22-like protein, and one is involved in nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD). The NMD factor may globally regulate multiple factors and impact thrombopoiesis, while tryptophanyl synthetase could affect thrombocyte development by influencing tryptophan incorporation into proteins.

Interestingly, only hereditary motor neuropathy (HMND9) is linked to tryptophanyl synthetase deficiency25,26. It is unclear if HMND9 affects platelet counts, though Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease is associated with thrombocytopenia27. Thus, an investigation of HMND9’s impact on platelets is needed. Additionally, the role of CD22 in thrombocyte development, including its expression in young thrombocytes and loss during maturation, requires validation in mammals. Interestingly, although the knockdown of si: dkey-24p1.1 reduced young thrombocytes, it did not result in a bleeding phenotype. Thus, it remains to be investigated whether thrombocytes produced after the knockdown have enhanced thrombocyte activity, compensating for the reduction in numbers. Likewise, the wars1 knockdown phenotype may also result in hyperactive mature thrombocytes that needs further investigation.

Vopp1’s role in boosting NFκB activity suggests its involvement in thrombocyte development28,29. The mucin1-like protein, linked to kidney disease and NFκB signaling, indicates it may also play a role in thrombocyte production30,31. Additionally, the discovery of a homeobox transcription factor adds to the repertoire of hox genes implicated in thrombocyte development13.

Interestingly, we have shown both Spi1a and Spi1b influence young thrombocyte numbers. Spi1b was chosen in this study as an initial step to confirm its role in thrombocyte development by mutant studies. Also, Spi1 knockout mice studies showed normal megakaryocyte numbers, prompting further investigation using zebrafish19.

We believe mature thrombocytes arise from young ones through maturation in circulation because we have shown the loss of GATA-1 and the gain of Fli-1 in circulating mature thrombocytes and also by the loss and gain of expression of many genes by single-cell RNA sequencing6,10. In the spi1b knockdown and mutants, young thrombocytes are selectively reduced, possibly due to their accelerated maturation. Since the average half-lives of young thrombocytes (one day) is approximately one-fourth of that of mature thrombocytes (4.5 days) (unpublished results), these differences may obscure changes in mature cell numbers in 48 h post knockdown analysis. Alternatively, the young and mature thrombocytes may arise from bipotential thrombocyte precursor cells or from entirely different populations of progenitor cells. Future studies are needed to resolve this. In this context, it must be noted that in our studies on microRNA knockdowns, we found let-7b and mir-223 knockdowns resulted in reduced spi1a and spi1b transcripts. However, knockdown of spi1a and spi1b and measuring total thrombocytes did not show thrombocyte reduction. This is again because young thrombocytes are less abundant and are masked by the more abundant mature thrombocytes.
