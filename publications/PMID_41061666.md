The mechanisms mediating calcium transport into and out of the mitochondrial
matrix have critical implications for signaling, bioenergetics, and cell death.
Zhang et al.1 propose that the protein TMEM65, recently identified as a key
component of the mitochondrial calcium efflux machinery, functions as the
mitochondrial sodium/calcium exchanger. Their report encourages critical
re-examination of the components required for mitochondrial calcium handling.

## Full Text

Mitochondria serve as the ATP-producing “powerhouse” of the cell and as central integrators and mediators of signal transduction.2 The most studied and evolutionary conserved second messenger regulating cell signaling is calcium (Ca2+). As such, mitochondrial calcium (mCa2+) exchange is an essential process regulating metabolism, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, mitochondrial ultrastructure, and cell death.3 Numerous proteins are implicated in mCa2+ exchange, with the mitochondrial calcium uniporter channel responsible for rapid mCa2+ uptake4,5 and the mitochondrial sodium/calcium/lithium exchanger (NCLX; gene name: SLC8B1) chiefly responsible for mCa2+ efflux.6 The importance of NCLX for mCa2+ homeostasis in excitable tissues is emphasized by the fact that acute deletion of this gene in adult cardiomyocytes leads to mCa2+ overload, fulminant heart failure, and lethality within weeks.7

In a recent study published in Nature Cell Biology, Zhang et al. challenge the prevailing view that NCLX is the main mediator of mitochondrial sodium (Na+)/Ca2+ exchange (mito-NCX) by proposing that transmembrane protein 65 (TMEM65) is responsible for this phenomenon.1 This report reproduces earlier work identifying TMEM65’s essential role in mito-NCX.8–10 It also raises intriguing questions about the precise mechanism(s) of TMEM65’s function and its crosstalk and cooperation with other exchangers such as NCLX.

Zhang et al. investigate the role of TMEM65 as a Na+/Ca2+ exchanger from a biophysical perspective based on the aforementioned prior publications.8–10 They demonstrate that TMEM65 localizes to the inner mitochondrial membrane of HEK cells, the appropriate place to influence Ca2+ flux between the matrix and intermembrane space. Next, they show that knockdown (KD) of TMEM65 limits and overexpression of TMEM65 augments Na+-dependent mCa2+ efflux, consistent with previous results.8–10 They demonstrate that KD of TMEM65 limits lithium (Li+)-dependent mCa2+ efflux, complementary to the finding that TMEM65 overexpression enhances Li+-dependent mCa2+ efflux.10

The new hypothesis proposed is that TMEM65 mediates mito-NCX, independent of NCLX. The authors use glutaraldehyde crosslinking to suggest that epitope-tagged TMEM65 dimerizes, and through in silico modeling hypothesize that a TMEM65 dimer forms a pocket capable of binding Ca2+. This proposed Ca2+-binding site includes residues D132, N163, S166, and D1678; mutagenesis to ablate their charge disrupts Na+-dependent mCa2+ efflux, consistent with prior findings.8,10 The observation that tryptophan fluorescence of purified TMEM65 changes upon addition of calcium1 indicates a conformational alteration upon binding Ca2+. Whether this shift in fluorescence is ablated with mutation of the proposed Ca2+ binding residues remains to be tested. It is also unclear if TMEM65’s Ca2+-binding activity requires homodimer formation.

The definitive proof required to establish if TMEM65 can itself mediate mitoNCX would be electrophysiological experiments demonstrating that the purified protein can move positive charge (Na+ and Ca2+ ions) across a membrane, or experiments using fluorescent reporters to measure real-time changes in Ca2+ and Na+ concentrations across a lipid bilayer. Zhang et al. approach these techniques by measuring the accumulation of 45Ca2+ in reconstituted proteoliposomes containing purified TMEM65. They report that TMEM65 enhances 45Ca2+ radioactivity of proteoliposomes, and that this effect is sensitive to a mito-NCX inhibitor, CGP-37157, and to the concentration of Na+ and Li+ outside the proteoliposomes.1 Unfortunately, this experiment cannot distinguish simple binding of Ca2+ to TMEM65 protein embedded in the proteoliposome membrane from actual transport of Ca2+ across the membrane. The paper’s central conclusion that TMEM65 is a Na+/Ca2+ exchanger must be considered in light of this limitation. Indeed, in intact fibroblasts, deletion of NCLX abolishes TMEM65’s effect to enhance mCa2+ efflux,8 arguing against a truly independent role of TMEM65 in mito-NCX.

Much work remains to understand mechanistically how TMEM65 enhances mCa2+Unresolved questions that efflux. need to be answered include: where Na+ (and/or Li+) may bind within TMEM65, whether Na+ and/or Li+ competes with Ca2+ for binding to the same site, the potential electrogenicity of ion exchange, and how electrophysiological data support or refute any ion exchange stoichiometry suggested by available ion-binding site(s) within TMEM65.

Finally, Zhang et al. offer evidence that TMEM65 is a direct target of CGP-37157, a benzothiazepine used as a tool to inhibit mito-NCX. They identify several residues that may form a hydrophobic pocket for GCP-37157 binding in a TMEM65 homodimer and report that F125 and A174 contribute to the sensitivity of mito-NCX to CGP-371571. It is important to note that these experiments were conducted in TMEM65-KD and TMEM65-knockout (KO) cells in which endogenous NCLX was not disrupted. Thus, it is uncertain whether the Na+-dependent mCa2+ efflux that was observed in these studies is via NCLX vs. via TMEM65 itself. Could CGP-37157 bind independently to both proteins? If not, can CGP-37157 binding to TMEM65 indirectly influence the function of NCLX, i.e., through direct molecular interactions between these two proteins? To this point, deletion of TMEM65 reduces the molecular weight of NCLX-containing complexes,8 suggesting that these proteins interact in some physical capacity, whether directly or within a macromolecular complex containing multiple proteins. It would be informative for future experiments to test these possibilities. Regardless, the identification of specific amino acids critical for pharmacologic inhibition of mito-NCX is a useful step toward the design of new strategies to modulate this process.

This study has broad implications for the treatment of pathologies driven by disrupted mCa2+ homeostasis, including myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, neurodegeneration, and cancer. Might TMEM65 be a more tractable target for therapeutic manipulation or gene therapy than NCLX, given its smaller size (240 vs. 584 residues) and simpler structure (3 vs. 13 transmembrane domains)? Could targeting TMEM65 directly be sufficient to beneficially alter mCa2+ accumulation in these diseases? Future translational studies are sure to address these questions. It will also be interesting for future work to test the specific impact of TMEM65 on mitochondrial Na+ signaling and its implications for mitochondrial bioenergetics and ROS production.

Zhang et al. provide compelling evidence supporting a critical role for TMEM65 in mCa2+ extrusion.
