Tissue-selective chemoattractants direct lymphocytes to epithelial surfaces to
establish local immune environments, regulate immune responses to food antigens
and commensal organisms, and protect from pathogens. Homeostatic
chemoattractants for small intestines, colon and skin are known1,2, but
chemotropic mechanisms selective for respiratory tract and other non-intestinal
mucosal tissues remain poorly understood. Here we leveraged diverse omics
datasets to identify GPR25 as a lymphocyte receptor for CXCL17, a
chemoattractant cytokine whose expression by epithelial cells of airways, upper
gastrointestinal and squamous mucosae unifies the non-intestinal mucosal tissues
and distinguishes them from intestinal mucosae. Single-cell transcriptomic
analyses show that GPR25 is induced on innate lymphocytes before emigration to
the periphery, and is imprinted in secondary lymphoid tissues on activated B and
T cells responding to immune challenge. GPR25 characterizes B and T tissue
resident memory cells and regulatory T lymphocytes in non-intestinal mucosal
tissues and lungs in humans and mediates lymphocyte homing to barrier epithelia
of the airways, oral cavity, stomach, and biliary and genitourinary tracts in
mouse models. GPR25 is also expressed by T cells in cerebrospinal fluid and
CXCL17 by neurons, suggesting a role in central nervous system (CNS) immune
regulation. We reveal widespread imprinting of GPR25 on regulatory T cells,
suggesting a mechanistic link to population genetics evidence that GPR25 is
protective in autoimmunity3,4. Our results define a GPR25-CXCL17 chemoaffinity
axis with the potential to integrate immunity and tolerance at non-intestinal
mucosae and the CNS.

## Full Text

Lymphocyte trafficking is essential to the establishment, organization and function of the immune system. Innate lymphocytes are primed in the thymus to migrate to frontline barriers in mucosal tissues and skin. Naive conventional T cells and B cells are pre-programmed to home to secondary lymphoid organs where they are activated in response to antigens arriving through afferent lymphatics. During activation they are ‘imprinted’ by environmental factors (presented by stromal cells and migratory dendritic cells) to migrate to target tissues, where they carry out effector, regulatory and surveillance functions. Adhesion receptors for vascular addressins provide regional (for example, gastrointestinal tract versus skin) specificity, but constitutively expressed ‘homeostatic’ chemoattractant cytokines (‘chemokines’) expressed by epithelial cells are pivotal in regulating lymphocyte homing and establishing distinct lymphocyte compartments in various barrier tissues. Secreted chemokines bind glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and are presented by stromal elements and by venous endothelial cells, providing ‘chemoaffinity’ cues for tissue-specific endothelial recognition by blood-borne lymphocytes and haptotactic migration within tissues. Barrier-defining chemokine/receptor pairs have been identified that recruit lymphocytes selectively to the small intestine (CCL25/CCR9), colon (GPR15LG/GPR15) and skin (for example, CCL27/CCR10)1,2,5. Lymphocyte trafficking also disseminates immune responses to distant sites: intranasal vaccination provides protective immunity in the vagina and cervix6,7, a process that involves migration of antigen-specific T cells through the blood to the genitourinary mucosa, where subsets establish long-term resident memory8,9. Intestinal exposure to food antigens and intestinal flora induces regulatory T (Treg) cells that provide systemic immune tolerance10, preventing allergic reactions to pulmonary exposure and suppressing inflammatory responses to cross-reactive autoantigens in the central nervous system (CNS)11. The mechanisms for dissemination of immune responses and cross-talk between organs remain unclear. Moreover, mechanisms of lymphocyte localization to some of the most clinically significant organs in the body, including the respiratory tract and upper gastrointestinal and genitourinary systems, have remained poorly understood.

The mammalian lung features terminal alveoli, creating a vast surface area for air exchange but also for exposure to pathogens and allergens. The respiratory tract is uniquely susceptible to airborne agents, but is also exposed to food antigens and oral microbiota inhaled during mouth breathing, as well as intestinal microbes delivered by hand-to-mouth or hand-to-nose transmission or contamination. The respiratory immune system must maintain a strict balance between tolerance and inflammation in the face of this broad challenge with antigens and organisms. Given the critical importance of pulmonary homeostasis to survival, we reasoned that evolutionary pressures may have favoured development of a homeostatic lymphocyte chemoattractant programme for the respiratory tract. Here we harnessed the wealth of data generated by the genomic and single-cell transcriptomic revolutions to identify a homeostatic respiratory chemoattractant receptor and its ligand and elucidate their functions. The results provide a comprehensive picture of a chemoaffinity mechanism shared by the airways, non-intestinal mucosal tissue (NIMT) and the CNS, and suggest a prominent role for the pathway in dissemination of regulatory and tissue resident lymphocytes for systemic immunity and immune homeostasis.

scRNA-seq analysis of activated tonsil B cells revealed GPR25 upregulation in the transition of naive and immature dividing cells to plasma cells and memory B cells (MBCs) (Fig. 2c). GPR25 expression is particularly enriched among the tissue-homing FCRL4+ B cell subset, a subset identified as tissue resident sentinel B cells within mucosal epithelia18 (Fig. 1c). Thus GPR25 is imprinted in tonsils during effector differentiation of activated B cells and T cells.

GPR25 is induced on Treg cellsTissue-specific homing receptors are imprinted during lymphocyte activation in lymphoid tissues, conferring targeted migration of antigen-specific T cells19–22. To assess the tissue-specific imprinting of GPR25, we examined the transcriptomes of T cells from human thoracic lymph nodes (TLNs, lung-draining) and mesenteric lymph nodes (MLNs, intestine-draining) (Supplementary Table 1). We compared GPR25 with the gene for the small intestinal homing receptor CCR9, which in mice is selectively imprinted in MLNs. As expected, CCR9 was expressed by T cells in human MLNs and nearly absent in TLNs (Fig. 2d). By contrast, T cells in both tissues expressed GPR25 (Fig. 2d–g).In TLNs, CD4 T effector (Teff) and Treg cells express GPR25 similarly (Fig. 2e). By contrast, MLN mature Treg cells (identified by high expression of FOXP3 and IL2RA; ‘Treg hi’ cells) more prominently express GPR25 than CD4 Teff cells (Fig. 2e). On average, approximately 80% of Treg hi cells in MLN were GPR25+ (Fig. 2e), with mean expression greater than that of their Treg hi cell counterparts in TLN (Fig. 2e,g). Moreover, although cells with high GPR25 expression in TLN were enriched for TH1, TH17 and Treg cells, cells with high GPR25 in MLN were principally enriched for Treg hi cells (Fig. 2f). Almost all GPR25+ Treg hi cells in MLN co-expressed CCR9, suggesting local induction in response to gut antigens and metabolites (Fig. 2g). Consistent with this, GPR25+ Treg hi cells align late along a pseudotime trajectory from naive CD4 cells; and in MLN, GPR25 expression arises ‘later’ than CCR9 (Extended Data Fig. 3a). Most GPR25+ Treg and Teff cells in the small intestinal lamina propria also co-expressed CCR9 (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Co-expression of CCR9 probably explains the presence of GPR25+ Treg cells in the lamina propria, as the GPR25 ligand (identified below) is poorly expressed under homeostasis in the intestines. Together, these data show that GPR25 is imprinted on T cells in gut as well as in lung-draining lymphoid tissues. Imprinting of GPR25 on Treg cells in gut-associated lymphoid tissues may position Treg cells to contribute to the systemic dissemination of tolerance to gut microbes and food antigens.

Identification of a GPR25 ligandWe considered potential characteristics of a ligand for GPR25 on the basis of structural features of the receptor. GPR25 is well conserved in vertebrate evolution and contains a canonical DRYLAVV motif in the third transmembrane sequence (Fig. 3a). It has a conserved negatively charged N-terminal extracellular peptide comprising a DY candidate sulfation site characteristic of many leukocyte chemoattractant receptors for positively charged polypeptides of the chemokine family. The closest relative of GPR25 is the colon homing receptor GPR15 (ref. 23). CoINPocket analysis24 indicates that the ligand binding pocket of GPR25 shares similarity to those of GPR15 and bradykinin, apelin and chemerin receptors that, similar to GPR15, are activated by C-terminal peptides of their cognate ligands (Fig. 3b). On the basis of these features, we postulated that a physiological attractant for GPR25 would be a chemokine-like protein (molecular weight (MW) < 25 kD) with a net positive charge (isoelectric point (pI) > 8, facilitating charge-based receptor and GAG binding), and that it would comprise an evolutionarily conserved C-terminal peptide for receptor insertion and activation. We identified 455 genes encoding known or predicted secreted peptides in the expected pI and MW range (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table 2). Of these, 142 had homologues in multiple species and displayed well-conserved C-terminal peptide sequences (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Table 3; alignment of C terminus of orphan chemokine CXCL17 is shown). To further enrich probable candidates, we assessed patterns of tissue expression: as GPR25 expression is limited to lymphocytes, we reasoned that expression of a physiological ligand within tissues might correlate with infiltration of GPR25+ cells and thus with GPR25 message. We evaluated the correlation of GPR25 with genes for ligand candidates across 49 non-lymphoid tissues with bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) gene profiles from the Human Protein Atlas (Supplementary Tables 1 and 4). Tissue expression profiles of GPR25 and CXCL17 are illustrated (Fig. 3e).These criteria converged to identify the orphan chemokine CXCL17 as a candidate GPR25 ligand. The full-length CXCL17 protein comprises six cysteines but may be cleaved in vivo to release a C-terminal peptide with four cysteines (‘4CysCXCL17’) and a predicted MW of 6.6 kD (ref. 25). The peptide displays anti-microbial activity at micromolar concentrations26 and is implicated in angiogenesis and tumour progression27. It has been reported to attract myeloid cells but not bulk lymphocyte populations28–31, although published data are inconsistent and the chemotactic potency of CXCL17 for myeloid cells has been questioned30. A report that GPR35 is a receptor for CXCL17 is now considered incorrect30,32,33. CXCL17 activates the promiscuous mast cell receptor MRGPRX2 (ref. 34) and can modulate CXCR4 activity on cells by means of GAG binding35. CXCL17 does not assume a classical chemokine conformation30 and evolved independently of the classical chemokine family23: it is first found in the genome after divergence of the mammals from other tetropods, and may have emerged in response to the immune challenges associated with evolution of the mammalian lung. It has a predicted MW of about 14 kD and a pI of about 11, and a highly conserved C terminus. Consistent with our hypothesis, in silico modelling predicts insertion of the conserved C-terminal FALPL peptide of CXCL17 into the GPR25 signalling pocket (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 4). An immunoglobulin Fc-h4CysCXCL17 chimeric fusion protein ‘tracer’ binds to cells expressing GPR25, and binding is inhibited by free native h4CysCXCL17 (Fig. 3g).

CXCL17 does not assume a classical chemokine conformation30 and evolved independently of the classical chemokine family23: it is first found in the genome after divergence of the mammals from other tetropods, and may have emerged in response to the immune challenges associated with evolution of the mammalian lung. It has a predicted MW of about 14 kD and a pI of about 11, and a highly conserved C terminus. Consistent with our hypothesis, in silico modelling predicts insertion of the conserved C-terminal FALPL peptide of CXCL17 into the GPR25 signalling pocket (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 4). An immunoglobulin Fc-h4CysCXCL17 chimeric fusion protein ‘tracer’ binds to cells expressing GPR25, and binding is inhibited by free native h4CysCXCL17 (Fig. 3g).

DiscussionOur studies identify GPR25 as a lymphocyte chemoattractant receptor for the NIMT/CNS-expressed chemoattractant CXCL17, and show that GPR25 mediates lymphocyte homing to the respiratory, upper gastrointestinal, biliary and genitourinary tracts. CXCL17, similar to GPR15LG, shares chemokine-like features and functions yet evolved independently of the classical chemokine family, suggesting it should be renamed. We propose that these chemoattractant cytokines be designated CLCL1 and CLCL2 (for chemokine-like leukocyte chemoattractant ligands) and their receptors CLCRs (chemokine-like leukocyte chemoattractant receptors).We provide an atlas of GPR25 regulation in human lymphocyte development and of GPR25 and CXCL17 expression in target tissues: innate lymphocytes upregulate GPR25 in the thymus before migrating to barrier tissues, and effector and regulatory B cells and T cells are imprinted with GPR25 during peripheral immune responses. Our analyses show that GPR25 characterizes resident CD8 T cells and FCRL4+ B cells, populations that serve as immune sentinels and modulators in barrier mucosae44,45. CXCL17 is most highly expressed in the airways, suggesting the respiratory tract may serve as a central hub for a GPR25–CXCL17 defined immune network. The data also suggest a prominent role for GPR25 in Treg cell dissemination. CD4+ Treg cells in each of the CXCL17-expressing tissues including the CNS express GPR25. More surprising is the imprinting of GPR25 on gut-associated Treg cells, cells thought to have an important role in oral tolerance and prevention of autoimmune responses to commensal organisms. GPR25-dependent Treg cell tropism to the respiratory system, NIMT and CNS may suppress systemic immune responses to benign food and gut microbes, whereas effector cell migration may contribute to systemic immune surveillance. Indeed, in a study of acute and recurring herpes infection, Srivastava et al.39 showed that Cxcl17−/− mice recruit fewer CD8 TRM and Teff cells to the vaginal mucosa and have reduced protection of the mucosa and of dorsal root ganglia against viral replication and latency. These results are consistent with involvement of the GPR25–CXCL17 axis in protective anti-viral responses at mucosal and neuronal sites. Further animal models will be required to fully elucidate the protective and pathogenic roles of this pathway in infectious diseases, cancer and autoimmune pathologies, and to understand its interplay with other chemoattractant programmes46 that can contribute to lymphocyte recruitment to mucosal tissues, including lungs and NIMTs.Discovery of the NIMT/CNS chemoaffinity programme presents opportunities to understand unexplained aspects of immune system integration and dysregulation in disease. GPR25 induction and directed migration of T cells may explain the effectiveness of intranasal vaccination in protecting the genitourinary tract. As discussed, GPR25-dependent dissemination of Treg cells may help prevent autoimmunity to self-antigens recognized through ‘molecular mimicry’ by T cells responding to intestinal microbes and food antigens. Poor Treg cell dissemination could explain the association observed between reduced GPR25 expression and susceptibility to autoimmune diseases including multiple sclerosis, uveitis, ankylosing spondylitis, primary biliary cholangitis, and coeliac and inflammatory bowel diseases3,4,47. Recent studies in rat models of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis suggest that pathogenic T cells undergo ‘licensing’48 in the respiratory tract before migrating to the CNS to induce disease: GPR25 could mediate lung localization for licensing, and/or migration to or within the CNS. GPR25-dependent recruitment of Treg or Teff cells could contribute to proposed pro- and anti-tumour effects of CXCL17 in cancers29. Future studies must address these possibilities through preclinical and clinical investigation. Our studies also suggest opportunities for disease monitoring and therapy: antibodies to GPR25 may allow monitoring of vaccine responses and identification of cells involved in pulmonary or NIMT tolerance or inflammation in disease settings. Engineered expression of GPR25 may enhance adoptive Treg cell therapies for autoimmune diseases. Thus, GPR25 and its ligand define a core axis of the integrated mucosal immune system with broad implications for understanding and manipulating immunity and inflammation. Our data represent a valuable resource for future investigations into the biology of respiratory/NIMT/CNS lymphocyte traffic and its role in systemic immunity and immune pathogenesis.

GPCR binding site similarity analysisPairwise GPCR binding site similarity data were obtained from Supplementary Dataset 1 of ref. 83. The GPCR-CoINPocket score was used to quantify the similarity between GPCR binding sites on the basis of their contact strength profiles. The pairwise similarity data were compiled into a matrix format, and the GPCR labels were matched to official HGNC gene symbols, with any outdated symbols manually updated. Briefly, GPCR-CoINPocket performs a sequence alignment for the transmembrane domains of all GPCRs analysed, and using the ligand interaction patterns across all Pocketome entries, the pairwise similarities of GPCR transmembrane domain sequences were calculated and normalized to control for evolutionarily conserved transmembrane domain regions.
