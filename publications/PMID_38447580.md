Batten disease, the most prevalent form of neurodegeneration in children, is
caused by mutations in the CLN3 gene, which encodes a lysosomal transmembrane
protein. CLN3 loss leads to significant accumulation of glycerophosphodiesters
(GPDs), the end products of glycerophospholipid catabolism in the lysosome.
Despite GPD storage being robustly observed upon CLN3 loss, the role of GPDs in
neuropathology remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that GPDs act as potent
inhibitors of glycerophospholipid catabolism in the lysosome using human cell
lines and mouse models. Mechanistically, GPDs bind and competitively inhibit the
lysosomal phospholipases PLA2G15 and PLBD2, which we establish to possess
phospholipase B activity. GPDs effectively inhibit the rate-limiting
lysophospholipase activity of these phospholipases. Consistently, lysosomes of
CLN3-deficient cells and tissues accumulate toxic lysophospholipids. Our work
establishes that the storage material in Batten disease directly disrupts
lysosomal lipid homeostasis, suggesting GPD clearance as a potential therapeutic
approach to this fatal disease.

Introduction

Lysosomes degrade macromolecules and faulty organelles to recycle their nutrient content. Mutations in lysosomal genes cause lysosomal storage disorders (LSDs) and have been linked to age-associated diseases including neurodegeneration and cancer 1-4. While the function of proteins encoded by several LSD genes is known, the molecular mechanism of LSD pathology is yet to be determined. Batten disease, a family of life-limiting neurodegenerative LSDs, also known as neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (NCL), is caused by mutations in fourteen genes, the most common of which is the juvenile form of NCL caused by mutations in the CLN3 gene 5-7.

Recent study from our lab found that loss of CLN3 leads to massive intralysosomal accumulation of glycerophosphodiesters (GPDs), degradation products of glycerophospholipids (phospholipids hereafter), in human cells, mouse and yeast models 8 (Figures S1A-F). Follow-up studies identified such accumulation in zebrafish models of Batten disease 9. Consistent with their massive accumulation in Batten disease lysosomes, we found that the cerebrospinal spinal fluid levels of glycerophosphoinositol (GPI), one of the accumulated GPDs (Figure S1B), can faithfully differentiate Batten disease patients from controls 8. In agreement with these findings, levels of GPI in the circulation were also identified as a potential biomarker for Batten disease across species including humans, pigs and mice 10. Altogether, these results strongly suggest an important role for GPDs in Batten disease pathology, however, its molecular basis is yet to be studied.

Understanding the role of storage material in disease pathology across most LSDs remains a challenge mainly because we lack the knowledge of their effects on intralysosomal environment 11. Here, we use a combination of organellar lipidomics from cells and tissues, biochemical fractionation, enzyme assays and high-resolution isotope-tracing experiments to establish the role of GPDs in disrupting lysosomal phospholipid catabolism. To provide mechanistic insights into their role, we further unraveled the machinery responsible for lysosomal phospholipid catabolism. We identified and rigorously characterized two phospholipase B enzymes that generate GPDs; PLA2G15 generates GPDs from all phospholipids with high efficiency and PLBD2 prefers inositol-containing lipids. We demonstrate that GPDs bind and inhibit phospholipases, especially their lysophospholipase function, leading to the accumulation of neurotoxic lysophospholipids in Batten disease lysosomes. These findings support the notion that accumulation of GPDs is vital to pathogenesis and offer a promising target for the development of therapeutic strategies for Batten disease. Furthermore, our work provides a paradigm to study the mechanistic role of storage material in disease pathology in other LSDs.

RESULTSCLN3-deficient lysosomes accumulate toxic lysophospholipidsWe have recently found GPDs to massively accumulate in the lysosomal lumen of CLN3-deficient cells and tissues (Figures S1A-F) 8. Given the alterations in GPDs, we sought to quantitate the levels of their upstream phospholipid precursors in lysosomes and whole cells derived from CLN3 knockout HEK293T cells and mouse brain and liver tissues using optimized, sensitive multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions for each class (Figures S1A and S1C). Our analysis revealed a significant increase in most lysophospholipid species (Lyso-PLs) in lysosomes derived from CLN3-deficient HEK293T cells (Figure 1A and Table S1) and CLN3-deficient mouse brains and livers when compared to their wildtype counterparts (Figure 1B and Table S1). This increase was restricted to the lysosome, while whole cell (Figure 1C) and tissue levels (Figures 1D and 1E) minimally changed if any. Of note, Lyso-PLs are well-established toxic, detergent-like lipids that are known to disrupt biological membranes 12. Changes in more upstream phospholipids were mixed and minimal, with a slight increase in phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and phosphatidylinositol (PI), and in phosphatidylglycerol (PG) in some instances (Figures 1A and 1B). The robust increase in lysosomal levels of Lyso-PLs across lipid species indicates a potential inhibition of phospholipid catabolism at the step where Lyso-PLs are deacylated to release GPDs in CLN3-deficient lysosomes.Excess GPDs reduce lysosomal phospholipase activityTo quantify the effects of CLN3 loss on phospholipid degradation in the native lysosomal environment, we leveraged an established method to conjugate lipid tracers to bovine serum albumin (BSA) for efficient endocytic internalization and delivery (Figure 2A) 8,13,14. Using this approach, we delivered labeled Phosphatidylcholine (D9-PC) and Phosphatidylglycerol (D5-PG) to lysosomes of wildtype and CLN3 KO HEK293T cells, whose uptake is similar between the two genotypes (Figure 2A and S2A). We then monitored the release of labeled Lyso-PLs and GPDs using LysoIP coupled with mass spectrometry. Consistent with inhibited lipid catabolism, Lyso-PLs (D9-LPC and D5-LPG) and GPDs (D9-GPC and D5-GPG) appeared at significantly slower kinetics in CLN3-deficient HEK293T cells compared to wildtype in early time points (Figures 2B, 2C and S2B). As previously reported 8 and consistent with our data from steady-state metabolomics, labeled Lyso-PLs eventually accumulate in CLN3-deficient lysosomes at longer time points (Figures S2C and S2D), possibly due to inhibition of Lyso-PL deacylation by trapped GPDs, the label form of which also accumulates at same time point (Figure S2E). Similar reduction in phospholipase activity is evident in purified lysosomes derived from CLN3-deficient HEK293T cells (Figure S3A) and Cln3−/− mouse livers compared to those of wildtype controls across several time points (Figures S3B-D). Reduction in enzyme activity can be associated with enzyme insufficiency or inhibition 4,15-17. We observed no significant changes in CLN3-deficient lysosomes in the protein levels of two lysosomal phospholipases that we later identified as well as SPNS1, a Lyso-PL transporter we and others recently discovered, thus ruling out defects in export (Figure S3E) 13,18. To test whether lysosome-mediated phospholipid degradation is inhibited by the GPD storage material rather than by other functions of CLN3 that influence the abundance of phospholipases, we preincubated the lysosomal lysates derived from wildtype mouse livers with excess GPDs and monitored the catabolic rate of the phospholipases using labeled precursors (Figure 2D). Indeed, excess GPDs reduced phospholipase activity in lysosomal lysates compared to vehicle-treated controls demonstrated by monitoring labeled Lyso-PLs (Figure 2E) and labeled GPDs (Figure 2F), with GPC being the most potent inhibitor among all GPDs. These results suggest that GPDs, the Batten disease storage, are inhibitors of lysosomal phospholipases.PLA2G15 and PLBD2 are the major lysosomal phospholipasesGPDs are generated via the deacylation of phospholipids by either one enzyme, in which phospholipase B (PLB) hydrolyzes both acyl groups, or a two-independent step mechanism that involves the sequential removal of acyl groups by phospholipase A (PLA) and lysophospholipase A (LPLA) (Figure S1A) 19-23. While other phospholipases can also catabolize phospholipids and thus generate different products, degradation by PLA/LPLA or PLB is an active mechanism in the lysosome as evident by the accumulation of GPDs upon CLN3 loss 8. The machinery responsible for the complete lysosome-mediated phospholipid degradation is still elusive. PLB is a recently described subclass of hydrolases 23-26 and no acidic PLB is reported despite reports of two acidic PLAs known for their sn2 deacylation and transacylation activities 20,23,27-29. Thus, the machinery capable of generating all five GPDs in lysosomes is yet to be determined. To this end, we employed a classical biochemical fractionation approach coupled with mass spectrometry to identify acid phospholipases capable of generating GPDs from phospholipids (Figure 3A). Isolated lysosomes were separated into various fractions followed by deuterated lipid assay to identify fractions with phospholipase activity by monitoring Lyso-PL and GPD generation as well as the concomitant decrease in their phospholipid substrates D5-PG and D9-PC (Figure 3A). Phospholipase activity was restricted to one fraction (Fraction E) (Figures 3B, S4A and S4B). Further separation of fraction E identified a few sub-fractions that retained phospholipase activity (Figures 3C, S4C and S4D), and all sub-fractions were analyzed by proteomics (Figure 3C). Proteomic analysis, see methods, identified two candidate phospholipases: Phospholipase A2 Group XV (PLA2G15) (Figures 3D, S4E and S4G) and Putative phospholipase B-like precursor (PLBD2) (Figures 3E, S4F and S4H) both of which are known lysosomal proteins 19,30,31. PLA2G15 was previously established as a LPLA against lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) but was later reported to have PLA and transacylase function against some phospholipids 20,27,28,31-34, while PLBD2 was never tested for phospholipase activity after its initial discovery 30.PLA2G15 and PLBD2 possess phospholipase B activityTo validate and explore the enzymatic activity of PLA2G15 and PLBD2, we purified a His-tagged version of both proteins to homogeneity (Figures S5A and S5B). Using thin layer chromatography 35, we confirmed the PLA activity of PLA2G15 and PLBD2 (Figure S5E). Using fluorescence-based assay with substrates that allowed for only sn1 or sn2 cleavage of phospholipids 36, we found that both enzymes possess PLB activity with PLA2G15 being a more active phospholipase (Figure 4A).Given the lower phospholipase activity of PLBD2 (Figure 4A), we speculated that it could have substrate specificity. Indeed, using a mass spectrometry-based approach, we observed lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI) to be its preferred, almost exclusive, Lyso-PL substrate while PLA2G15 was active against all Lyso-PLs (Figure 4B). Finally, we mutated the nucleophilic serine S198A 34 and S392A in the active site of PLA2G15 and the lipase motif (GXSXG) of PLBD2, respectively. While these variants retained their purity, folding and thermal stability (Figure S5C, S5D and S5F) 34, their activity against phospholipids, monitored by the release of GPDs, was completely abolished, thus confirming the phospholipase B activity of PLA2G15 and PLBD2 (Figure 4C and S5G). To test the role of PLA2G15 and PLBD2 in vivo, we first generated HEK293T cells depleted for either enzyme using CRISPR technology (Figure S6A) and then tagged their lysosomes with Tmem192-3xHA (LysoTag) (Figure S6B) to purify lysosomes (Figure S6C). Consistent with our in vitro data, lysosomal lysates from PLA2G15- and PLBD2-deficient HEK293T cells have significantly lower phospholipase activity in fluorescence-based assay (Figure 4D), with the decrease being more dramatic in those derived from PLA2G15-deficient ones. To directly test for their role in degrading phospholipids and generating GPDs in the lysosome, we measured the levels of GPDs in whole cells and lysosomes of the knockout cells. Lysosomes from PLA2G15-deficient cells have significantly lower levels of all GPDs, demonstrating its capacity to generate all five GPDs while those from PLBD2-deficient cells exhibited a significant depletion of only GPI (Figure 4E). Interestingly, changes in lysosomal GPDs are reflected at the whole-cell level for GPG, GPI and GPS, suggesting that the lysosome is the major site of PG, PI, and PS hydrolysis. Moreover, PLA2G15-deficient lysosomes accumulate most Lyso-PLs (Figure S6D and Table S2) consistent with previous reports from PLA2G15-deficient mice 16,20 while lysosomes from PLBD2-deficient cells accumulate only some Lyso-PLs including LPI 18:0 (Figure S6D and Table S2). Finally, lysosomes from cells deficient for CLN3 and PLA2G15 (Figure S6E) accumulate more Lyso-PLs compared to CLN3-deficient cells (Figures S6F and S6G and Table S3). Altogether, these results in conjunction with our in vitro data (Figures 4B and 4C) indicate that inositol-containing phospholipids are the preferred PLBD2 substrates and PLA2G15 is the dominant lysosomal acyl hydrolase with a phospholipase B function.PLA2G15 has high catalytic efficiencyTo study the effect of GPDs on phospholipases, we first sought to establish enzyme kinetics and catalytic efficiency of PLA2G15 because of its dominant role in the lysosome. Using rigorous mass spectrometry-based enzyme assays, we determined its pH optimum (Figure S7A) 32 and optimized reaction conditions (Figure S7B). Standard curves were generated for each expected product to extrapolate the product concentrations (Figure S7C) and the kinetic parameters were calculated (Figures 5A-5C).Aside from its moderate activity towards LPS (kcat/Km = ~104 M−1 s−1), PLA2G15 enzyme kinetic measurements show high catalytic efficiencies (kcat/Km = ~105 − 106 M−1 s−1) against all Lyso-PLs (LPG, LPE, LPC and LPI) (Figure 5A). Phospholipase B enzymes have a two-step catalytic mechanism and the kinetic knowledge of these steps aids in understanding enzyme regulation 24-26,37. Kinetic analyses of PLA2G15 against PG and PC, monitoring the Lyso-PL products from the first step LPG and LPC, demonstrated a higher catalytic PLA efficiency (kcat/Km = ~107 − 108 M−1 s−1) compared to LPLA efficiency (Figure 5B). This is even more evident when we use a non-hydrolyzable PC C16(Plasm)-18:1 phospholipid (Figure 5B). Additionally, monitoring GPD products in the same reaction showed PLA2G15 has high catalytic PLB efficiency (kcat/Km = ~105 − 106 M−1 s−1) against PG, PS and PI and moderate efficiency (kcat/Km = ~104 M−1 s−1) against PE and PC (Figure 5C). These PLB efficiencies were comparable to the LPLA efficiencies (Figure 5A), indicating that LPLA step is the rate-limiting step in PLB reaction. Acyl chain preference has been reported for phospholipases 31,33,34,38. indeed, we found that acyl chain length affects PLA2G15 activity towards LPG with higher activity for long-chain unsaturated fatty acids (Figure 5D). Our results establish PLA2G15 as a major lysosomal phospholipase B with high catalytic efficiency.GPDs inhibit lysosomal phospholipases in a competitive mannerGiven reduced lysosomal phospholipase activity in CLN3-deficient lysosomes (Figures 2, S2 and S3), we asked if GPD storage directly inhibits PLA2G15 and PLBD2. Indeed, we find that excess GPDs inhibit the activity of both phospholipases (Figure S8A). Consistent with our initial results (Figures 2D-2F), increasing GPD levels inhibits LPLA activity of PLA2G15 and PLBD2 (Figure 6A and S8B), similar to amiodarone on PLA2G15 (Figure S8C), a well-established inhibitor of phospholipases 15,17. To gain additional mechanistic insights, we focused on the major phospholipase PLA2G15. Interestingly, GPC inhibits LPLA activity more potently than PLA activity (Figure 6B) 39, consistent with our observation of Lyso-PL accumulation in Batten disease. Next, because GPC is the most potent inhibitor (Figure 6A), we determined its IC50 to be around 0.9 mM (Figures 6C, S8D and S8E), a concentration that is lower than that estimated in CLN3-deficient lysosomes (Figure S8F), thus mechanistically explaining how GPD accumulation in Batten disease causes Lyso-PL accumulation.Inhibitors usually bind enzymes to decrease their activity 37,40. Consistent with this notion, we find GPDs to specifically bind to lysosomal phospholipases at a single- to a double-digit millimolar concentration with the highest affinity toward GPC (Figure 6D and S8G). Of importance, no binding was observed to the glycerol backbone (Figure S8H). Reversible inhibitors either bind to the active site to compete with substrates or bind at other sites noncompetitively or uncompetitively 37. The millimolar binding profile inkled that GPDs likely bind to the active site of PLA2G15. Indeed, PLA2G15 (S198A) showed no significant binding to GPC (Figure S8I). Finally, inhibition kinetics using a Lineweaver-Burk’s plot confirmed GPDs as competitive inhibitors for PLA2G15 (Figures 6E and S8J). Altogether, these data demonstrate that GPDs, a recently discovered Batten disease storage, act as competitive inhibitors of lysosomal phospholipases, leading to the accumulation of the toxic intermediates Lyso-PLs (Figure 6F). Thus, our work unravels enzyme inhibition mechanisms, a step essential for therapeutic development in these devastating diseases 37,40.

Using thin layer chromatography 35, we confirmed the PLA activity of PLA2G15 and PLBD2 (Figure S5E). Finally, we mutated the nucleophilic serine S198A 34 and S392A in the active site of PLA2G15 and the lipase motif (GXSXG) of PLBD2, respectively. To test the role of PLA2G15 and PLBD2 in vivo, we first generated HEK293T cells depleted for either enzyme using CRISPR technology (Figure S6A) and then tagged their lysosomes with Tmem192-3xHA (LysoTag) (Figure S6B) to purify lysosomes (Figure S6C). Consistent with our in vitro data, lysosomal lysates from PLA2G15- and PLBD2-deficient HEK293T cells have significantly lower phospholipase activity in fluorescence-based assay (Figure 4D), with the decrease being more dramatic in those derived from PLA2G15-deficient ones. To directly test for their role in degrading phospholipids and generating GPDs in the lysosome, we measured the levels of GPDs in whole cells and lysosomes of the knockout cells. Lysosomes from PLA2G15-deficient cells have significantly lower levels of all GPDs, demonstrating its capacity to generate all five GPDs while those from PLBD2-deficient cells exhibited a significant depletion of only GPI (Figure 4E). Interestingly, changes in lysosomal GPDs are reflected at the whole-cell level for GPG, GPI and GPS, suggesting that the lysosome is the major site of PG, PI, and PS hydrolysis. Moreover, PLA2G15-deficient lysosomes accumulate most Lyso-PLs (Figure S6D and Table S2) consistent with previous reports from PLA2G15-deficient mice 16,20 while lysosomes from PLBD2-deficient cells accumulate only some Lyso-PLs including LPI 18:0 (Figure S6D and Table S2). Finally, lysosomes from cells deficient for CLN3 and PLA2G15 (Figure S6E) accumulate more Lyso-PLs compared to CLN3-deficient cells (Figures S6F and S6G and Table S3). Altogether, these results in conjunction with our in vitro data (Figures 4B and 4C) indicate that inositol-containing phospholipids are the preferred PLBD2 substrates and PLA2G15 is the dominant lysosomal acyl hydrolase with a phospholipase B function.

PLA2G15 has high catalytic efficiencyTo study the effect of GPDs on phospholipases, we first sought to establish enzyme kinetics and catalytic efficiency of PLA2G15 because of its dominant role in the lysosome. Using rigorous mass spectrometry-based enzyme assays, we determined its pH optimum (Figure S7A) 32 and optimized reaction conditions (Figure S7B). Standard curves were generated for each expected product to extrapolate the product concentrations (Figure S7C) and the kinetic parameters were calculated (Figures 5A-5C).Aside from its moderate activity towards LPS (kcat/Km = ~104 M−1 s−1), PLA2G15 enzyme kinetic measurements show high catalytic efficiencies (kcat/Km = ~105 − 106 M−1 s−1) against all Lyso-PLs (LPG, LPE, LPC and LPI) (Figure 5A). Phospholipase B enzymes have a two-step catalytic mechanism and the kinetic knowledge of these steps aids in understanding enzyme regulation 24-26,37. Kinetic analyses of PLA2G15 against PG and PC, monitoring the Lyso-PL products from the first step LPG and LPC, demonstrated a higher catalytic PLA efficiency (kcat/Km = ~107 − 108 M−1 s−1) compared to LPLA efficiency (Figure 5B). This is even more evident when we use a non-hydrolyzable PC C16(Plasm)-18:1 phospholipid (Figure 5B). Additionally, monitoring GPD products in the same reaction showed PLA2G15 has high catalytic PLB efficiency (kcat/Km = ~105 − 106 M−1 s−1) against PG, PS and PI and moderate efficiency (kcat/Km = ~104 M−1 s−1) against PE and PC (Figure 5C). These PLB efficiencies were comparable to the LPLA efficiencies (Figure 5A), indicating that LPLA step is the rate-limiting step in PLB reaction. Acyl chain preference has been reported for phospholipases 31,33,34,38. indeed, we found that acyl chain length affects PLA2G15 activity towards LPG with higher activity for long-chain unsaturated fatty acids (Figure 5D). Our results establish PLA2G15 as a major lysosomal phospholipase B with high catalytic efficiency.

Of importance, no binding was observed to the glycerol backbone (Figure S8H). Reversible inhibitors either bind to the active site to compete with substrates or bind at other sites noncompetitively or uncompetitively 37. The millimolar binding profile inkled that GPDs likely bind to the active site of PLA2G15. Indeed, PLA2G15 (S198A) showed no significant binding to GPC (Figure S8I). Thus, our work unravels enzyme inhibition mechanisms, a step essential for therapeutic development in these devastating diseases 37,40.

Inhibition of phospholipases leads to accumulation of Lyso-PLs, toxic intermediates in phospholipid degradation, in CLN3-deficient lysosomes derived from cells and tissues. Lysophospholipids are known to have neurotoxic properties 12 and could potentially contribute to neurodegenerative phenotypes.

Phospholipids are important structural components in the cell and play critical roles in different biological processes 19,21,23,41-43. While lysosome-mediated phospholipid degradation has been described following the discovery of the lysosome 44 the mechanism and machineries of the degradation process are not fully understood. In this work, we identified and validated two phospholipases, PLBD2 and PLA2G15, both of which generate GPDs from phospholipids. We show that PLBD2 is a lysosomal phospholipase B that prefers inositol-containing lipids. However, we do not exclude other potential physiological substrates for this enzyme, as it may require post-translational modifications or cofactors to enhance its activity 30,45. The lysosomal localization of PLBD2 is well-established 30. A recent report identified it as a senescence molecule closely related to immune cell infiltration and skin aging, suggesting a role in these processes 46. Future studies should focus on understanding the role of the different variants and modifications of PLBD2, including its matured form, to fully understand its physiological functions 30. This finding explains the need for a pathway to efflux excess lysophosphatidylcholine from the lysosome through SPNS1, which we and others recently discovered 13,18. In Batten disease lysosomes, excess GPDs, in particular GPC, bind and competitively inhibit PLA2G15 leading to accumulation of Lyso-PLs and disruption of phospholipid degradation. Of importance, in previous work, we identified that lysosomes synthesize specialized lipid called Bis(monoacylglycero)phosphate (BMP) through the function of BMP synthase (BMPS), encoded by another Batten disease gene CLN5
14. BMPs are essential for supporting the function of lipid hydrolases including that of PLA2G15 47. Thus, we speculate that disrupting lysosomal phospholipid catabolism is a unifying feature of different forms of Batten disease.

Phospholipases play important roles in cellular signaling, lipid homeostasis and inflammatory processes 20,22,23,48. The presence of highly active PLA2G15 enzyme is consistent with the continuous flow of membrane trafficking to lysosomes for recycling, which requires efficient acid phospholipases to prevent lipid dysregulation, a common defect associated with human diseases 49-52.

Altogether, our work provides mechanistic insights into lysosomal phospholipid metabolism in normal physiology and disease conditions 20,21,23. Specifically, we elucidated a molecular mechanism underlying the biochemical pathology upon CLN3 loss thus providing avenues for developing therapeutics that target GPDs in the lysosome.

Limitations of the studyThis work unravels the pathological role of Batten disease storage, glycerophosphodiesters (GPDs) in disrupting lysosomal phospholipid catabolism by inhibiting the major phospholipases PLA2G15 and PLBD2 leading to accumulation of Lyso-PLs in the lysosome. However, we do not show the direct link between the neurotoxic Lyso-PLs and lysosomal dysfunction in CLN3 deficiency. While we elucidate the molecular function of two major phospholipases PLA2G15 and PLBD2, post-translational modifications and cofactors that may be needed to maximize the activity of the latter remain to be determined. Additionally, given residual GPD levels in PLA2G15-deficient cells and the ability of these lysosomes to generate Lyso-PLs, we predict the presence of additional unidentified lysosomal phospholipid hydrolases that our biochemical method might have missed.

Synthesis of GlycerophosphodiestersHigh millimolar concentrations of standard GPDs were prepared for the inhibition assays and method development on the mass spectrometer. Liver phosphatidylinositol (Liver PI), phosphatidylserine (18:1 PS), phosphatidylglycerol (18:1 PG), and phosphatidylethanolamine (18:1 PE) from Avanti were saponified to produce GPI, GPS, GPG, and GPE in the following manner: 100 mg of each lipid was dissolved in a clean 20 ml scintillation vial that contained a magnetic stirring bar with a 12 ml solution of 2:1 chloroform:methanol. NaOH (4 ml of 2 M) was added to the mixture and the reaction was stirred at room temperature for 2 hours. To neutralize the reaction, HCl (4 ml of 2 M) was used, and GPDs were extracted with 8 ml water into separate vessels twice. The aqueous phases were combined, washed with 8 ml chloroform, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and lyophilized. The lyophilates were dissolved in a minimum amount of methanol, and the desired products were isolated from the precipitants by paper filtration to separate the crude product from inorganic salts.

The resulting solution was sonicated in a water bath for 10 min to form small unilamellar vesicles, which were then dried again. The dried liposomes were then resuspended in water and their concentration was measured using the Stewart assay. To create liposomes containing lysophospholipids, the lipids were dried under vacuum and then resuspended with water to create a solution with the desired concentration used for the assay. Phospholipase enzyme assaysRecombinant PLA2G15-6xHis at concentrations of either 2.5 nM or 100 nM was mixed with lysophospholipids and glycerophospholipids substrates of varying concentrations in 50 mM Sodium Acetate:Acetic Acid at pH 4.5 and 150 mM NaCl. The glycerophospholipids were in liposomes with non-cleavable 18:1 diether PC while lysophospholipids were dissolved in aqueous buffer. The mixture was incubated for 30 seconds at 37 °C and heat-inactivated at 95 °C for 5 min. Controls which consisted of every component except the enzyme were also prepared for each substrate to account for background in each experiment. To ensure linearity of velocity, a time course was performed at several substrate concentrations. The phospholipase reaction mixtures were transferred to plastic autosampler vials to monitor glycerophosphodiesters (GPDs) release. In the case of lysophospholipids (Lyso-PLs) release, the reaction was diluted with acetonitrile and transferred to glass autosampler vials. For kinetic studies, corresponding standard GPDs or Lyso-PLs were measured in increasing concentration to extrapolate the concentrations of the products. For candidate phospholipase comparison, recombinant PLA2G15-6xHis, PLBD2-6xHis, and their mutants at a concentration of 100 nM were mixed with 1 μM lysophospholipids and glycerophospholipids substrates in 50 mM Sodium Acetate: Acetic Acid at pH 4.5 and 150 mM NaCl. For pH optimum determination, Sodium Acetate:Acetic Acid, HEPES, and Boric acid buffers were used at their respective pH ranges.
