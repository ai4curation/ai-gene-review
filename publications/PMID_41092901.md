Hsp70 and Hsp40 molecular chaperones form a central machinery that remodels
client proteins involved in numerous biological processes. Here, we integrated
cryo-electron microscopy and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy to
determine the architecture of the full-length Hsp70-Hsp40 machinery. The
structure of the complex in a physiologically inhibited state reveals distinct
regulatory mechanisms. In the active state, the Hsp40 glycine-phenylalanine
(G/F)-rich region acts as a pseudo-substrate for Hsp70, directly modulating
refolding. This region also maintains Hsp40 in an autoinhibited state; upon
binding to Hsp70, the inhibition is disrupted, exposing a cryptic client-binding
site that enables client engagement and refolding. Transitions between these
states are central to controlling refolding efficiency. Disrupting either the
autoinhibited state or the G/F-Hsp70 interaction impairs function and elicits a
compensatory heat shock response in cells. Our findings uncover the regulatory
dynamics of a fundamental chaperone system, with broad implications for
understanding protein homeostasis and the cellular response to stress.

INTRODUCTION

The Hsp70-Hsp40-NEF (nucleotide exchange factor) system is evolutionarily conserved across species1–3,4–6. Members of the Hsp70 family form the core of this machinery7–11, as ATP binding and hydrolysis, coupled to NEF-induced ADP-to-ATP exchange12,13, regulate the dynamic binding and release of client proteins. Hsp40s, also referred to as J-domain proteins14, have two key roles: they stimulate Hsp70 ATPase activity through their J domains, and they facilitate the recognition and binding of unfolded proteins, subsequently presenting them to Hsp70 for further processing15–20. Hsp70 consists of an N-terminal nucleotide-binding domain (NBD), which is connected through a conserved hydrophobic linker to the substrate-binding domain (SBD). The SBD is comprised of SBDβ (a β-sandwich subdomain) followed by SBDα (an α-helical subdomain), and then an unstructured C-terminal region (Figure 1A)7. Hsp70s undergo transitions between two states: the “docked” state, in which NBD and SBDβ are docked resulting in low affinity for client, and the “undocked” state in which SBDβ and SBDα form a high-affinity client-binding pocket21–24. Three events allosterically shift the conformation of Hsp709,25,26: (i) interaction with Hsp40, (ii) interaction with a client protein, and (iii) ATP binding and hydrolysis. ATP binding promotes the “docked” state whereas ADP or the absence of nucleotide the “undocked” state7. Hsp70 interaction with NEF induces ADP release and rebinding of ATP, thus promoting client release and resetting Hsp70 for another cycle12.

Class B Hsp40s, along with class A, represent the most prevalent types and consist of four domains: an N-terminal J-domain, a glycine-phenylalanine (G/F)-rich linker region, a pair of client-binding domains (CBD1 and CBD2), and a C-terminal dimerization domain (DD) (Figure 1A)27. Mutations in the G/F region can affect function and are associated with various diseases28–35, but the precise roles of this region remain to be defined.

Structural and biochemical studies of the Hsp70-Hsp40 machinery have elucidated important molecular interactions, including the binding of Hsp70 to clients7,36–39, J-domain-mediated stimulation of Hsp70 ATPase activity40,41, and the recognition of unfolded clients by Hsp4042–44. In contrast, how Hsp70 and Hsp40 assemble into a functional complex remains unclear, since structures of the complex formed by full-length Hsp70 and full-length Hsp40 have not yet been determined. Here, we used cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy to determine the architecture of the Hsp70-Hsp40 machinery. Along with functional assays in vitro and in vivo, our findings reveal how these chaperones synergize to act on unfolded proteins.

RESULTSArchitecture of the Hsp70-Hsp40 machinery in complex with a regulatory proteinThe Hsp70 (DNAK) and Hsp40 (class B; DNAJ2) from T. thermophilus form a ~195 kDa complex with 2:2 stoichiometry and a dissociation constant ~40 μM (Figure S1A). The hetero-tetrameric complex exhibits substantial flexibility, as indicated by NMR analyses (Figure S1B). To stabilize the chaperone complex, we used DafA (8.7 kDa; Figure 1A), a T. thermophilus protein that binds and inhibits the activity of the Hsp70 machinery45. We obtained a ternary complex containing two copies of each of the proteins, resulting in a hetero-hexamer of ~215-kDa (Figure S1C). We determined the cryo-EM structure of the hetero-hexameric Hsp70-Hsp40-DafA at a resolution of 4.9-Å (Figures S2A, S2B, and Table S1). To build an atomic model we used an integrative approach combining the cryo-EM map with NMR, X-ray crystallography, and AlphaFold. Notably, an AlphaFold-generated structural model of the hetero-hexamer predicted accurately all protein-protein interfaces observed in the cryo-EM map, as detailed below. The final atomic model revealed a rhombus-shaped architecture (~170 Å long and ~110 Å wide), with the dimerization domain (DD) of both Hsp40 protomers located at the apex and the NBD of both Hsp70 protomers at the bottom (Figure 1B, 1C). Hsp70 and Hsp40 form a chamber that encloses the two DafA molecules. The coiled-coil Hsp40 DD mediates a homo-dimer, which is known to be tight (Kd ~10 nM)42. The two Hsp70 subunits form a homo-dimeric interface at the bottom of the complex via their NBDs. DafA forms a four-helix bundle with parallel coiled-coils, with helix 4 mediating homo-dimerization. Hsp70 and Hsp40 interact through one single interface, formed by the C-tail of Hsp70 and the CBD2 of Hsp40 (Figure 1C).Each J domain makes extensive contacts with both DafA subunits (Figure 1C). The helix 2 and the HPD motif of the J domain interact with the helix bundle of DafA, similarly to the interactions previously reported for E. coli DafA and the J domain of E. coli CbpA46. DafA binds to a surface on the J domain that overlaps with the Hsp70-binding site40, which would prevent the interaction of the J domain with Hsp70 NBD necessary for stimulating ATPase activity. The N-terminal tail of DafA penetrates into the client-binding site of Hsp70, with its Leu2 side chain making extensive contacts with the hydrophobic pocket of SBDβ (Figure 1C). These interactions were further confirmed by NMR NOESY analysis of the complex formed by a peptide derived from the DafA N-tail and the Hsp70 SBDβ (Figure S1D). Thus, DafA inhibits the Hsp70-Hsp40 machinery through two distinct mechanisms: it blocks access of protein clients to Hsp70 and it prevents the docking of the J domain onto Hsp70, thereby suppressing its ATPase activity.In the hetero-hexameric complex, since the client-binding site of both Hsp70 subunits is occupied by the DafA N-tail, Hsp70 adopts the “undocked” conformation, with the NBD making no contacts with either SBDα or SBDβ21. The two Hsp70 subunits appear to form a homo-dimeric interface at the bottom of the complex via their NBDs, but the density in the cryo-EM map corresponding to the NBDs of the two Hsp70 subunits is not well resolved, indicating flexibility (Figure S2). The tendency of the NBD to homo-dimerize was supported by a crystal structure we determined of the isolated NBD, which featured a dimer (Figures S3A, S3B, and Table S2). We modeled the dimeric NBD coordinates into the corresponding density in the cryo-EM map (Figures 1B,C). A similar NBD-NBD′ interface was observed in the dimeric structure of E. coli Hsp70 in the ATP state47. It was reported that a dimeric Hsp70 is required for a strong interaction with Hsp4047,48, which is consistent with our finding that the two chaperones form a 2:2 complex. This suggests that the ATP-bound, dimeric form of Hsp70 is the species likely to engage with client-loaded Hsp40.To complement our cryo-EM analyses and the AlphaFold modeling, we characterized by NMR the hetero-hexameric Hsp70-Hsp40-DafA complex, which confirmed the binding interfaces (Figures 1D and S1D–F). The flexibility of NBD relative to the core of the complex was further corroborated by NMR, with the majority of the peaks present in the 1H-15N TROSY HSQC spectrum of the hetero-hexamer attributable to the NBD (Figure S1G). Moreover, the NMR analysis of the hetero-hexamer suggested that the two NBDs interact with each other. When compared to a monomeric Hsp70 variant (Hsp70mon; Figures S3C–E)49, the NBD residues showing chemical shift changes (Figure S3F) in the hetero-hexamer are located at the NBD dimeric interface (Figure S3G). Of note, we observed a distinct, less populated 2D class in the cryo-EM sample of the hetero-hexameric complex, wherein the two NBDs are detached from each other (Figure 1E), indicating that the NBDs are capable of transitioning between open and closed states.Structural characterization of the active Hsp70-Hsp40 complexTo understand how Hsp70 and Hsp40 assemble and function as a dynamic chaperone machinery, we studied the hetero-tetrameric (Hsp70:Hsp40 2:2) complex by NMR (Figures S1B, S4A, and S4B). The NMR data indicated that the NBDs of the two Hsp70 protomers interact with each other in the hetero-tetramer, similar to their interaction in the hetero-hexamer (Figure 2A, black arrows and Figure S3H). Furthermore, our NMR analyses showed that Hsp70 interacts with Hsp40 through three distinct interfaces (Figures 2A, S1B, and S4A–E). The primary interface (Figure 2A, green arrows) involves the interaction between the Hsp70 C-tail and the Hsp40 CBD242, akin to their interaction in the hetero-hexameric complex (Figure 1C). Disruption of this interface by mutation or deletion of the Hsp70 C-tail abolished Hsp70-Hsp40 complex formation (Figure S1H). The second interface involves the Hsp40 J domain and the Hsp70 NBD (Figure 2A, red arrows), the typical interaction required for ATPase activity stimulation40.The third binding interface, to our knowledge, has not been described before, and is formed by the Hsp70 client-binding pocket and the G/F region of Hsp40 (Figure 2A; purple arrows). To confirm this interaction, we assessed by NMR the effect of an Hsp70 variant carrying a deletion in the SBDβ loop49 (referred to as Hsp70SBDm) that abolishes client binding. Compared to wild type Hsp70, Hsp70SBDm caused a weaker effect to Hsp40, with the G/F region not affected (Figures S4D–I). Thus, the G/F region of Hsp40 interacts with the Hsp70 client-binding pocket.The G/F region mediates an Hsp40 autoinhibited stateTo understand the role of the G/F region, we determined the NMR structure of the J-G/F fragment (residues 1–116), encompassing the J domain (residues 1–72) and the entire G/F region (residues 81–110) (Table S3). The structure of the J-G/F fragment, which is preserved in the full-length Hsp40 (Figure S5A), shows the J domain forming the typical four-helix bundle, with the G/F region assembling around a cluster of six Phe residues that pack against helices 3 and 4 of the J domain (Figure 2B). Residues Gly95–Gly102 form a short α helix (α5). The extensive interface between the J domain and the G/F region explains the pronounced chemical shift effect observed on the J domain in the presence of the G/F region (Figure 2C). This structural arrangement would sterically interfere with the docking of the J domain onto Hsp70, as the G/F region would clash with the SBDβ (Figure 2D). This observation suggests that the G/F region has an autoinhibitory role on Hsp40 function.To test this hypothesis, we compared the binding of full-length Hsp40, the isolated J domain, or the J-G/F fragment to Hsp70 in the nucleotide free state, bound to ADP or bound to ATP. As mentioned earlier, nucleotide binding modulates the population of “docked” and “undocked” conformations, which is variable among Hsp70 homologs50. For the T. thermophilus Hsp70, we detected only the “undocked” conformation in the nucleotide-free or ADP-bound state, whereas in the ATP-bound state the “docked” state was ~5% populated (Figure S5B). We observed stronger interaction of Hsp40 with Hsp70-ATP (Figure S5C) compared to Hsp70-apo (Figure S1A), which can be attributed to the increase in the “docked” state promoted by ATP binding. The isolated J domain exhibited low intrinsic affinity for the “undocked” state of Hsp70 (apo Hsp70 or Hsp70-ADP; Figure S5D), and increased binding to Hsp70-ATP, where the J domain forms contacts with both the NBD and SBDβ (Figure S5E). We also assessed the Hsp70mon variant, which is unable to interact with another Hsp70 molecule –a condition that favors the “undocked” conformation– and found that it exhibited ~30% “docked” population in the ATP-bound state (Figure S5B)47,51,52. The isolated J domain bound strongly to Hsp70 mon-ATP, whereas the J-G/F fragment did not bind (Figures 2E, S5F, and S5G), confirming the autoinhibitory effect of the G/F region. However, in the full-length Hsp40, both the J domain and the G/F region bound to Hsp70-ATP (Figure S5H) indicating that the autoinhibition exerted by the G/F region is relieved. The energy required to overcome the autoinhibition in the full-length proteins is provided by the primary binding interface mediated by the Hsp70 C-tail and Hsp40 CBD2.The G/F region from human DNAJB1 and DNAJB6 was previously reported to inhibit binding of their J domain to Hsp7029,32,53. We note that the mechanism of inhibition we find for T. thermophilus type B Hsp40 is distinct from that in either DNAJB1 or DNAJB6, because of the different disposition of the G/F region relative to the J domain (Figure S5I): binding to Hsp70 by DNAJB1 and DNAJB6 results in the G/F region sterically clashing with the NBD, whereas in the T. thermophilus type B Hsp40 the G/F region sterically clashes with SBDβ without hindering the docking to the NBD (Figure 2D).Structural basis for the interaction between the Hsp40 G/F and Hsp70 SBDβThe inability of the isolated J-G/F fragment to bind to Hsp70 presumably results from the tight packing of the G/F region against the J domain (Figures 2B, 2D). We engineered four J-G/F variants with amino acid substitutions (E52A, I65E/Y69G, S94E, and F101A), predicted to impair the packing between J and G/F, thereby alleviating the autoinhibitory mechanism (Figures 2C and S6A). NMR analysis showed that the G/F region is detached from the J domain in all four variants (Figures 2C and S6A). Nearly all of the resonances corresponding to J residues were visible, whereas the resonances from the G/F region were broadened beyond detection, due to conformational averaging54. Unlike the J-G/F fragment, the variants exhibited binding to Hsp70 (Figures 2F, S6B). We should note that this binding is not merely due to the release of G/F inhibition, since the isolated J domain binds weakly to Hsp70; instead, binding of the variants to Hsp70 is the result of the synergistic effect between the G/F region binding to Hsp70 SBDβ and the J domain docking onto NBD.To directly probe the interaction of Hsp70 SBDβ with the G/F region, while circumventing its conformational heterogeneity and the relatively weak binding, we prepared fused constructs between Hsp70 SBDβ and different peptide sequences from the G/F region. Peptides encompassing Phe101 and/or Phe106 showed binding to Hsp70 SBDβ (Figures S6C and S6D), confirming that this specific G/F region engages Hsp70 SBDβ. We solved by NMR the structure of the Hsp70 SBDβ fused to the G/F peptide corresponding to residues 103–109. The structure shows the G/F region fitting snugly into the client-binding pocket of SBDβ, effectively acting as a pseudo-substrate (Figure 2G). Phe106 mediates the primary interactions, nestling deep inside the pocket and forming nonpolar contacts with a number of residues lining the SBDβ pocket (Val395, Val430, Ile432, Ile466, Val468) and a favorable aromatic stacking interaction with SBDβ Phe420. The latter interaction could explain the preference of SBDβ for Phe residues in the G/F region. The Gly residue following Phe106 appears to allow the latter to enter deeply inside the pocket. Because of its similarity, the motif encompassing Phe101 is expected to engage SBDβ in a similar manner. Only one Phe residue fits inside the SBDβ pocket because of its small size. NMR characterization of SBDβ fused to a longer G/F peptide (residues 98–109) that encompasses both Phe101 and Phe106 indicates a dynamic interplay where two distinct regions of the G/F region alternately bind to SBDβ (Figure S6D). Substitution of both Phe101 and Phe106 in full-length Hsp40 markedly decreases its binding to full-length Hsp70 (Figure S6E).The G/F region binds to unfolded clientsEfficient docking of the J domain onto Hsp70 necessitates the unfolding of the G/F region due to its steric clash with SBDβ (Figure 2D). Our docking model positions Phe106 of the G/F region in close proximity (within ~8 Å) to the client-binding pocket of SBDβ (Figure S6F), suggesting their interaction would be favorable. Notably, the AlphaFold-generated structural model between J-G/F and Hsp70 in the “docked” state places Phe106 inside the SBDβ pocket, in agreement with our NMR structure, and shows the G/F region partially unfolded (Figure 2H). Unfolding of the G/F region would expose its Phe cluster (Phe85, Phe88, Phe93, Phe97) to solvent (Figure 2H). Because several chaperones employ solvent-exposed aromatic residues to bind to unfolded clients42,55,56, we hypothesized that this aromatic cluster in the G/F region may serve a similar function.To test this hypothesis, we investigated the interaction of full-length unfolded alkaline phosphatase (PhoA) and a fragment of the maltose binding protein (MBP331–396), both previously used as model clients42,55,56, with J-G/FS94E, one of the variants that disrupted the Hsp40 autoinhibited state (Figure 2C). The NMR data showed extensive interactions between J-G/FS94E and hydrophobic segments of PhoA and MBP331–396, whereas the wild-type J-G/F showed no such interaction (Figures 2I, S6G). This finding indicates that the G/F region acts as a cryptic client-binding site, which is activated for binding only when autoinhibition is relieved. Considering the conservation of the Phe cluster across the Hsp40 family57, it is likely that other Hsp40 chaperones utilize this site to engage with clients. Collectively, our results demonstrate that the G/F region performs dual roles through separate segments: one segment interacts with the client-binding pocket in SBDβ, while another segment engages unfolded client proteins.The Hsp40 G/F region elicits client release from Hsp70We posited that these dual roles of the G/F region may enable it to promote client release from Hsp70. First, through the action of Phe106 binding into the client-binding pocket of Hsp70 SBDβ, which is mutually exclusive with the binding of client proteins; and, second, through the proximity to the Hsp70 SBDβ of the G/F region, which when open can directly bind to and compete for the client. To evaluate this hypothesis, we used a fluorescein-labelled peptide (F-NR), previously employed as a model client peptide for Hsp7058, and monitored the effect of Hsp40 addition. The data show that Hsp40 displaces the client peptide from Hsp70, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 8.6 μM (Figures 2J, S6H, and S6I). The Hsp40F106A variant impairs peptide release. By contrast, the Hsp40F101A markedly increases it (Figure 2J). We attributed this to the disruption of the Hsp40 autoinhibited state, which reveals the client-binding site within the G/F region. Indeed, the Hsp40S94E variant, which also disrupts the Hsp40 autoinhibited state, increases peptide release from Hsp70 (Figure 2J).Binding of Hsp40 G/F to Hsp70 SBDβ is conserved among speciesWe asked whether the G/F region of class A Hsp40 (DNAJ1) from T. thermophilus (Figure S7A) also binds to Hsp70 SBD. We examined the J domain (residues 1–73) and the J-G/F fragment (residues 1–100) of class A Hsp40 and found that the G/F region (residues 80–90) interacts with the J domain, similar to our observations with class B Hsp40. Based on NMR-detected chemical shift perturbation data (Figure S7B) and supported by the AlphaFold-predicted structure (Figure S7C), the interaction between J and G/F in class A is weaker compared to class B, and thus autoinhibition is expected to be less pronounced. Indeed, the class A J-G/F fragment binds to Hsp70 with the G/F region interacting with the peptide-binding pocket in SBDβ (Figures S7D–F), and this interaction stabilizes the binding of the J domain to Hsp70 NBD that is inherently weak (Figure S7D). Class A J-G/F does not bind to Hsp70SBDm (Figure S7G), highlighting the importance of the G/F-Hsp70 SBDβ interaction in stabilizing the complex.Given the conservation of the XFG motif (X is preferably a hydrophobic residue) in Hsp40s (Figure S7H)57, we hypothesized that the interaction of the Hsp40 G/F region with the Hsp70 SBDβ may be a conserved feature across species. We selected the E. coli Hsp40s DnaJ (class A) and CbpA (class B), and the human DNAJB6 to test this hypothesis using NMR. The G/F region of all three proteins interacted with their respective Hsp70 SBDβ indicating that this interaction is indeed conserved (Figures S7I–M).A compromised G/F region impairs chaperone activityTo assess the functional role of the Hsp40 G/F region, we examined how mutations that disrupt the autoinhibited state or its interaction with Hsp70 affect the ability of the Hsp70-Hsp40 machinery to refold denatured luciferase. The F106A substitution had a modest impact, whereas the F101A had a more pronounced effect (Figure 3A and S8A). Interestingly, the double substitution (F101A/F106A) resulted in a very inefficient refolding machinery, likely due to the combined effects of weakened G/F binding to Hsp70 SBDβ and impaired Hsp40 autoinhibition. This conclusion was further supported by the S94E substitution, which disrupts the autoinhibited state and similarly showed a significant effect on refolding efficiency (Figure 3A). Analogous mutations in E. coli DnaJ and CbpA caused a pronounced decrease in luciferase refolding efficiency (Figures 3B, 3C, S8B, and S8C). Consistent with these findings, equivalent mutations in three human Hsp40s (DnaJA2, DnaJB1, and DnaJB6; Figure S7H) produced the same defects in refolding activity (Figure 3D), underscoring the evolutionary conservation of this regulatory mechanism. These findings indicate that an intact autoinhibitory mechanism is required for the optimal function of the machinery.To further assess the functional consequences of disrupting the interaction between G/F and Hsp70 SBDβ, we used CRISPR-Cas9 tools to generate E. coli strains bearing mutations F98A and/or F102A (corresponding to F101A and F106A in T. thermophilus, respectively) in DnaJ or CbpA. Growth assays in liquid culture at 37 °C and 42 °C showed that the DnaJ mutants exhibited a growth impairment during the exponential phase at both temperatures (Figures 3E and S8D–H), while CbpA mutants exhibited a milder growth impairment (Figures S8D–H).Next, we examined luciferase refolding in vivo, during both the exponential (OD=0.5) and early stationary (OD=2) phases. Paradoxically, we observed enhanced luciferase refolding activity in the strains harboring the DnaJ mutants compared to the wild-type strain (Figures 3F, S8I and J), with the effect being more pronounced during exponential phase (Figure S8I). In addition, when strains underwent heat shock treatment, the DnaJ mutants showed much higher residual luciferase activity, indicating a pre-existing heat shock response (Figure 3G).To explore further the heat shock response elicited in the DnaJ mutant strains, we performed ribosome profiling assays on wild-type E. coli cells as well as strains carrying the dnaJ and cbpA mutations. The differential expression analysis revealed a pronounced heat shock response in the dnaj mutant cells at 37 °C, characterized by drastically increased expression of numerous heat shock genes, such as dnaK, grpE, clpB, dnaJ, and lon (Figures 3H and 3I). The increased production of these chaperones in cells harboring the dnaj mutation accounts for the observed enhancement in luciferase activity compared to the wild-type dnaJ strain (Figures 3G, S8F, and S8G). The amplified heat shock response stems from the compromised chaperone functionality59. These observations demonstrate the essential role of the Hsp40 G/F region in the effectiveness of the Hsp70-Hsp40 chaperone system in vivo.Assembly of the Hsp70-Hsp40 hetero-tetrameric complexBy integrating our findings with prior data, along with molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, we built a structural model of the Hsp70-Hsp40 hetero-tetrameric complex in the “docked” conformation (Figure 4A). As we showed by NMR (Figure S1B) and ITC (Figure S1H), the primary link between the two chaperones occurs through the Hsp70 C-tail binding to the Hsp40 CBD2. The G/F region binds to unfolded clientsEfficient docking of the J domain onto Hsp70 necessitates the unfolding of the G/F region due to its steric clash with SBDβ (Figure 2D). Efficient docking of the J domain onto Hsp70 necessitates the unfolding of the G/F region due to its steric clash with SBDβ (Figure 2D). Notably, the AlphaFold-generated structural model between J-G/F and Hsp70 in the “docked” state places Phe106 inside the SBDβ pocket, in agreement with our NMR structure, and shows the G/F region partially unfolded (Figure 2H). Unfolding of the G/F region would expose its Phe cluster (Phe85, Phe88, Phe93, Phe97) to solvent (Figure 2H). Because several chaperones employ solvent-exposed aromatic residues to bind to unfolded clients42,55,56, we hypothesized that this aromatic cluster in the G/F region may serve a similar function.

To test this hypothesis, we investigated the interaction of full-length unfolded alkaline phosphatase (PhoA) and a fragment of the maltose binding protein (MBP331–396), both previously used as model clients42,55,56, with J-G/FS94E, one of the variants that disrupted the Hsp40 autoinhibited state (Figure 2C). The NMR data showed extensive interactions between J-G/FS94E and hydrophobic segments of PhoA and MBP331–396, whereas the wild-type J-G/F showed no such interaction (Figures 2I, S6G). This finding indicates that the G/F region acts as a cryptic client-binding site, which is activated for binding only when autoinhibition is relieved. Considering the conservation of the Phe cluster across the Hsp40 family57, it is likely that other Hsp40 chaperones utilize this site to engage with clients. Collectively, our results demonstrate that the G/F region performs dual roles through separate segments: one segment interacts with the client-binding pocket in SBDβ, while another segment engages unfolded client proteins.

The Hsp40 G/F region elicits client release from Hsp70We posited that these dual roles of the G/F region may enable it to promote client release from Hsp70. First, through the action of Phe106 binding into the client-binding pocket of Hsp70 SBDβ, which is mutually exclusive with the binding of client proteins; and, second, through the proximity to the Hsp70 SBDβ of the G/F region, which when open can directly bind to and compete for the client. To evaluate this hypothesis, we used a fluorescein-labelled peptide (F-NR), previously employed as a model client peptide for Hsp7058, and monitored the effect of Hsp40 addition. The data show that Hsp40 displaces the client peptide from Hsp70, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 8.6 μM (Figures 2J, S6H, and S6I). The Hsp40F106A variant impairs peptide release. By contrast, the Hsp40F101A markedly increases it (Figure 2J). We attributed this to the disruption of the Hsp40 autoinhibited state, which reveals the client-binding site within the G/F region. Indeed, the Hsp40S94E variant, which also disrupts the Hsp40 autoinhibited state, increases peptide release from Hsp70 (Figure 2J).

Here we provide the structural basis for the assembly of the Hsp70-Hsp40 chaperone complex and reveal novel interactions that impinge on the functionality of the chaperone machinery. A key element in these interactions is the Hsp40 G/F region, which exerts its effects through at least three distinct mechanisms.

The first mechanism involves its C-terminal segment, which encompasses Phe101 and Phe106 in T. thermophilus Hsp40. This segment acts as a pseudo-substrate and binds into the SBDβ client-binding pocket, which helps stabilize the initial Hsp70-Hsp40 complex (Figure 2A, G). The second mechanism through which the G/F region exerts its function is by controlling the Hsp40 autoinhibited state. Our data indicate that disruption of the autoinhibited state by mutants in the T. thermophilus class B Hsp40 compromises the chaperone activity (Figure 3A). Of interest, autoinhibition is not as strong in the T. thermophilus class A Hsp40. A similar trend was also observed in S. cerevisiae class A (Ydj1) and class B (Sis1) Hsp40s35. The differences in autoinhibition mechanisms could account, at least partly, for the different functions performed by class A and class B Hsp40 chaperones.

The third mechanism involves the binding of clients by the G/F region. Client binding, along with the docking of the J domain onto Hsp70, can work synergistically to relieve autoinhibition and facilitate client transfer to Hsp70. This could serve as a control mechanism to ensure that only a client-loaded Hsp40 forms a productive complex with Hsp70 to stimulate its ATPase activity. After ADP release, the G/F region can also be involved in the timely displacement of the client from the Hsp70 SBD (Figure 2J), accelerating its release and initiating a new cycle of client re-binding, if needed. Various NEFs have been proposed to directly displace clients, likely using a similar mechanism by physically interacting with the SBD5,61–63. In several eukaryotic Hsp70 systems, failure of NEFs to directly displace substrates has been proposed to cause loss of function and disease62.

In the nucleotide-free or ADP-bound state of Hsp70, the “undocked” conformation is favored, and the hetero-tetrameric Hsp70–Hsp40 complex appears to form a chamber (Figure 4D) that could, in principle, facilitate client folding by providing a protected environment, analogous to GroEL64,65. Such an arrangement may help explain how the Hsp70–Hsp40 machinery blocks aggregation of unfolded states and stabilizes native conformations of client proteins36,66,67. In this model, the chamber is encircled by client-binding sites on both Hsp70 and Hsp40 (Figure 4D), effectively constraining clients within close proximity. Because this chamber is absent in the “docked” state, its formation would be expected to depend on ATP binding and hydrolysis. Future studies will be required to directly test whether this putative chamber contributes to client folding in cells.

In light of the present data, the Hsp70-Hsp40–driven client-refolding cycle can be further refined. The client-loaded Hsp40 (Figure 5B, 5C) interact with Hsp70-ATP, which exists in the “docked” conformation, to form a 2:2 complex (Figure 5B, 5D). The Hsp70 C-tail displaces the segment of the client that is bound to Hsp40 CBD2. Docking of the J domain to NBD and SBDβ disrupts the autoinhibited state thereby enabling the G/F region to engage the client and the SBDβ. The proximity of the G/F region to the client-binding site in SBDβ may facilitate client transfer over to Hsp70. Upon ATP hydrolysis, Hsp70 switches to the “undocked” state (Figure 5B, 5E), which has high affinity for the client. ADP release by NEF will reset the cycle and switch Hsp70 to the “docked” state, which has low affinity for the client. G/F binding to the SBDβ will assist with the release of the client from the Hsp70. The remodeling of the complex, driven by ATP binding and hydrolysis, alters the arrangement and spacing of the client-binding sites (Figure 5E). These changes in the relative positions of the binding sites may create pulling forces that contribute to the unfolding or disaggregation of client proteins68,69.

Limitations of the studyOur structural analyses were performed on the bacterial Hsp70–Hsp40. Although we demonstrate that the G/F loop–driven regulatory mechanism of the refolding process is conserved in class A and B Hsp40 proteins, including several human orthologs, the eukaryotic systems likely incorporate additional layers of regulation and exhibit greater substrate selectivity. The G/F motif binds both the Hsp70 SBD and client substrates, suggesting a critical interplay between these interactions that is not yet fully understood and will require further investigation. Moreover, DafA is unique to bacteria and absent in eukaryotes, suggesting that eukaryotic Hsp70–Hsp40 complexes are maintained in an inactive state through alternative mechanisms. Our data provide snapshots of specific conformational states, but the highly dynamic transitions between these states are not yet fully resolved. Finally, our study does not yet capture the structure of a fully assembled Hsp70–Hsp40–NEF–client complex, which will be important for defining the complete chaperone cycle.

Finally, our study does not yet capture the structure of a fully assembled Hsp70–Hsp40–NEF–client complex, which will be important for defining the complete chaperone cycle.

Stoichiometry of the Hsp70-Hsp40-DafA complexAn earlier study112 suggested that the T. thermophilus Hsp70–Hsp40–DafA complex has a molecular mass of approximately 300 kDa, based on size-exclusion chromatography (calibrated with globular protein standards). Raw negative-stain images reported in the earlier study suggested a stoichiometry of 3:3:3. However, because this complex is elongated, its larger radius of gyration (Rg) causes it to appear larger than a more compact protein of the same true mass, leading to an overestimation of its molecular weight. We have used SEC-MALS to show that the complex has a molecular mass of 210 kDa consisting of a 2:2:2 stoichiometry. The cryo-EM analysis confirmed this. The stoichiometry we report here is further supported by a work on the complex between the homologous proteins in E. coli CbpM and CbpA, which form a 2:2 complex.
