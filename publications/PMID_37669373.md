Protein import into chloroplasts is powered by ATP hydrolysis in the stroma.
Establishing the identity and functional mechanism of the stromal ATPase motor
that drives import is critical for understanding chloroplast biogenesis.
Recently, a complex consisting of Ycf2, FtsHi1, FtsHi2, FtsHi4, FtsHi5, FtsH12,
and malate dehydrogenase was shown to be important for chloroplast protein
import, and it has been proposed to act as the motor driving protein
translocation across the chloroplast envelope into the stroma. To gain further
mechanistic understanding of how the motor functions, we performed membrane
association and topology analyses on two of its subunits, FtsHi1 and FtsHi2. We
isolated cDNA clones encoding FtsHi1 and FtsHi2 preproteins to perform in vitro
import experiments in order to determine the exact size of each mature protein.
We also generated antibodies against the C-termini of the proteins, i.e., where
their ATPase domains reside. Protease treatments and alkaline and high-salt
extractions of chloroplasts with imported and endogenous proteins revealed that
FtsHi1 is an integral membrane protein with its C-terminal portion located in
the intermembrane space of the envelope, not the stroma, whereas FtsHi2 is a
soluble protein in the stroma. We further complemented an FtsHi1-knockout mutant
with a C-terminally tagged FtsHi1 and obtained identical results for topological
analyses. Our data indicate that the model of a single membrane-anchored pulling
motor at the stromal side of the inner membrane needs to be revised and suggest
that the Ycf2-FtsHi complex may have additional functions.

## Full Text

Most chloroplast proteins are encoded by the nuclear genome and synthesized in the cytosol as higher molecular mass preproteins with an N-terminal transit peptide. Proteins are imported into chloroplasts by the TOC–TIC (translocon at the outer and inner envelope membrane of chloroplast) translocon complex consisting of components in and around the two envelope membranes. Several translocon components have been shown to function in the basic steps of the import process. For example, Toc75 is the protein-translocating channel across the outer membrane, whereas Tic20 is the major component of the inner-membrane channel. Tic110, an integral inner membrane protein with a large soluble domain located in the stroma, functions as a stroma-side receptor for transit peptides and a scaffold for other stromally located translocon components (1).

Protein import into the chloroplast requires ATP hydrolysis in the stroma (2). The ATP is presumed to be consumed by stromally localized motor proteins that use the energy of ATP hydrolysis to translocate preproteins across the double-membrane envelope into the stroma. The identity of the ATPase motors has been actively investigated. The 93-kDa heat shock protein (Hsp93, also named ClpC) was the first ATPase shown to be involved in chloroplast import (3, 4). Hsp93 is a stromal protein peripherally associated with the inner envelope membrane (5, 6). It is a member of the AAA+ (ATPases associated with various cellular activities) family of proteins, which form hexameric ring complexes and use the energy of ATP hydrolysis to create pulses of pulling force to actively translocate their substrate proteins through the central pore of the hexameric ring (7). Subsequently, stromal 70-kDa heat shock cognate protein (cpHsc70) was also shown to be required for protein import (8, 9). Importantly, it was shown that increasing the Km for ATP hydrolysis of stromal cpHsc70 translated into an increased Km for ATP usage by chloroplasts for preprotein import, revealing that cpHsc70 ATPase activity dominates the energetics of the import reaction (10). Since Hsp93 directly binds to transit peptides of translocating preproteins at early stages of import (11), and cpHsc70 is present in the translocon complex throughout import and well after the preproteins have been processed into their mature forms, it was proposed that initial pulling by Hsp93, accompanied by continuous translocation via Brownian ratchet motions provided by cpHsc70, is required for efficient preprotein translocation into the stroma (11). Later, it was shown that chloroplast heat shock protein 90 (Hsp90C) also specifically copurified with bound preproteins and other translocon components and that the Hsp90 inhibitor, radicicol, reversibly inhibited ATP-dependent protein import (12). Therefore, Hsp90C also contributes to ATP-dependent protein translocation into the stroma.

Recently, a 2-MDa inner envelope membrane-bound complex consisting of Ycf2 (hypothetical chloroplast open reading frame2), FtsHi1 (filamentous temperature sensitive protein H inactive1), FtsHi2, FtsHi4, FtsHi5, FtsH12, and NAD+-malate dehydrogenase (MDH) was shown to directly interact with translocation intermediates arrested at the early stage of import in the translocon (13). A revised model was then proposed, with this Ycf2–FtsHi–MDH complex (hereafter referred to as the FtsHi complex) as the sole ATP-driven motor in the stroma for chloroplast protein import (13). FtsH proteins also belong to the AAA+ protein family that forms hexameric-ring complexes. Bacterial FtsH proteins are membrane-bound metalloproteases. Five Arabidopsis FtsH homologs (FtsHi1 to i5) lack the zinc-binding motif and are therefore presumed to lack proteolytic activity. These proteins are therefore called FtsHi (FtsH-inactive).

Since the FtsHi complex has been proposed to act as the stromal import motor, its FtsHi subunits have been depicted as integral inner-membrane proteins with their ATPase domains protruding into the stroma and these ATPase domains forming a single hetero-hexameric complex for pulling preproteins into the stroma (Fig. 1A and refs. 13–16). However, topological analyses of the complex subunits were inconclusive. The topology of these subunits was analyzed using isolated, inverted (stroma-side out) inner-membrane vesicles (IMV) treated with thermolysin or polyethylene glycol maleimide (PEG-Mal) (13). Inner-membrane proteins exposed to the stromal side should be thermolysin-sensitive in inverted IMV, as demonstrated by complete degradation of the control protein Tic110. Although it was suggested that all subunits had the same topology as Tic110, i.e., with their ATPase domains located in the stroma, many of the components were relatively resistant to thermolysin in the inverted IMV. The authors concluded that there had been some limitations to the assays and that the precise membrane topology of the complex subunits should be studied further (13). In addition, although transmembrane domains have been predicted for all of the FtsHi complex subunits apart from MDH, whether these subunits are truly integral membrane proteins has only been investigated for FtsH12 and FtsHi4. FtsH12 is indeed an integral inner envelope membrane protein with its C-terminal ATPase domain located in the stroma (17, 18). FtsHi4 was also shown to be an integral membrane protein, but it was shown to localize in the thylakoid membrane (19).

Determining how the FtsHi complex subunits associate with the inner membrane and establishing their respective membrane topologies are essential for understanding the functional mechanism of the motor complex. Here, we performed topology, membrane association, and suborganellar fractionation analyses on two of the subunits, FtsHi1 and FtsHi2. We used intact chloroplasts, not IMV, to avoid the possible problems of mixed orientations of isolated membrane vesicles. We analyzed both in vitro imported preproteins and endogenous proteins. We further complemented an ftshi1 mutant with a C-terminally tagged FtsHi1 and confirmed the results of our topological analysis. Our results show that FtsHi1 is an integral membrane protein with its C-terminal ATPase domain located in the intermembrane space, whereas FtsHi2 is a soluble protein in the stroma.

ResultsImport of FtsHi1 and FtsHi2 Preproteins into Chloroplasts.We isolated cDNAs encoding Arabidopsis FtsHi1 and FtsHi2 preproteins (prFtsHi1 and prFtsHi2, respectively) (Fig. 1B) and imported the in vitro-translated [35S-Met]-labeled preproteins into isolated pea chloroplasts. prFtsHi1 was synthesized as a preprotein that migrated slightly above the 110-kDa marker (Fig. 1C). After being imported into chloroplasts, it was processed to a thermolysin-resistant mature protein migrating to ~105 kDa, whereas the nonimported prFtsHi1 was degraded by thermolysin (Fig. 1C, lanes 5 and 6). This result reveals that the size of mature FtsHi1 is ~105 kDa and that prFtsHi1 has a cleavable transit peptide of ~6 kDa, agreeing well with the UniProt prediction that the first 54 amino acids of prFtsHi1 functions as a transit peptide (Fig. 1B). To investigate FtsHi1 topology, chloroplasts after import were treated with 200 μg/mL trypsin. Trypsin can penetrate the chloroplast outer but not inner membrane, so outer-membrane and intermembrane-space proteins can be digested but stromally localized domains would be protected from the treatment (20), as demonstrated by the degradation of Toc75 and protection of Tic110 from digestion (Fig. 1C, lane 7). Imported mature FtsHi1 was mostly degraded by trypsin but, similar to Toc75, a very small amount remained detectable (Fig. 1C, lane 7). Two of the four predicted FtsHi1 transmembrane spans exhibit high consensus scores (0.86 and 0.92, Fig. 1B), but the other two have much lower scores (0.55 and 0.52). As shown in Fig. 3A, endogenous FtsHi1 was solely present in the pellet fraction, as was the case for Toc75 and chlorophyll a/b binding protein (CAB), indicating that FtsHi1 is an integral membrane protein. In contrast, like the small subunit of RuBP carboxylase (RBCS), FtsHi2 was almost exclusively present in the supernatant fraction. A very small amount of FtsHi2 was detected in the pellet fraction of hypotonically lysed chloroplasts, which was not the case for alkaline or high-salt extraction treatments, suggesting that FtsHi2 is mostly present as a soluble protein. In comparison, Hsp93 and cpHsc70 were also mainly present in the supernatant fraction, but a substantial amount of Hsp93 and cpHsc70 was also detected in the pellet fractions, in agreement with previous results showing that these two proteins are peripherally but stably associated with the inner membrane (5, 6, 9).Since FtsHi2 is a soluble protein, it could be located in the stroma or within the thylakoid lumen. Therefore, we performed chloroplast fractionation experiments to determine its location. Isolated Arabidopsis chloroplasts were fractionated into the outer envelope membrane, inner envelope membrane, stroma, and thylakoid fractions (Fig. 3B). FtsHi2 was located in the stromal fraction, with a portion also associated with the two envelope membrane fractions, similar to the stromal marker protein GS2. For chloroplast fractionation shown in Fig. 3B, chloroplasts were lysed in a high density in a 0.6 M sucrose solution in order to separate the outer from the inner envelope membranes. It has been demonstrated previously soluble stromal proteins tend to stick to the envelope membrane fractions under the fractionation conditions used (24). Considering that an equal amount of proteins was analyzed for each fraction but the stroma contains much more protein than the envelope membranes (we obtained ~7 mg and ~16 µg of proteins in the stromal and inner membrane fractions, respectively), the majority of FtsHi2 is localized in the stroma. No FtsHi2 was detected in the thylakoid fraction. In comparison, the 33-kD subunit of oxygen-evolving complex (OE33), a soluble thylakoid luminal protein, was located exclusively in the thylakoid fraction. We also confirmed the subchloroplast localization of FtsHi1. It is located in the two envelope fractions, with slightly greater abundance in the inner envelope fraction, similar to the inner membrane marker protein Tic20, whereas the outer membrane marker protein Toc75 was more abundant in the outer membrane fraction. The most N-terminal A domain is prone to degradation and in isolated intact chloroplasts the full-length Toc159 (Toc159AGM) and an 86-kDa fragment comprising the G and M domains (Toc159GM) are often both observed (ref. 25 and Fig. 4E, lane 7). After thermolysin treatment, the A and G domains were degraded, so all Toc159 molecules were converted to the 52-kDa M-domain fragment with a signal intensity similar to those of original Toc159(AGM) plus Toc159(GM). We further explored the protease digestion pattern of ARC6 as an additional control. ARC6 is a component of the chloroplast division machinery. It is an integral inner membrane protein with its N terminus located in the stroma and C terminus in the intermembrane space. When an antibody against the stromally localized N-terminal domain (28) was hybridized to chloroplasts treated trypsin plus chymotrypsin, a protease-cleaved fragment was clearly observable. Furthermore, the sensitivity of the Toc159 M domain and Toc75 to trypsin plus chymotrypsin, as well as the resistance of Tic110 to the same protease combination, indicate that these two proteases had crossed the outer membrane but had not gained access to the stromal space under our experimental conditions. These results further confirm that the C-terminal portion of FtsHi1 is located in the intermembrane space of the envelope.

DiscussionThe FtsHi complex has been proposed as the motor that pulls preproteins across the chloroplast envelope into the stroma. Essential elements of this model include the formation of a hetero-hexameric ring complex at the inner envelope membrane and a stromal localization of the ATPase domains of its subunits, as depicted in several previous publications (refs. 13–16 and Fig. 1A). However, here, using in vitro imported preproteins, antibodies against the C terminus of endogenous proteins, and epitope tag antibodies against complementing proteins in transgenic plants, we have demonstrated that FtsHi1 has its ATPase domain located in the intermembrane space, not in the stroma, and that FtsHi2 is mostly soluble and does not seem to be stably associated with membranes (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, the subplastidial location of FtsHi4 needs to be verified (19), and the membrane topology and membrane association of the remaining subunits, FtsHi5 and Ycf2, also need to be investigated. Nonetheless, our results on FtsHi1 and FtsHi2 already indicate that the current model (Fig. 1A) of an FtsHi hetero-hexameric ring complex pulling preproteins into the stroma needs to be revised.The size of the FtsHi complex was estimated to be 2 MDa based on blue native gel electrophoresis (13). However, the largest marker on the gel in that study was 880 kDa, so size estimation was based on migration of the subunits to approximately the same region at the top of the gel. Therefore, the complex may be even larger than 2 MDa and it may actually contain several hetero- and homohexameric subcomplexes. The stoichiometry of the subunits in the FtsHi complex also remains unclear. It has been shown that the abundance of FtsH12 was about twofold those of FtsHi1 and FtsHi2 in a gel slice containing the FtsHi complex, and five-fold that of FtsHi4 (29), further supporting that the region may contain several subcomplexes of differing abundances. These subcomplexes may function at different steps of the import process, with some steps occurring in the intermembrane space and some in the stroma. Nonetheless, AAA+ protein complexes exert a pulling, not pushing, force. Thus, the functions of the FtsHi subunits located in the intermembrane space require further analyses. Since the FtsHi proteins are presumed to lack protease activity, they are unlikely to function in a similar fashion to the mitochondrial intermembrane space iAAA protease YME1 (30, 31). The mitochondrial outer membrane AAA+ protein Msp1 is recruited to clogged TOM (translocase of the outer membrane of mitochondria) complex during stress to dislocate preproteins from the TOM complex (32, 33). It is possible that FtsHi proteins in the chloroplast intermembrane space, such as FtsHi1, may also exert a similar function in un-clogging the TIC complex. Interestingly, the FtsHi complex was proposed to work together with the TIC complex component Ycf1(Tic214) in the stroma to facilitate protein import (13, 16). However, recent structural studies of the TOC–TIC supercomplex from Chlamydomonas have revealed that Ycf1 actually protrudes into the intermembrane space (26, 27), i.e., the same location as determined herein for FtsHi1. Therefore, FtsHi1 may work together with Ycf1 to facilitate protein translocation through the intermembrane space.None of the six ATPase subunits of the FtsHi complex—FtsHi1, FtsHi2, FtsHi4, FtsHi5, FtsH12, and Ycf2—are found in the Poaceae family of monocots, including rice and maize (34). If the FtsHi complex is the sole motor for chloroplast protein import, it is not clear how the chloroplasts of these plants achieve protein translocation into the stroma. It has been proposed that these plants have evolved to use the sole remaining FtsHi protein, FtsHi3, as their chloroplast motor protein (16). This hypothesis remains to be tested. Nevertheless, the three chaperone proteins first shown to be important for import, i.e., Hsp93, cpHsc70, and Hsp90C, are universally conserved in all branches of the plant kingdom, from glaucophytes to all flowering plants (35, 36). It is possible that the FtsHi complex together with the three chaperone ATPases all contribute to different aspects of the protein translocation process. The FtsHi complex, where present, may increase translocation efficiency or it may be essential under certain growth or stress conditions encountered by some plant species.

The FtsHi complex has been proposed as the motor that pulls preproteins across the chloroplast envelope into the stroma. Essential elements of this model include the formation of a hetero-hexameric ring complex at the inner envelope membrane and a stromal localization of the ATPase domains of its subunits, as depicted in several previous publications (refs. 13–16 and Fig. 1A). However, here, using in vitro imported preproteins, antibodies against the C terminus of endogenous proteins, and epitope tag antibodies against complementing proteins in transgenic plants, we have demonstrated that FtsHi1 has its ATPase domain located in the intermembrane space, not in the stroma, and that FtsHi2 is mostly soluble and does not seem to be stably associated with membranes (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, the subplastidial location of FtsHi4 needs to be verified (19), and the membrane topology and membrane association of the remaining subunits, FtsHi5 and Ycf2, also need to be investigated. Nonetheless, our results on FtsHi1 and FtsHi2 already indicate that the current model (Fig. 1A) of an FtsHi hetero-hexameric ring complex pulling preproteins into the stroma needs to be revised.

The size of the FtsHi complex was estimated to be 2 MDa based on blue native gel electrophoresis (13). However, the largest marker on the gel in that study was 880 kDa, so size estimation was based on migration of the subunits to approximately the same region at the top of the gel. Therefore, the complex may be even larger than 2 MDa and it may actually contain several hetero- and homohexameric subcomplexes. The stoichiometry of the subunits in the FtsHi complex also remains unclear. It has been shown that the abundance of FtsH12 was about twofold those of FtsHi1 and FtsHi2 in a gel slice containing the FtsHi complex, and five-fold that of FtsHi4 (29), further supporting that the region may contain several subcomplexes of differing abundances. These subcomplexes may function at different steps of the import process, with some steps occurring in the intermembrane space and some in the stroma. Nonetheless, AAA+ protein complexes exert a pulling, not pushing, force. Thus, the functions of the FtsHi subunits located in the intermembrane space require further analyses. Since the FtsHi proteins are presumed to lack protease activity, they are unlikely to function in a similar fashion to the mitochondrial intermembrane space iAAA protease YME1 (30, 31). The mitochondrial outer membrane AAA+ protein Msp1 is recruited to clogged TOM (translocase of the outer membrane of mitochondria) complex during stress to dislocate preproteins from the TOM complex (32, 33). It is possible that FtsHi proteins in the chloroplast intermembrane space, such as FtsHi1, may also exert a similar function in un-clogging the TIC complex. Interestingly, the FtsHi complex was proposed to work together with the TIC complex component Ycf1(Tic214) in the stroma to facilitate protein import (13, 16). However, recent structural studies of the TOC–TIC supercomplex from Chlamydomonas have revealed that Ycf1 actually protrudes into the intermembrane space (26, 27), i.e., the same location as determined herein for FtsHi1. Therefore, FtsHi1 may work together with Ycf1 to facilitate protein translocation through the intermembrane space.

None of the six ATPase subunits of the FtsHi complex—FtsHi1, FtsHi2, FtsHi4, FtsHi5, FtsH12, and Ycf2—are found in the Poaceae family of monocots, including rice and maize (34). If the FtsHi complex is the sole motor for chloroplast protein import, it is not clear how the chloroplasts of these plants achieve protein translocation into the stroma. It has been proposed that these plants have evolved to use the sole remaining FtsHi protein, FtsHi3, as their chloroplast motor protein (16). This hypothesis remains to be tested. Nevertheless, the three chaperone proteins first shown to be important for import, i.e., Hsp93, cpHsc70, and Hsp90C, are universally conserved in all branches of the plant kingdom, from glaucophytes to all flowering plants (35, 36). It is possible that the FtsHi complex together with the three chaperone ATPases all contribute to different aspects of the protein translocation process. The FtsHi complex, where present, may increase translocation efficiency or it may be essential under certain growth or stress conditions encountered by some plant species.
