Aberrant remodeling of DNA damage repair pathways after radiotherapy constitutes
a primary driver of tumor radioresistance and compromised therapeutic efficacy.
Ubiquitination has been reported as a critical post-translational modification
governing the DNA damage response and repair cascade. However, the precise
molecular mechanisms underlying ubiquitination-mediated radioresistance remain
poorly characterized. The E3 ubiquitin ligase Deltex 3 (DTX3) is validated as a
negative regulator of tumor radioresistance that improves radiotherapy efficacy
in vitro and in vivo models. Mechanistically, DTX3 physically interacts with
nucleophosmin 3 (NPM3), catalyzes K27-linked polyubiquitination of NPM3 at the
Lys102 residue, and consequently triggers proteasomal degradation of NPM3. Loss
of NPM3 further destabilizes the RUVBL1-TIP60 complex, diminishes H4K16
acetylation, and impairs homologous recombination repair. Consistently, elevated
NPM3 expression counteracts DTX3-mediated radiosensitization. Clinically, low
DTX3 levels are correlated with inferior overall survival and elevated
post-radiotherapy recurrence across multiple human malignancies. The study
reveals DTX3 as a potent suppressor of homologous recombination repair and a
promising therapeutic candidate to overcome tumor radioresistance.

Introduction

Radiotherapy (RT) is a cornerstone of cancer treatment and the second most common therapeutic modality after surgery. Despite its widespread use, radioresistance remains a major clinical challenge and a key determinant of therapeutic efficacy. DNA damage repair (DDR) pathways regulate tumor radiosensitivity and are central to the development of radioresistance [1]. Radiation‐induced DNA double‐strand breaks (DSBs) are primarily repaired through nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) and homologous recombination repair (HRR), with NHEJ being error‐prone and HRR providing high fidelity [2]. Dysregulated expression of factors involved in NHEJ or HRR can confer radioresistance and promote tumor progression [3, 4]. Therefore, targeting DDR signaling pathways has emerged as a promising strategy to overcome tumor radioresistance [5].

The ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) is a dynamic regulatory network that controls protein stability and function through coordinated ubiquitination and deubiquitination. As a major post‐translational modification system, the UPS regulates protein turnover and signaling pathways, thereby influencing DNA repair, metabolic reprogramming, immune evasion, and cellular stress responses. These processes collectively modulate tumor radiosensitivity [6]. For example, USP7 deubiquitinates and stabilizes TREX1, leading to excessive clearance of cytosolic double‐stranded DNA and attenuation of the immune response, thereby promoting radioresistance [7]. USP18 regulates the TRIM21–TRIM29 axis via ubiquitination to enhance radioresistance in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) [3]. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, NEDD4L‐mediated ubiquitination of KLF5 suppresses radioresistance [8]. In addition, the E3 ligase TRIM21 promotes ubiquitination and degradation of MLPH, thereby reducing radioresistance in glioblastomas [9]. We previously demonstrated that BRCC3 deubiquitinates and stabilizes PPP1CC, activates DNA‐PKcs, and enhances radiation‐induced NHEJ repair, thereby increasing radioresistance in NPC [10]. Collectively, these findings highlight the central role of ubiquitination and deubiquitination in regulating radiosensitivity. Targeting components of the UPS may therefore provide novel strategies to overcome tumor radioresistance [11]. However, the specific roles and mechanisms of E3 ligases in DDR signaling and radioresistance remain incompletely defined.

The human Deltex protein family comprises five members with E3 ubiquitin ligase activity: DTX1, DTX2, DTX3, DTX3L, and DTX4. To date, studies investigating the involvement of the Deltex family in DNA repair remain limited, with most research focusing on DTX2 and DTX3L. For example, DTX2 recognizes and recruits ADP‐ribosylated proteins through its C‐terminal domain (CTD) and ubiquitinates proteins involved in DDR, thereby modulating the DDR process [12]. A recent study by Djerir et al. further demonstrated that DTX2 is recruited to DSB sites and stably retained there through its WWE domain and conserved C‐terminal Deltex domain, where it participates in HRR‐dependent DSB repair [13]. DTX3L, another member of the Deltex family, monoubiquitinates histone H4 at lysine 91, thereby facilitating the recruitment of 53BP1, and enhancing cellular sensitivity to DNA‐damaging agents [14]. Overexpression of DTX3L promotes the ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of Tudor interacting repair regulator (TIRR), thereby abolishing the inhibitory effect of TIRR on 53BP1. This process reduces HRR efficiency and renders prostate cancer cells susceptible to poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors [15]. Moreover, DTX3L interacts with BAL1 and recruits BRCA1 in a PARP1‐dependent manner to regulate DSB repair [16]. Collectively, these findings indicate that Deltex family proteins, particularly DTX2 and DTX3L, play important roles in the DDR pathway and DSB repair. Nevertheless, no previous studies have reported a functional role for DTX3 in DDR.

In this study, by analyzing RNA sequencing data, we screened Deltex 3 (DTX3) as an E3 ligase that may reverse radioresistance. Further functional experiments confirmed that the overexpression of DTX3 reversed the radioresistance of tumor cells. Mechanistically, DTX3 mediates the ubiquitination and degradation of nucleophosmin 3 (NPM3), thereby disrupting the formation of the RuvB‐like protein 1‐lysine acetyltransferase (RUVBL1–TIP60) complex, reducing histone H4K16 acetylation, and impairing HRR. Clinically, low DTX3 expression is associated with poor RT‐related prognosis. These findings highlight the critical role of DTX3 in DDR signaling and radiosensitivity and identify it as a potential therapeutic target to overcome tumor radioresistance.

Discussion and Conclusions

Our study identified regulators of HRR suppression following RT. DTX3 was identified as a key molecule suppressing HRR and reversing tumor radioresistance via ubiquitination‐mediated regulation of the NPM3–RUVBL1 axis. Mechanistically, DTX3 binds NPM3 and facilitates the ubiquitin‐dependent degradation of NPM3, thereby inhibiting the formation of the RUVBL1–TIP60 complex, reducing histone H4K16 acetylation, and consequently decreasing HRR efficiency. Clinically, low DTX3 expression is associated with poor prognosis and correlates with locoregional recurrence after RT. Our findings provided mechanistic support for DTX3, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, as a modulator of tumor radiosensitivity and identified a potential target to overcome tumor radioresistance (Figure 8H).

DTX3 is a member of the DTX family, with a C‐terminal RING‐type E3 ligase domain, a motif rich in prolines, and an N‐terminal WWE‐rich region. Notably, the structure of DTX3 contains a special truncated NTD that lacks homology with other DTX proteins [28]. DTX3 is primarily expressed in the nucleus and plays a dual role in the invasion, metastasis, and proliferation of various tumors. As a tumor suppressor, DTX3 ubiquitinates NOTCH2 to inhibit the proliferation and migration of esophageal cancer cells [29]. In triple‐negative breast cancer, the DTX3‐mediated ubiquitination of NOTCH4 is diminished by DCAF13, resulting in enhanced tumor invasion and migration [30]. In papillary thyroid carcinoma, DTX3 promotes the ubiquitination of XRCC5 to suppress epithelial–mesenchymal transition [31]. In bladder cancer, DTX3 suppresses invasion and metastasis by degrading NICD, thereby inhibiting Notch signaling [32]. Conversely, Wang et al. reported that DTX3, as an oncogenic factor, promotes ovarian cancer growth by stabilizing mutant p53 through disruption of the MDM2–mutant p53 interaction [33]. These diverse functions highlight the multifaceted roles of DTX3, likely arising from substrate selectivity in different cellular contexts. In this study, we identified two notable characteristics of DTX3: first, it is strongly associated with RT response and tumor radioresistance; second, it inhibits RUVBL1–TIP60 complex formation by targeting and promoting the K27‐linked ubiquitination‐mediated degradation of NPM3, thereby preventing DDR signal transduction. Our findings contributed to a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms by which DTX3 inhibits tumor radioresistance and further illustrated the complexity of ubiquitination regulatory networks in tumor progression.

Such opposing tumor‐suppressive and oncogenic activities indicate that DTX3 functions in a context‐dependent manner, influenced by intrinsic cellular characteristics and extrinsic environmental signals. Our findings suggest that DNA damage stress may be an important factor influencing DTX3 function. Under irradiation, DTX3 preferentially interacts with NPM3, promoting its degradation and thereby inhibiting RUVBL1‐TIP60 complex formation. This process reduces H4K16 acetylation and impairs HRR. NPM3 represents a newly identified substrate of DTX3, adding to the panel of known DTX3 substrates including NOTCH family proteins, XRCC5 and mutant p53. Here, several factors may influence the functional consequences of DTX3 activity, including (1) NPM3 expression levels in tumor cells, (2) p53 status, and (3) the availability of specific substrates within the tumor microenvironment. In tumors with high NPM3 expression and intact p53 signaling, DTX3‐mediated NPM3 degradation may contribute to tumor suppression and enhance radiosensitivity. In contrast, in tumors with low NPM3 expression, mutant p53 mutations, or other predominant DTX3 substrates, such as NOTCH family proteins, XRCC5, or mutant p53, DTX3 may exert different biological effects through alternative substrates. Further studies are needed to determine how these factors interact to shape the biological functions of DTX3 and whether this context dependence affects its potential as a therapeutic target across different tumor types.

Ubiquitination regulates multiple cellular processes. Although canonical K48‐ and K63‐linked ubiquitination are well characterized, noncanonical ubiquitination remains poorly understood. In recent years, K27‐linked ubiquitination has attracted broad attention because of its diverse biological functions across various processes, including protein degradation, selective autophagy, regulation of kinase activity, subcellular localization of proteins, protein–protein interactions, and signal transduction [34]. Various E3 ligases promote substrate degradation by mediating K27‐linked ubiquitination. For example, DTX2 degrades TfR1 via K27‐linked ubiquitination to prevent sepsis‐induced myocardial injury [35]. The E3 ubiquitin ligase MARCH2 promotes K27‐linked ubiquitination and degradation of TNF‐R1 at lysine 340 [36]. The E3 ligase MYLIP targets HIF‐1α and catalyzes K27‐linked polyubiquitination, subsequently inducing proteasomal degradation of HIF‐1α [37]. We revealed that the E3 ligase DTX3 mediates NPM3 degradation through K27‐linked ubiquitination and participates in DSB repair signaling, providing mechanistic insights into DDR induction by irradiation.

The NPM family includes NPM1/2/3 and participates in chromatin remodeling, genomic stability, ribosome biogenesis, DNA replication, and transcription, with significant implications for tumorigenesis [38]. NPM3 is a predominantly nuclear, nucleic acid–binding protein associated with chromatin [39]. NPM3 mediates immune escape in gastric cancer by promoting the transcriptional activation of NPM1 [40]; NPM3 also promotes the proliferation and migration of human LUAD cells in vitro [41]. Gu et al. found that NPM3 is overexpressed in prostate cancer and may serve as a marker of disease invasiveness [42]. However, to date, there are no reports on the function and mechanism of NPM3 in tumor radiosensitivity. Here, NPM3 was identified as a novel ubiquitin‐substrate of DTX3, which is associated with enhanced radioresistance. DTX3 mediates ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of NPM3, inhibiting HRR and overcoming tumor radioresistance. This finding expands our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms by which NPM3 modulates tumor radioresistance.

RUVBL1 is an AAA+ ATPase that participates in diverse chromatin remodeling functions [43]. It plays crucial roles in both NHEJ and HRR. Ubiquitinated RUVBL1 can interact with β‐catenin, thereby enhancing NHEJ repair in breast cancer cells [4]. Additionally, RUVBL1 is typically associated with RuvB‐like protein 2 and TIP60, localizes to the nucleus, and promotes the formation of H4K16Ac, which suppresses the binding of 53BP1 to DSB sites and promotes HRR [23]. Our findings indicated that RUVBL1 directly interacts with NPM3, and the DTX3–NPM3 axis inhibits the formation of the RUVBL1–TIP60 complex, thereby reducing H4K16 acetylation and promoting the recruitment of 53BP1 to DSB, which impedes HRR. Overall, we delineated the regulatory effects of the DTX3–NPM3 axis on RUVBL1–TIP60 complex formation and its potential mechanism in modulating HRR.

Although this study elucidated the role of the DTX3–NPM3–RUVBL1 axis in reversing tumor radioresistance, some limitations should be acknowledged and addressed in future studies. First, clinical evidence is largely derived from a single‐center cohort and public datasets; validation in multicenter cohorts spanning diverse tumor types is needed to confirm the universality and specificity of the axis expression patterns and radiosensitivity relationships. Second, our experimental systems predominantly rely on in vitro cell assays and subcutaneous xenograft models, which may not fully recapitulate the complexity of human tumors in vivo; future studies should incorporate orthotopic tumor models and patient‐derived xenografts to better simulate RT responses in a human physiological context. Third, although we demonstrated that DTX3 reverses radioresistance by degrading NPM3 and enhances radiosensitivity, the clinical translational potential and feasibility remain to be systematically evaluated. Future efforts should focus on specificity‐oriented interventions that promote DTX3 expression or augment its E3 ligase activity and assess therapeutic efficacy, safety, and the potential for resistance development.

To conclude, our data suggested an important role of the DTX3–NPM3–RUVBL1 axis in the regulation of tumor DDR signaling and radiosensitivity (Figure 8H). Low DTX3 expression is associated with poor response to RT, suggesting that DTX3 may serve as a biomarker and therapeutic target for inhibiting radioresistance in cancer, warranting further investigation as a potential combination therapy with RT.
