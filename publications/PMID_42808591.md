# Targeted mutagenesis of a thermophilic mevalonate kinase for efficient catalysis at lower temperatures.

Thermophile-derived enzymes are of interest for cell-free biocatalysis due to
their intrinsic thermostability, which simplifies purification via heat
treatment and correlates with enhanced activity retention under cell-free
reaction conditions. However, thermal stability often comes at the expense of
slower turnover rates for reactions that need to be conducted at ambient
temperature out of concern for cofactor stability and to reduce energy
requirements. This study demonstrates a targeted engineering strategy to
increase the room temperature activity of the thermophilic mevalonate kinase
from Methanocaldococcus jannaschii. Site-saturation mutagenesis of
evolutionarily variable residues within the mevalonate binding pocket generated
numerous mutants with increased activity at 22°C. Fifteen mutants demonstrated a
two- to four-fold increase in activity, and the most active mutant, I10S,
achieved a five-fold increase in kcat relative to the wild-type. Importantly,
the I10S mutant retained thermostability comparable to the wild-type, and a
minimal decline in long-term stability was observed. This mutagenesis strategy
may have broader applications towards tuning thermophilic enzyme activity at
desired operating temperatures to enhance the efficiency of cell-free reactions
while reducing operating costs.

Introduction

Isoprenoids are a diverse class of molecules with valuable applications ranging from pharmaceuticals, to alternative bioplastics and biofuels (George et al., 2015). There has been great interest in developing synthesis methods for isoprenoids to replace current production methods which involve either extraction from plants or using complex chemical syntheses (Quílez del Moral et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020). These existing methods often rely on toxic solvents and result in low yields due to the structural complexity of isoprenoids. In the last few decades, biocatalysis has developed as an alternative production strategy promising higher yields, cleaner processes, and more cost-effective synthesis methods for isoprenoid production.

Biocatalytic production of isoprenoids has traditionally emplo-yed the mevalonate pathway, derived from non-plant organisms, for production of isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP), a universal building block for isoprenoid products (Lange et al., 2000; Eisenreich et al., 2004). In bacteria and eukaryotes, the mevalonate pathway converts Acetyl-CoA, taken from glycolysis, to IPP (Lange et al., 2000). IPP is then isomerized to dimethylallyl pyrophosphate (DMAPP), and recombination of these building blocks results in highly varied isoprenoid, or terpene, products (Wang et al., 2019). Metabolic engineering has been applied towards the synthesis of diverse isoprenoids, with modest success, typically resulting in yields on the order of 0.01–1 g/L (Chang & Keasling, 2006; Immethun et al., 2013; Kang et al., 2024). Drawbacks of engineered whole-cell factories include competing pathways that limit yield, as well as the toxicity of intermediates or products, which results in limited production titers (Brennan et al., 2012; Chubukov et al., 2015; George et al., 2018). As an alternative to in vivo isoprenoid production, cell-free biocatalysis promises higher yields and mitigated toxicity concerns (GEN Biotechnology, n.d.). Foundational work by Rodriguez et al. demonstrated that cell-free reconstitution of the mevalonate pathway could produce IPP from mevalonate, at high concentrations up to 40 g/L (Rodriguez & Leyh, 2014). Further iteration since has resulted in titers of close to 15 g/L of monoterpene products starting from glucose, employing a pathway of over 25 enzymes, and demonstrating an order of magnitude improvement in titers as compared to in vivo systems (Korman et al., 2017).

Despite several cases of high titer production of isoprenoids through cell-free biocatalysis, industrial adoption of cell-free manufacturing is still limited. Enzyme instability poses a major challenge in cell-free catalysis, as denaturation results in loss of productivity and increased costs. Significant efforts have been dedicated to engineering mesophilic enzymes for improved stability (Shi et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2019). Engineering approaches, ranging from rational design to machine learning platforms such as PROSS, have been utilized to introduce stabilizing features such as hydrophobic core packing, hydrogen bonding, and backbone rigidity to improve enzyme stability (Goldenzweig et al., 2016). Stabilization efforts of mesophilic enzymes may be hindered by the stability-activity tradeoff, in which enhanced stability often results in reduced activity rates of the target enzyme (Liu et al., 2019). As an alternative starting point, enzymes derived from thermophilic organisms are of interest for cell-free systems, due to their evolved stabilizing features, such as denser core-packing and increased intermolecular bonding, which enable them to withstand extreme temperatures (Atalah et al., 2019; Littlechild, 2011). Thermotolerant enzymes enable rapid and scalable biocatalyst purification using heat treatment (Pessela et al., 2004; Ren et al., 2007). Beyond thermostability, the stabilizing features of thermophile-derived enzymes make them more resistant to other challenges found in cell-free catalysis (Atalah et al., 2019; Vieille & Zeikus, 2001). For example, thermophile-derived enzymes tend to be more resilient when challenged with solvents (Sherkhanov et al., 2020; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1369703x09002964  n.d.). Additionally, thermophilic enzymes may also retain activity for longer at moderate temperatures as compared to mesophilic counterparts, however experimental validation of this trend is still limited (Sarnik et al., 2025). Greater long-term activity retention would enable longer reaction lifetimes, recycling of the enzyme for multiple reactions, or even enabling continuous flow biocatalysis (Broumidis et al., 2025).

Despite offering numerous advantages, thermophile-derived enzyme turnover rates at ambient operating temperatures are typically lower compared to those of their mesophilic counterparts (Vieille & Zeikus, 2001; Bae & Phillips, 2004; Georlette et al., 2003). Often, ambient temperature is used for one-pot reactions to reduce energy demands, maintain cofactor stability, or ensure the stability of non-thermostable enzymes essential to the cascade (Sherkhanov et al., 2020). In such cases, the operating temperature may be significantly lower than the evolved temperature optima of the enzyme. This suboptimal performance necessitates higher enzyme loadings to match desired turnover rates, thereby increasing enzyme production burden and even potentially introducing colloidal instability (Mao et al., 2025).

Previously, the lower mevalonate pathway was ruggedized by replacing mesophilic enzymes with thermophilic variants, resulting in improved stability and productivity compared to the mesophilic pathway; however, the thermophilic enzymes exhibited slower reaction rates than the mesophilic enzymes (Sarnik et al., 2025). Specifically, the thermophile-derived mevalonate kinase from Methanocaldococcus jannaschii (MjMvk) was found to be five-fold slower at 22°C as compared to the mesophile-derived mevalonate kinase from Methanosarcina Mazei (Sarnik et al., 2025). To overcome this limitation, we investigated an enzyme engineering strategy for increasing the turnover rate of the thermophile-derived MjMvk at ambient operating temperatures.

Several efforts have focused on engineering thermophilic enzymes to increase their activity at ambient temperatures, using either directed mutagenesis or more targeted strategies (Li et al., 2017; Akanuma et al., 2019). For example, Akanuma et al. aligned the sequences of a thermophilic enzyme and its mesophilic counterpart, subsequently substituting active site residues in the thermophile with corresponding residues from the mesophilic enzyme. This approach required extensive mutagenesis within a broad radius around the active site to produce a more active mutant. Here, we employ an alternative strategy, focusing on a limited number of residue positions, and applying site-saturation mutagenesis to comprehensively explore the effect of all amino acid substitutions at each position. First, a multiple sequence alignment of archaeal mevalonate kinases was conducted, focusing on residues within a 5 Å radius of the mevalonate binding site, to identify binding-site residues that were not evolutionarily conserved. Site-saturation of these non-conserved residues resulted in identification of sixteen mutants with activity greater than the wild-type (WT) at 22°C. The most active mutant had over five-fold increased activity, ten-fold increase in KM, and only minimal destabilization as compared to the WT. The increased turnover rate of MjMvk at the desired operating conditions will serve to mitigate enzyme production burden and improve cell-free cascade efficiency.

Identification of target mutagenic residues in the mevalonate binding pocket

The number of potential mutation targets was reduced by focusing on amino acids within the substrate binding pocket (approximately one hydration shell around mevalonate) that are not evolutionarily conserved, thereby minimizing the risk of altering essential catalytic residues. Site-saturation of each non-conserved residue position was then conducted to assess the range of functional diversity achievable.

Initially, a crystallized MjMvk structure (PDB: 1KKH) was aligned with a previously crystallized archaeal mevalonate kinase bound to mevalonate, from Methanosarcina Mazei, (PDB: 6MDE), to determine the likely mevalonate orientation within MjMvk (Fig. S1) (Yang et al., 2002; Miller and Kung, 2018). The alignment RMSD was 0.942, indicating high similarity between the structures. Residues located within 5 Å, approximately one hydration-shell, of the mevalonate molecule were identified as binding-site residues. Conservation of these 14 residues was then determined using multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of archaeal mevalonate kinases (Fig. 1). Over 2000 sequences from the UniProt Database were aligned utilizing the ClustalOmega program. Conservation of each residue was determined based on frequency of appearance at that position (Fig. 1B, Fig. S2) (Madeira et al., 2024a). The WT MjMvk sequence has a lysine at residue position 8. In the MSA, lysine was found to be very highly conserved, as it appeared in approximately 90% of sequences at this equivalent position. In contrast, position 10 exhibited more amino acid diversity. Isoleucine was the most frequently observed residue at this position; however, tyrosine and phenylalanine also appeared in a substantial portion of sequences. A cutoff of <70% evolutionary conservation for non-conserved residues was selected to allow mutagenesis of residues with evolutionary bias (>50% conservation) while minimizing the risk of altering essential catalytic residues, which are likely to be highly conserved. Residues at positions 10, 154, and 196 exhibited less than 70% conservation, classifying them as non-conserved. Consequently, these residues were selected as targets for mutagenesis, shown in blue in Fig. 1C. The mechanism underlying the increased activity rates in I10 mutants was investigated by measuring enzyme kinetics and in silico analysis. In many cases, increased activity at low temperatures can be attributed to increased enzyme flexibility, at the cost of thermal stability (Isaksen et al. 2016). However, the I10S mutant displays higher catalytic efficiency without sacrifice of thermal stability. Thus, the targeted strategy of mutating non-conserved residues preserved global stability of the enzyme, suggesting the apparent increased activity is due to an alternate mechanism. One possibility is that mutations at the I10 residue modify the enzyme-substrate binding dynamics, facilitating more efficient substrate-product exchange at 22°C. This may be explained by the evolution of WT MjMvk to bind tightly to mevalonate to counteract entropic barriers to substrate binding at its optimal operating temperature of 70°C. A similar trend has been observed in enzymes derived from psychrophiles (organisms that grow at temperatures below 20°C), which tend to exhibit higher KM values compared to enzymes from mesophiles (Gerday, 2013). Importantly, high affinity for the substrate may relate to increased affinity for the product, which in turn reduces substrate-product turnover. This mechanism is consistent with the apparent increase in KM for the I10S mutant compared to WT. It is possible that the decreased substrate affinity of the I10S mutant may enable more efficient turnover at ambient temperatures, where entropic forces are reduced. Alternatively, the I10S mutation may affect activity by altering solvation of the active site or changing the substrate binding orientation. Minimal distances between the position 10 residue and mevalonate are indicated for each mutant. Isoleucine has the closest interaction to mevalonate, at 3.8 Å. The I10A mutant also presents a hydrophobic moiety, however the residue is smaller, now interacting with mevalonate at a distance of 5.7 Å. This lessened hydrophobic interaction may decrease affinity for mevalonate, thus resulting in the intermediate phenotype of I10A. Lastly, the I10S mutant has a minimum distance of 5.5 Å between polar groups on the serine and mevalonate molecule. At this distance it is possible repulsive forces are destabilizing the binding of mevalonate, resulting in the increased KM and corresponding higher kcat.

Developing a targeted engineering strategy for increasing MjMvk activity at 22°C

Increasing the turnover rates of the thermophile-derived MjMvk under ambient operating conditions could improve cell-free biocatalytic efficiency by decreasing enzyme production burden. To this end, we employed a site-saturation mutagenesis approach targeting non-conserved residues within the substrate-binding pocket, aiming to identify variants with enhanced catalytic activity. Residues within the mevalonate binding pocket were identified based on proximity to mevalonate, specifically within a 5 Å (or one-hydration shell) radius. Expanding the radius of residues around mevalonate, could help reveal more beneficial mutations, however at the cost of increasing experimental burden. With the 5 Å radius applied in this study, 14 residues were identified as ‘binding site residues’ and potential targets for mutagenesis.

Conservation analysis of the binding-site residues with an MSA of MjMvk against other archaeal mevalonate kinases revealed 93% conservation of the aspartic acid at position 155 in MjMvk. D155 is a well-established catalytic residue essential for coordinating the magnesium ion during mevalonate phosphorylation activity (Yang et al., 2002; McClory et al., 2019). This finding (i.e. D155 exhibiting <100% conservation) was expected, as some sequences in the Uniprot database are truncated, and there is a possibility of incorrect classifications as mevalonate kinases (Table S1). Nonetheless, the strong conservation of this key residue confirmed the accuracy of the high-throughput alignments and conservation analysis. Performing an MSA across thousands of mevalonate kinases (all archaeal sequences available in UniProt) allowed for the identification of residues with subtle plasticity, such as I10 with 58% conservation, which may not be apparent when aligning fewer sequences, as is standard for MSAs.

To select non-conserved residues, the <70% conservation cutoff allowed for mutagenesis of evolutionarily biased residues (>50% conserved). However, the upper limit of 70% minimized chances of mutating catalytically necessary residues. Future work investigating higher limits for conservation (e.g. mutating residues less than 80%, or 90% conserved) could help determine the upper limit where conservation directly relates to catalytic necessity. Using the <70% cutoff for conservation, residues I10, T154 and R196 were found to be non-conserved, and thus the final targets for mutagenesis. It was unexpected that R196 was a non-conserved residue, as a previously proposed mechanism by Yang et al. suggested this residue was involved in hydrogen-bonding with mevalonate (Yang et al., 2002; Chu et al., 2003). The other non-conserved residues, I10 and T154 had not been previously implicated as having a role in MjMvk activity.

It is possible that combining these top mutations results in incorrect binding orientation or otherwise prevents binding of the substrate rendering the enzyme inactive. Furthermore, when the I10S mutant was used as the parent for site-saturation mutagenesis of the T154 position, no mutants were identified with significantly improved activity over the I10S mutant (Fig. 3B-C). It was not entirely surprising that mutations within the binding pocket may not have cooperative effects that result in increased activity rates. Epistatic effects of mutations have been previously described, especially when mutating several residues within a small distance of each other (Starr & Thornton, 2016; Khersonsky et al., 2018). Given the elusiveness of cooperative mutations, our approach may be limited to the identification of beneficial single mutants; however, this has the advantage of reducing the likelihood of destabilizing the protein by minimizing the number of mutated residues. For engineering targets requiring greater activity improvements, machine-learning approaches such as FuncLib, designed for active site optimization, could help identify cooperative combinations of mutations without imposing an immense in vitro experimental burden (Khersonsky et al., 2018). Alternatively, high-throughput screening methods or selections may be necessary to enable massive engineering campaigns.

Characterization of stability of the I10S mutant confirmed that thermostability of the enzyme was not affected. No significant difference in response to heat treatment indicates the I10S mutation did not disrupt the thermostable structures in MjMvk (Fig. 4A) (Li et al., 2005). Long-term activity retention at ambient incubation temperatures was evaluated as a proxy for operating lifetime of the biocatalyst. This metric highlights the potential of MjMvk, or the I10S variant, for extended production runs, reuse across multiple batch reactions, or even enabling flow reactions without the need for constant replenishment of fresh enzyme. The long-term incubations at 22°C revealed the I10S mutant had lower activity retention over time compared to the WT. Importantly, despite the decline in activity over time, the I10S mutant retained over two-fold higher specific activity as compared to the WT, for up to 30 days of incubation. As such, the overall productivity of the I10S mutant would still surpass that of the WT for at least 30 days of use.

Kinetic analysis of the I10S mutant revealed a five-fold increase in kcat and a ten-fold increase in KM relative to the wild-type enzyme. Comparing these parameters to other mevalonate kinases is challenging due to the limited availability of kinetic data at 22°C (Table S3). Currently, the only reported value is for the mesophilic M. mazei mevalonate kinase, which exhibits a kcat of 1 s−1 at 22°C, approximately twofold higher than that of the I10S mutant (Sarnik et al., 2025).

In contrast, other mesophilic mevalonate kinases typically achieve kcat values between 10 to 30 s−1 (at temperatures of 25 to 37°C) and KM values ranging from 0.02 to 0.12 mM. These values correspond to catalytic efficiencies kcat / KM of 60 to 1080 s−1 mM−1. By comparison, the I10S mutant displayed a more modest kcat / KM of 2.3 s−1 mM−1when measured at 22°C. As the characterization of thermophile-derived mevalonate kinases remains limited, the I10S variant exhibits, to the best of our knowledge, the highest turnover rate for a thermophilic mevalonate kinase at this temperature. Given that thermophile-derived enzymes are essential for workflows involving heat-purification, this variant represents a valuable catalyst for the development of cost-effective cell-free production platforms.

ConclusionsIn conclusion, a targeted engineering strategy focusing on non-conserved residues within the substrate binding pocket increased ambient-temperature activity in a thermophile-derived enzyme. This approach generated 16 mutants with activity greater than WT MjMvk. However, a limitation of targeting a small region, such as the substrate binding pocket, is the potential for epistatic interactions between mutations. While numerous highly active single mutants were identified, combining top mutations did not yield cooperative effects. The most active single-mutant, I10S, demonstrated a five-fold increase in kcat at 22°C without compromising thermostability. This improvement in catalytic rate, at the desired operating temperature, reduces the enzyme production demand by lowering the enzyme quantity needed per cell-free reaction. While we have demonstrated the utility of this approach for MjMvk, further work is necessary to understand how broadly applicable this engineering approach is for other thermostable enzymes. Ultimately, this approach may have significant implications in improving the overall efficiency of cell-free biocatalytic processes that leverage the enhanced stability of thermostable enzyme variants and moreover, may have application in enhancing the utility of thermostable enzymes in other applications, including environmental biosensing.

Activity assay

A coupled assay using pyruvate kinase and lactate dehydrogenase (Pyk-Ldh mixture, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to track ATP consumption based on NADH (reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) oxidation resulting in a decrease of absorbance at 340 nm.
