# CRISPR-mediated proximity labeling unveils ABHD14B as a host factor to regulate HBV cccDNA transcriptional activity.

BACKGROUND: The long-term goal of chronic hepatitis B research is a functional
cure (HBsAg seroclearance). Although currently used nucleos(t)ide analogs can
efficiently inhibit viral replication, they do not reduce viral RNAs or proteins
produced from covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA), and rarely achieve a
functional cure. To overcome this situation, revealing the mode of the existence
of cccDNA is required, including identifying the interreacting proteins with
cccDNA. Here, we aimed to identify novel proteins that interact with cccDNA.
METHODS: Using an in vitro HBV infection model and a sequence-specific proximity
labelling method consisting of dead Cas9 and biotin identification (BioID2), we
comprehensively determined proteins that possibly interact with cccDNA. After
identifying the candidate proteins, the HBV RNA transcription levels were
examined by knocking out the associated genes.
RESULTS: We identified ABHD14B as a protein that interacts with cccDNA and
inhibits HBV RNA transcription from cccDNA. ABHD14B decreases the acetylation
levels of histone proteins that control the transcription levels of HBV RNA in
cccDNA. Moreover, ABHD14B interacts with TFII-I, which binds directly to cccDNA
in a sequence-dependent manner. These results suggest that the host protein,
ABHD14B, is recruited to cccDNA via the TFII-I protein, inhibiting HBV RNA
transcription from cccDNA by deacetylating cccDNA histones.
CONCLUSIONS: ABHD14B was newly identified as a suppressor of HBV RNA
transcription from cccDNA, which may improve our understanding of the mode of
existence of cccDNA, providing a basis for development of a functional cure.

INTRODUCTION

HBV, a member of the Hepadnaviridae family, is a partially dsDNA virus that primarily infects hepatocytes.
1
 On entry into the hepatocyte nucleus, the relaxed circular viral genome is repaired and converted into a covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) minichromosome. cccDNA serves as a transcriptional template for viral RNAs, including pregenomic RNA and subgenomic RNAs that encode viral proteins.1,2 Although current antiviral treatments, such as nucleos(t)ide analogs, can efficiently inhibit viral reverse transcription, they do not substantially reduce viral RNAs or proteins produced from cccDNA.
3
 This shortfall is problematic, considering viral RNAs and proteins can directly induce hepatic inflammation and carcinogenesis.1–3 In fact, patients who experience a functional cure (HBsAg seroclearance) have a more favorable clinical course with a reduced risk of hepatic complications, including HCC.1–3 Thus, a functional cure is the long-term goal of research on chronic hepatitis B treatment.
4
 Therefore, a drug that can target HBV cccDNA to reduce viral RNAs and protein expression is urgently required. However, the mechanism by which HBV cccDNA stably exists in the hepatocyte nucleus and is influenced by host factors remains unclear.

Research on HBV infection has been hampered by the lack of a robust cell culture system that can efficiently replicate the viral life cycle. However, a substantial breakthrough was achieved with the identification of sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP) as an entry receptor for HBV.
5
 This discovery led to the establishment of human cell lines that overexpress NTCP, permissive to HBV infection, allowing us to thoroughly study the complete viral life cycle in vitro.

Proximity-dependent biotin identification (BioID) is used to identify proteins in close proximity to the protein of interest in living cells.
6
 In this approach, a biotin ligase, such as BirA, or its engineered variant, such as BioID2,
7
 is fused to the protein of interest. This fusion protein biotinylates proteins in its immediate vicinity, enabling the subsequent purification and identification of biotinylated proteins.

CRISPR was originally discovered in bacteria as a part of the adaptive immune system against viruses and other foreign genetic elements.
8
 The discovery of this natural defense system paved the way for its subsequent adaptation as a powerful gene-editing tool in various organisms, revolutionizing biotechnology. This technology has undergone numerous derivations, making it possible to apply it in various research fields, such as in the development of dead Cas9 (dCas9), which resolves issues with recruiting a specific protein to a specific DNA sequence. Unlike wild-type Cas9, endonuclease activity of dCas9 was engineered to be catalytically inactive.
9
 Despite this inactivation, dCas9 retains its ability to bind to specific DNA sequences guided by complementary single-guide RNA (sgRNA). This feature allows the recruitment of specific proteins to a targeted DNA sequence by fusing dCas9 to effector domains.

By combining the BioID2 enzyme with the programmable DNA-binding capability of the dCas9 protein, biotinylation and identification of proteins associated with specific DNA regions have become possible.
10
 This innovative approach provides a powerful tool for studying protein-DNA interactions at specific DNA locations.

Revealing the mechanisms of cccDNA, including the cccDNA-interacting proteins, may establish a basis for the development of a functional cure for HBV. Therefore, we aimed to identify novel proteins that interact with cccDNA. Leveraging these technologies, such as the HBV in vitro infection model, BioID, and dCas9, we report an approach to identify host proteins that interact with HBV cccDNA, termed HBV-CRISPRassisted identification (HBV-CasID). Our platform identified the host protein ABHD14B as an important host factor that regulates viral transcription from HBV cccDNA. We further revealed the mechanism by which ABHD14B regulates the transcription of HBV cccDNA.
