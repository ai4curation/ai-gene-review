Tuberculosis is a major cause of mortality worldwide, with drug-resistant
strains complicating its treatment. We report the discovery of small-molecule
inhibitors targeting the gyrase of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the bacterium
responsible for tuberculosis. Gyrase, a type II topoisomerase, consists of two
subunits: GyrA, which breaks and rejoins DNA, and GyrB, which contains the
ATPase domain responsible for ATP binding and hydrolysis. An indole scaffold
identified by nuclear magnetic resonance fragment screening was grown into
drug-sized candidates through computational optimization, chemical synthesis and
functional selection. Several indole-containing gyrase inhibitors were found to
be specific for the GyrB subunit of M. tuberculosis. Structural studies using
double electron-electron resonance spectroscopy between the Mn(II) ion in the
catalytic site of GyrB and a nitroxide-labeled inhibitor provided insights into
the potential docking sites of the protein target. Key chemical descriptors
essential for gyrase binding were identified for the small-molecule inhibitors
using data-driven algorithms. Our results demonstrate the effectiveness of
fragment screening and computational optimization in drug discovery. The
inhibitors presented in this study are promising candidates for further
pharmacological studies in vivo.

Introduction

Infection with Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the causative agent of tuberculosis (TB), constitutes a major global health threat, with high morbidity and mortality rates, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (1). The emergence of multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant M. tuberculosis strains worldwide is further complicating the treatment of TB (2–4). In view of the rapid emergence of these drug-resistant M. tuberculosis strains, the task of antibiotic discovery has taken on increased urgency, giving rise to the need for new and effective lead small molecules to serve as clinical candidates for pharmaceutical development (5). Among the lead small molecules under development or in clinical use are those that target M. tuberculosis gyrase, also known as topoisomerase II. DNA gyrase plays a critical role in DNA replication by introducing negative supercoiling into the DNA helix by breaking and rejoining the double-stranded DNA molecule in an ATP-dependent manner (6). The enzyme consists of two subunits, GyrA and GyrB. GyrA contains the active site responsible for DNA cleavage and re-ligation, while GyrB harbors the ATPase activity (7). ATP binds to the GyrB subunit, facilitating the energy-dependent conformational changes required for gyrase activity (8). Manganese-substituted ions bind at the TOPRIM (topoisomerase–primase) domain in GyrB, responsible for ATP hydrolysis and DNA wrapping activities. The active site in GyrB contains a number of acidic residues essential for catalysis and coordinates metal ions necessary for the catalytic mechanism of DNA cleavage and re-ligation (9,10). DNA cleavage and strand passage occur at the active site on GyrA. Since this gyrase activity maintains the stability and proper functioning of the bacterial DNA, it is not surprising that gyrase has been—and continues to be—a promising target for the development of new antibiotics for the treatment of TB (11–15).

The activities of DNA cleavage and ATP hydrolysis by gyrase are interconnected, but inhibiting one may not fully disrupt its overall function. Some inhibitors specifically target ATP hydrolysis by binding to the GyrB subunit, leaving DNA cleavage and supercoiling by GyrA unaffected. Additionally, partial inhibition might still allow some gyrase function through allosteric modulation or conformational changes. These factors lead to variations in the relationship between individual activities and the overall function of gyrase (7,16–21).

In an earlier study, we employed nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) fragment-based and virtual screening (22) to identify small molecules that bind through the TOPRIM fold. This fold is consistently observed in numerous DNA processing enzymes, notably in DnaG-like primase and DNA gyrase (23) (Figure 1A). It was, therefore, anticipated that small-molecule inhibitors binding to the TOPRIM fold would cross-react with multiple enzymes sharing this fold and, as a result, kill the bacteria. Indeed, out of several DnaG-like primase inhibitors tested in our previous study, two also inhibit DNA gyrase (24). These inhibitors underwent optimization through modifications from dicarboxylate to diamide and the addition of electron-withdrawing groups at positions 4, 5, 6 and 7 of the indole ring, resulting in potent dual-action molecules targeting both primase and M. tuberculosis gyrase activities (24).

Building on previous research (24), our current study has identified an inhibitor that selectively targets the GyrB subunit of bacterial gyrase, but not the primase (Figure 1B). Using this inhibitor as a scaffold, we synthesized a set of gyrase-selective molecules and evaluated their inhibitory capabilities. Insights from cheminformatic analysis and double electron–electron resonance spectroscopy (DEER) experiments, respectively, informed our understanding of binding affinity and aided in selecting the best computational docking model for the inhibitor’s binding site on the GyrB subunit.

Results and discussionA small molecule with a unique selectivity for gyrase but not DnaG primaseIn drug discovery, designing small-molecule inhibitors that selectively target metabolic pathways is a key challenge. Optimization of the molecule–target interaction is required to transform a potential inhibitor into a lead compound for drug development. This process involves synthesizing and testing various molecules. In this context, optimizing the above-described indole-containing small-molecule derivatives has been a significant research path in our laboratory (22,24,25). Among the various molecules synthesized in the earlier rounds of optimization, one molecule designated VIIa (the molecular identifier has been changed to a Roman number to distinguish it from the molecule designated 7a in this study) showed unique selectivity for gyrase over DnaG primase (24) (Figure 1A–C). It is this selectivity of molecule VIIa for gyrase versus DnaG primase (Figure 1B) that will constitute the basis for the development of more effective antibiotics with fewer side effects.Design and synthesis of small-molecule libraryTo enhance the performance of molecule VIIa (Figure 1C) as a selective inhibitor of the GyrB subunit, a library of 33 indole derivatives was designed and synthesized (Table 1). Conclusions

The increasing resistance of M. tuberculosis to current treatments emphasizes the critical need for effective anti-TB therapies. Non-quinolone inhibitors such as gepotidacin offer new ways to combat antibiotic resistance in bacterial infections. Ongoing research on bacterial topoisomerase inhibitors shows promise for enhancing antibacterial treatments.

Our study presents the development of antibacterial agents that target the gyrase of M. tuberculosis. Our synthetic approach produced a subset of molecules with improved binding to and inhibition of the GyrB subunit of M. tuberculosis gyrase. By exploring SARs, we identified key chemical pharmacophore features that contribute to the binding and inhibition capabilities of the gyrase inhibitors (Supplementary Figure S5). We revealed that modifications to the pyrazole and pyridine rings, as well as the addition of bulky substituents, significantly impact the molecules’ binding affinity to GyrB. Our findings provide insights into the chemical features important for designing effective inhibitors of GyrB. Some features are important for ATP hydrolysis, and others for supercoiling activity. Although ATP hydrolysis and supercoiling are interconnected processes, they can be influenced by different factors, and their correlation may not always be direct.

Our research focuses on developing small-molecule inhibitors for the gyrase of M. tuberculosis and evaluating candidate molecules for their effectiveness against mycobacteria. This is crucial for improving anti-TB drug design and combating drug-resistant TB. Additional research is required to better understand the coupling between the two activities of DNA gyrase and the mechanisms that regulate them.
