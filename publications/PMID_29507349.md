Viruses employ a range of strategies to counteract the prokaryotic adaptive
immune system, clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats and
CRISPR-associated proteins (CRISPR-Cas), including mutational escape and
physical blocking of enzymatic function using anti-CRISPR proteins (Acrs). Acrs
have been found in many bacteriophages but so far not in archaeal viruses,
despite the near ubiquity of CRISPR-Cas systems in archaea. Here, we report the
functional and structural characterization of two archaeal Acrs from the lytic
rudiviruses, SIRV2 and SIRV3. We show that a 4 kb deletion in the SIRV2 genome
dramatically reduces infectivity in Sulfolobus islandicus LAL14/1 that carries
functional CRISPR-Cas subtypes I-A, I-D and III-B. Subsequent insertion of a
single gene from SIRV3, gp02 (AcrID1), which is conserved in the deleted
fragment, successfully restored infectivity. We demonstrate that AcrID1 protein
inhibits the CRISPR-Cas subtype I-D system by interacting directly with Cas10d
protein, which is required for the interference stage. Sequence and structural
analysis of AcrID1 show that it belongs to a conserved family of compact,
dimeric αβ-sandwich proteins characterized by extreme pH and temperature
stability and a tendency to form protein fibres. We identify about 50 homologues
of AcrID1 in four archaeal viral families demonstrating the broad distribution
of this group of anti-CRISPR proteins.

## Full Text

Prokaryotes have evolved multiple strategies to combat mobile genetic elements (MGEs) such as viruses and plasmids1. Of particular interest are the CRISPR–Cas adaptive immune systems, which are present in many bacteria and almost all archaea2–4. Short fragments of DNA originating from MGEs, known as spacers, are incorporated into CRISPR arrays between repeat sequences (a process dubbed ‘adaptation’) and subsequently transcribed and processed into mature CRISPR RNAs (crRNAs)5–8. During the ‘interference’ stage, the mature crRNA and a set of Cas proteins form a complex that recognizes and inactivates MGE-containing sequences complementary to the spacer portion of the crRNA by cleaving the MGE genome2,9. On the basis of distinct architectures of the effector complexes that are involved in interference, the CRISPR–Cas systems have been divided into class 1 and class 2, each encompassing three types (class 1, types I, III and IV; class 2, types II, V and VI), with several subtypes for each type2,4.

In the arms race between viruses and their hosts, viruses evade CRISPR–Cas immunity via mutational escape. In addition, some bacteriophages have been shown to encode anti-CRISPR (Acr) proteins that inhibit CRISPR–Cas by binding directly to and inhibiting specific subunits of the effector complexes10–12. The Acrs identified to date originate primarily from temperate bacteriophages, prophages and genomic islands but also from virulent phages associated with some bacterial phyla. The Acrs form 21 families of small proteins that appear to evolve fast, presumably as a result of the arms race with CRISPR–Cas systems, and have no detectable homologues in the current protein databases13. Such proteins have so far been shown to inhibit bacterial CRISPR–Cas systems of subtypes I-E, I-F, II-A and II-C11,13–18. Although CRISPR–Cas systems are encoded in almost all archaeal genomes, no Acrs have so far been identified in the genomes of the numerous known archaeal viruses or other MGEs19.

The crenarchaeon Sulfolobus islandicus LAL14/1 is a laboratory host for the lytic rod-shaped virus SIRV220. The LAL14/1 genome encodes five CRISPR arrays, four interference cas gene cassettes (one subtype I-A, one subtype I-D and two subtype III-B loci) and two adaptation cas gene cassettes (one subtype I-A and one subtype I-D). The 5 repeat arrays carry 13 spacers matching the SIRV2 genome, each with a cognate Protospacer Adjacent Motif (PAM) and 2–6 bp mismatches. Despite imperfect complementarity to the viral target, at least some of these spacers are predicted to be functional because the mismatches are located outside the seed sequence that is essential for interference21–24. Nevertheless, SIRV2 is able to propagate in LAL14/1 with an average viral yield of 30 virions per cell and causes host chromosome degradation, eventually leading to cell lysis25. The ability of SIRV2 to propagate in the presence of active CRISPR–Cas systems carrying matching spacers suggests that the virus could encode anti-CRISPR proteins.

Here, we report the functional and structural characterization of two Acrs encoded by SIRV2 and SIRV3. We initially identify a mutant of SIRV2 (SIRV2M) that arises after successive transfers of the virus in a CRISPR-deficient host. SIRV2M is characterized by a 4 kb deletion near one of the termini of the linear viral genome and has lost the capacity to infect the original host LAL14/1. By inserting SIRV3 genes that are conserved in the deleted 4 kb fragment into the SIRV2M genome, we further identify a single gene capable of restoring the SIRV2M infectivity in LAL14/1. Finally, we show that the product of this gene, AcrID1, is a conserved and compact dimeric αβ-sandwich protein conferring anti-CRISPR activity by interacting directly with Cas10d, the large subunit of the subtype I-D CRISPR–Cas effector complex in Sulfolobus islandicus.

AcrID1 is a compact αβ-sandwich protein comprising a five-stranded, anti-parallel β-sheet and two α-helices, both located on the same side of the sheet (Fig. 6a). The first β-strand of the sheet is broken in two (β1 and β1′) and the topology can therefore appropriately be described as α1-β1-β1′-α2-β2-β3-β4-β5. All six molecules in the ASU engage in identical dimers, thus compatible with the observed behaviour of the protein in solution (Fig. 4c). The dimer interaction is head to tail with the helices on the same side of the sheet and the two β1 strands adjacent to each other (Fig. 6b). The dimer has a total surface area of 10,920 Å2 of which about 1,900 Å2 are buried at the interface. Interactions between the monomers involve main chain hydrogen bonds between the β1 strands, and the dimer thus effectively consists of an extended, ten-stranded anti-parallel β-sheet structure, which is likely to be the biologically relevant form of the protein. The dimer interface is further stabilized by hydrophobic interactions in the region between the β-sheet and the helices and polar interactions between the helices themselves (Fig. 6b). In particular, the hydrophobic interactions, Ile19-Phe26 and Ile22-Leu24, which are present twice at the interface because of the twofold symmetry, as well as Met9-Met9, involve residues that are highly conserved among the homologous archaeal virus proteins (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 5). Furthermore, the protein contains a range of conserved surface residues, including Glu21, Lys34, Tyr55, Glu81, Arg92 and Trp91, which might be involved in protein–protein interactions (Fig. 6c). Packing of dimers in the crystal extends the β-sheet further, in this case with a 180° rotation perpendicular to the strands and thus with the helices on alternative sides (Supplementary Fig. 7). This negative charge density appears to be particularly pronounced in SIRV3 AcrID1. AcrID1 is a compact αβ-sandwich protein comprising a five-stranded, anti-parallel β-sheet and two α-helices, both located on the same side of the sheet (Fig. 6a).
