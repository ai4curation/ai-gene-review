# Pseudomonas putida Coordinates the Expression of two Manganese Oxidases and Optimizes Manganese Oxide Precipitation in Response to Aqueous Mn(II).
**Authors:** Gehin G, Carraro N, Gurfield K, van der Meer JR, Peña J
**Journal:** Environ Sci Technol (2025)

Lausanne, Switzerland.
(3)Energy and Environmental Sciences Area, Lawrence Berkeley National

Manganese (Mn) oxides are commonly precipitated by bacteria and fungi. Recent
studies have shown that Mn-oxidizing bacteria can harbor multiple Mn oxidases,
but environmental controls on the regulation of these enzymes are unknown. Here,
we examine the activation of the genes encoding for MnxG and McoA, two Mn
oxidases in Pseudomonas putida GB-1, in response to varying Mn(II)
concentrations. Using reporter gene fusion strains, we found that mnxG and mcoA
are activated only by a fraction of the cells in the population and at different
threshold Mn concentrations, with an increasing proportion of cells expressing
mnxG (<10 μM) and then mcoA (>10 to 500 μM). Kinetic modeling showed that
coexpression of mnxG and mcoA not only alleviates substrate saturation and
inhibition of MnxG at high Mn(II) concentrations but also results in an 8-fold
increase in the initial rate of Mn oxidation in the wild type compared to
strains lacking mnxG or mcoA. The population-level control of gene activation
and, ultimately, Mn oxide precipitation, in response to the initial Mn(II)
concentration, shows that P. putida fine-tunes the regulation of multiple Mn
oxidases to operate under varying environmental conditions.

Biogenic Mn oxidation kinetics have been reported
to follow Michaelis–Menten-like
kinetics for Pseudomonas and Leptothrix

kinetics in Bacillus. Given the diversity of Mn oxidizers and various mechanisms of Mn
biomineralization described to date (e.g., single Mn oxidases, multiple
Mn oxidases, and Mn oxidase complexes), it is not surprising that
different types of enzyme kinetics have been reported. When single
enzymes are responsible for substrate conversion in the absence of
dynamic disorder, enzyme kinetics tends to follow the Michaelis–Menten
behavior. However, multimers can be formed
when multiple enzymes are involved, creating allosteric kinetics with
sigmoidal trends.

is inhibited. To overcome this inhibition, mcoA is

of cooperativity could not be established here. Contrary to the Mn
oxidases complex found in Bacillus sp. PL-12, the predicted structure for the molecular complexation
of MnxG and McoA based on alphafold does not show multimer complexation
(Figure S10). This result suggests that
MnxG and McoA function independently yet cooperatively. Cooperation
may be enabled by the apparent higher tolerance of McoA to Mn­(II),
as suggested by the absence of inhibition at high Mn­(II) concentrations
in strain ΔmnxG (Figure

compared to MnxG (given by KsmcoA ≫ KsmnxG) could result from weak binding of Mn­(II)
by McoA, which can facilitate easy hand off of Mn­(II) to MnxG upon
oxidation of Mn­(II) to Mn­(III) or Mn­(IV) by MnxG, or the preference
of McoA for Mn­(III) instead of Mn­(II). Alternatively, a multimeric
complex could be formed with an additional cofactor not considered
in the alphafold simulation. Further research is needed to clarify
how these enzymes interact to enhance Mn oxidation at high Mn concentrations
and whether other chemical gradients (i.e., the pH and the O2 concentration) also affect gene activation. Additionally, these
conditions may impact the affinity of Mn oxidases for Mn­(II).
