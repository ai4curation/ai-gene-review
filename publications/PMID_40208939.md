Neural stem cells (NSCs) can self-renew and undergo differentiation via
asymmetric division. Dysregulation in the balance between self-renewal and
differentiation can lead to tumor formation or neurodevelopmental disorders.
However, the regulation of phosphatidylinositol transfer protein
(PITP)-dependent PI(4)P pools and myosin localization during asymmetric division
in dividing cells is not well established. Here, we show that the Golgi proteins
Arf1 and ARFGEF2/Sec71 control asymmetric division of Drosophila NSCs by
facilitating the localization of myosin II regulatory light chain, Sqh, to the
NSC cortex. Arf1 can physically associate with Sqh and Vibrator, a type I PITP
that stimulates phospholipid PI4K activity for PI(4)P production. Further, Arf1
and Sec71 facilitate PI(4)P localization to the cell cortex of neuroblasts. Our
data provide evidence that the Golgi proteins Arf1 and its GEF Sec71 facilitate
neuroblast polarity through phospholipid-dependent nonmuscle myosin II cortical
localization.

## Full Text

Self-renewal and differentiation are two key features of stem cells. Drosophila neural stem cells (NSC), or neuroblasts, are an excellent model for understanding stem cell asymmetry and homeostasis. Each neuroblast divides asymmetrically to give rise to a self-renewing neuroblast and a ganglion mother cell (GMC) that generates two postmitotic neurons (1). The central brain of Drosophila larvae contains two types of neuroblasts: ~90 “type I” neuroblasts that divide asymmetrically to generate a series of smaller GMCs which divide just once to produce two neurons, and ~8 “type II” neuroblasts which divide asymmetrically to self-renew and generate transient amplifying cells known as intermediate neural progenitors (INPs) (2–4). INPs undergo 4 to 5 rounds of divisions to self-renew and to produce GMCs that undergo a terminal division to produce neurons (2–4). If the limited self-renewing potential of INPs is unrestrained, they may revert back into neuroblasts, leading to tumor formation (2, 5–10).

Asymmetric cell division of neuroblasts depends on the apicobasal polarity. Apical polarity is established by the proper polarization of apical proteins such as the Par proteins which includes atypical PKC (aPKC), Bazooka (the Drosophila homolog of Par3), and Par6 (11–13). The Par complex is associated with Inscuteable (Insc) (14, 15), Partner of Insc (Pins), the heterotrimeric G protein Gαi, Locomotion defects (Loco), and Mushroom body defects (Mud), which are recruited to the apical cortex during mitosis (16–20). Apical proteins not only govern the positioning of basal proteins but also help orient the mitotic spindle (21, 22). Key basal cell fate determinants, such as Numb, Prospero (Pros), Brain tumor (Brat), and their adaptor proteins Partner of Numb (Pon) and Miranda (Mira), are essential for neuronal differentiation (1, 22–24). In neuroblasts, the nonmuscle myosin II regulatory light chain (MRLC) can regulate cytokinesis and localization of basal polarity proteins (25–27).

While myosin is known to interact with lipids indirectly through F-actin, the direct association between myosin and lipids has been less explored. Recent studies established a novel connection between phosphatidylinositol transfer protein (PITP)-dependent PI(4)P pools and myosin localization in dividing cells (26). The ability of Sqh to directly bind phospholipids, including PI(4)P, links myosin to phospholipids suggesting that Sqh may mediate the membrane localization of PI(4)P, thereby reinforcing its own cortical localization through interaction with Drosophila PITP, Vibrator/Giotto, and PI(4)P in neuroblasts (26). PI(4)P, has been shown to regulate protein trafficking at the Golgi complex and the biosynthetic trafficking of sphingolipids and sterols (28–32).

The ADP-ribosylation factor 1(Arf1), is a small GTPase and belongs to the Class I Arf family (33, 34). Arf1 is a key regulator of Golgi structure and regulates membrane traffic at trans-Golgi network (35). During mitosis, Arf1 modulates Golgi disassembly and serves to disperse peripheral Golgi proteins, which, in turn, assist in chromosome segregation and cytokinesis(36). Studies have shown that Arf1 is activated by the Sec7/BIG family on trans-Golgi(33). Mutations in Golgi proteins ARF1 and its guanine-nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) Sec71/ARFGEF2 cause malformations of cortical development (37–39). Recently, we have shown that the Golgi-resident GTPase Arf1 and Sec71/Arf1GEF promote Drosophila quiescent NSC reactivation and regeneration by regulating microtubule growth (40, 41). However, the potential function of Arf1 and its GEF ARFGEF2/Sec71 in dividing neuroblasts during brain development is not established.

In this study, we provide evidence that the Golgi proteins Arf1 and its GEF Sec71 facilitate neuroblast polarity through phospholipid-dependent nonmuscle myosin II cortical localization.

Similarly, the basal protein Mira displayed a strong crescent at the basal cortex during metaphase in control neuroblasts (Fig. 1 C and C′), whereas the localization of Mira was disrupted in 66.7% (Fig. 1 C, C′, and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S3B) of arf1 RNAi and 70% (Fig. 1 C and C′) of sec71 RNAi metaphase neuroblasts. The dominant-negative forms of Arf1T31N and Sec71DN (Fig. 1 D and D′) also showed obvious delocalization of Mira basal crescent during metaphase as compared to control (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, these defects in apicobasal polarity proteins aPKC and Mira upon arf1 RNAi were fully rescued by the expression of an RNAi-resistant arf1 transgene (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C–E).

Similarly, other apical proteins including, Bazooka, Par-6, Gαi and Inscuteable as well as basal proteins such as Numb in arf1 RNAi or Sec71DN metaphase neuroblasts no longer localized asymmetrically in the apical or basal cortex, exhibiting weak or cytoplasmic signals (Fig. 1 E–N′).

In sec71 mutant neuroblasts, during metaphase, aPKC was weak or delocalized in 41.6% clones (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 F–I) as compared to control clones (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 F–I). Mira localization was significantly disrupted in sec71EX11 clones (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 F–I). aPKC and Mira delocalization in sec71Ex11 metaphase neuroblasts could be completely rescued by overexpression of Sec71WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 F–I).

Depletion of arf1 and sec71 function using type I and type II driver resulted in stronger aPKC and Mira polarity defects (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 J–Q), further suggesting that Arf1 and Sec71 are required for neuroblast polarity establishment.

To understand whether neuronal differentiation is affected, we analyzed whether loss arf1 and sec71 affected the asymmetric localization of Mira in INPs using the INP-specific driver erm-Gal4 (earmuff-Gal4). Depletion of arf1 or sec71 by RNAi or expression of the dominant-negative forms of Arf1 and Sec71 did not obviously affect the asymmetric localization of Mira in INPs as compared to control (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B), suggesting that neuronal differentiation is not affected.

At telophase, mislocalized polarity proteins are often corrected and localize asymmetrically in many mutants with disrupted cell polarity at metaphase, known as a phenomenon named “telophase rescue” (44). However, apical segregation of aPKC was still defective at telophase in 21.4% of arf1 RNAi (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and D) and 20% of Sec71DN (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 F and G) neuroblasts. Similarly, the basal proteins, Mira and Numb remained mislocalized to the mitotic spindle or had weak basal localization in 22.6% and 27.3% of arf1 RNAi telophase neuroblasts respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C, E, I, and J) and 25% or 21.4% of Sec71DN (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 F, H, K, and L) neuroblasts respectively.

Live imaging of Mira-GFP in NSCs of arf1 RNAi and Sec71DN also showed a significant number of neuroblasts in which Mira-GFP basal crescent fails to be formed during metaphase as compared to control (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B and Movies S1–S7). In the remaining neuroblasts where Mira crescent was assembled at metaphase, the crescent was maintained until the completion of the cell division (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B and Movies S1–S7). Furthermore, telophase rescue was observed in the majority of the dividing neuroblasts (66.7% in arf1 RNAi and 75% in Sec71DN, SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and C and Movies S1–S7). This result suggests that Arf1 and Sec71 are likely required for neuroblast polarity establishment. Though a minority of type I neuroblasts showed cytokinesis defects upon loss of arf1 and sec71 using ase-Gal4 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), our live imaging analysis using insc-Gal4 Mira-GFP in control, arf1 RNAi, and Sec71DN for two consecutive divisions showed that all the neuroblasts were able to divide without obvious cytokinesis defects upon loss of arf1 and sec71 similar to control (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D and Movies S8–S10), suggesting cytokinesis defects unlikely contribute to the formation of ectopic neuroblasts.

Consistent with protein segregation defects at telophase, we observed altered daughter cell size difference at this stage. In wild-type telophase neuroblasts, the two daughter cell sizes were distinct, with a ratio D1 (larger daughter)/D2 (smaller daughter) of 2.1 to 2.2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 C and D). Upon arf1 and sec71 knockdown by RNAi, the ratio of daughter cell diameters was significantly decreased to 1.7 and 1.8, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). Similarly, in telophase neuroblasts overexpressing Arf1T31N and Sec71DN, the ratio of daughter cell diameters was decreased to 1.6 and 1.8 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 C and D) respectively. Consistent with our previous results (SI Appendix, Fig. S4) loss of arf1 or sec71 showed abnormal Mira segregation at telophase in these neuroblasts (SI Appendix, Fig. S6E). Furthermore, cell size measurements using the membrane marker mCD8-GFP showed similar defects in daughter cell ratios as seen in knockdown or dominant-negative forms of Arf1 and Sec71 compared to control (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 F–I). arf1 knockdown and overexpression of Sec71DN using the type I or type II neuroblast specific driver also showed defects in daughter cell ratios compared to control (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 J–M). Using Mira-GFP live imaging analysis we also quantified the frequency of asymmetric versus similar-size division. arf1 knockdown as well as the dominant-negative form Sec71 causes similar-size division of neuroblasts in 33.3% and 37.5% of the time, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). Moreover, live imaging of Jupiter-GFP (G147-GFP), a microtubule-binding protein controlled under its endogenous promoter (45), similar-size division of neuroblasts was observed in arf1 RNAi and Sec71DN (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 C and D and Movies S11–S13). These data suggest that arf1 or sec71-depleted neuroblasts often divide symmetrically, leading to altered cell fate and reduced cell volume of daughter cells leading to the formation of smaller lineages.

To understand whether neuroblasts with smaller cell size tend to display the polarity defects, we analyzed neuroblast diameter upon arf1 and sec71 knockdown where polarity of aPKC and Mira are disrupted. Keeping a diameter range of 9 to 12 µm as normal, we find that only a small percentage (26.7%) of neuroblasts upon loss of arf1 and (24.2%) upon loss of sec71 showed smaller diameters (less than 9 µm), while the majority of neuroblasts with polarity defects are with normal size (9 to 12 µm) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 E and F). These observations suggest that cell polarity defects upon Arf1 and Sec71 depletion are not correlated with smaller neuroblast sizes.

To test whether Arf1/sec71 regulates cell growth, we used temperature-sensitive tubulin-Gal80ts to allow the knockdown of arf1/sec71 or expression of Arf1T31N/Sec71DN only during the larval stages and labeled the nucleolar marker Fibrillarin, a marker for cell growth (6) along with Dpn, a nuclear marker for neuroblasts. The ratio of nucleolus to nuclear area, or neuroblast diameter and area show no significant change as compared to control (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A–J). In contrast, constitutive depletion of Arf1 or Sec71 by knockdown arf1/sec71 or overexpression Arf1T31N/Sec71DN under the NSC driver insc-Gal4, the ratio of nucleolus to nuclear area as well as cell diameter and area of NSCs are significantly reduced as compared to control (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 K–T). This suggests that perturbations in arf1 and sec71 do not affect neuroblast cell growth per se and that smaller neuroblast size might be due to earlier reactivation defects that we reported recently (40).

Taken together, our data suggest that Arf1 and Sec71 are important for apicobasal polarity during neuroblast asymmetric division and faithful segregation of these polarity proteins in dividing neuroblasts.

Arf1 and Sec71 Are Dispensable for Microtubule Growth in Dividing Neuroblasts.Our recent work demonstrated a role of Arf1 and Sec71 in regulating noncentrosomal microtubule growth in quiescent neuroblasts (40). To test whether Arf1 and Sec71 control neuroblast polarity by regulating microtubule growth, we carried out a microtubule regrowth assay in neuroblasts by treating larval brains on ice for efficient depolymerization of microtubules, followed by their recovery at 25 °C to allow microtubule regrowth over a period of time. UAS lines were crossed to the insc-Gal4, tub-Gal80ts driver at 25 °C and shifted to 18 °C for 40 h till larvae are about 24 h ALH, after which larvae were incubated for 3 or 4 d at 29 °C. Upon treatment with ice at t = 0 s, we find that spindle microtubules were effectively depolymerized in control as well as arf1 or sec71 RNAi metaphase neuroblasts. Interestingly, similar to control, all arf1 and sec71 mutant metaphase NSCs, intense spindle microtubules were reassembled around centrosomes and chromosome mass from as early as 30 s (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B) following recovery at 25 °C; the mitotic spindle completely reformed at 2 min following recovery (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B). These data suggest that the Golgi proteins Arf1 and Sec71 may not control neuroblast polarity by regulating microtubule growth in dividing neuroblasts.
