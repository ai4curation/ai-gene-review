Golgi_traff is a Pfam clan containing two members, Dymeclin (DYM) and HID1
domain-containing protein (HID). Interrogation of over 900 eukaryotic genomes
with sequence models showed that both are ancient eukaryotic genes, which have
exhibited different paths of gene loss, including from major taxonomic groups.
For example, the Metazoa have both genes, whereas the Viridiplantae and Dikarya
have lost HID and DYM, respectively. A unique replication event occurred within
the genus Schizosaccharomyces in that all sequenced species possess three
HID-encoding paralogs, whereas its nearest fungal relatives and other eukaryotes
are almost exclusively monogenic. A phylogenetic analysis of yeasts revealed
that the Golgi-resident paralog Human ortholog 3 (SPAC17A5.16) is more similar
to the HID of other yeasts than to its paralogs. Transmission electron
microscopy revealed that the SPAC17A5.16 mutant lacks a stacked Golgi apparatus
(GA) form, suggesting a role in maintaining GA structure. Altered proliferation
of the SPAC17A5.16 mutant in response to GA disrupting chemical agents indicated
a perturbation of GA-related functions. Structural models suggest SPAC17A5.16
has a long, disordered N-terminal region that may facilitate anchoring to GA
membranes. A modification to Schizosaccharomyces HID nomenclature is proposed to
reflect their evolutionary and functional characteristics. The potential of the
Golgi_traff clan to serve as a model for the diversification of protein function
according to the concepts of sub/neofunctionalization is discussed.

ResultsAsymmetric distribution of HID and DYM within eukaryotesStandard similarity searches by BLAST revealed that neither HID nor DYM were present in prokaryotes or archea and that eukaryotic organisms could have either, neither, or both proteins. Therefore, we undertook a systematic analysis of sequenced eukaryotic genomes to assess the distribution of both proteins. It was necessary to conduct this iteratively, whereby an initial HMM was created from the Pfam seed set for each of the proteins for interrogation of a small, but diverse, group of organisms for which GA proteins have been assembled (Barlow et al. 2018). Subsequently, protein and CDS HMMs were generated from this group and used to interrogate the available eukaryotic Refseq genomes and proteomes. The use of both protein and DNA interrogation of proteomes and genomes served to reveal the number of protein isoforms and the number of genes, respectively.The topology of the taxonomic tree derived from NCBI numeric identifiers for the small group of organisms was nearly identical to that shown in Barlow et al. (2018) and presented a diverse set of eukaryotic organisms to visually present the presence or absence of HID and DYM (Fig. 1; Table S2C). Both DYM and HID were represented across clades, but exhibited distinct patterns of presence within the various phyla. It revealed the lack of DYM in the Fungi, Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, and in a large group of the Alveolata. HID was absent from certain phyla of the Alveolata, the clades Haptista and Rhizaria, and the kingdom Viridiplantae.Figure 1.Presence of HID and DYM in diverse eukaryotic organisms. The global distribution of genes in the Refseq set mirrored that shown in Fig. 1 with the Metazoa having both HID and DYM, the Dikarya having HID, and the Viridiplantae having only DYM (Table S2D). In organisms, where duplicate genes were discovered, these could be traced to ploidy/genome duplication events, such as for the Blastocladiomycota (Amses et al. 2022) and the Mucuromycota (Ma et al. 2009). The more robust HMMs provided evidence through partial sequences for the presence of DYM or HID in various taxa and retrieved protist sequences that showed no similarity to proteins other than resident DYM or HID paralogs. The latter included examples of the explosion in pseudogene formation, such as that observed for Trichomonas vaginalis (Table S2C; Woehle et al. 2014).Replication and retention of three Schizosaccharomyces HID genesThe S. pombe genome sequencing project (Wood et al. 2002) and subsequent Schizosaccharomyces sequencing projects had revealed three orthologs of HID1, although they have been misannotated as DYM for S. japonicus, S. cryophilus, and S. octosporus. Thus, the species of this genus are unusual among eukaryotic organisms as each has three distinct paralogs. A comparison of the S. pombe sequences with human and other fungal HIDs is given in Fig. S1. The evolutionary divergence of the genus was well-reflected in the HIDs. Schizosaccharomyces pombe and S. japonicus genomes share ~55% amino acid identity among proteins (Rhind et al. 2011), but SPAP27G11.12, SPBP19A11.07c, and SPAC17A5.16 were ∼34%, ∼20%, and ∼37% identical to the corresponding S. japonicus ortholog, respectively (Table S2E). Furthermore, there has been substantial diversification of primary sequence of S. pombe HIDs with those of later appearing species (Table S2E) and they have undergone shuffling with both chromosome location and orientation varying amongst S. japonicus, S. pombe, and S. octosporus (Fig. S2).To understand better the relationship among the three paralogs and the orthologs of other fungi, we conducted a phylogenetic analysis, which comprised 84 HID proteins from the fungal species of Barlow et al. For all Schizosaccharomyces species, Ortholog 3 appeared to be more similar to the HID of the Pneumocystis than to either of its paralogs (Fig. 2; Liu et al. 2009). Asymmetric distribution of HID and DYM within eukaryotes

Standard similarity searches by BLAST revealed that neither HID nor DYM were present in prokaryotes or archea and that eukaryotic organisms could have either, neither, or both proteins. Therefore, we undertook a systematic analysis of sequenced eukaryotic genomes to assess the distribution of both proteins. It was necessary to conduct this iteratively, whereby an initial HMM was created from the Pfam seed set for each of the proteins for interrogation of a small, but diverse, group of organisms for which GA proteins have been assembled (Barlow et al. 2018). Subsequently, protein and CDS HMMs were generated from this group and used to interrogate the available eukaryotic Refseq genomes and proteomes. The use of both protein and DNA interrogation of proteomes and genomes served to reveal the number of protein isoforms and the number of genes, respectively.

The topology of the taxonomic tree derived from NCBI numeric identifiers for the small group of organisms was nearly identical to that shown in Barlow et al. (2018) and presented a diverse set of eukaryotic organisms to visually present the presence or absence of HID and DYM (Fig. 1; Table S2C). Both DYM and HID were represented across clades, but exhibited distinct patterns of presence within the various phyla. It revealed the lack of DYM in the Fungi, Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, and in a large group of the Alveolata. HID was absent from certain phyla of the Alveolata, the clades Haptista and Rhizaria, and the kingdom Viridiplantae.

Although there are no experimentally determined structures of either DYM or HID, the Computed Structural Models yield potential explanations for the targeting of eukaryotic HIDs, particularly the variation observed among those of S. pombe. The global topologies of human HID1 and DYM are very similar (Fig. 5A), which likely explain their colocalization, even when the GA is dissociated upon treatment with BFA (Wang et al. 2011). The major difference between the two proteins is the presence of a C-terminal region of ∼120 residues in HID1, for which AlphaFold2 was not able to determine a structural feature. This C-terminal disordered region is also present in SPAC17A5.16 (Fig. 5B). In addition, SPAC17A5.16 has a predicted N-terminal disordered region that is about 80 residues longer than that predicted for human HID1. The C-terminal disordered region appears to be present in SPAP27G11.12, but not the N-terminal disordered region, and both disordered regions are greatly reduced in SPBP19A11.07c compared to SPAC17A5.16 (Fig. 5C). The prediction that these features are also present in the corresponding paralogs of S. japonicus suggest that these features appeared and were modified before diversification of the species (Fig. S5). There is some difference in size of the disordered regions, but this would be expected noting that membrane proteins differ between the two species due to the different membrane fatty acid content (Makarova et al. 2020).

DiscussionThe extensive distribution for both DYM and HID, particularly encompassing single-cell eukaryotes, suggests a more fundamental roles in cellular/GA function than attributed by work on multicellular organisms. However, a major impediment to understanding their functions is knowing the breadth of organisms in which they are present. We sought to resolve this through a direct search of available eukaryotic Refseq genomes. Due to limited initial seed sets in Pfam and the frequent misannotation of HID or DYM within genome sequencing projects, it was necessary to conduct the search in an iterative process to obtain robust protein and CDS HMMs. For example, taxa representing the Chlorophyta, which contain the higher plants and algae were not present in the DYM pfam seed group even though they possess DYM, and almost half of the sequences in the pfam DYM seed set were HIDs. In addition, the usefulness of the two-prong approach to interrogate both the genome and annotated proteome of each organism was exemplified through the searches within the ciliate Stramenopiles. The proteome search revealed two HIDs and a DYM for Paramecium tetraurelia, a HID for Tetrahymena thermophila, and neither HID nor DYM for Ichthyophthirius multifiliis. In contrast, searches of the genomes for the corresponding genes using the CDS HMMs were negative, except for T. thermophila, for which a matching sequence was suitable to confirm the presence of the gene and for reconstructing the coding sequence for the HID. The lack of positive hits of DNA sequences for the P. tetraurelia and I. multifilliis were due to the presence of multiple internal eliminated sequences in the HID genes (Arnaiz et al. 2012) and the lack of HID, respectively. Multiple HIDs in P. tetraurelia and the lack of HID in I. multifilliis stem from a whole genome duplication event and genome reduction, respectively, compared to the genome of T. thermophila (Coyne et al. 2011). In contrast, the ability of the HMM-based search to retrieve partial CDS sequences, some of which could not be reconfirmed by BLAST, may reflect either the power of this approach to identify genes and pseudogenes in organisms with rudimentary annotation or to identify gene fusions and chimeras. Overall, this interrogation revealed the broad distribution and ancient eukaryotic origins of the Golgi_traff proteins, and which may have been GA-associated in accordance with the early development of the GA (Barlow et al. 2018). Based on both our computation and experimental findings, we propose a modification to current nomenclature regarding Schizosaccharomyces HIDs. We suggest that SPAC17A5.16, SPBP19A11.07c, and SPAP27G11.12 be referred to as Hid1, Hid2, and Hid3.
