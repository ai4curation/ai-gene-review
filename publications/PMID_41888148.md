Gamma-glutamyl carboxylase (GGCX) is the sole enzyme responsible for gamma
carboxylation of glutamate in a vitamin K-dependent manner. This process is
crucial for blood coagulation, bone metabolism, vascular calcification, and
other biological processes because gamma carboxylation is essential for the
maturation of clotting factors, anticoagulation factors, and some
coagulation-unrelated factors. Despite these essential roles, the catalytic
mechanism of GGCX remains incompletely understood. Here, we present the cryo-EM
structures of human GGCX complexed with five typical substrates, including two
clotting factors and three coagulation-unrelated factors. These structures not
only elucidate the recognition mechanism for the propeptide but also reveal
three distinct modes for substrate loading. Among them, the GGCX-MGP complex
structure reveals a specific mode to load a substrate with an active glutamate
residue at the N-terminus of the propeptide. Moreover, these structural
observations are supported by our in vitro carboxylation and epoxidation assays.

Introduction

The vitamin K-dependent (VKD) gamma carboxylation of glutamate is an ancient posttranslational modification1. Carboxylation is required for the normal function of VKD proteins, including clotting factors (thrombin, factor IX2, and factor X3), anticoagulation factors (protein C4, protein S5, and protein Z6), and some coagulation-unrelated factors, such as osteocalcin (BGP)7, matrix Gla protein (MGP)8, proline-rich Gla proteins (PRGPs)9, and growth arrest-specific protein 6 (Gas6)10. These proteins play essential roles in blood coagulation, bone metabolism11, vascular calcification12, and other biological processes13. Defects in gamma carboxylation have long been known to cause combined vitamin K–dependent coagulation factor deficiency (VKCFD), which is a bleeding disorder caused by impaired clotting factor formation14. Recently, some nonbleeding disorders have been reported as a result of deficiencies in coagulation-unrelated factors12.

The carboxylation process is catalyzed by gamma-glutamyl carboxylase (GGCX), which was initially cloned in 199115. This enzyme utilizes reduced vitamin K (VKH2) as a cofactor, which provides energy for substrate carboxylation when it is oxidized to vitamin K2,3-epoxide (VKO), a reaction known as epoxidation15. VKO is then reduced by vitamin K2,3-epoxide reductase complex subunit 1 (VKORC1) to vitamin K quinone (VK) and further reduced back to VKH2 by vitamin K reductase, which is called the vitamin K cycle16,17. Warfarin, a clinically used anticoagulant, acts as an inhibitor of VKORC1, thereby blocking the vitamin K cycle and inhibiting the maturation of clotting factors to prevent thrombosis18–20.

Coagulation-related factors typically contain multiple active glutamates organized into a Gla (gamma-carboxyl glutamate) domain after carboxylation. At the N-terminus of the Gla domain, these factors often include propeptides that facilitate interaction with carboxylase21,22. Moreover, binding of the propeptide has been reported to be essential for the full activity of GGCX23. In addition to the active glutamate residues within the Gla domain, MGP has an additional active glutamate located at the N-terminus of the propeptide in this protein8. Despite recent publications on GGCX structures24–28, it remains unclear how this additional glutamate of MGP accesses the catalytic site of GGCX.

GGCX is an ER membrane protein with a transmembrane domain (TMD) at its N-terminus and a luminal domain (LD) at its C-terminus29. Recently, several structures of GGCX and its complex with various substrates have been published, elucidating the structural basis for propeptide recognition, substrate loading, vitamin K coordination, and CO2 trapping24–28.

Here, we conducted a cryo-EM investigation on human GGCX, successfully obtaining complex structures with typical substrates, including clotting factors (FIX and FX) and coagulation-unrelated factors (PRGP1, BGP, and MGP). Our GGCX structures not only reinforce these previous structural observations but also elucidate three distinct modes for propeptide recognition and substrate loading. Notably, the GGCX-MGP complex structure reveals a specific mechanism for loading the active glutamate at the N-terminus of the propeptide into the catalytic cavity, thereby providing insights into the loading mechanism for substrates with carboxylation sites located at the N-terminus of the propeptide.

In these GGCX-substrate complex structures, LD2 and LD3 primarily comprise the propeptide recognition site (PRS) to recognize the propeptide of the substrates (Fig. 2a). PRS can be further divided into three subsites: PRS1, PRS2, and PRS3 (Fig. 2a–d). Sequence alignment revealed that the residues of the propeptide are highly conserved among various substrates at these sites (Fig. 2e). To facilitate structural description, these residues were renumbered according to the previous numbering system for FIX, counting them in reverse from the mature form (Supplementary Fig. 4c)21.

At PRS1, three hydrophobic residues (-17 to -15) are recognized by an open hydrophobic surface (Fig. 2b). Among these residues, Phe-16 is invariant among all GGCX substrates, underscoring its essential role in recognition (Fig. 2e). Alanine substitutions of the residues at positions -16 and -15 of MGP (F-16A and I-15A) significantly diminished the stimulation of the propeptide on vitamin K epoxidation (Fig. 2f). Additionally, alanine substitution of residue -16 in FIX has been shown to impair carboxylation activity21. These observations collectively confirm the essential roles of PRS1 in propeptide recognition.

PRS2 restricts the residue at the -10 position to be small in size. The side chain of the -10 residue is positioned close to the GGCX surface, allowing space only for small residues, such as alanine and glycine (Fig. 2c). Correspondingly, the residues at the -10 position are highly conserved, with alanine and glycine observed at this site among various substrates (Fig. 2e). Consistent with our structural analysis, the substitution of residue -10 with leucine reduced the stimulatory effect of the MGP propeptide on the epoxidation activity of GGCX (Fig. 2f). Additionally, the carboxylation activity was also reduced when alanine at position -10 was substituted with glutamate (A-10E) in a previous report21.

PRS3 contains two hydrophobic cavities that exclusively accommodate hydrophobic residues at the -7 and -6 positions. The first cavity is larger and deeper, providing sufficient space for bulky residues at the -7 position, such as phenylalanine in MGP. In contrast, the second cavity is narrower and shallower, allowing only for smaller hydrophobic residues such as leucine, valine, and isoleucine (Fig. 2d, e). Alanine substitutions of residues -7 and -6 also reduced the propeptide’s stimulation on the epoxidation activity (Fig. 2f).

Despite the similar recognition mechanisms for the propeptides, the binding affinities for different substrates vary significantly. Notably, the BGP propeptide interacts with GGCX at a significantly lower affinity than the other propeptides do30. At the -10 position, BGP features glycine, whereas other substrates contain alanine. Although glycine satisfies the small size requirement for residue selection at this position, as restricted by PRS2, it may weaken the interaction between the propeptide and GGCX. In support of this, the binding affinity between GGCX and BGP can be restored by alanine substitution of this glycine27,31. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the alanine substitution variant of BGP significantly increased the stimulation on GGCX’s epoxidation activity compared with the WT version (Fig. 2g).

A similar mechanism for propeptide recognition has been reported in a recent publication, in which three PRS sites were designated as sites 1–324. Furthermore, other structural investigations provide further support for this propeptide recognition mechanism25–28.

GGCX serves as an essential enzyme that catalyzes the carboxylation of glutamate on clotting factors, anticoagulation factors, and some coagulation-unrelated factors. Here, we present the cryo-EM structures of GGCX complexed with five typical substrates, including FIX, FX, PRGP1, BGP, and MGP. These structures elucidate the recognition mechanism for propeptides and illustrate three distinct loading modes for these substrates.

In contrast to other substrates, BGP contains a second recognition sequence at the C-terminus of the carboxylation site. Both the propeptide and the second recognition sequence were suggested to be essential for the efficient carboxylation of BGP34. In this study, we specifically disrupted the interface between the second recognition sequence and GGCX. The resulting mutants displayed reduced epoxidation activities when BGP was used as the substrate, further emphasizing the essential role of this interface in the efficient carboxylation of BGP.

Most of our structural observations are corroborated with recently published structures24–28, whereas our GGCX-MGP structure reveals a distinct substrate loading mode (mode 3) to load a substrate with a carboxylation site at the N-terminus of the propeptide (Fig. 3a). This structure explains how GGCX carboxylates the glutamate residue at the N-terminus of the propeptide, such as MGP. MGP also contains several other carboxylation sites on the C-terminus of the propeptide. These C-terminal sites may be loaded into the catalytic cavity in a mode similar to that of FIX, FX and PRGP1, as supported by a recently published GGCX-MGP structure28. Despite this information, the carboxylation order of these sites and the mechanisms by which GGCX coordinates their carboxylation remain unclear.

Cholesterol has been suggested to bind GGCX and regulate the protein level of GGCX in the cell25. Here, we also observed cholesterol densities at the same site across all our structures. More importantly, our high-resolution structures allow us to clearly distinguish the smooth and rough faces of cholesterol, especially in the 2.65 Å structure of GGCX-FIX (Supplementary Fig. 6e).

GGCX catalyzes the carboxylation of glutamate residues on its substrates, a process that is coupled with vitamin K epoxidation. Our structural investigation revealed the spatial arrangement of the glutamate residue and vitamin K within the catalytic cavity. In addition to the substrates glutamate and vitamin K, both CO2 and O2 are critical for the catalytic process, serving roles in carboxylation and vitamin K epoxidation, respectively. Despite their essential roles, it remains unclear about the tunnel through which CO2 and O2 can enter the catalytic cavity.

We determined two EM maps for the GGCX and FIX propeptide complex: GGCX-proP1 and GGCX-proP2. In the GGCX-proP1 structure, peptide-like densities are absent in the catalytic cavity, as expected. However, peptide-like densities are still observed in the GGCX-proP2 map. Initially, we attributed these densities to the C-tail of GGCX, given that GGCX has the capacity to catalyze self-carboxylation on this region35,36. However, these peptide-like densities do not disappear when the C-tail truncated form of GGCX is utilized in the preparation of the GGCX-propeptide complex, contrary to our initial attribution (Supplementary Fig. 7). An alternative explanation is that these densities may derive from the C-terminal twin-Strep tags fused to GGCX for purification, as glutamate residues are also present in these tags. In spite of these unsolved questions, our structural investigation identified a distinct substrate loading mode, which provides a foundation for further mechanistic investigations of the enzymatic function of GGCX.

Protein expression and purification

The cDNA of human GGCX (NCBI reference sequence NM_000821.6) was amplified from the HEK293T cell-derived cDNA library and subcloned into the pCAG vector with C-terminal 10×His and twin-Strep tags. All the mutants were generated with a standard two-step PCR-based strategy using primers listed in the Supplementary Data 1. The cDNAs of human MGP (NCBI reference sequence NM_001190839.3), FIX (NCBI reference sequence NM_000133.4), FX (NCBI reference sequence NM_000504.4), PRGP1 (NCBI reference sequence NM_000950.3) were also amplified from the HEK293T cell-derived cDNA library and subcloned into the pCAG vector with C-terminal Flag tag. pCMV-BGP-3×Flag (NCBI reference sequence NM_199173.6) was purchased from Mailgene, Beijing and subcloned into the pCAG vector with C-terminal Flag tag, as well.

FreeStyleTM 293-F suspension cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were cultured in SMM 293-TII (SinoBiological) without any vitamin K additives at 37 °C and supplied with 5% CO2. When the cell density reached 2.0 × 106 cells per mL, the cells were transiently transfected with the plasmids and polyethylenimines (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai). For GGCX and its mutants, approximately 1 mg of expression plasmids was premixed with 3 mg of polyethylenimines in 50 ml of fresh medium and incubated for 15–30 min before transfection. For the GGCX-substrate complexes, approximately 0.75 mg GGCX- and 0.76 mg substrate-encoded plasmids were mixed with 3 mg of polyethylenimines in 50 mL of fresh medium and incubated for 15–30 min before transfection. The 50 ml mixture was then added to 1 L of cell culture and incubated for 15–30 min. Cells were collected 48 h after transfection and resuspended in buffer containing 25 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl and protease inhibitor cocktails (Sigma-Aldrich).

The resuspended cells were solubilized with 1% (w/v) LMNG (Anatrace) at 4 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation at 27,000 × g for 1 h, the supernatant was collected and incubated with Strep-Tactin ®XT 4Flow® (IBA) resins. After rinsed with buffer A containing 25 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.005% LMNG, the proteins were eluted with buffer A plus 50 mM biotin (Harveybio).

For GGCX purification, the eluate from the first affinity purification was loaded onto NTA resin (Qiagen). After rinsing with buffer A, the targeted protein was eluted with buffer A plus 250 mM imidazole, which was then concentrated and further purified by size exclusion chromatography (Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL, Cytiva) in buffer A. 10 µM propeptide of FIX was included throughout purification to prepare the cryo-EM sample of GGCX-proP complex.

For purification of GGCX-substrate complexes, the eluate from the first affinity purification was loaded onto anti-FLAG M2 resin (Sigma-Aldrich). After rinsing with buffer A, the targeted protein was eluted with buffer A plus 0.2 mg/mL Flag peptide (Bankpeptide Biological Technology Co., Ltd.), which was then concentrated and further purified by size exclusion chromatography (Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL, Cytiva) in buffer A.

Cryo-EM data processing

In total, 2,603/1,010/694/614/610/2,422 movies were collected for the GGCX-FIX/GGCX-FX/GGCX-PRGP1/GGCX-BGP/GGCX-MGP/GGCX-proP/GGCXΔC-proP samples, respectively. After motion correction and manual picking, 2,381/943/570/588/597/2,257/3,241 micrographs were selected. A total of 3,740,174/1,507,917/827,707/872,183/875,081/3,395,731/4,746,495 particles were extracted.

For GGCX-FIX/-FX/-PRGP1/-BGP/-MGP, the extracted particles were applied into Heterogeneous Refinement, resulting in 1,002,321/377,520/404,555/310,295/260,359 good particles. These good particles were applied into Ab-Initio Reconstruction with 3 classes, yielding 620,927/229,257/378,611/193,004/138,030 good particles. After Non-uniform Refinement, these good particles generated 3D reconstructions at resolutions of 2.65/2.83/2.75/2.94/3.33 Å.

For the GGCX-proP complex, the extracted particles were applied into Heterogeneous Refinement and Ab-Initio Reconstruction, resulting in 336,238 good particles. These good particles were chosen as seeds to select more particles from the initial particles40, yielding 813,585 good particles. These particles were then analyzed through 3D classification and Heterogeneous Refinement, resulting in 504,282 good particles. After one round of 3D classification with a mask on the luminal domain, two distinct classes were identified. These two classes served as good references to conduct another round of Heterogeneous Refinement, resulting in 141,403 particles for Class 1 and 280,090 particles for Class 2. After another round of Ab-Initio Reconstruction, 116,512 good particles for Class 1 and 262,676 good particles for Class 2 were selected, which yielded 3D reconstructions at resolutions of 3.31 Å and 2.94 Å, respectively. These two maps are designated GGCX-proP1 and GGCX-proP2, respectively.

For the GGCXΔC-proP complex, the extracted particles were subjected to 2D classification, Heterogeneous Refinement, and Ab-Initio Reconstruction, resulting in 144,977 high-quality particles. Following the update of bin1 particles, a 3.35 Å EM map was generated for the GGCXΔC-proP complex.

Resolutions were estimated with the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation 0.143 criterion41 with high-resolution noise substitution42. The local resolution maps were calculated using “Local Resolution Estimation” in cryoSPARC39.

All structural refinement was carried out by PHENIX46 in real space with secondary structure and geometry restraints. All structural figures were prepared with ChimeraX47.
