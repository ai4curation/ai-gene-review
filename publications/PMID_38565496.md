Autophagy - a highly regulated intracellular degradation process - is pivotal in 
maintaining cellular homeostasis. Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) is a 
fundamental mechanism regulating the formation and function of membrane-less 
compartments. Recent research has unveiled connections between LLPS and 
autophagy, suggesting that phase separation events may orchestrate the 
spatiotemporal organization of autophagic machinery and cargo sequestration. The 
Unc-51-like kinase (ULK)/autophagy-related 1 (Atg1) family of proteins is best 
known for its regulatory role in initiating autophagy, but there is growing 
evidence that the functional spectrum of ULK/Atg1 extends beyond autophagy 
regulation. In this review, we explore the spatial and temporal regulation of 
the ULK/Atg1 family of kinases, focusing on their recruitment to LLPS-driven 
compartments, and highlighting their multifaceted functions beyond their 
traditional role.

A growing number of biomolecular condensates have been identified, highlighting the significance of phase transitions in their assembly and function. Recently, changes in condensate dynamics have been linked to signaling associated with autophagy [5, 6], stress granule (SG) formation [7, 8], and cell migration [9]. ULK/Atg1 structure and interactionsThe ULK/Atg1 family of proteins are evolutionarily conserved serine/threonine kinases that play a central role in the regulation of autophagy, a cellular process involved in the lysosome-mediated degradation and recycling of cellular components [10]. In yeast, the vacuolar enzyme aminopeptidase I (Ape1) is constitutively delivered to the vacuole through a form of selective autophagy, known as cytoplasm-to-vacuole targeting (Cvt) (Figure 1b) [24]. Ape1 dodecamers undergo LLPS to form gel-like condensates, which slowly coalesce with low internal mobility [6]. Ultimately, depending on the context, ULK/Atg1 orchestrates different aspects of selective autophagy dynamics across yeast to mammals.

Although recombinant p62 proteins do not undergo mono-component phase separation, even when present at very high concentrations (>100 μM), they can undergo LLPS when mixed with long ubiquitin chains [29, 30]. p62 phase separation, which relies on homo-oligomerization mediated by its Phox1 and Bem1p (PB1) domain and ubiquitin binding by its ubiquitin-associated (UBA) domain, is influenced by the valency of ubiquitin chains and clinically relevant p62 mutations (e.g., M404T, G411S) that impact p62’s binding affinity for ubiquitin [29, 31, 32]. p62 phase separation is also facilitated by oligomerization with NBR1 (neighbor of BRCA1 gene 1) [31, 32]. There is a positive correlation between the factors that influence the LLPS of p62 in vitro and the formation of p62 bodies in cells [29], further supporting the hypothesis that p62 bodies are phase-separated compartments.

A recent study demonstrated the co-condensation of ULK with p62 bodies through direct ULK1-p62 interaction [33]. ULK-mediated phosphorylation of p62 may in turn influence the behavior of p62 body constituents. For example, ULK1-mediated phosphorylation of p62 at S403 and S407 (or S405 and S409 in mouse) enhances p62’s binding affinity for ubiquitin [34–36], which is predicted to enhance ubiquitin-dependent p62 condensation [29, 30]. Phosphorylation at these sites has also been shown to be required for efficient degradation of autophagic cargo [34]. Phosphorylation of p62 by ULK1 also occurs at S349 (human) [33, 37], which resides within a five amino acid residue region (347–352) of p62 that is required for binding to KEAP1, an adaptor of the Cullin 3-based E3 ubiquitin ligase [38, 39]. The phosphorylation of p62 at S349 stabilizes its interaction with KEAP, which does not affect the partitioning of KEAP1 into p62 bodies, but rather inhibits KEAP1 outflow [33]. The sequestration of KEAP1 within p62 bodies allows the stabilization of transcriptional activator nuclear receptor factor 2 (NRF2) (Figure 2b), enabling NRF2 to stimulate the expression of certain autophagy-related genes (e.g., p62 and Atg5) and genes encoding key antioxidant enzymes that are important for maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis [40]. The phosphomimic p62 S351E (S349 in humans) knock-in mice exhibit NRF2 hyperactivation and growth retardation, a phenotype also observed in KEAP1-knockout mice, highlighting the physiological significance of ULK activity in regulating the phase partitioning of p62 condensates. Ultimately, depending on the context, ULK/Atg1 orchestrates different aspects of selective autophagy dynamics across yeast to mammals.

ULK/Atg1 regulates SG disassemblySGs are widely studied biomolecular condensates located in the cytoplasm, formed in response to various intracellular and environmental stressors such as high temperature, oxidative stress, and viral infection [41]. SGs are ribonucleoprotein complexes composed of translationally stalled mRNA and proteins, such as RNA binding proteins (RBPs), translation initiation factors, and ribosomal proteins. These liquid-like ribonucleoprotein granules are extremely dynamic, undergo rapid assembly and disassembly, and can evolve into solid-like aggregates under pathological conditions (prolonged stress, expression of clinically relevant disease-associated mutant proteins) in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and related disorders [1, 8]. Hundreds of different proteins and thousands of different RNAs have been identified as components of SGs [42–48]; under the appropriate conditions, these macromolecules form a system-spanning network. The central node of this network is Ras-GTPase-activating protein binding protein (G3BP). In a simple in vitro system, RNA potentiates G3BP LLPS, which is further modulated by intramolecular interactions through IDRs and post-translational modification of G3BP. In cells, G3BP drives SG formation through complex coacervation involving multivalent interaction with mRNA and other SG components [42–44].It was recently observed that ULK activity is required for the efficient disassembly of SGs via a process that does not require other components of the autophagy-initiating ULK complex [49]. Endogenous ULK is recruited to SGs and phosphorylates valosin-containing protein (VCP) at several serine and threonine sites (Figure 3a) [49]. VCP is a ubiquitous AAA+ ATPase that segregates proteins from macromolecular complexes and is frequently mutated in patients with ALS, frontotemporal dementia, and inclusion body myopathy (IBM) [50, 51]. Phosphorylation of VCP by ULK activates its ATPase activity, allowing VCP to disentangle the SG protein network. The fact that ULK deficiency in muscle leads to IBM-like pathology in mice, including cytoplasmic accumulation of stress granule-associated RBPs (similar to what is observed in transgenic mice expressing disease-causing VCP mutants), and that pharmacologically enhancing ULK activity markedly accelerates dissolution of pathological SGs, highlights the possibility of targeting ULK for treating IBM and related degenerative disorders [49]. It is worth noting that the ULK interactome includes large clusters of RBPs [49], raising the possibility that ULK (through interaction and/or phosphorylation) may also alter RNA-protein granule dynamics more broadly by influencing the ability of some of these proteins to engage in physiologically relevant phase transitions.

ULK/Atg1 influences focal adhesion dynamicsFocal adhesions (FAs) are transmembrane subcellular structures that serve as major signaling nodes that translate mechanical stimuli to biological cues that dictate cellular behavior, including cell migration [52–54]. FAs consist of a transmembrane heterodimeric integrin receptor engaging in matrix binding and a cytoplasmic protein assembly attached to the cytoskeleton. The cytoplasmic compartment of FAs contains hundreds of proteins, a considerable proportion of which are adaptor proteins. These adaptors harbor tandem protein binding motifs, enabling them to engage in multivalent interactions, a prerequisite for LLPS [55–60].Increasing evidence implicates an active role of LLPS in organizing FAs. FAs constitute viscoelastic fluid-like, highly dynamic structures that undergo rapid material exchange with the surrounding cytoplasm [55, 56, 59–61]. Several recent studies have revealed the phase separation properties of FA-associated proteins [55, 57, 59–61]. For example, condensates exhibiting liquid-like properties can be reconstituted on lipid membranes in vitro using a minimal set of FA-associated proteins (e.g., paxillin and focal adhesion kinase (FAK)) at physiological concentrations [55–57]. Importantly, condensates that mirror the biochemical composition of FAs and efficiently accelerate cell spreading can be reconstituted in cells using an optogenetic approach in which the dimerization of paxillin is induced by light [57]. The in vitro phase separation potential of paxillin and deletion derivatives also positively correlates with their ability to drive FA assembly in cells, underscoring the important role of paxillin LLPS in orchestrating high-order assembly of FA [57].ULK has recently been shown to directly bind to and phosphorylate paxillin at S32 and S119, which elevates the threshold concentration of paxillin required for LLPS in vitro by reducing paxillin homotypic oligomerization and also weakening heterotypic interactions between paxillin and other FA proteins, such as FAK [62]. Therefore, by altering the biophysical properties of paxillin, ULK alters focal adhesion dynamics in a way that favors the disassembled non-condensed state, impeding cell migration and mechano-transduction (Figure 3b). This function of ULK does not require other components of the autophagy-inducing ULK complex [62]. Given the importance of FA signaling and migration in tumor metastasis, it is noteworthy that targeting the ULK-paxillin signaling axis suppresses the metastatic potential of breast cancer cells in a xenograft model and that reduced ULK levels correlate with poor prognosis in patients with breast cancer [62–64].

One such process involves the regulation of the Hippo-Yorkie signaling pathway in Drosophila [66]. Phosphorylation of Yorkie by Atg1 in Drosophila at sites that are conserved in mammals inhibits Yorkie activity without affecting its ability to localize to the nucleus (Figure 4) [66], raising the possibility that the growth-inhibiting properties of ULK/Atg1 may result from altering the dynamics of Yorkie-containing condensates.Cell death is another process that has the potential to be regulated by ULK-dependent changes in condensation dynamics. ERES-associated proteins enable the recruitment of components necessary to assemble ERES and the formation of transport carriers from the ER. Many ERES-associated proteins are highly disordered [74–77]. In Drosophila S2 cells, specific stress conditions including amino acid starvation inhibit the protein transport through the secretory pathway and lead to the formation of membrane-less assemblies known as ‘Sec bodies’. Closing remarksRecent investigations have provided significant insights into the spatiotemporal regulation and function of ULK/Atg1. Depending on the cell state, the functions of ULK/Atg1, many of which involve biomolecular phase transitions, may vary. For example, under basal physiologic conditions ULK/Atg1 regulates protein and membrane trafficking [81–84], FA dynamics [62], and cell growth [66]. Upon metabolic challenge, the function of ULK may be redirected towards the regulation of autophagy [12], RNP granule dynamics [49], or other processes (e.g. catabolic glycolysis [85, 86]), and in cases of extreme stress, ULK may contribute to cell death [71]. Although ULK/Atg1’s specific roles in the regulation of different processes involving biomolecular condensation needs to be further evaluated (see Outstanding Questions), current examples raise the possibility that depending on the cellular context, the recruitment of ULK into physiologically relevant biomolecular condensates through its interactions with a diverse array of partners provides a means of concentrating ULK to promote trans-autophosphorylation and activation. Upon activation, ULK/Atg1 activity may in turn regulate biomolecular condensate dynamics via two distinct mechanisms: ULK1/Atg1 can either regulate the condensation threshold of biomolecular condensates through direct phosphorylation of core constituents (such as Atg13 and paxillin) or modulate the material property of biomolecular condensates by phosphorylating enzymes involved in regulating condensate dynamics (such as VCP).Given that ULK’s activity is linked to many physiological conditions, such as nutrient and energy availability and redox status [33, 87, 88], ULK has the potential to differentially regulate phase-separation dynamics in a condensate and context specific manner. Considering that ULK is the only kinase within the core autophagy machinery, various efforts have been devoted to pharmacologically inhibiting or activating ULK for therapeutic purposes [89, 90]. However, the potential side effects of these therapies that may target autophagy-unrelated functions of ULK must be taken into consideration. Approaches that target a specific function of ULK/Atg1 or its ability to be recruited into specific phase separated compartments, instead of its kinase activity in general, may be more favorable for disease treatment with minimal side effects.
