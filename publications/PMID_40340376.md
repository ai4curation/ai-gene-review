The 17-hydroxylase and 17,20-lyase activities of cytochrome P450 17A1 are
required for androgen biosynthesis, which is the target of the prostate-cancer
drug abiraterone acetate. Cytochrome b5 (b5) stimulates the 17,20-lyase activity
8-fold in reconstituted systems containing P450-oxidoreductase (POR); however,
the mechanism of the b5 effect and the rate-limiting step(s) of these catalytic
cycles are not known. Using stopped flow spectroscopy and rapid chemical quench
under single-turnover conditions, we determined the effects of b5 on rates of
individual steps of the 17-hydroxylase and 17,20-lyase reactions. Steps prior to
and including oxygen binding were rapid for both reactions (>9 s-1), and rates
of dehydroepiandrosterone release (4-5 s-1) were also fast and not increased by
b5. Starting with 17-hydroxypregnenolone-bound oxyferrous P450 17A1, the
electron transfer rate was slower from b5 than from POR (2.9 ± 0.2 versus 7.4 ±
0.1 s-1), whereas return to ferric P450 17A1 was faster with b5 than from POR
(1.7 ± 0.3 versus 1.3 ± 0.1 s-1). Using the same conditions as electron transfer
experiments for rapid chemical quench, rates of dehydroepiandrosterone formation
were equivalent with reduced POR or b5 (2.4 ± 0.4 versus 2.3 ± 0.3 s-1,
respectively); b5 reduced hydrogen peroxide formation under multiple turnover
conditions. We conclude that rates of electron transfer and product formation
for the 17,20-lyase reaction starting with reduced oxyferrous P450 17A1 are
similar and partially rate-limiting to either POR or b5. These data suggest that
the b5 effect on the 17,20-lyase reaction manifests only during multiple
turnover conditions rather than enhancing single-turnover kinetics.

While the rate of the second electron transfer is rate-limiting for camphor hydroxylation catalyzed by bacterial P450cam (P450 101), eukaryotic P450s generally exhibit much slower product formation rates and poorer coupling efficiencies than P450 101. Lipids and detergents were obtained as follows: 1,2-dilauroyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DLPC) from Doosan Serdary Research Laboratory (Toronto, Canada), 1-stearoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (PE) from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL), α-[4-(1,1,3,3-tetramethylbutyl)phenyl]-w-hydroxy-poly(oxy-1,2-ethanediyl) (Anapoe-X-305) from Anatrace (Maumee, OH); polyoxyethylene-9-lauryl ether (Brij) from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, MO), and Emulgen 911 was generously provided by Kao Chemical (Japan).

P450 17A1 Plasmid Construction, Expression in Escherichia coli, and Purification.Human P450 17A1 with N-terminal modification and C-terminal hexahistidine tag was expressed in Escherichia coli C41 cells using plasmid pLW01-mh17A1His614 as previously described.4 Using a linear gradient of 5-100 mM histidine in buffer, the red fractions eluted from the Ni-NTA column were further purified on a hydroxyapatite column, omitting the Octyl Sepharose column as previously employed.4 All fractions containing a single P450 17A1 band on SDS-PAGE were pooled and concentrated by centrifugal ultrafiltration. The protein solution was exchanged five times with 100 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.4, containing 30% (v/v) glycerol, 1 mM TCEP, and 0.02% (v/v) Emulgen 911. The exchanged solution was concentrated using a PD-10 column (GE Healthcare), sterilized with a 0.22 μm filter, and stored in small aliquots at −80 °C. Purified P450 17A1 resolved as a single band on SDS-PAGE with a specific content of 13-14 nmol P450/mg protein using an extinction coefficient of 91 mM−1cm−1 for the ferrous-CO complex.15,16

Kinetics of Substrate Binding.The rates of Preg, 17Preg, and DHEA binding to ferric P450 17A1 were determined using a SF61DX2 stopped-flow spectrophotometer (Hi-Tech Scientific). The purified protein was loaded onto and eluted from a Sephadex G-25 column (13.5 x 1.5 cm) equilibrated with 100 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.4, 15% (v/v) glycerol (elution buffer) to completely remove the excess Prog added during purification. The protein was diluted to a concentration of 3 μM in elution buffer containing 100 μM DLPC and loaded in syringe 1 of the stopped flow spectrophotometer housed at 25 °C. Aliquots (100 μL) from syringe 1 were rapidly mixed with an equal volume of elution buffer containing substrate (50-100 μM) from syringe 2. Kinetic traces were recorded in photodiode array mode to obtain complete spectra from 330 to 700 nm (200 scans, logarithmic interval, over 150 s), and the change from low-spin to high-spin state was derived from the absorbance change from 417 to 395 nm. Rate constants and amplitudes were calculated by fitting the absorbance data with multiple exponential functions using Kinetic Asyst2 software provided by Hi-Tech.

Rates of Product Formation Under Single-Turnover Conditions by Rapid Chemical Quench.P450 17A1-substrate complex (2 μM P450 17A1 plus 80 μM Preg or 17Preg) and 0.1 μM methyl viologen was incubated with a 50-fold molar excess of phospholipid and one molar equivalent of POR or b5 in elution buffer overnight at 4 °C on a cold plate in an anaerobic glove box (Coy Technologies). P450 17A1 + POR or P450 17A1 + b5 were reduced with 3-3.5 or 2 electron equivalents, respectively, with a standardized solution of sodium dithionite under anaerobic conditions in the glove box while monitoring with a Nanodrop 2000c (ThermoFisher), to limit product formation to a single reaction cycle. The reduced mixtures were loaded into syringe 1 of the QFM-400 rapid chemical quench apparatus (Molecular Kinetics Inc). Syringe 2 was charged with air-saturated elution buffer (256 μM O2 at 25 °C) containing all components except proteins, and the reaction temperature was maintained at 22-25 °C using a circulating water bath. Aliquots (50 μL each syringe) were rapidly mixed, and at designated times, the reaction was rapidly quenched with 50 μL of 2 M HCl from syringe 3. Mixing and quenching were carried out in the glove box manually without the rapid quench apparatus for mixing times >1 s. Typically, 15-20 samples were collected during the time course of the reaction. Products for each reaction mixture (Preg → 17Preg and 17Preg → DHEA) were quantified by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) after extraction and derivatization as oximes as described.4 Aliquots (10 μL) of quenched reaction mixture and 190 μL deionized water were transferred into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes and mixed with 150 μL methanol, 220 μL acetonitrile, and 100 μL deuterated internal standard steroids (Preg, 17Preg, DHEA) in acetonitrile. The tubes were vortexted on VWR multi-tube vortexer for 4 min at high speed and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 5 min. The organic supernatant was transferred to 2 mL autosampler vials and mixed with 300 μL water and 1 mL methyl-t-butyl ether (MTBE). After mixing on the vortexer for 4 min and standing at room temperature for 5 min, the lower aqueous layer was aspirated and discarded, and the organic layer was dried under nitrogen at 40 °C. The dried extracts were resuspended in 50 μL of 1 M ammonium hydroxide and 100 μL of 1 M hydroxylamine and heated at 90 °C for 30 min. The reaction mixtures were cooled to room temperature, diluted with 300 μL water, and vortexed for 4 min with 1 mL MTBE. After standing for 5 min, the lower aqueous layer was aspirated and discarded, and the organic layer was dried under nitrogen at 40 °C. The steroid standards and samples were reconstituted in 100 μL 50% (v/v) aqueous methanol, vortexed for 15 seconds, and transferred to the 350 μL vial inserts for LC-MS/MS using mass ratio monitoring and quantitation using an external calibration curve with simultaneous internal standard measurement to correct for recovery and ion suppression as described.4

Rates of Electron Transfer from Ferrous b5 to Oxyferrous P450 17A1-substrate Complexes.

Using isosbestic points from auto-oxidation experiments, wavelengths used to monitor the second electron transfer were 442 and 433 nm for oxyferrous P450 17A1 or 412 and 422 nm for b5 with Preg or 17Preg substrates, respectively. As observed with reduced POR, a fraction of the rapidly formed oxyferrous P450 17A1-Preg complex and ferrous b5 both convert to their ferric states faster when mixed than the auto-oxidation rates of either protein alone, with triphasic kinetics (Figure 6, Table 2). About 30-45% of both the oxyferrous P450 17A1-Preg complex and b5 converted to ferric states in the most rapid phase at rates of 2.6 ± 0.4 and 3.7 ± 0.5 s−1, respectively. For the same experiment with oxyferrous P450 17A1-17Preg complex and b5, the corresponding rate constants of the first phase are 1.7 ± 0.3 and 2.9 ± 0.2 s−1, respectively (Table 2). The slower rate constants of the second and third phases are consistent with oxyferrous P450 17A1-substrate auto-oxidation and/or uncoupling processes, within a factor of 2 (Tables 1 and 2).

In comparing the kinetics of oxyferrous P450 17A1-substrate transitions to the ferric state with POR or b5 as the reductant, the rapid phases are <1.5-fold faster from experiments with b5 than those with POR. Conversely, rate constants of redox partner oxidation are consistently slower for b5 than for POR, as expected from the reduction potentials of the two proteins (+25 or −270 mV, respectively). The rate constants with which b5 and oxyferrous P450 17A1 return to their ferric proteins are fairly similar, whereas the rate constants with which POR oxidizes are 3.3-5.7 times faster than the oxyferrous P450 17A1-substrate complexes return to the ferric protein. In addition, the amplitude for the rapid phases of ferric P450 17A1 formation with both substrates are ~40% with b5 versus ~20% when the low-potential POR is the electron donor. These results indicate that b5 can provide the second electron to a fraction of the oxyferrous P450 17A1-substrate complex at a rate similar to the rate of electron transfer from reduced POR.

Product Formation Rates Under Single-Turnover Conditions Using Rapid Chemical Quench.To determine how the electron-transfer rates measured in the previous experiments compare with rates of product formation, we performed single-turnover kinetics using a rapid chemical quench under the same conditions. Starting with preformed P450 17A1-substrate complex and redox partner(s) that are all stoichiometrically reduced with sodium dithionite in syringe 1 and air-saturated buffer in syringe 2, the reaction is limited to one turnover. The reaction progression was sampled after mixing times of 5 to 1000 ms (Figure 7). Because the rate of oxygen binding to the ferrous P450 17A1-Preg complex is fast (>50 s−1, Table S1) relative to electron transfer, the scheme isolates the rates of the second electron transfer and subsequent chemical steps (Figure 1). The rates of Preg hydroxylation to 17Preg (7.3 ± 1.1 and 9.7 ± 1.2 s−1) and of 17Preg conversion to DHEA (2.4 ± 0.4 and 2.3 ± 0.4 s−1) were similar using POR or b5, respectively, as the source of the second electron (Table 3). Using a mixture of DLPC + PE for reconstitution, the rates of DHEA product formation from 17Preg substrate with POR or b5 were 2.6 or 3.8 s−1, respectively, and almost twice as much DHEA was formed as with DLPC alone (not shown). Thus, the relative rates of electron transfer and product formation using POR versus b5 under single-turnover conditions are insufficient to explain the b5-mediated increase in steady-state 17,20-lyase activity.

Hydrogen Peroxide Formation.Single-turnover experiments using equimolar amounts of P450 17A1, POR, and b5 together are difficult to interpret, because POR efficiently reduces ferric b5 faster than the oxyferrous P450 17A1-substrate complex. Nevertheless, we directly assessed hydrogen peroxide formation under steady-state conditions during the 17,20-lyase reaction using P450 17A1, POR, and 17Preg with or without b5. Control experiments (Figure 8A) demonstrate that the NADPH solution (but not control NADP+ solution) alone contributes 2 μM hydrogen peroxide to the incubation mixture. In the absence of P450 17A1, POR with NADPH generates an additional stoichiometric 5 μM hydrogen peroxide over 20 min (7 μM total, NADPH oxidase activity). Inclusion of b5 delays but does not prevent the generation of an additional 5 μM hydrogen peroxide, which is consistent with POR reduction of b5 first and then of oxygen. These findings suggest that even the weak, transient interaction of POR with a P450 incapable of turnover is sufficient to compete with NADPH oxidase activity. Further incubation of the P450 17A1-17Preg complex with NADPH and POR generates hydrogen peroxide over time, but less than incubation of POR with NADPH alone (Figure 8A,B). DISCUSSIONThe 17,20-lyase activity of P450 17A1 and the b5-mediated enhancement of this reaction have been the topic of many studies, due to both mechanistic intrigue and physiologic importance as the gateway to all androgen biosynthesis. Using purified proteins in a reconstituted system, we found that b5 may substitute for POR as the source of the second electron to support both activities, which we could not demonstrate previously using native P450 17A1 in yeast microsomes.26 The rates of electron transfer, however, are not faster for b5 than for POR. Furthermore, rapid chemical quench experiments demonstrate that the electron transfer from b5 in the absence of POR yields product for both reactions at rates similar as when the electron derives from POR under single-turnover conditions. These results are consistent with other studies that found electron transfer from b527,28 but did not quantify product formation. The major finding of this series of studies, however, is that none of these rate enhancements account for the b5-mediated 8-fold increase in steady-state rate of product formation26 and coupling efficiency4 of the 17,20-lyase reaction observed in several systems that include both POR and b5.The prior study27 using human P450 17A1 with rat POR and rabbit b5 found similar rates of electron transfer between b5 and the oxyferrous P450 17A1-17Preg complex (~1 s−1) as reported here. In contrast, that same study reported a 10-fold slower rate of electron transfer when reduced POR replaced b5 and an auto-oxidation rate of POR (1.4 s−1) too fast to perform a meaningful experiment. Furthermore, the deduced rate of electron transfer from POR (~0.1 s−1) is too slow to account for product formation, as determined from our rapid chemical quench experiments. Potential explanations for the discrepancy in results with POR include the different species of POR used in the two studies, under-reduction of POR, and exposure to oxygen.Consistent with most P450 enzymes studied, the rates of substrate binding, reduction of the ferric P450 17A1-substrate complex, and oxygen binding are fast (>9 s−1 at 10 μM substrate). We also observed biphasic kinetics for substrate binding, and prior studies found that the data fit best to a conformational selection model.29 The rates of the second electron transfer and product formation (1-8 s−1) are not markedly slower than several preceding steps, and direct measurements show that product (17Preg and DHEA) release rates with or without b5 (1-5 s−1) are also similar. The rates of redox partner oxidation are consistently slightly faster than the disappearance of the oxyferrous P450 17A1-substrate species and fastest for POR oxidation, as was seen for P450 2B4.12,17 Also similar to P450 2B4, the oxyferrous P450 17A1-substrate complex reverted back to the ferric protein slightly faster (fast phase) when reduced by b5 than POR, which for P450 2B4 was shown to reflect stabilization of the hydroperoxo intermediate in the presence of POR versus rapid protonation in the presence of b5.12 Taken together, these data demonstrate that no step in the P450 17A1 cycle can be considered primarily rate-limiting, consistent with the modest intermolecular deuterium kinetic isotope effects for the 17-hydroxylase reactions.6The first phases of electron transfers from POR or b5 to the oxyferrous P450 17A1-substrate complexes are sufficiently fast to account for product formation, as determined from the rapid chemical quench experiments (Tables 2 and 3). The rates of the slower phases are either consistent with auto-oxidation processes or up to 2-fold faster. After receiving the second electron, the oxyferrous P450 17A1-substrate complex partitions to uncoupled reactions (hydrogen peroxide release) or product formation in the first phase. The second phase might also represent further reduction to water in this highly uncoupled system. The multiphasic kinetics for the second electron transfer is consistent with conformational heterogeneity for oxyferrous P450 17A1-substrate complexes and differences in these conformational ensembles imposed upon interactions with POR versus b5. Our data do not distinguish among the proposed chemical mechanisms for the 17,20-lyase reaction, but the multiphasic kinetics might also reflect two or more mechanistic pathways for the 17,20-lyase and/or uncoupling reactions. The liposomes used for these studies are a surrogate for intracellular membranes, and the high concentrations of protein and glycerol required for these experiments only approximate the native environment. The observed rates are best viewed as relative rates for comparisons among the different steps, substrates, and redox partners for each experiment. We could not directly monitor spectroscopically any additional intermediates between the oxyferrous P450 17A1-17Preg complex and the resting ferric state that involve proton transfers and/or bonding changes. To compensate for the technical limitations and constraints of spectroscopic kinetic studies, we conducted rapid chemical quench studies to measure product formation. Not surprisingly, the rapid chemical quench experiments afford much faster rates for single turnovers than for steady-state kinetics (~30-fold for the 17,20-lyase reaction with b5),4,23 which reflects the poor coupling efficiency and partially rate-limiting product release rates. The lower yield of DHEA (Table 3) than seen in steady-state kinetics can be attributed to the suboptimal activity from reconstitution with DLPC alone31,32 and/or conditions used to prevent protein precipitation, which were necessarily identical to conditions used in the electron transfer experiments. In contrast to steady-state kinetic experiments, only one equivalent of either POR or b5 but not both proteins were used to isolate the electron transfer rates of either redox partner in these single-turnover experiments. Nevertheless, the results presented herein are reproducible and largely consistent with results obtain using other assay systems such as yeast microsomes,6,26 for which P450 content is too low to perform spectroscopic experiments.

The hydrogen peroxide generation experiments directly demonstrated the improved coupling of the 17,20-lyase reaction in the presence of b5. CONCLUSIONIn summary, we found that no step is primarily rate-limiting for the 17,20-lyase reaction of P450 17A1, and product dissociation rates are not faster in the presence of b5. Rates for the second electron transfer are slightly faster from reduced POR versus b5, but the single-turnover DHEA formation rates are similar with POR or b5. When reconstituted with POR, addition of b5 reduces peroxide formation during steady-state turnover for the P450 17A1-catalyzed 17,20-lyase reaction with 17Preg substrate. The multiphasic kinetics of decay suggest that the oxyferrous P450 17A1-substrate complex is heterogeneous, which might reflect different species that either undergo uncoupling reactions or catalyze the 17,20-lyase reaction via one or more mechanisms. The minimal effect of b5 on the rates of microscopic steps support the conclusion that the dominant effect of b5 on steady-state kinetics is to increase the coupling of NADPH oxidation to product formation for the 17,20-lyase reaction. Our data, however, do not define the chemical mechanism of the reduced uncoupling, reveal the effect of b5 on active-site geometry, or identify the nature of the iron-oxygen species responsible for catalyzing the 17,20-lyase reaction.
