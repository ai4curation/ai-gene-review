Bacterial cell envelope polymers are often modified with acyl esters that
modulate physiology, enhance pathogenesis and provide antibiotic resistance.
Here, using the D-alanylation of lipoteichoic acid (Dlt) pathway as a paradigm,
we have identified a widespread strategy for how acylation of cell envelope
polymers occurs. In this strategy, a membrane-bound O-acyltransferase (MBOAT)
protein transfers an acyl group from an intracellular thioester onto the
tyrosine of an extracytoplasmic C-terminal hexapeptide motif. This motif
shuttles the acyl group to a serine on a separate transferase that moves the
cargo to its destination. In the Dlt pathway, here studied in Staphylococcus
aureus and Streptococcus thermophilus, the C-terminal 'acyl shuttle' motif that
forms the crucial pathway intermediate is found on a transmembrane microprotein
that holds the MBOAT protein and the other transferase together in a complex. In
other systems, found in both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria as well as
some archaea, the motif is fused to the MBOAT protein, which interacts directly
with the other transferase. The conserved chemistry uncovered here is widely
used for acylation throughout the prokaryotic world.

Bacterial cells surround themselves with complex cell envelopes that feature a variety of polymers important both for basic cellular functions and for interactions with entities in the local environment1. These cell envelope components include peptidoglycan and diverse secondary cell wall polymers, in addition to lipoteichoic acids (LTAs), which are major components of the Gram-positive cell envelope that are anchored in the outer leaflet of the cytoplasmic membrane2. Bacteria frequently acylate their cell envelope polymers to regulate their own physiology3–5 and to shield themselves from both antibiotics6–8 and host immune system effector molecules7,9–11. How this acylation of extracytoplasmic polymers is carried out using high-energy acyl donors made inside the cell is largely unknown.

Composed of species-variable repeat units linked by phosphodiester bonds (Extended Data Fig. 1a), LTAs and the structurally related wall teichoic acids (WTAs) are commonly modified with d-alanine esters10–12 that modulate the charge of the phosphate-rich backbone to increase cell fitness under a variety of conditions, including infection13–15. d-alanine modifications on LTAs and WTAs control activity of cell wall autolysins3,16,17, allow bacteria to evade innate immune responses10,11,18–20, and increase resistance to cationic antibiotics8,11,21, suggesting Dlt pathway proteins in pathogens could be targets for antimicrobial agents8,22. Teichoic acid d-alanylation has even been shown to provide tolerance against phages23 and to support host-microbiome symbiosis24.

Four proteins encoded by the dlt operon have been identified as necessary for teichoic acid d-alanylation across organisms12,25 (Fig. 1a). Functions for two of these proteins, the cytosolic proteins DltA and DltC, have been established: DltA uses ATP to adenylate free d-alanine and then transfers it onto the phosphopantetheinyl arm of the aminoacyl carrier protein DltC as a thioester26–28. The functions of the other two proteins, DltB and DltD, have not been elucidated. The polytopic membrane protein DltB is a member of the membrane-bound O-acyltransferase (MBOAT) protein family that is conserved throughout all domains of life29. DltC binds to DltB, and a structure of the DltBC complex led to a model for how LTA d-alanylation occurs30. In this model, DltC inserts its d-alanylated arm into DltB’s central tunnel, and LTA binds in this tunnel from the extracellular surface of the membrane to receive d-alanine directly from DltC. This model was called into question by a study showing that DltD, which contains an extracellular SGNH hydrolase-like domain31, is required for LTA d-alanylation32. The presence of a Ser-His-Asp catalytic triad in DltD suggested it could form a serine ester intermediate with d-alanine. However, DltD’s extracellular domain cannot fit into DltB’s tunnel, implying there must be another pathway intermediate to serve as a d-alanyl donor for DltD.

In reactions containing the five wild-type proteins, we observed signals corresponding to d-[14C]alanine-modified DltC and DltD on an SDS-PAGE gel imaged by autoradiography (Fig. 4b; Extended Data Fig. 7a–b; Supplementary Fig. 2). Inspired by a report on the internal thioester cleavage mechanism of human complement protein C451, as well as previous use of a lysine analog to trap acyl-enzyme intermediates52, we wondered if transfer would occur to a DltX mutant in which the invariant C-terminal motif tyrosine was replaced with a lysine (DltXY56K). We observed a signal consistent with a d-[14C]alanine-modified DltX intermediate (Fig. 4c; Extended Data Fig. 8a–b; Supplementary Fig. 2). When we used 3xFLAG-DltXY56K in the reconstitution, the signal shifted to a higher mass range in the SDS-PAGE autoradiograph, confirming that it was due to a modification on DltX (Fig. 4c). The signal was also dependent on the position of the lysine because we observed no labeling of mutants in which the C-terminal motif tyrosine was replaced with phenylalanine and the flanking residues were individually replaced with lysine. Lack of labeling for these mutants could not be attributed to disrupted binding of the C-terminal motif to DltB’s central cavity because making the same lysine substitutions in DltX containing the tyrosine at motif position 3 did not prevent transfer of d-[14C]alanine to DltD (Extended Data Fig. 8c). (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4; Supplementary Tables 14–17). Our results support the model for LTA d-alanylation shown in Fig. 4d, in which DltX acts downstream of DltB and upstream of DltD as part of a cascade of covalent intermediates, with the tyrosine of its C-terminal acyl shuttle motif serving to ferry d-alanine from the phosphopantetheinyl arm of DltC to DltD’s catalytic serine.Many other pathways contain a C-terminal acyl shuttle motifWe wondered whether the strategy we have uncovered for LTA d-alanylation is conserved in pathways beyond those encoded by dlt and dlt-like operons. An acylated tyrosine intermediate was recently identified on an internal loop in an acyltransferase that belongs to a class unrelated to MBOAT proteins53. This suggested that the role for tyrosine as a nucleophile in acylation pathways could be widespread. Many cell envelope polymer acylation systems, such as those responsible for acetylation of peptidoglycan in Neisseria gonorrhoeae54 and Helicobacter pylori55, alginate in Pseudomonas aeruginosa56,57, and secondary cell wall polysaccharides in Bacillus anthracis and cereus5,58, include both MBOAT proteins like DltB as well as DltD-like proteins; a similarly composed system of unknown function even appears to be present in some archaea (Fig. 4e; Extended Data Fig. 9a). For the alginate acetylation pathway, there is evidence that the MBOAT protein and DltD-like protein interact directly59, and AlphaFold modeling suggests this is also true for the other pathways (Extended Data Fig. 9b; Supplementary Fig. 5).Though none of these systems contain an ORF encoding a DltX-like protein, an alignment of >14,000 of the MBOAT proteins showed that all contained a six-amino acid C-terminal motif very similar to that of DltX. (Fig. 4e; Extended Data Fig. 9c; Supplementary Table 18). In these proteins, the motif, which again is predicted to bind in the membrane-spanning tunnel (Fig. 4f; Extended Data Fig. 9d; Supplementary Fig. 6), lies at the end of the second of two additional transmembrane helices not found in DltB (Extended Data Fig. 10). Furthermore, in all but two of these systems’ MBOAT proteins, the residue in motif position 3 was an invariant tyrosine. We propose that these invariant tyrosines are essential for acylation, receiving acyl groups from cytosolic donors and then passing them to the DltD-like transferases. Notably, the two exceptions contained serine or cysteine at motif position 3, and it is tempting to speculate that these residues also form covalent intermediates.

DltX contains a functionally critical C-terminal motif

For further clues to DltX function, we examined the amino acid sequence conservation across 895 distinct DltX proteins from Bacillota (Firmicutes) (Supplementary Table 13). While there are some conserved residues in the transmembrane portion of the protein, the most striking conservation is at the C-terminal end (Fig. 3a), which is predicted to be extracellular (Extended Data Fig. 2a). The last six residues, which we designate the C-terminal motif, are all conserved, with the highest conservation in motif positions one (100% Phe/Tyr), three (100% Tyr), and six (>99% Phe, with the remainder Tyr). Furthermore, in no instance is the terminal phenylalanine followed by another amino acid. This high degree of conservation suggested a critical role for the C-terminal motif in the function of DltX. Indeed, a DltX variant missing the final six amino acids was unable to complement the loss of wild-type DltX (Fig. 3b, top; Extended Data Fig. 4a) even though it retained the ability to form a complex with DltB and DltD (Fig. 3b, bottom). We concluded that DltX has a role in the Dlt pathway beyond enabling formation of the DltBDX complex, and that the C-terminal motif is required for this role.

We next tested two sets of S. aureus strains expressing DltX variants with changes to the C-terminal motif for growth on tunicamycin. One set of strains contained a conservative substitution at each motif position so we could assess whether any of the C-terminal motif residues were strictly essential for LTA d-alanylation. We found that a conservative substitution was tolerated at all motif positions except position 3, where substituting tyrosine with phenylalanine prevented growth on tunicamycin (Fig. 3c; Extended Data Fig. 4b). The second set of strains queried the terminal phenylalanine, testing whether it could be removed or whether an alanine insertion was tolerated before or after it (Fig. 3d; Extended Data Fig. 5a). Although a tyrosine substitution was tolerated (Fig. 3c), the terminal phenylalanine could not be removed, nor could alanine or a second phenylalanine be inserted before it. Moreover, insertion of alanine after the phenylalanine was only tolerated when this DltX variant was expressed at a high level (Extended Data Fig. 5b). DiscussionAcylation of extracellular polymers is an important contributor to virulence in a wide variety of bacterial species, but how transfer occurs from cytosolic donors to polymers outside the cytoplasmic membrane has been a longstanding question. We showed here that LTA d-alanylation requires a three-member membrane protein complex, DltBDX, to transfer activated d-alanine from a cytosolic carrier protein onto the extracellular LTA polymer. Transfer requires a six-amino acid C-terminal motif on DltX that contains an invariant tyrosine, and we have shown that this tyrosine plays a direct role in the chemistry of the d-alanyl transfer process, forming an acylated intermediate. This function makes DltX unusual amongst membrane microproteins, which are thought to act primarily as regulators of larger proteins and protein complexes60,61. We have also shown that a similar tyrosine-containing motif is found at the C-termini of many MBOAT proteins involved in acyl group transfer to other bacterial polymers, including peptidoglycan and alginate. This motif undoubtedly plays an analogous role in these other systems, acting as an acyl group shuttle that moves the acyl group from a cytosolic donor—likely acetyl-CoA in most cases—to an extracytoplasmic transferase domain. It has not escaped our attention that this shared chemistry involving a short region of highly conserved residues makes these systems ideal targets to explore for peptidomimetic inhibitors that block virulence, especially because such inhibitors would act at the external surface of the cytoplasmic membrane.

Co-immunoprecipitation of FLAG-tagged proteins from S. aureus.A modified version of a previously published protocol was used64. S. aureus strains BJS158, BJS159, and BJS338 were used for these experiments. Co-immunoprecipitations that are directly compared in figure panels were performed in parallel on the same day. ~5 colonies per culture were used to start 25-mL cultures of a given strain in TSB with 10 μg/mL erythromycin. Cultures were grown overnight at 30 °C with aeration via shaking. The overnight cultures were then diluted 1:100 (v:v) into 1 L of TSB with 10 μg/mL erythromycin and 1 mM IPTG, and these large cultures were incubated either at 37 °C (for BJS158 vs. BJS159) or 30 °C (for BJS159 vs. BJS338) for ~7 hours. Cells were pelleted at 4 °C (5,000 x g), and the pellet for each strain (after removal of supernatant) was resuspended in 35 mL of 1x PBS, pH 7.4 before being pelleted once more. The supernatant was removed again, and the pellets were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. On the day of the co-IP, pellets were thawed and then resuspended in 30 mL of lysis buffer (1x PBS, pH 7.4 with 10 μg/mL DNase I, 20 μg/mL RNase, 200 μg/mL lysostaphin, 5 mM MgCl2, and 1 EDTA-free cOmplete™ protease inhibitor cocktail tablet per 50 mL liquid total). The resuspension was tumbled at 37 °C for 1 hour to promote lysis. Then, the cells were cooled on ice before passaging through an EmulsiFlex-C3 cell disruptor (Avestin) ~5 times at 15,000–20,000 psi to lyse. Debris from the lysis was collected by centrifugation (10,000 x g, 15 min., 4 °C), and the membrane fraction was then collected from the supernatant by ultracentrifugation (140,000 x g, 45 min., 4 °C). The membrane pellet was resuspended in 3 mL of membrane extraction buffer (1x PBS, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 1% w/v DDM) using a Dounce homogenizer (Wheaton), and the mixture was tumbled at 4 °C for 1 hour. To remove any remaining debris, the mixture was subjected to a further round of centrifugation (10,000 x g, 15 min., 4 °C), and then the supernatant was added to a tube containing 3 mL of “no salt” wash buffer (1x PBS, pH 7.4, 1% w/v DDM) to dilute the salt. This sample was then incubated with 37.5 μL (packed volume) of pre-equilibrated α-FLAG M2 magnetic beads (Sigma) at 4 °C for 1 hour, rocking. After incubation, the liquid was separated from the magnetic beads using a magnetic tube rack. The beads were then washed four times (5 minutes rocking at 4 °C) with 500 μL of wash buffer with a gradient of DDM concentrations (all 1x PBS, pH 7.4 with 200 mM NaCl plus 0.5% w/v DDM for the first wash, 0.25% w/v DDM for the second, 0.125% w/v for the third, and 0.0625% w/v for the fourth). To elute bound proteins, the magnetic beads were resuspended in 125 μL elution buffer (1x PBS, pH 7.4 with 200 mM NaCl, 0.05% w/v DDM, and 150 μg/mL 3xFLAG peptide), and samples were incubated (rotating) for 15 minutes at room temperature. The supernatant was then collected after separating the magnetic beads using the magnetic rack, and a second elution step was performed. Elution fractions were concentrated down to ~50 μL using a 0.5-mL 3 kDa MWCO Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter unit. Only the samples from the first elution rounds are shown in the images in the main text figures. These samples were incubated with the appropriate amount of 6x SDS-PAGE loading buffer for 30 minutes at room temperature and then run on a 4–20% polyacrylamide tris-glycine (TGX) gel (140 V for 15 minutes then 180 V until the dye front ran to the bottom of the gel). The gel was stained with Imperial stain (Thermo Fisher) for 2 hours at room temperature and destained with MilliQ water. Protein bands (and corresponding empty slices of gel from the control sample elution lane) were excised and stored in MilliQ water before submission for mass spectrometry analysis at the Harvard Medical School Taplin Mass Spectrometry Facility.
