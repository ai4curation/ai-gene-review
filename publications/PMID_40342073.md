Clostridioides difficile is an anaerobic enteric pathogen that disseminates in
the environment as a dormant spore. For C. difficile and other sporulating
bacteria, the initiation of sporulation is a regulated process that prevents
spore formation under favorable growth conditions. In Bacillus subtilis, one
such mechanism for preventing sporulation is the prokaryotic 5-oxoprolinase,
PxpB (KipI), which impedes the activation of the main sporulation kinase. In
addition, PxpB functions as part of a complex that detoxifies the intermediate
metabolite, 5-oxoproline (OP), a harmful by-product of glutamic acid and its
derivatives. In this study, we investigate the orthologous Pxp proteins in C.
difficile to determine their roles in the regulation of sporulation and
metabolism. Through deletion of the pxpAGBC operon, we show that, unlike in B.
subtilis, the Pxp (Kip) proteins have no significant impact on sporulation.
However, we found that the pxp operon encodes a functional oxoprolinase that
facilitates detoxification of OP. Furthermore, our data demonstrate that PxpAGBC
not only detoxifies OP but also allows OP to be used as a nutrient source that
supports the growth of C. difficile, thereby facilitating the conversion of a
toxic by-product of metabolism into an energy source.

INTRODUCTION

Clostridioides difficile is an anaerobic Gram-positive pathogen that causes illnesses ranging from abdominal pain and diarrhea, to life-threatening pseudomembranous colitis (Dubberke and Olsen, 2012; Finn et al., 2021). C. difficile infections are easily spread and difficult to contain because the spore form of the bacterium is resistant to disinfectants and allows the pathogen to survive nearly indefinitely in a dormant state (Ali et al., 2011; A.N. Edwards et al., 2016; Shen, 2020). Although the structure and general form of C. difficile endospores are similar to other species, the environmental and nutritional conditions that lead to sporulation vary greatly among spore-forming bacteria (Lee et al., 2022; Mehdizadeh Gohari et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2019). While some nutritional requirements and regulatory factors are shared among the sporulating Firmicutes, it is not possible to predict the role in sporulation of a given factor based on the presence of orthologs with a known function in another spore-former (Edwards et al., 2014a; A. N. Edwards et al., 2016; Edwards et al., 2022).

Much of what is known about sporulation and nutrition in Gram-positive bacteria is based on extensive studies performed in the model spore-forming species, Bacillus subtilis (Errington, 2003; Sonenshein, 2007, 2000). In B. subtilis, sporulation initiates with the accumulation of the activated (phosphorylated) master regulator, Spo0A. The activation of Spo0A in B. subtilis is subject to a succession of negative and positive regulatory inputs that control the flow of phosphoryl groups to Spo0A via a phosphorelay. A key driver of the sporulation phosphorelay is the sensor kinase, KinA, which can be inhibited by the anti-kinase PxpB, resulting in decreased sporulation (Wang et al., 1997). In turn, the co-transcribed factor, PxpC, binds to PxpB to prevent PxpB interference with KinA activation of the phosphorelay (Jacques et al., 2011, 2008). Though the functions of PxpB and PxpC are known for B. subtilis, their roles in the sporulation regulation of other species have not been described.

While sporulation regulation is one function of the PxpB and PxpC proteins in B. subtilis, other studies have demonstrated metabolic functions of these proteins (Niehaus et al., 2017). In B. subtilis, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas putida, PxpB and PxpC bind with PxpA (also known as KipO, KipI, and KipA) to form an enzyme that metabolizes 5-oxoproline (Li et al., 1988; Niehaus et al., 2017; Seddon et al., 1984; Seddon and Meister, 1986). 5-oxoproline (OP), or L-pyroglutamic acid, is a ubiquitous waste product formed in bacteria from the spontaneous cyclization of glutamate, glutamine, and ɣ-glutamyl phosphate (Niehaus et al., 2017; Seddon et al., 1984). Free OP is also formed from the action of the pyrrolidone carboxyl peptidase (Pcp) which cleaves N-terminal 5-oxoproline from cyclized glutaminyl or glutamyl on peptides and is found in many prokaryotic organisms (Awadé et al., 1992, 1994; Doolittle and Armentrout, 1968). Accumulation of 5-oxoproline is toxic to eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells and impairs growth, but organisms that encode PxpABC orthologs can convert OP to glutamic acid, thereby preventing OP toxicity (Kumar and Bachhawat, 2010; Park et al., 2001; Vetter et al., 2019).

In this study, we sought to determine whether the C. difficile Pxp orthologs function as regulators of sporulation and/or metabolism of 5-oxoproline. Our results demonstrate that, unlike B. subtilis, PxpB and PxpC in C. difficile do not play a major role in sporulation initiation. We also demonstrate that the pxpAGBC operon encodes a functional oxoprolinase that allows for growth in OP. Further, our results indicate that 5-oxoproline is not only detoxified by C. difficile, it is an effective and valuable nutrient source. Overall, our data indicate that the Pxp proteins function primarily as a 5-oxoprolinase that promotes the growth of C. difficile in the presence of this toxic metabolite.

RESULTSIdentification and disruption of the PxpAGBC orthologs in C. difficileThe putative PxpB and PxpC proteins of C. difficile were identified in the strain 630 genome by sequence similarity to the Pxp proteins of B. subtilis (Figure S1A). The C. difficile PxpB and PxpC are encoded immediately downstream of orthologs of pxpA and pxpG (CD630_13840–13870), which are also found within the B. subtilis pxp locus (Figure S1A). C. difficile does not encode three additional genes (pxpI, pxpR, and lipC) that are encoded within the B. subtilis pxp region. We performed nested PCR on C. difficile cDNA templates and demonstrated that the pxbAGBC genes are transcribed as a polycistronic unit (Figure S1B). Based on the similarities between the shared proteins, we hypothesized that the C. difficile Pxp proteins may function as regulators of sporulation and metabolism, as observed in B. subtilis.PxpAGBC does not inhibit sporulation in C. difficileTo assess the Pxp protein functions in C. difficile, we generated a pxpAGBC (MC2375) mutant by allelic exchange and replacement with an ermB cassette. As a control, the pxpAGBC operon was restored at the pxp locus (Table 1, Figure S2) and the resultant strain was verified by whole-genome sequencing. As PxpB is an inhibitor of sporulation in B. subtilis, we next examined the impact of deleting the pxp genes on C. difficile sporulation. Sporulation assays were performed on 70:30 sporulation agar, comparing the pxpAGBC mutant to the parent strain (Figure 1). Deletion of the pxpAGBC operon had no apparent effect on sporulation frequency. The pxpAGBC deletion phenotype in C. difficile is in contrast to the results observed in B. subtilis, where pxpBC mutants produce more spores and over-expression of pxpB reduces sporulation (Wang et al., 1997). These results strongly suggest that unlike in B. subtilis, PxpB does not inhibit a sporulation-specific kinase in C. difficile.Since the Pxp complex did not inhibit sporulation in our standard assay, we considered that the Pxp proteins may conditionally influence sporulation, as observed in B. subtilis. In B. subtilis, the pxp operon is induced by glucose and repressed by glutamine (Wang et al., 1997). Previous studies in C. difficile found that the pxpAGBC operon is repressed by proline through PrdR, and by glucose, partially through CcpA, but the impact of these factors on pxp expression was modest (2–3 fold) (Antunes et al., 2012; Bouillaut et al., 2019). Data from our pilot experiments suggested that the secondary bile salt deoxycholate (DCA) induced transcription of the pxpAGBC genes. We further examined the effects of bile salts on expression of the pxp operon by assessing pxpB transcription. To this end, wild-type C. difficile was grown in BHIS broth supplemented with physiologically-relevant concentrations of deoxycholate (DCA, 0.5 mM), chenodeoxycholate (CDCA, 0.5 mM), lithocholate (LCA, 0.05 mM), cholate (CA, 2 mM), taurocholate (TA, 10 mM), glycocholate (GCA, 10 mM), taurochenodeoxycholate (TCDCA, 10 mM), or glycochenodeoxycholate (GCDCA, 1.5 mM) (Northfield and McColl, 1973), and samples were collected for qRT-PCR analysis. The hydrophobic bile salts DCA, CDCA, LCA, and GCDCA significantly increased pxpB expression, while CA and TA did not (Figure 2A). Further, we found that bile-dependent induction of pxpB transcription followed a dose-dependent pattern (Figure 2B). To determine if the detergent properties of bile salts played a role in pxpB expression, C. difficile was also exposed to sub-inhibitory concentrations of the detergents Triton X-100 and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (Figure 2A). Both detergents induced pxpB expression, but the differences were not statistically significant. These data demonstrate that pxp expression is robustly induced by several bile salts that impact growth, and that pxp induction is not universal to all bile acids or detergents.Since expression of the pxp genes are significantly induced, we investigated the upstream GntR regulator (CD1383) to assess potential involvement in pxp regulation (Figure S3A). We observed a modest (1.8 fold), but statistically significant increase in CD1383 transcription with the addition of CDCA, suggesting it may be involved in bile-dependent pxp regulation. To further examine the contribution of the CD1383 regulator to pxp regulation, we used a nisin-inducible CRISPRi to repress CD1383 gene expression (Edwards and McBride, 2023; Müh et al., 2019). A scrambled untargeted control sgRNA was included as a negative control. Expression of the targeted sgRNA-CD1383 resulted in a 20-fold decrease in CD1383 expression (Figure S3B). However, reduction of CD1383 expression had minimal impact on pxpB expression, which was reduced less than 2-fold. No differences in in pxpB expression were observed for the CRISPRi knockdown or control strain in the presence of 0.5 mM CDCA (not shown). These results suggest that CD1383 does not substantially impact expression of the pxp operon under the conditions tested.Previous studies demonstrated that some bile salts repress C. difficile growth and spore formation through undetermined mechanisms (Sorg and Sonenshein, 2009; Theriot et al., 2016; Usui et al., 2020). Examination of growth in the various bile salts in our hands showed that CDCA, DCA, LCA, GCDCA, and GCA repressed growth, similar to previous findings (Figure S4). Based on the induction of pxp expression and the growth impacts of these bile salts, we chose a representative bile acid, CDCA, to test for potential effects on sporulation through the Pxp locus (Figure S5). The addition of CDCA decreased spore production in the wild-type and pxp mutant strains proportionally, with similar decreases in the sporulation ratios across strains. Thus, even with induction of pxp expression by CDCA, sporulation was not differentially impacted in the wild-type or pxp mutants, further supporting that the Pxp locus does not have the role in C. difficile sporulation regulation observed in B. subtilis.C. difficile uses the toxic byproduct 5-oxoproline (OP) as a nutrient sourceAs mentioned, the PxpABC oxoprolinase complex detoxifies the metabolic byproduct 5-oxoproline to glutamic acid in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (Niehaus et al., 2017; Seddon et al., 1984). In other bacteria, Pxp oxoprolinase activity has the dual effect of preventing the accumulation of toxic amounts of OP, while also allowing OP use as a nitrogen source (Niehaus et al., 2017; Ratcliffe et al., 1983). To determine if the predicted C. difficile PxpAGBC orthologs are involved in 5-oxoproline metabolism, we grew wild-type, the ΔpxpAGBC mutant, and complement strain in Defined Minimal Media (DMM) with and without 30 mM 5-oxoproline or 30 mM glutamic acid (Figure 3). In DMM, the pxpAGBC mutant grew more slowly than the parent strain, suggesting an intrinsic growth defect in the absence of the Pxp complex (Figure 3D). The growth defect of the pxp mutants was partially restored in the presence of glutamic acid (Figure 3 B,F), suggesting that additional nutrients help to alleviate the growth defect. However, when grown in the presence of OP, the pxpAGBC mutant demonstrated further growth retardation than observed in DMM alone (Figure 3B,E). In contrast, the parent strain 630∆erm experienced improved growth and a 10% increase in culture density when OP was added to the medium, indicating that the Pxp complex is both necessary for the utilization of OP and the prevention of deleterious effects by OP (Figure 3 A,E). Further, the growth of the wild-type strain in OP was the same as the growth observed with the addition of glutamic acid, suggesting that OP is as readily utilized as a nutrient as the metabolic product, glutamic acid. These data demonstrate that the pxpAGBC operon encodes proteins responsible for 5-oxoprolinase function, and that utilization of 5-oxoproline promotes C. difficile growth.5-oxoproline is structurally analogous to L-proline with an added carbonyl group on the 5-carbon. To assess the mechanisms available for OP catabolism, we investigated whether OP could substitute for proline for energy generation by the C. difficile proline reductase (Bouillaut et al., 2013; Jackson et al., 2006). To do so, we generated a deletion mutant in the proline reductase gene, prdA, and evaluated growth in DMM with and without OP (Figure 3C). The final cell density of the prdA mutant improved in the presence of OP by 26%, which was greater than the growth enhancement of wild-type in OP (10%). These data indicate that OP is not a substrate for proline reductase. In addition, the greater growth enhancement of the prdA mutant by OP suggests that the impact of OP in the wild-type is obscured by proline reductase activity.C. difficile virulence is dependent on the production of two major toxins, TcdA and TcdB, which are produced in response to nutrient deprivation.(Antunes et al., 2011; Dineen et al., 2010; Martin-Verstraete et al., 2016). To determine if the ability to utilize OP impacts toxin production, we assessed the TcdA and TcdB produced in WT and ∆pxpAGBC grown in TY broth (Figure 4, Figure S6). The pxpAGBC mutant produced 1.7-fold more toxin on average than the WT, suggesting that oxoprolinase activity has a suppressive effect on toxin production.Considering that the bile acid CDCA increased the expression of the pxp genes, we examined whether CDCA could further improve C. difficile growth in OP. To do so, we added 0.1 mM CDCA with and without 30 mM OP to the DMM cultures and evaluated the effects of each variable on growth (Figure 5). No further enhancement of OP-dependent growth was observed when CDCA and OP were both added to the medium. These data suggest that the addition of CDCA does not enhance the ability of C. difficile to utilize exogenous 5-oxoproline for growth.As OP affects C. difficile growth, we considered that OP utilization may affect sporulation. We extended our sporulation assays to test the impact of exogenous OP on the sporulation frequencies of the pxp mutants and parent strain, but observed no effect on sporulation outcomes relative to sporulation medium lacking OP (Figure S5). Further, we examined the expression of pxpB in the presence of OP and glutamic acid, and observed only modest changes in pxpB expression, suggesting that these nutrients do not substantially alter expression of the pxp operon (Figure S7). Thus, OP promotes the growth of C. difficile through utilization by the Pxp oxoprolinase, but OP does not induce pxp expression or spore formation.The B. subtilis oxoprolinase enables the use of OP as a nitrogen source, but not a carbon sourceIn the initial characterization of the B. subtilis Pxp/Kip oxoprolinase, it was observed that the oxoprolinase activity facilitated OP utilization as a nitrogen source (Niehaus et al., 2017). However, the OP-dependent enhancement of growth observed with C. difficile suggests that the Pxp oxoprolinase allows OP to be used as either a carbon source, nitrogen source, or both a carbon and nitrogen source to support growth. These results led us to question whether the Pxp oxoprolinases of C. difficile and B. subtilis share the capacity for growth enhancement by OP and if B. subtilis Pxps facilitate the use of OP as a carbon source. To test these hypotheses, we obtained previously generated pxpB and pxpC B. subtilis deletion mutants and further investigated their growth profiles in the presence of OP with and without the addition of succinate as a carbon source (Koo et al., 2017). Succinate was chosen as the carbon source to avoid the production of glutamic acid and/or 5-oxoproline. The growth of wild-type B. subtilis, ∆pxpB, and ∆pxpC mutants were compared in Niehaus Defined Medium (NDM) with added succinate, OP, or both (Figure 6). When succinate served as the sole carbon source, poor growth was observed for all strains, but the pxpB and pxpC mutants demonstrated significantly less growth than the parent strain (Figure 6A). Importantly, OP alone did not support the growth of any B. subtilis strain and led to decreased cell density for both pxp mutants (Figure 6B). Addition of both succinate and OP to the medium resulted in considerable growth for the parent strain that was not observed for either the pxpB or pxpC mutant (Figure 6C). These observations reinforce the previous findings of OP toxicity in the absence of the pxp genes and demonstrate that OP is not an adequate sole carbon source for B. subtilis.

In B. subtilis, the pxp operon is induced by glucose and repressed by glutamine (Wang et al., 1997). Previous studies in C. difficile found that the pxpAGBC operon is repressed by proline through PrdR, and by glucose, partially through CcpA, but the impact of these factors on pxp expression was modest (2–3 fold) (Antunes et al., 2012; Bouillaut et al., 2019). Data from our pilot experiments suggested that the secondary bile salt deoxycholate (DCA) induced transcription of the pxpAGBC genes. We further examined the effects of bile salts on expression of the pxp operon by assessing pxpB transcription. To this end, wild-type C. difficile was grown in BHIS broth supplemented with physiologically-relevant concentrations of deoxycholate (DCA, 0.5 mM), chenodeoxycholate (CDCA, 0.5 mM), lithocholate (LCA, 0.05 mM), cholate (CA, 2 mM), taurocholate (TA, 10 mM), glycocholate (GCA, 10 mM), taurochenodeoxycholate (TCDCA, 10 mM), or glycochenodeoxycholate (GCDCA, 1.5 mM) (Northfield and McColl, 1973), and samples were collected for qRT-PCR analysis. The hydrophobic bile salts DCA, CDCA, LCA, and GCDCA significantly increased pxpB expression, while CA and TA did not (Figure 2A). Further, we found that bile-dependent induction of pxpB transcription followed a dose-dependent pattern (Figure 2B). To determine if the detergent properties of bile salts played a role in pxpB expression, C. difficile was also exposed to sub-inhibitory concentrations of the detergents Triton X-100 and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (Figure 2A). Both detergents induced pxpB expression, but the differences were not statistically significant. These data demonstrate that pxp expression is robustly induced by several bile salts that impact growth, and that pxp induction is not universal to all bile acids or detergents.

Since expression of the pxp genes are significantly induced, we investigated the upstream GntR regulator (CD1383) to assess potential involvement in pxp regulation (Figure S3A). We observed a modest (1.8 fold), but statistically significant increase in CD1383 transcription with the addition of CDCA, suggesting it may be involved in bile-dependent pxp regulation. To further examine the contribution of the CD1383 regulator to pxp regulation, we used a nisin-inducible CRISPRi to repress CD1383 gene expression (Edwards and McBride, 2023; Müh et al., 2019). A scrambled untargeted control sgRNA was included as a negative control. Expression of the targeted sgRNA-CD1383 resulted in a 20-fold decrease in CD1383 expression (Figure S3B). However, reduction of CD1383 expression had minimal impact on pxpB expression, which was reduced less than 2-fold. No differences in in pxpB expression were observed for the CRISPRi knockdown or control strain in the presence of 0.5 mM CDCA (not shown). These results suggest that CD1383 does not substantially impact expression of the pxp operon under the conditions tested.

Previous studies demonstrated that some bile salts repress C. difficile growth and spore formation through undetermined mechanisms (Sorg and Sonenshein, 2009; Theriot et al., 2016; Usui et al., 2020). Examination of growth in the various bile salts in our hands showed that CDCA, DCA, LCA, GCDCA, and GCA repressed growth, similar to previous findings (Figure S4). Based on the induction of pxp expression and the growth impacts of these bile salts, we chose a representative bile acid, CDCA, to test for potential effects on sporulation through the Pxp locus (Figure S5). The addition of CDCA decreased spore production in the wild-type and pxp mutant strains proportionally, with similar decreases in the sporulation ratios across strains. Thus, even with induction of pxp expression by CDCA, sporulation was not differentially impacted in the wild-type or pxp mutants, further supporting that the Pxp locus does not have the role in C. difficile sporulation regulation observed in B. subtilis.

The B. subtilis oxoprolinase enables the use of OP as a nitrogen source, but not a carbon sourceIn the initial characterization of the B. subtilis Pxp/Kip oxoprolinase, it was observed that the oxoprolinase activity facilitated OP utilization as a nitrogen source (Niehaus et al., 2017). These results led us to question whether the Pxp oxoprolinases of C. difficile and B. subtilis share the capacity for growth enhancement by OP and if B. subtilis Pxps facilitate the use of OP as a carbon source. To test these hypotheses, we obtained previously generated pxpB and pxpC B. subtilis deletion mutants and further investigated their growth profiles in the presence of OP with and without the addition of succinate as a carbon source (Koo et al., 2017). Succinate was chosen as the carbon source to avoid the production of glutamic acid and/or 5-oxoproline. The growth of wild-type B. subtilis, ∆pxpB, and ∆pxpC mutants were compared in Niehaus Defined Medium (NDM) with added succinate, OP, or both (Figure 6). When succinate served as the sole carbon source, poor growth was observed for all strains, but the pxpB and pxpC mutants demonstrated significantly less growth than the parent strain (Figure 6A). Importantly, OP alone did not support the growth of any B. subtilis strain and led to decreased cell density for both pxp mutants (Figure 6B). Addition of both succinate and OP to the medium resulted in considerable growth for the parent strain that was not observed for either the pxpB or pxpC mutant (Figure 6C). These observations reinforce the previous findings of OP toxicity in the absence of the pxp genes and demonstrate that OP is not an adequate sole carbon source for B. subtilis.

DISCUSSIONIn this study, we investigated the role of the PxpAGBC factors in the regulation of sporulation and the ability of C. difficile to detoxify the metabolite 5-oxoproline. We found that PxpB and PxpC do not have major effects on C. difficile sporulation, in contrast to B. subtilis, in which PxpB impedes sporulation (Wang et al., 1997). The absence of a sporulation effect by the C. difficile Pxp proteins is not completely surprising given the considerable differences in the genetic pathways and factors involved in sporulation initiation between B. subtilis and C. difficile (Edwards et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2022; Mehdizadeh Gohari et al., 2024). In B. subtilis, PxpB binds to the sporulation activating kinase, KinA to repress spore formation (Jacques et al., 2011, 2008). However, C. difficile does not encode a clear KinA ortholog and the phosphotransfer proteins that function in the regulation of sporulation initiation appear to suppress spore formation (Edwards et al., 2022). Moreover, it is not known how the Bacillus KinA-PxpB-PxpC interactions are controlled or if such signals are present or relevant for C. difficile. While C. difficile Pxps have no apparent role in sporulation, there are many sporulating species that encode Pxp orthologs, but their functions have not been investigated. Hence, it is possible that other Clostridial PxpB proteins function as sporulation kinase inhibitors—most likely those with histidine kinases that activate Spo0A.The most impactful result of this investigation was the finding that the PxpAGBC complex not only functions as a 5-oxoprolinase, but that 5-oxoproline supports enhanced C. difficile growth. For B. subtilis, the conversion of OP to glutamic acid provides a detoxification function and supplies an excellent nitrogen source for the bacterium, but glutamic acid cannot serve as a sole carbon source for B. subtilis (Belitsky et al., 2000; Commichau et al., 2008). The enhanced growth observed for wild-type C. difficile with OP suggests that PxpAGBC facilitates the use of OP as a carbon source, nitrogen source, or both a carbon and nitrogen source (Fig. 3). However, if and how PxpAGBC metabolizes OP to generate energy is not clear. We observed similar cell growth of C. difficile in OP and in equal concentrations of glutamic acid, which is curious. The conversion of OP to glutamic acid costs energy, so cells would be expected to grow better with glutamic acid than with OP, due to the net difference in energy gained. The growth differences for C. difficile in OP and glutamic acid suggest that OP can be utilized by mechanisms that glutamic acid cannot.It was previously observed by Fletcher et al. that the concentration of OP increases during C. difficile colonization in a mouse model of infection, though the cause of the OP increase is not known (Fletcher et al., 2018). The presence of both exogenous and endogenously generated OP necessitates detoxification by both the intestinal microbiota and the host. The fact that C. difficile growth is enhanced by a metabolite that is toxic to some other species could greatly impact competition with other intestinal microbes and may provide a significant nutritional advantage to support C. difficile proliferation during infection. Further, the growth advantages afforded to C. difficile by PxpAGBC-mediated 5-oxoproline metabolism may be shared by other species that encode Pxp orthologs.

Sporulation assaysEthanol-resistant sporulation assays were performed as previously described (Childress et al., 2016; DiCandia et al., 2022; Edwards et al., 2022; Edwards and McBride, 2017, 2016). Briefly, C. difficile strains were grown in BHIS broth supplemented with 0.1% TA and 0.2% fructose to germinate spores and prevent spore formation, then diluted in BHIS and grown to log-phase (OD600 =0.5). Cultures were plated onto 70:30 sporulation agar, with or without 0.5 mM chenodeoxycholate (Calbiochem), or 30 mM of neutralized 5-oxoproline (L-pyroglutamic acid, Sigma-Aldrich), as indicated. After 24 h, cells were plated for vegetative cell counts or treated with ethanol, diluted, and plated for spore enumeration on BHIS with 0.1% TA. Sporulation frequency was calculated as the number of ethanol-resistant spores divided by the total number of cells. A spo0A mutant (MC310) was used as a negative control to verify vegetative cell death during ethanol treatment. At least four independent experiments were performed, and sporulation frequency data were analyzed by one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s or Šídák’s multiple comparison test, as indicated (GraphPad Prism v10.0.2).

Toxin QuantificationTcdA and TcdB toxins were quantified as previously described (DiCandia et al., 2024; Edwards et al., 2020; Wetzel et al., 2024). Briefly, strains were grown in BHIS broth to an OD600 of 0.5 and diluted 1:10 in TY medium, pH of 7.4 and grown for 24 h. After 24 h, cells were harvested, and the supernatants were assayed for the presence of C. difficile TcdA and TcdB toxins. Total toxin was quantified in technical duplicate for eight biological replicates (due to assay variability) using a C. difficile toxin ELISA kit (TGCbiomics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Technical duplicates were averaged and normalized per mL of supernatant. Statistical significance was determined using a paired two-tailed Student’s t-test comparing the wild-type and the ΔpxpAGBC mutant using GraphPad Prism v10.3.0.
