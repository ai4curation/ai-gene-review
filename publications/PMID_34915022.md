Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a common pediatric musculoskeletal
disorder worldwide, characterized by atypical spine curvatures in otherwise
healthy children. Human genetic studies have identified candidate genes
associated with AIS, however, only a few of these have been shown to
recapitulate adult-viable scoliosis in animal models. Using an F0 CRISPR
screening approach in zebrafish, we demonstrate that disruption of the dynein
axonemal heavy chain 10 (dnah10) gene results in recessive adult-viable
scoliosis in zebrafish. Using a stably segregating dnah10 mutant zebrafish, we
showed that the ependymal monocilia lining the hindbrain and spinal canal
displayed reduced beat frequency, which was correlated with the disassembly of
the Reissner fiber and the onset of body curvatures. Taken together, these
results suggest that monocilia function in larval zebrafish contributes to the
polymerization of the Reissner fiber and straightening of the body axis.

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a common pediatric disorder characterized by atypical three-dimensional curvatures, with rotation of the spinal column (Cheng et al., 2015). The prevalence of AIS is approximately 1.0–5.0% among children worldwide (Yılmaz et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2015). Approximately 10% of AIS patients suffer severe physical and mental issues due to progressive spine curvature (Cheng et al., 2015). Analysis of familial scoliosis and the recognition of a high concomitance of scoliosis in twin studies demonstrate that AIS is a multifactorial disease with a strong genetic predisposition (Hadley Miller, 2000; Wise et al., 2008). A growing number of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) from diverse ethnic backgrounds have mapped a range of candidate loci and implicated several candidate genes associated with this disorder, including LBX1, BNC2, GPR126, AJAP1, and PAX1 (Kou et al., 2013; Ogura et al., 2015; Sharma et al., 2015; Takahashi et al., 2011; Zhu et al., 2017). Recently, exome sequencing studies have identified additional rare variants associated with AIS in different genes, including within POC5, HSPG2, AKAP2, SLC39A8, and FLNB (Baschal et al., 2014; Haller et al., 2018; Jiang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2016; Patten et al., 2015). Additional analysis of exome data has identified enrichment of potentially deleterious variants in FBN1/2, extracellular matrix (ECM), and cilia-associated genes in AIS patients (Baschal et al., 2018; Buchan et al., 2014a; Haller et al., 2016), and variants in axonemal dynein genes associated with AIS (Wang et al., 2020). Altogether, these human genetics studies suggest that AIS is a complex polygenic disease with significant genetic heterogeneity.

The zebrafish model system has been a powerful tool to functionally test the role of human AIS-associated genes. For example, poc5, slc39a8, and mapk7 —loci implicated by human genetic studies of AIS— show scoliosis phenotypes when disrupted in zebrafish (Gao et al., 2017; Haller et al., 2018; Patten et al., 2015). Moreover, targeted knockdown of two axonemal dynein assembly factor genes, dnaaf1 and zmynd10, recapitulate scoliosis in adult zebrafish (Wang et al., 2020). In addition, forward genetic studies in zebrafish have identified several mutant zebrafish lines displaying adult-viable scoliosis without vertebral patterning defects, which resemble the pathology of human AIS (hereafter called scoliosis) (Buchan et al., 2014b; Gray et al., 2021; Grimes et al., 2016; Hayes et al., 2014; Rose et al., 2020; Tian et al., 2018; Troutwine et al., 2020; Vesque et al., 2019).

The collective analysis of these established models of scoliosis in zebrafish suggests a model where the disruption of ependymal cell cilia motility causes alterations in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow, leading to disruption of the Reissner fiber (RF), which can lead to reduced urotensin neuropeptide signaling to the dorsal musculature, ultimately contributing to scoliosis in zebrafish as reviewed in (Bagnat and Gray, 2020; Bearce and Grimes, 2021). However, recent studies demonstrated that the loss of multiciliated ependymal cells in geminin coiled-coil domain containing (gmnc) mutant zebrafish do not display scoliosis phenotypes despite showing clear indications of ventriculomegaly or hydrocephalus. Instead, the reduction of the motile monocilia regulatory transcription factors foxj1a/foxj1b and gmnc was necessary to generate scoliosis (D’Gama et al., 2021). This new data suggests that the function of motile monocilia is more important for focusing our understanding of the pathogenesis of scoliosis in zebrafish.

The RF is a glycoprotein-rich extracellular thread, which stretches from the third cerebral ventricle down the central canal in most chordate lineages (Munoz et al., 2019). Dysfunction of motile monocilia in the central canal of the spine in embryos is associated with disrupted RF assembly and severe ventral ward body curvatures (Cantaut-Belarif et al., 2018). Moreover, the continuous assembly of the RF during larval stages of development in zebrafish is essential for maintaining spine stability in larval zebrafish (Rose et al., 2020; Troutwine et al., 2020) and the assembly of the RF during larval development is dependent on two components of cilia physiology protein tyrosine kinase 7a (ptk7a) (Rose et al., 2020) and kinesin family member 6 (kif6) (Troutwine et al., 2020). The discovery of additional factors important for monocilia motility will generate a more comprehensive understanding of the regulators of scoliosis.

Cilia motility is in part controlled by the concerted force generation of both inner and outer axonemal dynein arms on the peripheral microtubule doublets (Viswanadha et al., 2017). In this study, we isolated a novel frameshift-truncating mutation in the dynein axonemal heavy chain 10 (dnah10) gene, which encodes a component of the inner dynein arm (Maiti et al., 2000; Myster et al., 1997). We used high-speed confocal imaging of Foxj1a-Arl13B-GFP labeled motile monocilia in the spinal cord central canal, which showed a reduced beat frequency of monocilia lining the spinal canal in dnah10ut28 mutant zebrafish. Using an endogenous RF-labeling scospondin-GFPut24 knock-in zebrafish line, we also demonstrated a range of RF assembly defects that arose concurrently with the reduction of monocilia motility in the central canal, which correlated with the onset and severity of spine curvatures in dnah10ut28 mutant zebrafish. This study demonstrates a new role for dnah10 for monocilia beating in the central canal and suggests that monocilia beating is required to maintain the polymerized structure of the RF, independent of secretion of the Reissner material, which is essential for the development of a straight body axis in zebrafish.

ResultsA C-terminal truncating mutation of dnah10 causes scoliosis in zebrafishHuman genetic studies of AIS have implicated genes involved in cilia function such as POC5 (Mathieu et al., 2021b; Patten et al., 2015), TTLL11 (Mathieu et al., 2021a), and several axonemal dyneins and dynein associated factors (Wang et al., 2020). Here, we sought to functionally test several dynein axonemal heavy chain genes (dnah1, dnah3, dnah7l, dnah7, dnah9, and dnah10) in zebrafish (Figure 1A), using a pooled, multiplexing CRISPR/Cas9 approach (Wu et al., 2018). For each target we used four independent dnah gene-specific guide RNAs injected as a ribonuclear Cas9 protein (Supplemental Table S1). Post-injection we performed a systematic screen to identify any atypical phenotypes including body curvature or scoliosis at 3-, 5-, and 30-days post fertilization (dpf). Only dnah1 and dnah10 injected embryos demonstrated atypical body curvatures (Figure 1A, B and Supplemental Table S2). However, by 30 dpf only dnah10 targeted F0 zebrafish displayed adult-viable scoliosis (12%; n=192)(Figure 1C and Supplemental Table S2).We next sought to generate a stable segregating mutation of dnah10 by targeting CRISPR/Cas9-dependent mutagenesis to the region encoding the microtubule-binding domain of the Dnah10 protein. To identify a stable mutant line, we outcrossed F0 dnah10 targeted zebrafish that displayed scoliosis to expediently isolate potentially deleterious dnah10 mutations in F1 families. We next incrossed siblings within distinct F1 families to screen for heterozygous carriers as shown in the schematic (Figure 1D). Progeny from one of these crosses showed recessive body curvature in larval fish and scoliosis in adult zebrafish (Figure 1E). Finally, we bottlenecked a single dnah10 mutation by outcrossing from an individual F2 male dnah10 mutant zebrafish. We characterized a frameshift variant in dnah10 (c.10304_10307del [p. To facilitate the visualization of RF dynamics in dnah10ut28/ut28 mutants we crossed these to the scospondin-GFPut24 gene fusion knock-in zebrafish line (Troutwine et al., 2020). scospondin-GFPut24 embryos display a fluorescently labeled RF, which continually moves in a rostral-caudal direction (Figure 4A, A’; Video S4). In contrast, the RF material moved in a disorganized manner in scospondin-GFPut24;dnah10
ut28/ut28 mutant embryos (Figure 4B, C; Video S5). We observed heterogeneous defects of RF assembly scospondin-GFPut24;dnah10 ut28/ut28 mutants (Figure 4H and Video S5) including: (i) the formation of bolus or punctate Scospondin-GFP protein filling the central canal (17%; n=29) (Figure 4D); (ii) the complete absence of RF in sections of the central canal (14%; n=29) (Figure 4E); (iii) absence of a fiber with only diffuse Scospondin-GFP protein filling the central canal (48%; n=29) (Figure 4F); or (iv) a continuous RF surrounded with diffuse Scospondin-GFP protein in the central canal (21%; n=29) (Figure 4G). We also observed heterogeneity in the ability of the RF and Scospondin-GFP labeled material to move along the central canal including moving in non-linear, meandering patterns (Figure 4J), slowly traversing in a rostral direction, or simply not moving within the central canal (Figure 4L, M and Video S5). Altogether, these data demonstrate that heterogeneous alterations in RF assembly and its typical rostral-caudal motility are correlated with heterogeneous defects in monocilia motility lining the brain and central canal and that this process is partially dependent on dnah10 function.
