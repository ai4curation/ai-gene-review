Antibody and cell-based therapeutics targeting cell surface receptors have
emerged as a major class of immune therapeutics for treating cancer. However,
the number of cell surface targets for cancer immunotherapy remains limited.
Glypican-3 (GPC3) is a cell surface proteoglycan and an oncofetal antigen. In
this study, we report a large-scale tumor-associated GPC3 co-immunoprecipitation
(CoIP)-proteomic study using liver cancer xenograft tumors in mice. We
identified 153 GPC3-associated proteins through mass spectrometry. To identify
potential drug targets, we categorized GPC3-associated proteins based on their
subcellular locations using UniProt annotations, with a focus on extracellular
proteins. Additionally, we annotated differentially expressed proteins in
hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) versus non-tumor liver samples based on the
literature, analyzed expression levels in tumor versus normal tissues using TCGA
and GTEx databases via GEPIA, and identified prognostic liver cancer markers
according to GEPIA. Among GPC3-associated proteins, Immunoglobulin Superfamily
Member 1 (IGSF1), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), FAT Atypical Cadherin 1 (FAT1),
Formin 1 (FMN1), and Guanylate Cyclase 2C (GUCY2C), were identified as potential
therapeutic targets. Furthermore, we validated the direct protein interaction
between GPC3 and AFP through immunoprecipitation with purified proteins and
through co-localization imaging using immunofluorescence microscopy. This study
provides large proteomic datasets related to GPC3-associated proteins, enhancing
our understanding of glypican biology in cancer cells and offering a new
approach to identifying immunotherapy targets.

Introduction

Glypican-3 (GPC3) is a cell surface proteoglycan and an oncofetal antigen that has been developed as a target for cancer immunotherapy, including antibody and cell-based therapies (1–3). While it is rarely expressed in healthy adult human tissues, it is found in over 70% of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) specimens (4, 5) as well as fetus and placenta tissues (6, 7). Intriguingly, GPC3 promotes cell growth in HCC (8) but may have contrasting effects on fetus development (9). Therefore, understanding the interaction partners of GPC3 is crucial to understand its oncogenic role in liver cancer, as these partners may contribute to its function and include potential drug targets. This comprehensive survey of GPC3-associated proteins could reveal new liver cancer targets similar to GPC3 that are yet to be discovered.

Our goal was to utilize a proteomics method to identify cell surface proteins suitable for targeting by antibody or CAR-based therapies. Coimmunoprecipitation (coIP) is an effective way for isolating target-associated proteins in their native form, which can then be identified by mass spectrometry (10–12). Affigel-10, which is compatible with the coIP-MS, serves to pull down the clean sample enriched with target-associated proteins, avoiding contamination by antibodies due to its covalent binding to the pull-down capturing reagent (10, 11). In addition, we propose that an ideal control for the coIP-MS should be a blocking reagent that occupies the antibody paratope without interacting with target-associated proteins (12).

We previously developed a high-affinity anti-GPC3 antibody, YP7, targeting the C-terminal sequence of GPC3 (13–15). The YP7 antibody does not appear to affect the GPC3 signaling pathways such as Wnt or Yap (16). In this study, we reported a large-scale tumor-associated GPC3 co-immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry study using HepG2 xenograft tumors and the YP7 antibody, with the C-terminal peptide recognized by YP7 serving as the blocking reagent in the control.

We identified 153 GPC3-associated proteins, including 22 located on the plasma membrane. Annotation of the subcellular locations and expression levels of these proteins allow us to discuss several overexpressed cell surface proteins in cancer that could serve as potential drug targets. We further validated the direct interaction of GPC3 and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) in cell-free immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence microscopy for colocalization. The data presented here should enable the rapid assessment of these proteins for their suitability as candidates for antibody and cell-based immunotherapy.

coIP for proteomics analysis

Mouse anti-GPC3 monoclonal antibody YP7 was used to pull down the GPC3 and associated proteins. To prepare the beads, 200 µl of Affigel-10 (Biorad) resin was quickly washed with cold PBS for three times and then incubated with 2 mg of the YP7 antibody in PBS, rotating at 4°C for 4 hours. The coupling reaction was stopped by the adding 200 µl of 0.7 M ethanolamine, pH 7.8, and rotating overnight. The beads were then washed three times with Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 1% TritonX100 before use. Inactive Affigel-10 was prepared similarly but without incubation with YP7.

A HepG2 xenograft tumor was hand-homogenized in a tissue grinder with homogenization buffer containing 10 mM Tris HCl pH7.4, 320 mM sucrose, and protease inhibitors (Roche Complete protease inhibitor cocktail). The homogenate was then centrifuged at 700 g for 10 min at 4°C to collect the supernatant. Protein concentration was determined using Bradford method. The first batch of co-IP had 40 mg proteins in the homogenate, while the second batch had 63 mg. They were solubilized in TBS 1% TritonX-100 and rotated at 4°C for 2 hours. Supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 16,500 g at 4°C for 20 min, and then pre-cleaned with inactive Affigel-10 by rotating overnight at 4°C.

The YP7 conjugated beads were divided into two equal halves; one was blocked by 1 mg GPC3 511–560 peptide (DGMIKVKNQLRFLAELAYDLDVDDAPGNSQQATPKDNEISTFHNLGNVHS) that was used to produce YP7 antibody as described (13), and the other incubated with a peptide corresponding to the last 50 amino acid (NH2-VQKLLGPHVEGLKAEERHRPVRDWILRQRQDDLDTLGLGLQGGIPNGYLV-COOH) of mesothelin (17) should not block the YP7-GPC3 interaction. The beads were then incubated with the precleaned homogenate by rotating at 4°C for 1 day. After that, the beads were washed with TBS 1% TritonX100 twice, then TBS 1% TritonX100 supplemented with 500 mM NaCl twice, and lastly, twice with TBS. The beads were transferred to clean 1.5 ml tubes during the wash. After the last wash, the supernatant was removed. Then, the proteins, pulled down by the beads, were eluted by addition of 50 µl 2x Laemmli sample buffer to each of the 100 µl beads. The mixture was incubated at 50°C for 10 minutes and then 95°C for 5 minutes with frequent agitation.

The elution was separated through SDS-PAGE and visualized by staining with PageBlue protein staining solutions. Differential bands as well as the rest of the IP lane and control lane were cut as gel slices and subjected to proteomics analysis.

Proteomics analysisThe SDS-PAGE gel cuts were destained and digested overnight at 37°C in a buffer with 25mM NH4HCO3, pH 8.4, and 20 ng/μL trypsin. Tryptic peptides were extracted with 70% acetonitrile and 5% formic acid, lyophilized, reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid, and subjected to RPLC-MS/MS analysis through stepwise elution from a C-18 nanoRPLC column and the Agilent 1100 nanoLC system coupled to a linear ion trap mass spectrometer (LTQ). Tandem mass spectra were searched against the UniProt human proteomic database with SEQUEST software using fully tryptic cleavage constraints. For a peptide to be considered legitimately identified, it must achieve stringent charge state and cross correlation (Xcorr) scores of 1.9 for [M + H]1+, 2.2 for [M + 2H]2+, 3.1 for [M + 3H]3+, and a minimum delta correlation (ΔCn) of 0.08. The list of protein accession numbers was analyzed by UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprot/).

GPC3 is associated with AFP.

AFP was identified as one of the interaction candidates from the MS analysis. AFP is an oncofetal glycoprotein secreted by HCC and is well-known as a liver cancer biomarker, serving as a standard for early diagnostic practices for many years (23, 24). GPC3 and AFP are reported co-expressed on the same liver progenitor cells (25), with their expression regulated by alpha-fetoprotein regulator 2 (26). To validate the direct association between AFP and GPC3, we incubated purified AFP-Fc and GPC3-His proteins in vitro, followed by immunoprecipitation using protein A agarose. The results show that AFP pulls down GPC3, but not MUC16, a control protein (Figure 4A). AFP is secreted in large quantities as a soluble protein by HCC (24). Therefore, we incubated HCC (Hep3B) cell culture supernatant with GPC3 protein and precipitated the complex with a GPC3 antibody. The results demonstrate that soluble AFP in the HCC cell supernatant was pulled down by GPC3 (Figure 4B), indicating an association between AFP and GPC3.

To verify the role of heparan sulfate chain of GPC3 in the interaction, we used a GPC3 mutant lacking heparan sulfate chains (GPC3-ΔHS) for the incubation with Hep3B cell supernatant. The results show that the GPC3-ΔHS also precipitates AFP, suggesting that the GPC3 core protein might bind AFP (Figure 4C). Furthermore, to confirm the colocalization of GPC3 and AFP in HCC, we created a EGFP-GPC3 construct and a GPC3 construct without heparan sulfate chains (EGFP-GPC3-ΔHS), where EGFP stands for enhanced green fluorescence protein. The results showed AFP co-localizes with both EGFP-GPC3 and EGFP-GPC3-ΔHS on the cell membrane, but not with EGFP alone (Figure 4D). Therefore, the methodology used in our proteomics study is robust for this purpose.

To identify potential cell surface target for cancer therapy, we utilized GEPIA2 to annotate GPC3 and its associated proteins. To establish criteria for selecting potential cell surface proteins for cancer therapy, we examined surface proteins that have been targeted in the clinic settings and found that many share similar characters (Supplemental Table 3). Specifically, a significant number of these proteins exhibit a maximum tumor expression/normal tissue expression ratio (max T/N, as per GEPIA) greater than 2, with a maximum normal tissue expression (max N) of less than 200 TPM. For instance, surface proteins meeting these criteria include MET, B7H3/CD276, FOLR1, CD19, CD20/MS4A1, CD79b, CD25/IL2RA, CD33, PD1/PDCD1, and CTLA4, while surface proteins that narrowly miss these criteria include EpCAM and LAG3. Notably, these targets constitute 12 out of 20 clinically approved cell surface targets. Thus, we believe that this criterion is effective for screening tumor-associated antigens (TAA) on the cell surface for cancer therapies.

We identified and validated AFP as a GPC3-associated protein. AFP is a well-established liver cancer serum marker used in HCC diagnosis according to the NCCN guideline. Similar to GPC3(2), AFP is not expressed in normal adult tissues; its expression is slightly upregulated in cirrhosis but greatly elevated in HCC. These findings suggest that AFP and GPC3 are closely correlated biomarkers for HCC.

We identified IgSF1 as a potential tumor target. Similar to GPC3 and AFP, IgSF1 exhibits high expression in fetal tissues, particularly in fetal liver, while its expression in adult tissues is low except in anterior pituitary gland. It is worth noting that the pituitary gland is situated outside the blood brain barrier (28) and is accessible to therapeutic antibody treatment; therefore, the specificity and safety of IgSF1 as a therapeutic target should be carefully evaluated. Mutations in IgSF1 are associated with central hypothyroidism (CeH), hypoprolactinemia, delayed puberty, macro-orchidism, and increased body weight (29, 30). According to TCGA, IgSF1 is highly expressed in thyroid tumor and is upregulated in HCC (31).

FAT1 is identified as a GPC3-associated protein in this coIP study. According to GEPIA2, its expression is elevated in kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma and colon adenocarcinoma. NCI GDC data portal indicated that FAT1 is the 13th most frequently mutated genes in all cancers. FAT1 alterations are observed in 20.5% glioblastoma, 7.1% colorectal cancer, and 6.7% head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) according to one report (32), and 29.8% of HNSCC according to another (33). FAT1 functions as a tumor suppressor by inhibiting Wnt signaling through its interaction with β-catenin, thereby antagonizing its nuclear localization (34). Moreover, FAT1 facilitates the assembly of a signaling complex involving canonical Hippo signaling components, resulting in phosphorylation and inactivation of YAP1 (33). We previously reported that FAT1 associates with GPC3 through FAT1’s extracellular EGF-like domains (27). However, unlike its suppressive role in other tumors, FAT1 is upregulated in HCC cells and contributes to cell migration. Under hypoxia conditions, both GPC3 and FAT1 are required for hypoxia-induced EMT-related gene expression, suggesting that both proteins promote HCC progression (27).

We identified FMN1 as a GPC3-associated protein. FMN1 is potentially a cell surface protein. Its normal expression in adult appears to be restricted to retina and testis, both of which are immune privileged. FMN1 exhibits high expression in skin subcutaneous melanoma. It is induced by mechanical force and enhances cancer cell invasiveness by acting on the microtubule lattice (35).

We also identified GUCY2C as a GPC3-associated protein. GUCY2C is located on the luminal surface of intestinal epithelium and serves as the cell surface receptor for heat-stable enterotoxins, which are responsible for acute secretory diarrhea (36). Additionally, it is found in a subset of hypothalamus neurons and plays a role in appetite regulation (37). Metastatic lesions originating from colorectal cancer lose apical-basolateral polarization, making accessible to drugs in the bloodstream. In mouse models, GUCY2C-targeting CAR-T cells demonstrates minimal toxicity to normal tissues (38, 39). In phase II trials, the GUCY2C-targeting antibody drug conjugate (ADC) TAK-264 (formerly MLN0264) achieved an objective response rate (ORR) of 6% in stomach or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma (40), and an ORR of 3% in advanced or metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma (41). Grade ≥3 adverse events were observed in 35~37% of patients in both trials, including anemia, diarrhea, and neutropenia. Furthermore, the GUCY2C-targeting vaccine Ad5-GUCY2C-PADRE safely induced GUCY2C-specific CD8+ (but not CD4+) T cell response in 40% of patients and elicited GUCY2C-specific antibodies in 10% of colon cancer patients after surgical resection (42, 43). These findings suggest that the GUCY2C is a promising and safe cancer target for cancer therapy; however, the optimal therapeutic format is yet to be determined.

In this study, we identified several prognostic proteins as GPC3-associated proteins in HCC, and the majority of these proteins exhibit higher expressions that correlate with an unfavorable prognosis. We further validated the interaction of AFP and GPC3 by immunoprecipitation in a cell-free system and immunofluorescence microscopy. This finding aligns with the understanding that GPC3 is a functional tumor target that promotes HCC tumor growth (44–46). It suggests that the function of GPC3, a tumor-associated proteoglycan, may be elucidated through its associated proteins. Different prognostic patterns are observed across various cancer types highlight the influence of cellular context on the function of these genes.

ConclusionIn this large-scale proteomic analysis of native GPC3-associated proteins, we identified 153 GPC3-associated proteins in human HCC xenograft tumors. We annotated their subcellular locations, differential expressions in cancers and normal tissues, prognostic power, and biological pathways; we identified GPC3-associated cell surface proteins such as AFP, FAT1, IGSF1, FMN1, and GUCY2C as a potential target for liver cancer. Additionally, we found that a significant number of GPC3-associated proteins are unfavorable to HCC prognosis compared to those linked to favorable outcomes. The large-scale proteomic study, based on a known tumor-associated antigen (GPC3) in liver cancer, may provide a new framework for identifying immunotherapy targets in other tumor types. Notably, we validated the direct interaction between GPC3 and AFP in HCC. The identified cell surface proteins should be considered preliminary candidates for therapeutic antibody and cell therapy targets, pending further evaluation of their oncogenic function and safety.
