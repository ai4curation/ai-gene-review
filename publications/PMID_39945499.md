Verticillium longisporum is a soil-borne fungal pathogen that causes vascular
disease predominantly in Brassicaceae. We have previously reported that the
receptor of the plant defense hormone jasmonoyl-isoleucine (JA-Ile), CORONATINE
INSENSITIVE 1 (COI1), is required in roots for the fungus to proliferate
efficiently in the shoot, suggesting the presence of a mobile root-borne signal
that influences the outcome of the disease in shoots. This function of COI1 in
promoting susceptibility is independent of JA-Ile. To explore the underlying
mechanisms, in this study we compared the root transcriptome of the Arabidopsis
coi1 mutant with those of the susceptible JA-Ile-deficient allene oxide synthase
(aos) mutant and the susceptible wild-type (WT). The biggest difference between
the transcriptomes was due to 316 immunity-related genes that were
constitutively higher expressed in coi1 as compared to the susceptible
genotypes. Interfering with the expression of a sub-group of these genes
partially suppressed the coi1-mediated tolerance phenotype. We therefore
hypothesize that secreted defense compounds encoded by genes constitutively
expressed in coi1 are transported to the shoot with the transpiration stream
where they accumulate and interfere with fungal growth. In addition, we found
that 149 of the 316 COI1-repressed genes were induced in the WT and aos upon
infection, reaching similar expression levels as in mock-treated coi1. These
were not further induced in coi1 upon infection. Thus, the repressive effect of
COI1 is either lifted or overridden upon infection with V. longisporum.

Introduction

Plant roots are in close contact with a wide range of commensal, mutualistic, and pathogenic microorganisms that densely populate soil environments. The ascomycete fungus Verticillium longisporum is a soil-borne pathogen with a host range largely restricted to Brassicaceae. In particular, V. longisporum poses an increasing threat to the production of Brassica napus, an economically important host crop in Europe (Eynck et al., 2007; Depotter et al., 2016). Notably, V. longisporum is also able to propagate in the model plant Arabidopsis (Iven et al., 2012; Ralhan et al., 2012; Reusche et al., 2012). It penetrates roots and uses xylem vessels to spread systemically in its host. Infection with V. longisporum causes stunted growth, clearing of veins, leaf chlorosis, and premature senescence (Ralhan et al., 2012; Depotter et al., 2016).

Successful pathogens have evolved sophisticated strategies to overcome the immune systems of their hosts, which are activated as soon as a potential pathogen is recognized. Recognition is mediated by membrane-residing receptors that interact with conserved microbial molecules, such as flagellin, chitin, or Necrosis and Ethylene-inducing Peptide 1 (Nep1)-like proteins (Chinchilla et al., 2006; Kaku et al., 2006; Oome et al., 2014). These are called microbe- or pathogen-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs or PAMPs). The resulting immune response, called PAMP-triggered immunity (PTI), interferes with microbial propagation, unless the pathogen has evolved specialized effectors that compromise immune signalling, a phenomenon called effector-triggered susceptibility (ETS) (Chisholm et al., 2006; Jones and Dangl, 2006; Dodds et al., 2024). The resulting high pathogen load triggers the synthesis of plant defense hormones, namely salicylic acid (SA) or jasmonoyl-isoleucine (JA-Ile) in combination with ethylene (Bürger and Chory, 2019).

Shortly after the induction of SA and JA-Ile/ethylene biosynthesis, only one of these pathways dominates the defense response, depending on the type of pathogen invading (Spoel et al., 2007). Generally speaking, SA-mediated responses are deployed against biotrophic pathogens, which feed off living cells, while the JA-Ile/ethylene defense pathway is launched against necrotrophic pathogens, which kill the cells to feed on the remains (Thomma et al., 1998; Glazebrook, 2005). Verticillium longisporum can be regarded as a hemi-biotroph as it starts off as a biotroph, spreading in the vascular system of the host plant without causing too much damage. Later, however, before forming microsclerotia as resting structures, it starts to become necrotrophic and destroys the tissue (Depotter et al., 2016).

SA and JA-Ile synthesis and the corresponding signalling pathways are activated in petioles of soil-grown Arabidopsis plants infected with V. longisporum, but the resulting defense mechanisms do not influence fungal growth, or do so only marginally (Ralhan et al., 2012). Paradoxically, V. longisporum, as well as another soil-borne vascular ascomycete, Fusarium oxysporum, require the JA-Ile receptor CORONATINE INSENSITIVE 1 (COI1) for successful completion of their life cycles in Arabidopsis plants (Thatcher et al., 2009; Ralhan et al., 2012). Notably, this function of COI1 in promoting susceptibility is independent of plant-derived JA-Ile. In contrast to wild type plants and the JA-biosynthesis mutant allene oxide synthase (aos), infected coi1 mutants show less severe disease symptoms in shoots, where relatively low fungal amounts are detected only at late stages of infection. Reciprocal grafts between coi1 and the WT have revealed that the JA-Ile-independent COI1 function is required in the roots to cause susceptibility to F. oxysporum and V. longisporum in the shoots (Thatcher et al., 2009; Ralhan et al., 2012). It has been suggested that COI1-mediated susceptibility to F. oxysporum is caused by fungal JA-Ile or a fungal JA-mimic that ‘hijacks’ COI1 to induce a mobile root-derived factor that promotes senescence, and thus a more favourable environment for fungal growth in the shoot (Thatcher et al., 2009).

To further explore the susceptibility-inducing role of COI1 in roots of Arabidopsis plants infected with V. longisporum, we performed an RNA-sequencing analysis of mock-treated and V. longisporum-infected roots of the susceptible WT, aos mutant, and the SA biosynthesis-impaired salicylic acid induction-deficient 2 (sid2) mutant, and of the tolerant coi1 mutant. Interestingly, coi1 differed from the other genotypes by already having increased expression of immunity-related genes in mock-treated roots. Further analyses supported the hypothesis that secreted defense compounds encoded by these constitutively expressed genes reach the shoot with the transpiration stream where they cause compromised propagation of the fungus despite early colonization rates similar to those observed in the WT.

Infection with V. longisporum leads to enhanced expression of genes related to xylem formation and immunity

In order to explore the JA-Ile-independent function of COI1 in promoting susceptibility to V. longisporum in infected roots, we conducted transcriptome analysis of WT and coi1 roots in control and infected plants. Assuming that the root-borne susceptibility-promoting signal is generated in the xylem, we selected 10 dpi as the sampling time-point for root material because we had previously observed GFP-tagged V. longisporum in the xylem of roots at 7 dpi and xylem alterations in leaves at 10 dpi (Ralhan et al., 2012). In order to distinguish canonical JA-Ile/COI1-dependent genes from those that are dependent on COI1 but independent of AOS, we included aos mutant plants in the experiment. In addition, to obtain a more comprehensive picture of the role of the classical defense hormones in the transcriptional response of Arabidopsis roots to V. longisporum infection, we also analysed the sid2 mutant, which is deficient in pathogen-induced SA synthesis (Wildermuth et al., 2001). Since coi1 and aos plants are male sterile (Xie et al., 1998; von Malek et al., 2002), we genotyped seedlings obtained from heterozygous AOS/aos and COI1/coi1 plants before the infection experiments and used both of the respective outcrossed wild-type plants (WTaos, WTcoi1) from each population as controls (Supplementary Fig. S1). RNA was derived from four replicate experiments, each comprising shoot or root samples combined from 12 plants per genotype and treatment. Comparable amounts of fungal DNA were detected in shoots of all genotypes at 10 dpi (Supplementary Fig. S4), supporting our previous findings that initial colonization of Arabidopsis is unaffected in coi1 compared to the WT and aos (Ralhan et al., 2012).

The root transcriptomic data (Supplementary Table S4) were first examined using principal component (PC) analysis (Fig. 1A), which showed that WTaos, WTcoi1, aos, and sid2 were consistently closely grouped together. Differences on the PC1 axis occurred upon infection and affected these four genotypes in the same way. Hence, JA-Ile and SA did not strongly affect the transcriptional output of V. longisporum-infected roots. On the other hand, the transcriptome of coi1 roots differed from those of the other genotypes, and the difference became less after infection.

DiscussionIn this study, we aimed to elucidate a JA-Ile-independent function of COI1 that operates in roots and leads to enhanced tolerance of shoots against V. longisporum. Our experiments yielded three sets of novel data on the interaction between V. longisporum and Arabidopsis roots, which are discussed below. First, V. longisporum infection leads to the induction of cell wall remodelling genes and SA-related defense genes in the absence of increased levels of JA and SA. Second, the repressive action of COI1 on defense-related genes might be systemically released or overridden upon infection. Third, enhanced levels of root-borne, xylem-mobile defense compounds in the coi1 mutant might be responsible for impaired fungal propagation in the shoot.In contrast to other root pathogens, V. longisporum infection does not lead to a hormonal imbalance in rootsTranscriptome analyses of Arabidopsis roots infected with V. longisporum have been performed previously in two independent studies (Iven et al., 2012; Ulrich et al., 2021). Ulrich et al. (2021) did not observe any significant changes at the transcriptional level at 4 dpi when axenically grown plantlets were cultivated on agarose without any additional nutrients to avoid saprophytic growth of the fungus. In contrast, Verticillium-induced gene expression was detected when seedlings were continuously cultivated on synthetic medium with nutrients; under these conditions, the fungus was able to colonize the cortex, but entry into the xylem seemed to be hampered (Iven et al., 2012; Reusche et al., 2014). At 3 dpi, expression of genes involved in tryptophan biosynthesis and tryptophan-derived secondary metabolism was induced, leading to the synthesis of antimicrobial compounds (Iven et al., 2012). This was subsequently confirmed by an analysis of the translatome after infection (Froeschel et al., 2021). Marker genes of the pathway for tryptophan-derived secondary metabolism such as CYP79b2 and CYP79b3, which were up-regulated in the axenic system (Iven et al., 2012), were not induced in soil-grown plants at 10 dpi in our study (Supplementary Table S4). At this stage, fungal DNA was already detectable in the shoot (Supplementary Fig. S4), indicating colonization of the xylem. GO term enrichment analysis of genes induced in soil-grown WT, aos, and sid2 plants showed preferential up-regulation of cell wall remodelling genes (Supplementary Fig. S6). VND7 was induced, which encodes a master transcriptional regulator of de novo xylem formation (Supplementary Table S5; Yamaguchi et al., 2011; Reusche et al., 2012). In contrast, VND7 was not induced in previous studies when axenically grown plants were infected (Iven et al., 2012; Fröschel et al., 2021). This discrepancy under different conditions might be explained by the elicitation of specific defense responses upon colonization of the cortex, as observed early under axenic conditions at 3 dpi (Iven et al., 2012; Fröschel et al., 2021), while cell wall remodelling processes dominate the transcriptome upon establishment of the fungus in the xylem at 10 dpi.The patterns of gene induction in sid2 and aos showed considerable overlap with those of the two WTs (Supplementary Fig. S5), indicating that SA and JA did not play a major role in the induction. This was further supported by the fact that the abundance of transcripts of key biosynthesis enzymes of the pathways were either not or only slightly changed upon infection, such as ICS1 (SA), AOS (JA), and 12-OXOPHYTODIEN REDUCTASE 3 (JA) (Supplementary Table S4). JAR1 expression was even significantly reduced, correlating with reduced JA-Ile levels (Supplementary Fig. S7B).Apart from the studies of V. longisporum-infected roots mentioned above, only a few transcriptomes of pathogen-infected Arabidopsis roots have been published to our knowledge. Our transcriptome can be best compared with that of roots infected by F. oxysporum (Lyons et al., 2015), which was also from samples of soil-grown plants (collected at 6 dpi). Although F. oxysporum and V. longisporum are both vascular fungi, they seem to elicit quite distinct transcriptional responses, with F. oxysporum causing the activation of genes involved in JA synthesis and signalling. This might be either a consequence of root damage inflicted by F. oxisporum-derived phytotoxic compounds or, alternatively, of JA-Ile or JA-Ile mimics synthesized by the fungus initiating a feed-forward loop leading to endogenous JA synthesis. Moreover, Lyons et al. (2015) reported the activation of auxin-induced genes, which might be a direct or indirect response to specific effector proteins. The strong cell wall remodelling response that we observed in V. longisporum-infected roots was not observed after F. oxysporum infection and was most likely initiated by a V. longisporum-specific effector.Infection with the hemi-biotrophic pathogen Phythophthora parasitica induces SA- and JA-dependent signaling pathways during penetration (Attard et al., 2008). Later, during invasive growth, the SA and JA pathways are de-activated and ethylene-mediated signaling predominates. Similar to what has been reported for the V. longisporum root transcriptome at 3 dpi (Iven et al., 2012), genes involved in tryptophan and camalexin synthesis are activated at all stages (Attard et al., 2014). Plasmodiophora brassicae induces the generation of abnormal tissue in the root, resulting in the formation of galls. Transcriptome analysis performed on infected samples at 17, 20, and 24 dpi have shown that auxin-mediated processes are highly activated (Irani et al., 2018). The necrotrophic fungus Macrophomina phasaeolina triggers JA responses (Schroeder et al., 2019).In conclusion, roots can respond with the activation of the SA or JA pathway depending on the attacker. At 10 dpi with V. longisporum, our transcriptome analysis of mutants deficient in SA or JA synthesis complemented by measurements of SA and JA concentrations showed that activation of SA and JA biosynthesis and signalling did not occur. In contrast, V. longisporum triggers both pathways in petioles at 15 dpi (Ralhan et al., 2012).Induction of immunity-related genes might involve systemic inactivation of the repressive function of COI1 at SARD1-dependent promotersDespite the absence of increases in SA or JA (Supplementary Fig. S7), a set of immunity-related genes was induced in V. longisporum-infected roots, including SARD1 and its target genes (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. S6). In leaves infected with foliar pathogens, expression of SARD1 is triggered locally upon activation of MAMP-signalling even in npr1 and sid2 mutants (Sun et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2020), while in systemic leaves, basal levels of SA, the SA receptor NPR1, and the hormone-like metabolite N-hydroxy-pipecolic acid are required for induction (Bernsdorff et al., 2016; Nair et al., 2021; Yildiz et al., 2021). In leaves, enhanced SARD1 expression is sufficient to activate ICS1 and other genes and the resulting enhanced SA concentrations further amplify gene expression in a feed-forward loop (Sun et al., 2015).Based on our observation that SARD1 and its target genes were constitutively expressed in coi1 roots and that expression of the majority of these genes was not further enhanced significantly upon infection (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. S9; Supplementary Table S11), we postulate a COI1-dependent induction mechanism in roots. We assume that induction of SARD1 and its target genes is due to the release of the repressive function of COI1 upon infection, leading to the up-regulation of SARD1, which in turn can activate target genes. Since SARD1 target genes were not activated in roots of SARD-OX plants (Fig. 5), we postulate that these genes are also repressed by COI1 (Fig. 7). The absence of COI1 in coi1 or its inactivation after infection in WT plants would allow SARD1 to activate these genes. The resulting high local levels of SARD1 might then activate target genes by overriding the repressive effect of COI1. In this scenario, it would be a matter of coincidence that highly induced local expression leads to similar expression levels as in coi1. In view of pre-activation of signalling components in coi1 similar to what is observed in SAR leaves (Bernsdorff et al., 2016), we would expect further induction in coi1. However, only 21 of the 149 ‘coi1up / Vlind’ genes showed significantly enhanced transcript levels after infection in coi1 (Supplementary Table S11).To differentiate between these two alternative models, i.e. systemic vs. local induction of SARD1 and its target genes, reporter lines under the control of the respective promoters should be established. However, the microscopy required for identifying the co-localization of the expression of the reporter gene with that of GFP-tagged Verticillium in soil-grown roots is very challenging. Therefore, we decided to perform a split-root experiment in order to test for systemic induction of marker genes. Given that increased SARD1 levels were not able to activate target gene expression in the SARD1-OX lines (Fig. 5) whilst similarly increased SARD1 levels were able to do so in coi1 (Fig. 4), we assumed that COI1 needs to be inactivated to allow SARD1 to function at its target promoters. In fact, we observed weak induction of SARD1 and its target genes in systemic roots (Fig. 6; Supplementary Figs S14, S15). We therefore tentatively conclude that systemic signalling through the root–shoot–root path can at least partially overcome the repressive function of COI1 at SARD1 and at SARD1-regulated promoters (Fig. 7). For SARD1 and LPT4.4, this effect might be more pronounced in the vicinity of infection sites while it is strong at the ECS1 promoter. ChIP experiments with leaves have identified SARD1 at 80 of the 149 ‘coi1up / Vlind’ genes, including SARD1 (Sun et al., 2015). SARD1 binding sites are not enriched in the 167 ‘coi1up’ genes that were not induced (Fig. 3A). Thus, we hypothesize that COI1 is inactivated only at SARD1-regulated promoters.However, systemic induction was not limited to the ‘coi1up / Vlind’ group of genes, and we also found systemic induction of ERF54, which was not repressed by COI1. ERF54 is part of gene regulatory networks that are activated by the PHLOEM INTERCALATED WITH XYLEM (PXY) receptor kinase that promotes vascular cell division and organization (Smit et al., 2020) and during xylem development (Taylor-Teeples et al., 2015). Thus, ERF54 is likely to be involved in Verticillium-induced initiation of xylem hyperplasia. In addition to being developmentally regulated, ERF54 expression is induced by various abiotic cues such as heat, drought, or salt stress (Takahashi et al., 2004; Feng et al., 2005; Bechtold et al., 2008; Li et al., 2019), and biotic cues such as chitooctaose or type III effectors (Truman et al., 2006; Libault et al., 2007). This raises the possibility that the inducing signal is distinct from the one that inactivates COI1. Alternatively, a common systemic signal might activate ERF54, SARD1, LTP4.4, and ECS1 in a COI1-independent manner.The nature of the postulated signals remains enigmatic and they could be of fungal or plant origin. As noted above, the 149 ‘coi1up / Vlind’ genes are induced in SAR leaves. N-hydroxy pipecolic acid (NHP) is known to activate the SAR program in a manner that requires basal levels of SA and NPR1 (Nair et al., 2021; Yildiz et al., 2021). We consider this mechanism to be unlikely with respect to systemic activation of COI1-repressed genes in roots since the NHP biosynthesis genes FLAVIN MONOXYGENASE1 and the L-lysine alpha-aminotransferase ALD1 were not induced upon infection (Supplementary Table S5). More work is required to elucidate the mechanism of induction of the 149 ‘coi1up / Vlind’ genes.Genes constitutively de-repressed in coi1 might cause the tolerance phenotype of the shootThe aim of the study was to obtain information on the mechanism of COI1-mediated tolerance against V. longisporum. Grafting studies have shown that COI1 but not AOS has to be expressed in roots for successful completion of the life cycles of the two vascular pathogens V. longisporum and F. oxysporum in shoots (Thatcher et al., 2009; Ralhan et al., 2012). The tolerance phenotype is not due to up-regulation of the SA pathway, as shown by analysis of coi1 plants with reduced SA levels such as coi1 nahG or coi1 sid2 (Thatcher et al., 2009; Ulrich et al., 2021). Since initial colonization of the shoot was similar in coi1 to that in the WT and aos (Supplementary Fig. S4) (Thatcher et al., 2009; Ralhan et al., 2012), local defense responses do not explain the phenomenon. Instead, it has to be postulated that mobile signals that mediate either susceptibility or resistance are misregulated in coi1. Since several F. oxysporum strains are able to produce JA-Ile (Oliw and Hamberg, 2019), it has been hypothesized that fungal JA-Ile or fungal mimics of JA-Ile ‘hijack’ COI1 to initiate processes in the root that promote senescence in the shoot (Thatcher et al., 2009), with the fungus profiting from weakened cell walls and the transport of metabolites from mesophyll cells to the vascular system in the senescent tissue. This scenario is unlikely for V. longisporum, because potential fungal mimics of JA-Ile or JA-Ile would be expected to induce JA marker genes such as JAZs, THIONIN2.1 (AT1G72260), and PLANT DEFENSIN 1.2 (AT5G44420) in aos roots, which was not the case (Supplementary Table S4).Only four of the induced genes showed expression that was reduced by three-fold or more in coi1 compared with aos and the other susceptible genotypes (Supplementary Table S7), and none of them gives rise to an obvious hypothesis concerning their potential involvement in susceptibility. However, the hypothetical susceptibility-promoting signal might be generated earlier or later than at 10 dpi and/or might not be detected at the transcript level.Interestingly, 316 genes related to innate immunity, SAR, and cell death were constitutively up-regulated in coi1 roots (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S9), most of them coding for signalling proteins such as receptor kinases and transcription factors. Highly expressed defense compounds might be secreted into the xylem vessels so that they accumulate in the shoot when being continuously produced in the root, such as extracellular chitinases (AT4G01700), hydrolases (AT4G19720), peroxidases (AT2G38390), or other antimicrobial compounds such as germin-like proteins (AT5G39110, AT5G39120, AT5G39130, AT5G39150, AT5G39160, AT5G39180, AT5G39190) (Pei et al., 2019, 2020). Some of these are likely to be less expressed in the coi1 sard1 cbp60g triple-mutant, which might explain the partially reduced tolerance observed in these plants (Fig. 4B). The sard1 cbp60g double-mutant was not more susceptible to the fungus as compared with the WT (Fig. 4B), emphasizing that constitutive expression is important. This is different from the results of a previous study that showed infections with V. dahliae were slightly but significantly more successful on sard1 cbp60g (Qin et al., 2018). Our results can be regarded as supporting the notion that constitutive expression of immunity-related genes in coi1 leads to the accumulation of root-to-shoot mobile defense compounds that counteract fungal growth in the shoot.

Our experiments yielded three sets of novel data on the interaction between V. longisporum and Arabidopsis roots, which are discussed below. First, V. longisporum infection leads to the induction of cell wall remodelling genes and SA-related defense genes in the absence of increased levels of JA and SA. Second, the repressive action of COI1 on defense-related genes might be systemically released or overridden upon infection. Third, enhanced levels of root-borne, xylem-mobile defense compounds in the coi1 mutant might be responsible for impaired fungal propagation in the shoot.
