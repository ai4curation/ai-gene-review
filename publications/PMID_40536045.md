The escalating threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) necessitates the urgent
development of novel antibiotics, particularly those against multidrug-resistant
gram-negative bacteria. These pathogens possess formidable structural defenses,
including an impermeable outer membrane (OM) and robust efflux pumps, which
hinder antibiotic efficacy. A promising therapeutic target is the lipoprotein
transport system (Lol), which is essential for bacterial viability. The LolABCDE
complex ensures the precise localization of lipoproteins, which is crucial for
maintaining outer membrane integrity and cell survival. Disruption of this
system results in severe structural and functional defects that lead to
bacterial cell death. This review explores the recent advances in small-molecule
inhibitors targeting the Lol system and examines the mechanisms of LolABCDE
function, along with the structural and biochemical characteristics of its
components. We also summarize the progress made in the discovery and
optimization of inhibitors, including their modes of action, resistance
mechanisms, and therapeutic potential. Despite significant progress, challenges,
such as limited drug permeability and emerging resistance, continue to hinder
therapeutic success. Overcoming these challenges necessitates strategies to
enhance inhibitor selectivity and bypass the intrinsic defense mechanisms of
gram-negative bacteria. This review aims to provide insights into the potential
of targeting the Lol system as a novel strategy for combating antimicrobial
resistance and developing effective therapies against gram-negative bacterial
infections.

In 2015, the AstraZeneca research team identified pyridineimidazole compounds (1 and 2) as inhibitors of the LolCDE complex through high‐throughput phenotypic screening of over 1.2 million compounds (Table 1).[

29

] Compound 1 exhibited limited efficacy against wild type E. coli ATCC25922 with a MIC of 32 µg mL−1. This compound also demonstrated efficacy against wild type Haemophilus influenzae but was ineffective against Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Table 1, entries 3–5). Even a P. aeruginosa strain lacking five efflux pumps (∆mexABDXY) remained resistant (Table 1, entries 4 and 5), suggesting that resistance may be linked to OM permeability issues or a low similarity of LolCDE targets.[

Additionally, SMT‐738 did not show any significant hemolytic activity, further supporting its potential safety for human use.

In the UTI model, SMT‐738 effectively reduced bacterial load in the bladder and kidneys, whereas in the BSI model, it cleared bacteria from the bloodstream and vital organs. In a lung infection model, SMT‐738 lowered bacterial counts in the lung tissues, demonstrating its potential to treat respiratory infections caused by gram‐negative pathogens.

In addition to its efficacy, SMT‐738 was well tolerated in animal models with no observable toxicity or adverse effects. These results, combined with its favorable pharmacokinetic profile, underscore SMT‐738′s potential as a novel treatment for infections caused by MDR gram‐negative bacteria, particularly carbapenem‐resistant Enterobacteriaceae.

Mechanistic investigation into the mode of action revealed a unique and selective inhibition of LolE. Whole‐genome sequencing of abaucin‐resistant A. baumannii mutants identified distinct mutations in or upstream of the lolE gene. These included point mutations A362T and Y394F, as well as an upstream intergenic G→A mutation that was shown to result in ∼4‐fold upregulation of lolE expression by qRT‐PCR. Functionally, these mutations conferred up to a 16‐fold increase in abaucin MIC compared to the wild‐type strain, suggesting both target site modification and overexpression‐mediated resistance. The A362T mutation in A. baumannii LolE was predicted to be spatially adjacent to residue I365 of E. coli LolE—located near the triacylated cysteine of lipoprotein substrates—based on homology modeling using RoseTTAFold and structural alignment with the E. coli LolCDE complex (PDB: 7ARI), supporting the hypothesis that abaucin interferes with lipoprotein accommodation at the lipid‐binding site.

Further evidence supporting this mode of action came from CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) experiments. Conditional knockdown of lolE in A. baumannii using three independent guide RNAs led to a 4‐ to 8‐fold reduction in abaucin MIC, consistent with increased susceptibility upon reduced target expression. In contrast, the strain harboring the upstream intergenic mutation showed increased lolE expression and higher resistance, mirroring the multicopy suppression phenotype observed with other target‐specific antibiotics. Microscopy‐based phenotypic analysis provided further phenotypic validation. Wild‐type A. baumannii cells treated with abaucin showed dose‐dependent morphological changes, including swelling and decondensation of nucleoids, paralleling the morphological defects observed in E. coli cells lacking proper Lol function. Importantly, abaucin treatment did not induce cell lysis in nutrient‐depleted phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), nor did it disrupt the membrane in NPN uptake assays, distinguishing its action from general membrane‐lytic agents.

In vivo efficacy of abaucin was demonstrated in a murine wound infection model. Topical administration of abaucin effectively controlled A. baumannii proliferation in the infected wound site, further highlighting its therapeutic potential. Crucially, abaucin exhibited minimal antibacterial activity against 53 strains of human commensal microbiota, including skin and gut isolates, compared to broad‐spectrum antibiotics such as ampicillin and ciprofloxacin. The low off‐target activity of abaucin underscores its value as a species‐selective agent capable of minimizing dysbiosis and reducing the selective pressure for resistance in nontarget species.

Summary and Outlook

The growing crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) demands urgent innovation in the development of effective antibiotics, particularly against multidrug‐resistant gram‐negative bacteria. These pathogens are fortified by robust defenses, including an impermeable outer membrane and active efflux pumps, rendering them especially challenging to target. Among the various strategies under exploration, the bacterial lipoprotein transport system has emerged as a compelling therapeutic target owing to its essential role in lipoprotein localization, OM integrity, and overall bacterial survival. The LolABCDE complex, a core component of this system, facilitates the sequential transport of lipoproteins from the inner membrane (IM) to the OM, ensuring proper cell envelope assembly and function. Disruption of this pathway induces lethal cellular stress, making it a viable avenue for the development of novel antibiotics.

This review outlines the structural and functional mechanisms of the LolABCDE complex and highlights recent advances in small‐molecule inhibitors targeting this pathway. Notable inhibitors, such as lolamicin and G0507, demonstrate the promise of the Lol system as an antibiotic target. Lolamicin, a gram‐negative‐specific inhibitor, binds to the transmembrane domains of the LolC and LolE subunits, disrupting lipoprotein transport via competitive inhibition. Its potent activity against Enterobacteriaceae, coupled with minimal effects on the gut microbiome, positions it as a strong candidate for clinical development. However, resistance mutations in LolC and LolE, which interfere with binding interactions, present a challenge to sustained efficacy. Similarly, G0507—identified by high‐throughput phenotypic screening—effectively blocks lipoprotein trafficking and induces OM defects. Despite its activity in efflux‐compromised strains, G0507 lacks efficacy against wild‐type E. coli and shows reduced potency due to resistance‐associated mutations in the LolCDE complex.

Notably, although structural optimization of pyridineimidazole‐based inhibitors such as the AZ series and SMT‐738 has enhanced their antibacterial potency, direct biochemical evidence of binding (e.g., IC₅₀ or K
d values for LolCDE) remains unavailable. Their mechanisms of action have been inferred primarily from MIC profiling and resistance mutation analyses, underscoring the need for future biophysical validation using purified or reconstituted complexes. Furthermore, while natural inhibitors of LolB, such as procyanidin and stevioside, have demonstrated moderate binding affinity in vitro through fluorescence quenching assays, their antibacterial activity remains limited—likely due to poor OM permeability and unfavorable pharmacokinetic properties. In contrast, synthetic inhibitors targeting LolA and LolCDE generally exhibit greater efficacy but often lack direct biochemical validation. These observations highlight the need for integrated approaches that combine the binding specificity of natural products with the potency and drug‐like characteristics of synthetic compounds.

The discovery and development of Lol inhibitors continue to face major challenges, with OM permeability representing a key barrier—particularly for compounds like G0507, which are ineffective against wild‐type gram‐negative strains. To address this, strategies such as structural optimization, co‐administration with OM permeabilizers, and combination therapies have been proposed.
