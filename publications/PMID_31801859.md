## Full Text

Although increasing evidence has demonstrated that HSV-1 subverts host immune responses and establishes lifelong latent infection, the molecular mechanisms by which HSV-1 interrupts antiviral innate immunity, especially the cGAS/STING-mediated cellular DNA-sensing signaling pathway, have not been fully explored. Here, we show that β-catenin promotes cGAS/STING-mediated activation of the IFN pathway, which is important for cellular innate immune responses and intrinsic resistance to DNA virus infection. The protein kinase US3 antagonizes the production of IFN by targeting β-catenin via its kinase activity. The findings in this study reveal a novel mechanism for HSV-1 to evade host antiviral immunity and add new knowledge to help in understanding the interaction between the host and HSV-1 infection.

KEYWORDS: HSV-1, US3, β-catenin, type I IFN

ABSTRACT

The cGAS/STING-mediated DNA-sensing signaling pathway is crucial for interferon (IFN) production and host antiviral responses. Herpes simplex virus I (HSV-1) is a DNA virus that has evolved multiple strategies to evade host immune responses. Here, we demonstrate that the highly conserved β-catenin protein in the Wnt signaling pathway is an important factor to enhance the transcription of type I interferon (IFN-I) in the cGAS/STING signaling pathway, and the production of IFN-I mediated by β-catenin was antagonized by HSV-1 US3 protein via its kinase activity. Infection by US3-deficienct HSV-1 and its kinase-dead variants failed to downregulate IFN-I and IFN-stimulated gene (ISG) production induced by β-catenin. Consistent with this, absence of β-catenin enhanced the replication of US3-deficienct HSV-1, but not wild-type HSV-1. The underlying mechanism was the interaction of US3 with β-catenin and its hyperphosphorylation of β-catenin at Thr556 to block its nuclear translocation. For the first time, HSV-1 US3 has been shown to inhibit IFN-I production through hyperphosphorylation of β-catenin and to subvert host antiviral innate immunity.

IMPORTANCE Although increasing evidence has demonstrated that HSV-1 subverts host immune responses and establishes lifelong latent infection, the molecular mechanisms by which HSV-1 interrupts antiviral innate immunity, especially the cGAS/STING-mediated cellular DNA-sensing signaling pathway, have not been fully explored. Here, we show that β-catenin promotes cGAS/STING-mediated activation of the IFN pathway, which is important for cellular innate immune responses and intrinsic resistance to DNA virus infection. The protein kinase US3 antagonizes the production of IFN by targeting β-catenin via its kinase activity. The findings in this study reveal a novel mechanism for HSV-1 to evade host antiviral immunity and add new knowledge to help in understanding the interaction between the host and HSV-1 infection.

INTRODUCTION

The innate immune responses are the first line of host defense against invading viruses. Type I interferons (IFN-I) play a central role in the antiviral innate immune response ( 1 , 2 ). Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as retinoic acid-inducible gene I, melanoma differentiation-associated antigen 5, membrane-associated Toll-like receptors (TLRs), and several recently discovered cytosolic DNA sensors, recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns and induce IFN-I production ( 2 – 5 ). Cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) synthase (cGAS) has been identified as the principal sensor recognizing cytosolic aberrant DNA to produce cGAMP via its enzymatic activity ( 6 ). Then, cGAMP acts as an important secondary messenger to activate the stimulator of interferon gene (STING) and recruits TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) to activate IFN-regulatory factor 3 (IRF3). Then, IRF3, the pivotal transcription factor of IFN-β, binds to the promoter of IFN-β to boost the expression of IFN-β ( 6 – 8 ). The cascade reaction of IFN-β production is regulated by many adapter proteins. Recent studies have revealed that β-catenin is required for the optimal transcriptional activity of IFN-β and important for immune responses against viral infections ( 9 – 12 ).

β-Catenin is the key effector protein downstream of the Wnt signaling pathway. In resting cells, most β-catenin proteins at the cell membrane mediate the interaction of adherens junction molecules with the actin cytoskeleton ( 13 ). The cytosolic β-catenin is degraded by a cytoplasmic protein degradation complex containing glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK-3), adenomatous polyposis coli, axin, and protein phosphatase 2A. Binding to this complex, β-catenin is phosphorylated at amino acids Ser33, Ser37, Thr41, and Ser45 and then ubiquitinylated by the β-transducin repeat-containing protein and degraded by the 26S proteasome ( 14 ). Inhibition of the kinase GSK-3 dissociates β-catenin from the degradation complex and reduces GSK-3-dependent phosphorylation, leading to β-catenin accumulation and its subsequent nuclear translocation ( 15 – 17 ). In the nuclei, β-catenin associates with lymphoid enhancer factor/T-cell factor (LEF/TCF) and CBP/p300 and forms a transcriptional complex that induces the transcription of many target genes. In addition to regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, or survival, β-catenin plays a role in pathogenic infections ( 18 ). A recent study showed that β-catenin interacted with IRF3 through its C-terminal domain and facilitated the recruitment of p300 to the IFN-β promoter through IRF3, which enhanced IFN-β expression ( 12 ). The IFN-β production mediated by β-catenin can also occur independently of IRF3. Treatment with an inhibitor of GSK-3, such as lithium chloride (LiCl), leads to the nuclear accumulation of β-catenin, which results in the recruitment of TCF/β-catenin complexes over the IFN-β promoter region to upregulate the transcriptional capacity of IFN-β ( 16 ). Knockdown of β-catenin inhibited vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), Listeria monocytogenes , and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced IFN-β production ( 12 ). The cGAS/STING-mediated DNA-sensing signaling pathway is important for antiviral immune response and intrinsic resistance to DNA virus infection. However, the regulatory mechanisms for the cGAS/STING signaling pathway have not been completely elucidated. As we learn from the literature that the role of β-catenin in IFN-β activation has not been shown to be involved in DNA virus infection, it will be of great significance to reveal the role of β-catenin in the DNA-sensing signaling pathway and the underlying mechanisms by which DNA viruses evade it.

Herpes simplex virus I (HSV-1), a member of the subfamily Alphaherpesvirinae , has double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) that encodes over 80 viral proteins ( 19 ). HSV-1 US3, a serine/threonine (Ser/Thr) kinase, is conserved among all members of the subfamily Alphaherpesvirinae . During viral infection, US3 is involved in nuclear egress, virion maturation, and promotion of virus spread ( 20 , 21 ). It also activates mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 and inhibition of the translational repressor 4E-binding protein 1 to enhance the expression of viral genes ( 22 ). US3 prevents the apoptosis induced by viral infection and downregulates major histocompatibility complex class I surface expression in HSV-1-infected cells to facilitate viral replication ( 23 – 25 ). Our recent studies have demonstrated that US3 hyperphosphorylates IRF3 and NF-κB subunit p65 to inhibit IFN-β production ( 26 , 27 ). Although increasing evidence has shown that US3 is an important protein for viral infection, the mechanism by which US3 interrupts innate immunity, particularly the DNA-sensing pathway, has not been fully investigated.

In this study, we demonstrate that β-catenin is intimately involved in the DNA-sensing signaling pathway. Comprehensive screening experiments have revealed that US3 acts as an important inhibitor of the activation of the IFN-I signaling pathway induced by β-catenin. Furthermore, US3 interacted with β-catenin and hyperphosphorylated it at Thr556, blocking β-catenin nuclear translocation to reduce the production of IFN-β via its protein kinase activity. Infection with wild-type (WT) HSV-1, but not US3-deficient HSV-1 (ΔUS3 HSV-1) and its kinase mutant variants (K220M HSV-1 and D305A HSV-1), inhibited the production of IFN-β induced by β-catenin. For the first time, US3 has been shown to reduce IFN-I production through hyperphosphorylation of β-catenin to evade host antiviral immune responses.

RESULTS

β-catenin is required for optimal IFN-β production in the DNA-sensing signaling pathway.

To verify the role of β-catenin in the cGAS/STING-mediated cytosolic DNA-sensing signaling pathway, β-catenin was knocked out (KO) in HEK293T cells by CRISPR-Cas9 gene knockout technology to generate the β-catenin-KO-293T cell line ( Fig. 1A ). In HEK293T cells, ectopic expression of minimal amounts of cGAS and STING together significantly activated the IFN-β promoter ( 28 ). Based on this model, HEK293T cells and β-catenin-KO-293T cells were cotransfected with plasmids encoding cGAS/STING, along with the IFN-β promoter or the (pRDIII-I)4-Luc reporter (a luciferase reporter plasmid driven by tandem IRF binding site-positive regulatory domains III and I [PRDIII-I] of the IFN-β promoter), respectively, and subjected to dual-luciferase reporter (DLR) assays to detect the promoter activity. The absence of β-catenin inhibited the activation of both the IFN-β promoter and the (pRDIII-I)4-Luc reporter induced by cGAS and STING ( Fig. 1B and C ). Consistent with this, β-catenin deficiency downregulated the mRNA levels of IFN-β and downstream antiviral genes, such as IFN-stimulated gene 56 (ISG56) and ISG54, mediated by cGAS and STING ( Fig. 1D to F ). These findings indicated that β-catenin is required for the optimal activation of the cGAS/STING-mediated IFN-I signaling pathway.

FIG 1.

US3 inhibits the activation of IFN-β by targeting β-catenin.

It has been reported that β-catenin is an activator of IRF3 by increasing its transcription activity ( 11 , 12 ). To determine whether HSV-1 proteins could antagonize the expression of IFN-β induced by β-catenin, HSV-1 proteins were screened. US3, a protein kinase of HSV-1, was determined to possess the ability to inhibit IFN-β promoter activity and final IFN-β production induced by β-catenin. As shown in Fig. 2A , ectopic expression of β-catenin alone was not efficient at inducing activation of the IFN-β promoter in β-catenin-KO-293T cells. However, β-catenin enhanced the activity of the IFN-β promoter induced by IRF3/5D, the active form of IRF3, consisting of a 5-amino-acid-residue mutation in the C-terminal domain of IRF3 that leads to its nuclear translocation and activates the IFN-β promoter directly. Interestingly, the ectopic expression of US3 significantly downmodulated the activity of the IFN-β promoter induced by IRF3/5D and β-catenin.

FIG 2.

Our published data have verified that US3 interacts with IRF3 and hyperphosphorylates it at Ser175 to prevent IRF3 activation ( 27 ). A single-point mutant of IRF3/5D (IRF3/5D S175A) was applied to eliminate the inhibitory effect of US3 on IRF3. As expected, US3 downregulated the activation of the IFN-β promoter induced by IRF3/5D, but not that induced by IRF3/5D S175A ( Fig. 2B ). β-Catenin promoted the IFN-β promoter activity mediated by IRF3/5D S175A, and US3 reduced the activation of IFN-β induced by the combined effects of IRF3/5D S175A and β-catenin ( Fig. 2B ). The results of quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR) also showed that β-catenin increased the accumulation of IFN -β mRNA induced by IRF3/5D S175A, and ectopic expression of US3 had little effect on IRF3/5D S175A-mediated IFN -β mRNA transcription. However, US3 reduced the IFN -β mRNA expression induced by β-catenin and IRF3/5D S175A ( Fig. 2C ).

To further explore the antiviral role of β-catenin in HSV-1 replication, HEK293T and β-catenin-KO-293T cells were infected with WT HSV-1 or ΔUS3 HSV-1, as in our previous studies ( 26 ), and harvested at the indicated time points for viral plaque assay. β-Catenin deficiency resulted in an increase in viral replication of ΔUS3 HSV-1, but not WT HSV-1 ( Fig. 2D and E ). These data indicate that US3 downregulates the β-catenin-mediated activation of the IFN-β signaling pathway.

US3 inhibits the β-catenin-mediated IFN-β signaling pathway via its kinase activity.

To determine whether the Ser/Thr kinase activity of US3 is required for its inhibition of the β-catenin-mediated IFN-β signaling pathway, kinase-dead (KD) US3 mutant plasmids (K220M and D305A) were constructed and applied to detect the effect on β-catenin-mediated IFN-β production. As shown in Fig. 3A , activation of the IFN-β promoter induced by IRF3/5D S175A and β-catenin was strongly interrupted in the presence of US3. However, ectopic expression of US3 K220M or US3 D305A failed to inhibit activation of the IFN-β promoter driven by IRF3/5D S175A and β-catenin. Similar results are shown in Fig. 3B to D , where US3, but not its KD mutants, reduced the accumulation of IFN-β and its downstream ISG ( ISG56 and ISG54 ) mRNAs induced by IRF3/5D S175A and β-catenin.

FIG 3.

To reveal the impact of US3 on β-catenin during viral infection, cells were infected with WT HSV-1, ΔUS3 HSV-1, or the kinase-dead K220M HSV-1 and D305A HSV-1. Our findings demonstrated that the accumulation of IFN -β, ISG54 , and ISG56 mRNAs induced by IRF3/5D S175A and β-catenin was downregulated by WT HSV-1 but not ΔUS3 HSV-1 or the kinase-dead HSV-1 ( Fig. 3E to G ). Taken together, the results suggested that the kinase activity of US3 is essential for inhibition of the activation of the IFN signaling pathway induced by β-catenin.

US3 blocks the nuclear translocation of β-catenin.

Activation of the Wnt signaling pathway results in β-catenin accumulation and nuclear translocation. In nuclei, β-catenin interacts with transcription factors, leading to increased expression of target genes. To further explore the underlying mechanism by which US3 interrupts β-catenin activity, the subcellular localization of β-catenin was analyzed to address whether US3 impacts the nuclear translocation of β-catenin. LiCl, an inhibitor of GSK-3, was used to increase the accumulation of β-catenin in the nuclei. As a result, both infection with WT HSV-1 and ectopic expression of US3 significantly blocked the nuclear translocation of β-catenin induced by LiCl. However, infection with ΔUS3 HSV-1, K220M HSV-1, and D305A HSV-1 and ectopic expression of the kinase mutants of US3 failed to inhibit LiCl-induced β-catenin nuclear translocation ( Fig. 4A to D ). We also found that neither infection with HSV-1 nor overexpression of US3 impacted total β-catenin protein. The expression levels of UL42 (a viral protein of HSV-1) in L929 cells and LiCl-treated L929 cells were similar upon WT HSV-1 infection, while levels of UL42 protein were decreased in LiCl-treated L929 cells compared to those in L929 cells upon ΔUS3 HSV-1, K220M HSV-1, and D305A HSV-1 infection. This indicated that the replication of ΔUS3 HSV-1, K220M HSV-1, and D305A HSV-1, but not WT HSV-1, was inhibited by LiCl in L929 cells ( Fig. 4E and F ). Furthermore, immunofluorescence assays were carried out to verify the effect of US3 on the nuclear translocation of β-catenin. In mock-treated L929 cells, the endogenous expression of β-catenin was very low. Cells treated with LiCl induced the accumulation of β-catenin and its nuclear translocation. Infection with WT HSV-1, but not ΔUS3 HSV-1, K220M HSV-1, or D305A HSV-1, prevented the nuclear accumulation of β-catenin induced by LiCl ( Fig. 4G ).

FIG 4.

To reveal the effect of impaired nuclear translocation of β-catenin by US3 on the production of IFN-β, A549 cells were transfected with plasmids encoding IRF3/5D S175A and then treated with LiCl in the presence or absence of US3. The results showed that US3 hampered the accumulation of IFN -β mRNA induced by IRF3/5D-S175A and LiCl ( Fig. 4H ). These findings demonstrated that US3 inhibited the nuclear translocation of β-catenin to impair IFN-β activation mediated by β-catenin via its kinase activity.

US3 phosphorylates β-catenin at Thr556.

The kinase activity of US3 is required for US3-mediated inhibition of IFN-β production induced by β-catenin. This raises the hypothesis that US3 associates with and phosphorylates β-catenin. HEK293T cells were cotransfected with plasmids encoding US3, along with β-catenin, and L929 cells were transfected with US3, harvested, and then subjected to coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) experiments. As shown in Fig. 5A and B , US3 interacted with β-catenin.

FIG 5.

To test the hypothesis that β-catenin might serve as a substrate of the US3 protein kinase, HEK293T cells were cotransfected with plasmids encoding β-catenin, along with US3 or its relevant vector, and mass spectrometry was performed to identify the phosphorylation site of β-catenin by US3. The threonine at position 556 (Thr556) of β-catenin was identified as a site of phosphorylation by US3. To confirm this result, Western blotting (WB) was performed to demonstrate the additional slower-migrating form of β-catenin by US3. As the molecular weight of β-catenin was too high to show the shift, a plasmid encoding a truncated β-catenin from amino acids 394 to 781 (β-catenin–Myc ΔN) was generated. The results demonstrated that ectopic expression of US3, but not its KD mutants, resulted in the slower-migrating form of β-catenin ( Fig. 5C ).

To further confirm that β-catenin Thr556 is a US3 phosphorylation site, a β-catenin ΔN point mutant in which Thr556 was replaced by Ala (β-catenin–Myc ΔN T556A) was generated. As shown in Fig. 5D , β-catenin–Myc ΔN T556A could not be hyperphosphorylated by US3, indicating that US3 phosphorylated β-catenin at Thr556. Full-length β-catenin harboring the T556A mutant (β-catenin T556A) was generated to assess the role of β-catenin phosphorylation by US3 in the nuclear accumulation of β-catenin and activation of the IFN-β promoter. As shown in Fig. 5E to H , ectopic expression of US3 significantly blocked the nuclear translocation of β-catenin, but not β-catenin T556A. Both β-catenin and β-catenin T556A accelerated the IFN-β promoter activity induced by IRF3/5D S175A. However, US3 significantly inhibited β-catenin-mediated, but not β-catenin T556A-mediated, activation of the IFN-β promoter ( Fig. 5I ). The results of qRT-PCR also showed that β-catenin and β-catenin T556A upregulated similar accumulations of IFN -β mRNA induced by IRF3/5D S175A and that US3 reduced the IFN -β mRNA expression mediated by β-catenin, but not β-catenin T556A ( Fig. 5J ). Taken together, our data strongly demonstrate that HSV-1 encoding the kinase US3 interacts with β-catenin and hyperphosphorylates β-catenin at an atypical site, Thr556, to inhibit the cGAS/STING-mediated DNA-sensing signaling pathway.

DISCUSSION

During DNA virus infection, viral dsDNA and subcellular aberrant dsDNA induced by viral replication are recognized by the cytosolic DNA sensor. Through a cascade reaction, the IFN-β signaling pathway is activated to produce IFN-β and to induce the expression of various ISGs. In this study, we demonstrated the role of β-catenin in the DNA-sensing signaling pathway and revealed the underlying mechanisms used by HSV-1 to evade innate antiviral responses by targeting β-catenin.

β-Catenin, a kind of adhesion molecule, also acts as a transcription factor to regulate the transcription of target genes. It modulates the promoter activity of IFN-β via an IRF3-dependent PRDIII-I region by recruiting CBP/p300 ( 11 , 12 ). In addition, β-catenin upregulates the constitutive and virus-induced transcriptional capacities of IFN-β through TCF binding sites present on the IFN-β promoter independently of IRF3 ( 16 ). The nuclear translocation of β-catenin is very critical to both IRF3-depedent and IRF3-indpendent pathways. These studies on the role of β-catenin in IFN-β focus on intracellular bacteria and RNA virus infection. It is well documented that LRRFIP1 detected exogenous dsDNA through its nucleic-acid-binding domain and increased the production of IFN-I through β-catenin ( 12 ). However, the role of β-catenin in the cGAS/STING-mediated IFN-β signaling pathway remains unknown. In this study, we demonstrated that loss of β-catenin leads to a significant reduction of IFN-β production and to exogenous expression of a minimal amount of cGAS and STING, suggesting that β-catenin is required for optimal IFN-β production in the cGAS/STING-mediated signaling pathway.

β-Catenin has been demonstrated to be involved in cellular defense against different viruses and to be subverted by viral infection. Sendai virus infection resulted in the nuclear translocation of β-catenin and IFN expression by protein kinase C-activated histone deacetylase 6 ( 29 ). Interestingly, viral targeting of β-catenin transcription activity has been observed in the context of infections with H1N1 influenza virus, human cytomegalovirus, Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus, and human immunodeficiency virus type 1 ( 11 , 30 – 32 ). However, Ding et al. showed that β-catenin downregulated IRF3-mediated signaling in human CRC cells ( 33 ). The discrepancy might be explained by different posttranslational modifications of β-catenin induced by different Wnt stimulants, as well as the infection of different cell types with different viruses.

HSV-1, a typical dsDNA virus, has evolved multiple strategies to evade host antiviral responses and to produce lifelong infection in the host. Recently, studies from our laboratory have demonstrated that UL41 and VP22 target cGAS and UL46 targets TBK1 to inhibit cGAS/STING-mediated IFN-β production ( 28 , 34 , 35 ). However, whether HSV-1 protein or any viral protein inhibits the β-catenin-mediated cGAS/STING signaling pathway remains unknown. Based on the screening assay in our studies, US3, an HSV-1 kinase, was determined to downregulate β-catenin-mediated IFN-β production.

US3 is a tegument protein of HSV-1 that acts as a Ser/Thr protein kinase. During HSV-1 infection, Akt is activated only at early time points. However, it is activated throughout the replication cycle during US3-deficienct HSV-1 infection at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 ( 36 ). Akt is required for the promotion of IFN-β transcription downstream of TLR3 by enhancing the transcriptional activity of β-catenin ( 9 ). This hints that US3 might downregulate β-catenin expression by targeting Akt. Here, LiCl was used to inhibit the kinase activity of GSK-3 directly and to prevent β-catenin degradation, and the expression of β-catenin induced by LiCl in L929 cells was not affected under both ectopic expression of US3 and viral infection (MOI = 2). Our previous studies have revealed the role of US3 in the evasion of innate immune responses, including the hyperphosphorylation of IRF3 and NF-κB via its protein kinase activity. In this study, IRF3/5D with a single amino acid mutated was applied to demonstrate the role of US3 in inhibiting IRF3 and β-catenin-mediated IFN-β production by targeting β-catenin, and the underlying mechanism was phosphorylation of β-catenin by US3 at Thr556 to inhibit the nuclear translocation of β-catenin via its kinase activity. β-Catenin deficiency enhanced the replication of US3-deficienct HSV-1, but not WT HSV-1. This further confirms the antiviral activity of β-catenin and the immune evasion of US3 in β-catenin-mediated IFN-I production in the cytosolic DNA-sensing signaling pathway.

In summary, β-catenin is required for optimal IFN-β production in the DNA-sensing signaling pathway, and the HSV-1 protein kinase US3 was shown here for the first time to counteract activation of the IFN-β signaling pathway by targeting β-catenin. US3 inhibited the production of IFN-β by hyperphosphorylation of β-catenin via its kinase activity to facilitate viral replication ( Fig. 6 ). The findings in this study contribute novel knowledge about the underlying mechanisms used by HSV-1 to counteract host antiviral innate immunity and provide new insights into the host-virus interaction.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cells, viruses, and antibodies.

HEK293T, A549, L929, and Vero cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (Gibco-BRL) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/ml penicillin and streptomycin. The WT HSV-1 strain F, ΔUS3 HSV-1, K220M HSV-1, and D305A HSV-1 were propagated in Vero cells and titrated as previously described ( 37 ). The protease inhibitor cocktail and nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction reagents were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). Radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer was purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Mouse anti-Myc and anti-Flag monoclonal antibodies (MAbs) were purchased from Abmart (Shanghai, China). Mouse anti-β-catenin MAb and mouse and anti-β-actin MAb were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA). Rabbit anti-UL42 polyclonal antibodies (PAbs) were made by GL Biochem Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Plasmid construction.

To construct US3-Flag, we amplified the open reading frame (ORF) of US3 from plasmid US3-EYFP as previously described and cloned it into a pCMV-Flag vector (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The commercial reporter plasmid pRL-TK (RL stands for Renilla luciferase, and TK stands for thymidine kinase) was purchased from Promega Corp. (Madison, WI). Plasmids received as gifts include the following: (pRDIII-I)4-Luc ( 38 ), IRF3/5D ( 39 ), and an IFN-β promoter reporter plasmid ( 40 ).

Co-IP and Western blot analysis.

Co-IP assays and WB analysis were performed as previously described ( 41 ). Briefly, cells were transfected with plasmids as indicated in the figure legends. Harvested cells were lysed on ice with lysis buffer. The lysates were incubated with the antibodies referred to in the figure legends and protein A/G Plus-Agarose (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) overnight at 4°C. The beads were washed three times with lysis buffer, and WB analysis was performed as previously described to detect the interaction of proteins ( 42 ).

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR.

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s manual. Samples were dissolved with RNase-free water, digested with DNase I, and then subjected to reverse transcription as previously described. The cDNA was used as the template for qRT-PCR to detect the levels of IFN-β and ISGs, and 18S rRNA was used as an internal reference as previously described ( 43 ). The primers used in qPCR were as follows: IFN -β, forward, 5′-CCAACAAGTGTCTCCTCCAAAT-3′, and reverse, 5′-AATCTCCTCAGGGATGTCAAAGT-3′; ISG54 , forward, 5′-CTGCAACCATGAGTGAGAA-3′, and reverse, 5′-CCTTTGAGGTGCTTTAGATAG-3′; ISG56 , forward, 5′-TACAGCAACCATGAGTACAA-3′, and reverse, 5′-TCAGGTGTTTCACATAGGC-3′; 18S rRNA , forward, 5′-CGGCTACCACATCCAAGGAA-3′, and reverse, 5′-GCTGGAATTACCGCGGCT-3′.

Transfection and DLR assays.

Cells were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. HEK293T cells were transfected with reporter plasmids, such as IFN-β–Luc and the internal control plasmid pRL-TK, with or without expression plasmids, as indicated, by Lipofectamine 2000. At 24 h posttransfection, luciferase assays were performed with a dual-specific luciferase assay kit (Promega, Madison, WI) as described in our previous studies ( 44 , 45 ).

Immunofluorescence assays.

Immunofluorescence assays were performed as described previously ( 37 ). In brief, L929 cells were infected with WT HSV-1, ΔUS3 HSV-1, K220M HSV-1, and D305A HSV-1 for 6 h; treated with LiCl for 20 h; and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The cells were incubated with mouse anti-β-catenin MAb (diluted 1:100), followed by incubation with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Sigma-Aldrich). After each incubation step, the cells were washed extensively with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Samples were analyzed with a fluorescence microscope.

Establishment of β-catenin knockout HEK293T cells.

Potential guide RNAs (gRNAs) targeting the β-catenin gene were analyzed using the CRISPR design tool. Double-stranded oligonucleotides were cloned into the PX330-U6-Chimeric-BB-CBh-hSpCas9 vector (Addgene) and cotransfected into HEK293T cells. Monoclonal HEK293T cells were further characterized by WB to establish the β-catenin-KO-293T cells. The β-catenin gRNA target sequence is 5′-CACCCATGGACGTACGGCGC-3′.

Statistical analysis.

Data are represented as means and standard deviations (SD) when indicated, and Student’s t test was used for all statistical analyses with GraphPad Prism 5.0 software. Differences between groups were considered significant when the P value was <0.05.
