Kisspeptin receptor (KISS1R), belonging to the class A peptide-GPCR family,
plays a key role in the regulation of reproductive physiology after stimulation
by kisspeptin and is regarded as an attractive drug target for reproductive
diseases. Here, we demonstrated that KISS1R can couple to the Gi/o pathway
besides the well-known Gq/11 pathway. We further resolved the cryo-electron
microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of KISS1R-Gq and KISS1R-Gi complexes bound to the
synthetic agonist TAK448 and structure of KISS1R-Gq complex bound to the
endogenous agonist KP54. The high-resolution structures provided clear insights
into mechanism of KISS1R recognition by its ligand and can facilitate the design
of targeted drugs with high affinity to improve treatment effects. Moreover, the
structural and functional analyses indicated that conformational differences in
the extracellular loops (ECLs), intracellular loops (ICLs) of the receptor, and
the "wavy hook" of the Gα subunit may account for the specificity of G protein
coupling for KISS1R signaling.

INTRODUCTION

Peptide hormones are prominent signal molecules in the central and peripheral nervous systems. At least 150 nature neuropeptides have been identified, and most of them signal through G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) (1). In humans, the peptide kisspeptins (KPs) encoded by the KISS1 gene play an essential role in the regulation of reproductive physiology upon recognition by their receptor (KISS1R) (2–4). Initially, the product of the KISS1 gene is an unstable and inactive 145–amino acid prepolypeptide, which further requires proteolytic cleavage into shorter active peptide fragments with a suffix referring to their amino acid length [e.g., kisspeptin-10 (KP10), KP13, KP14, and KP54] (5). Great efforts have been exerted in developing synthetic KP analogs with satisfactory affinity and water solubility for the KP receptor (6), such as TAK448, which has notable therapeutic potential in the treatment of female reproductive disorders in a recent clinical trial (7). In addition to its well-established role in reproduction, the KP-KISS1R signaling system has recently been shown to be associated with the regulation of body weight (8), feeding (8), metabolism (9), energy balance (10), nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (11), and cancer immune surveillance (12).

Great efforts have been devoted to elucidating the molecular basis of the KP receptor activated by KP. For example, all active KP peptides commonly share the same C-terminal region containing the Arg-Phe-NH2 sequence characterized by the RF amide peptide family, which is responsible for binding to and activating KISS1R (5, 13). On the KP receptor side, molecular docking and mutagenesis analyses have determined several peptide-binding residues within the receptor, thus unveiling possible underlying mechanisms of KP recognition and KISS1R activation (14–17). However, these mechanisms may be imprecise because of a lack of clear structural information, which hinders the understanding of signal activation mechanisms and drug design targeting KP receptor.

Some GPCRs may primarily couple to a specific subtype of G protein to induce intracellular signal transduction, whereas some receptors can bind to multiple subtypes of G protein in response to extracellular stimuli, such as cholecystokinin A receptor (CCKAR) (18), somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2) (19), and angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R) (20) for class A GPCRs as well as aGPCR latrophilin 3 (ADGRL3) (21) and GPR110 (22) for class B GPCRs. The mechanism underlying the selectivity of G protein coupling remains one of the major issues in GPCR biology and has been proven to be immensely complicated (23–26). In addition to being primarily coupled to Gq/11 proteins, our study also indicated that KISS1R promiscuously signals through the Gi/o pathway (Fig. 1A). There is increasing recognition that specific G protein signaling pathways have therapeutic relevance, whereas other signaling pathways are often associated with adverse effects. Therefore, it is crucial to clarify the underlying mechanism that drives the preferential interaction of G protein subtype with KISS1R.

RESULTSStructure determination of TAK448-bound KISS1R-Gq, TAK448-bound KISS1R-Gi, and KP54-bound KISS1R-GqConsistent with the classification of KISS1R as a Gq/11-coupled receptor, both KP54 and TAK448 can induce Gq protein recruitment and robustly activate IP1 production in KISS1R-expressing human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells (fig. S2, A and B). Some receptors, such as CCKAR (18), SSTR2 (19), and AT1R (20), which can couple to downstream Gq/11 and Gi/o protein families, contain hydrophobic residues at position 34.51 (indicating Ballesteros-Weinstein numbering) within intracellular loop 2 (ICL2) (26). Similarly, KISS1R contains a bulky hydrophobic leucine at this position. Further functional assays, such as the NanoBiT-G protein dissociation assay (Fig. 1B and fig. S2C) and cyclic adenosine 3′,5′-monophosphate (cAMP) inhibition experiments (fig. S2, D to F), indicated that KISS1R can signal through the Gi/o pathway. The IP1 accumulation assay showed differential potency between TAK448 and KP54 in activating downstream Gq signaling (fig. S2A), consistent with previous studies (7). The kinetics of signaling are important when comparing potencies and efficacies between different ligands (27, 28); our results indicated no differences in potencies for cAMP inhibition when immediately tracing the kinetic signals after ligand addition (fig. S2, D to F).To stabilize the TAK448-bound KISS1R-Gq, TAK448-bound KISS1R-Gi, and KP54-bound KISS1R-Gq complexes, we used the NanoBiT tethering method, a common approach widely used in determining the structure of GPCR-G protein complexes. Specifically, we fused the large part of NanoBiT (LgBiT) to the C terminus of KISS1R and the small part of NanoBit (SmBiT) to the C terminus of the Gβ subunit (fig. S1, A and B) (29). For the assembly of the Gi complex, we used a dominant-negative version of Gi containing the G203A and A326S mutations (fig. S1D) (30). For the assembly of the Gq complex, the replacement of the N terminus of Gq with the first 18 residues of Gi enables it to bind scFv16, a fab fragment successfully used for stabilizing GPCR-G protein complexes (31) (fig. S1C). We coexpressed the receptors, Gα, Gβ, and Gγ in insect cells and purified the complexes following commonly used procedures to obtain GPCR-G protein complexes. Subsequently, we determined the complex structures of Gq and Gi by single-particle analysis using cryo-EM, achieving final resolutions of 2.68 Å for the TAK448-bound KISS1R-Gq complex (fig. S3), 3.00 Å for the TAK448-bound KISS1R-Gi complex (fig. S4), and 2.95 Å for the KP54-bound KISS1R-Gq complex (fig. The Gq interface is similar with other Gq-coupling receptors, including CCKAR, SSTR2, and GAL2R, with RMSD values of 1.67, 1.70, and 1.62 Å, respectively (fig. S6, E to G). Gq complexes have better overall maps, whereas the Gi complex has a low-quality ICL3 density region, so we speculated that these differences may reflect the ability of the G protein to couple to the receptor. Contribution factors for G protein selectivity of KP receptorMost GPCRs are traditionally believed to couple specifically with one subtype of G protein to transduce intracellular signals. However, our studies indicate that KISS1R could bind to multiple G protein subtypes. This special property renders KISS1R an ideal model for investigating the promiscuity and selectivity of G protein coupling.First, the wavy hook at the very end of the Gα α5 helix is thought to be a determinant of G protein coupling specificity, primarily due to differences in the last seven residues of the α5 helix (26). The overall conformations of α5 helix of the Gα subunit between the KISS1R-Gq and KISS1R-Gi complexes are similar although with distinct residues (Fig. 5B and fig. S11A). The L (−2) H5.25 and L (−7) H5.20 residues [superscripts refer to the common Gα numbering system (40)] in the α5 helix are extremely conserved across the G protein families and play a pivotal role in G protein coupling (fig. S11A). Both L352(−2) H5.25 and L347(−7) H5.20 in Gαq and L353(−2) H5.25 and L348(−7) H5.20 in Gαi create hydrophobic interactions with residues in TM3 and TM6 (R1403.50 and T1443.54, V2616.33, L2646.36, and V2656.37). However, notable differences were identified between the Gi- and Gq-coupled complexes. For instance, L353(−2) H5.25 and L348(−7) H5.20 in Gαi form more extensive hydrophobic interactions with M2275.61 and L2315.65 compared to the Gq-coupled complex because of a conformation change of the α5 helix (Figs. 5B and 6, A and E). Contribution factors for G protein selectivity of KP receptor

Most GPCRs are traditionally believed to couple specifically with one subtype of G protein to transduce intracellular signals. However, our studies indicate that KISS1R could bind to multiple G protein subtypes. This special property renders KISS1R an ideal model for investigating the promiscuity and selectivity of G protein coupling.

First, the wavy hook at the very end of the Gα α5 helix is thought to be a determinant of G protein coupling specificity, primarily due to differences in the last seven residues of the α5 helix (26). The overall conformations of α5 helix of the Gα subunit between the KISS1R-Gq and KISS1R-Gi complexes are similar although with distinct residues (Fig. 5B and fig. S11A). The L (−2) H5.25 and L (−7) H5.20 residues [superscripts refer to the common Gα numbering system (40)] in the α5 helix are extremely conserved across the G protein families and play a pivotal role in G protein coupling (fig. S11A). Both L352(−2) H5.25 and L347(−7) H5.20 in Gαq and L353(−2) H5.25 and L348(−7) H5.20 in Gαi create hydrophobic interactions with residues in TM3 and TM6 (R1403.50 and T1443.54, V2616.33, L2646.36, and V2656.37). However, notable differences were identified between the Gi- and Gq-coupled complexes. For instance, L353(−2) H5.25 and L348(−7) H5.20 in Gαi form more extensive hydrophobic interactions with M2275.61 and L2315.65 compared to the Gq-coupled complex because of a conformation change of the α5 helix (Figs. 5B and 6, A and E). In contrast to conserved residues L (−2) H5.25 and L (−7) H5.20, residues at positions (−3) H5.24, (−4) H5.23, and (−5) H5.22 are less conserved. The side chain of N351(−3) H5.24 in Gαq forms a polar interaction with the main chain carboxyl group of Y3237.53 and the backbone amine group of F3308.50. Because Gαi has G352(−3) H5.24 instead of aspartic acid in Gαq, the corresponding hydrogen bonds are absent in the KISS1R-Gi complex (Fig. 6, A and E). In addition, Y350(−4) H5.23 in Gαq extensively interacts with the receptor’s cytoplasmic cavity by forming hydrogen bonds with T772.39, D1393.49, R1403.50, and R154ICL2 (Fig. 6, B and O). In contrast, C351(−4) H5.23 in Gαi only creates a weak hydrogen bond with R1403.50 via its main chain carboxyl group (Fig. 6F). To summarize, these differences ensure that Gq engages receptors more tightly, which may explain why the KISS1R-Gq complex has a higher resolution and a better density map than the KISS1R-Gi complex.

DISCUSSIONTwo unresolved questions remain in the realm of peptide-GPCR pathway systems, which involve the underlying mechanism of the varied binding modes of peptide ligands and G protein coupling selectivity. KISS1R, well-known as a Gq/11-coupled receptor, regulates the secretion of gonadotropin-releasing hormone, which in turn regulates the reproductive health through the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal endocrine axis. Given that KISS1R contains a large hydrophobic leucine residue at position ICL234.51, which is also present in other secondary Gi/o-coupling receptors, we have further validated that KISS1R is a multipotent receptor capable of both coupling with Gq and Gi. These properties lead us to start structural study on KISS1R in complex with Gq and Gi.Kisspeptins belong to RF amide peptides because of the specific C-terminal domain that required for binding and activating KISS1R. A previous clinical study showed that TAK448, produced by modifying KP10 into a nonapeptide, exhibited noticeable therapeutic efficacy against certain female reproductive diseases. However, the underlying mechanism of the activation of the KP-KISS1R signaling pathway remains to be elucidated. In this study, the three high-resolution cryo-EM structures can help to reveal the mystery and provide fresh clues for designing more selective and potent drugs for KISS1R.It is widely believed that the distal C terminus of the Gα subunit is a major determinant for coupling to GPCRs (45–47). Also recently, it has been greatly thought to be important for G protein coupling selectivity. Also, there is substantial evidence supporting the importance of ICLs in GPCR-G protein coupling. For Gq/11 protein selectivity, ICL3 notably promotes Gq-coupled efficacy of CCKAR by interacting with hydrophobic patches of YS6.02, FH5.06, and AH5.09 within Gαq subunits (18), while position 34.51 of ICL2 in class A GPCR participates in secondary Gi/o coupling (26). In general, although KISS1R has an almost identical conformation from our study, the Gq and Gi proteins are oriented differently, resulting in different sizes of the receptor-G protein interface. The primary coupling of KISS1R to Gq can be explained by the maximal interface of the two KISS1R-G protein complexes. Furthermore, the structural comparison of these complexes reveals that wavy-hook residues of the Gα α5 helix, ICL2, and ICL3 play an important role in determining the Gi/o and Gq/11 selectivity. Mutants of KISS1R, such as L14834.51G and R15434.57A, preferentially impair specific Gq protein pathway compared to the Gi signaling pathway, which can be used to explore the physiological role of specific G protein pathways of KISS1R in vivo. It is increasingly recognized that different types of downstream GPCR signaling pathways may perform distinct functions through the same receptor (48–50). Therefore, it is worthwhile to intensively study the physiological roles and therapeutic opportunities of secondary G protein pathways of GPCRs (such as the Gi/o pathway of KISS1R). In general, the knowledge of KISS1R-Gi signaling pathway is limited according to published literature, as KISS1R was mainly thought to signal through Gq/11-mediated pathway or β-arrestin–dependent pathway (51). A published work highlighted the link between KP-KISS1R signaling and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) (9), suggesting that it could impair glucose stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) by suppressing cAMP synthesis in β cells. It is commonly believed that the cAMP–protein kinase A (PKA)-cAMP response element–binding protein (CREB) signaling axis accounts for the insulin secretion from β cells in adaptive response to hyperglycemia (52). Thus, we hypothesize that KISS1R likely activates the Gi signaling pathway, which decreases the cAMP level and ultimately contributes to T2DM under pathological conditions. Also, our study establishes a crucial structural basis for understanding the conformations of receptor binding to different G proteins, ligand recognition and selectivity, activation mechanism, and G protein interaction sites. This provides valuable information for subsequent physiological study and drug development targeting KISS1R.

MATERIALS AND METHODSConstruct cloning and protein expressionThe complete human KISS1R (residues 1 to 398) was subcloned into pfastbac1 vector (Gibco), where hemagglutinin (HA) signal peptide and FLAG tag followed by a BRIL epitope were labeled at N terminus, and LgBiT and 6 × His tag were added at C terminus. The desired G protein components included G203A/A326S-mutated dominant-negative Gαi1 (DNGαi1), Gαq (designed for scFv16 binding by replacing N-terminal 18 residues), and Gβ1γ2. The pFastbac A vector was used for DNGαi1 and Gαq cloning, while the pFastbac Dual vector is for Gβ1γ2 cloning. These G protein components (DNGαi1 or Gαq and Gβ1γ2) were coexpressed with KISS1R in Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) insect cells via the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system. Cells were infected with baculoviruses expressing DNGαi1 or Gαq, Gβ1γ2, and KISS1R (at the ratio of 5:1:30) when the cell density reached 4 × 106 cells/ml. After a 48-hour incubation at 27°C, the infected cells were centrifuged for 10 min at 3500g and then stored at −80°C. The primers for designing the point mutation for KISS1R are summarized in the table S4.Complex formation and purificationThe first step was to lyse the cell pellet in lysis buffer containing Hepes (pH 7.5, 10 mM), EDTA (1 mM), iodoacetamide (4 mg/ml), and agonist (KP54 for Gq-bound complex, TAK448 for Gq/Gi-coupled complex, 1 μM each). Cell membranes were obtained after centrifugation at 20,000 rpm. For the following cell membrane solubilization, a solubilization buffer containing Hepes (pH 7.5, 20 mM), NaCl (100 mM), iodoacetamide (4 mg/ml), leupeptin (2.5 μg/ml), benzamidine (0.16 mg/ml), lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG; 1%), cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS; 0.1%), glycerol (10%), and agonist (10 μM) was used to resuspend the cell membranes by glass dounce. To remove guanosine diphosphate, apyrase and MgCl2 (10 mM) were added and the mixture was incubated at 4°C overnight. After centrifugation at 20,000 rpm, the supernatant was incubated with anti-FLAG M1 affinity resin containing CaCl2 (2 mM) for 1 hour at 4°C. The incubated resin was centrifugated at 2000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C and then washed by a wash buffer containing Hepes (pH 7.5, 20 mM), NaCl (100 mM), CaCl2 (2 mM), LMNG (0.003%), and agonist (1 μM). The elution buffer containing Hepes (pH 7.5, 20 mM), NaCl (100 mM), LMNG (0.003%), CHS (0.004%), glyco-diosgenin (GDN) (0.001%), EDTA (5 mM), flag peptide (200 μM), and agonist (1 μM) was used for protein complex elution. After a 2-hour incubation on ice with purified scFv16 (0.25 mg), the elution fractions were subjected to size exclusion chromatography using a Superose 6 increase 10/300 column (GE) with running buffer containing Hepes (pH 7.5, 20 mM), NaCl (100 mM), LMNG (0.003%), CHS (0.004%), GDN (0.001%), and agonist (1 μM). By using a 100-kDa molecular weight cutoff concentrator, the peak fractions containing KISS1R-Gi complex and KISS1R-Gq complex were concentrated to 5 mg/ml for further cryo-EM sample preparation.Purification of scFv16The antibody scFv16 was purified according to the standard protocol for secreted proteins, and the baculovirus was prepared in the same manner as that of KP receptor. Trichoplusia ni (Hi5) insect cells were infected by the virus at a ratio of 1:40 when the insect cell density reached 2.5 × 106 /ml. The supernatant collected after 60 hours was loaded onto nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid resin column. The column was washed with the wash buffer containing Hepes (pH 7.5, 20 mM) and NaCl (500 mM), while the protein was eluted with a buffer containing Hepes (pH 7.5, 20 mM), NaCl (500 mM), and imidazole (250 mM). Subsequently, the eluted protein was concentrated and loaded onto a Superdex 200 increase 10/300 size exclusion column (GE Healthcare) to collect peak fractions. The collected protein solution was further concentrated followed by fast-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collectionThe amorphous alloy film (53) (CryoMatrix nickel titanium alloy film, R1.2/1.3, Zhenjiang Lehua Electronic Technology Co., Ltd.) was glow-discharged using a Tergeo-EM plasma cleaner. The purified complex sample (3.5 μl) was applied onto the grid and then blotted for 4 s with blotting force of 0 and quickly plunged into liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen using Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cryo-EM data were collected at the Kobilka Cryo-EM Center of the Chinese University of Hong Kong (Shenzhen) using a 300-kV Titan Krios Gi3 microscope. The raw movies were recorded with a Gatan K3 BioQuantum Camera at the magnification of 105,000, with the pixel size 0.83 Å. Inelastically scattered electrons were excluded using a GIF Quantum energy filter (Gatan, USA) with a slit width of 20 eV. Movie stacks were acquired with a defocus range of −1.0 to −2.0 μm with a total exposure time of 2.5 s, fragmented into 50 frames (0.05 s per frame), and a dose rate of 21.2 e/pixel per s. Semi-automatic data acquisition was performed using the SerialEM5.0 (54).Cryo-EM data processingThe image processing strategy followed the way as previously described. In brief, data with four-time binning was used for micrograph screening and particle picking, while data binned two times was used for particle screening and classification. The initial set of particles was extracted from the original clean micrograph, and the resultant dataset was used for final cleaning and reconstruction. Raw movie frames were aligned using MotionCor2 (55) with a 9 × 7 patch, and the contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated using Gctf and ctf in JSPR (56). Only micrographs with consistent CTF values, including defocus and astigmatism, were kept for further processing. For TAK448-KISS1R-Gq-scFv16 complex (PDB ID: 8XGO, no releasing), 7064 micrographs were retained from 7218 raw movies. A total of 6,575,367 particles picked from template picking were subjected to two rounds of 2D classification, reducing the number from 2,790,286 and to 1,921,962 through several rounds of ab initio refinement. These particles underwent nonuniform refinement and yielded a map at 2.70 Å. MATERIALS AND METHODS

Construct cloning and protein expressionThe complete human KISS1R (residues 1 to 398) was subcloned into pfastbac1 vector (Gibco), where hemagglutinin (HA) signal peptide and FLAG tag followed by a BRIL epitope were labeled at N terminus, and LgBiT and 6 × His tag were added at C terminus. The desired G protein components included G203A/A326S-mutated dominant-negative Gαi1 (DNGαi1), Gαq (designed for scFv16 binding by replacing N-terminal 18 residues), and Gβ1γ2. The pFastbac A vector was used for DNGαi1 and Gαq cloning, while the pFastbac Dual vector is for Gβ1γ2 cloning. These G protein components (DNGαi1 or Gαq and Gβ1γ2) were coexpressed with KISS1R in Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) insect cells via the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system. Cells were infected with baculoviruses expressing DNGαi1 or Gαq, Gβ1γ2, and KISS1R (at the ratio of 5:1:30) when the cell density reached 4 × 106 cells/ml. After a 48-hour incubation at 27°C, the infected cells were centrifuged for 10 min at 3500g and then stored at −80°C. The primers for designing the point mutation for KISS1R are summarized in the table S4.
