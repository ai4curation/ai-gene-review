Some capsid proteins built on the ubiquitous HK97-fold have accessory domains
imparting specific functions. Bacteriophage P22 coat protein has a unique
insertion domain (I-domain). Two prior I-domain models from subnanometer
cryoelectron microscopy (cryoEM) reconstructions differed substantially.
Therefore, the I-domain's nuclear magnetic resonance structure was determined
and also used to improve cryoEM models of coat protein. The I-domain has an
antiparallel six-stranded β-barrel fold, not previously observed in HK97-fold
accessory domains. The D-loop, which is dynamic in the isolated I-domain and
intact monomeric coat protein, forms stabilizing salt bridges between adjacent
capsomers in procapsids. The S-loop is important for capsid size determination,
likely through intrasubunit interactions. Ten of 18 coat protein
temperature-sensitive-folding substitutions are in the I-domain, indicating its
importance in folding and stability. Several are found on a positively charged
face of the β-barrel that anchors the I-domain to a negatively charged surface
of the coat protein HK97-core.

INTRODUCTION

Viruses are self-contained infectious agents that target all types of organisms ranging from animals and plants to bacteria and Archaea. The basic function of a virus is to encode a genome that takes over the host’s machinery in order to replicate. Icosahedral viruses also encode proteins that form a capsid, to protect the genome from the environment and host defenses. The dsDNA is packaged through the portal vertex, triggering scaffolding protein exit, capsid expansion, and maturation of the virus.

Coat proteins of tailed phages such as P22, the archaeal virus HSTV-1, Herpesviruses, the encapsulins, and small virus-like particles from Archaea, have no sequence homology but share a conserved HK97-fold (Abrescia et al., 2012; Akita et al., 2007; Baker et al., 2005; Pietila et al., 2013; Sutter et al., 2008). The HK97-fold is named after the first high-resolution structure for this class of protein (Fig. 1B), the coat protein of phage HK97 (Wikoff et al., 2000). This protein fold can be considered the most common in nature, as dsDNA tailed phages are the most populous entities in the biosphere (Krupovic et al., 2011; Suttle, 2007). The coat protein of phage P22 has a non-conserved accessory domain, inserted between the A- and P-domains (Fig. 1C, magenta). Here we refer to this non-conserved module as the insertion domain or ‘I-domain’. Initially named the ‘ED’ (extra density) (Chen et al., 2011; Jiang et al., 2003), or ‘telokin-like domain’ (Parent et al., 2010; Teschke and Parent, 2010), the function of the I-domain has not been unequivocally established. Other data point to the I-domain being involved in capsid size determination (Suhanovsky and Teschke, 2011). While the HK97-core of these coat protein models is similar, the non-conserved I-domains show marked differences making it difficult to reconcile biochemical and genetic data with the structures. As P22 capsids are often used as a platform for nanotechnology and display (Parent et al., 2012a; Patterson et al., 2014), fully understanding the structure of coat protein is crucial.

Here we describe the NMR solution structure of the isolated I-domain from phage P22 coat protein. The NMR structure shows the domain folds into a 6-stranded β-barrel related to the reductase/isomerase/elongation factor (RIEf) fold, and this structure differs substantially from both previous cryoEM models of the I-domain. The NMR structure is used in conjunction with 3.8 Å cryoEM data from procapsids to develop a refined model of the I-domain, as well as the full-length P22 coat protein. Based on the improved model, the I-domain appears to play roles in capsid stabilization and monomer folding, in large part through electrostatic complementation between the I-domain and the HK97-core of coat protein.

RESULTSNMR Structure of the I-domainThe solution structure of the I-domain (Fig. 2, Table 1) was determined using a segment of the intact P22 coat protein encompassing residues 223–345. This fragment was shown to adopt a stable tertiary structure by protease digestion, circular dichroism and one-dimensional NMR (Rizzo et al., 2013; Suhanovsky and Teschke, 2013). The core of the I-domain consists of a 6-stranded anti-parallel Greek key β-barrel (Fig. 2A, B) with a shear number of S = 10 (Murzin et al., 1994), and a −1, +3, +1, −5, +5 topology (Richardson, 1981). In Figure 2, the strands of the barrel are colored in dark to light magenta, moving from the N- to the C-terminus of the protein. Strands one and six close the barrel, bringing the N-and C-termini of the I-domain in close proximity (Fig. 2A, B). Outside of the β-barrel core, there is a small 1.5-turn α-helix (light blue), and a short three-stranded β-sheet (dark to light orange) with a +1, −1x strand connectivity. Strand i of the short sheet extends from strand 3 of the β-barrel, while strands ii, iii, and the short α-helix follow from strand 5 of the β-barrel (Fig. 2C). Strand 5 of the β-barrel is terminated by a proline in a cis configuration based on characteristic NOEs and chemical shifts (Shen and Bax, 2009). The cis peptide bond causes a bend in the chain following strand 5, which positions the small accessory β-sheet and α-helix on the bottom end of the barrel, opposite the I-domain chain termini. The top of the barrel, on the side nearest the N- and C-termini, is capped by mostly rigid loop connections between strands β2-β3 and β4-β5. In full-length coat protein, the N- and C-termini of the I-domain would be connected to domains A and P, further sheltering the top of the 6-stranded β-barrel.Except for the N- and C-termini and the two long loops, the NMR structure of the I-domain is well-defined (Fig. 2A), with a mean backbone RMSD of 0.48 Å over 90 rigid amino acids of 123 total. The side chains in the rigid core of the I-domain are also well defined with a mean heavy atom RMSD of 1.0 Å. Many of the hydrophobic residues in the core of the β-barrel show unique side chain conformations (Sup. Fig. S1A). The side chains of residues positioned on the solvent-accessible surface of the β-barrel often also have unique conformations (Sup. Fig. S1B).Backbone Dynamics of the I-domainThe backbone dynamics of the I-domain were characterized by 15N T1, T2, and 1H-15N NOE relaxation measurements (Alexandrescu et al., 1996; Farrow et al., 1994). A Model-Free analysis (Lipari, 1982) of the 15N relaxation data was used to extract time-scales (not shown) and amplitudes (S2 order parameters) for internal motions (Fig. 3A). The S2 order parameter varies from a value of 1 in the limit of no internal dynamics to a value of 0 in the limit of unrestricted motion. The β-barrel of the I-domain is rigid with S2 values close to 1.0, as is the accessory structure consisting of the short three-stranded β-sheet and α-helix (Fig. 3A). Most of the loop segments connecting elements of regular secondary structure are also rigid on the pico- to nanosecond timescale. Regions of the I-domain that show S2 order parameters below 0.85, indicative of flexibility, are designated by the red symbols in Fig. 3A. Flexible regions include the chain termini, residue G266 in a turn between strands 2 and 3 of the β-barrel, and two long loops. The first long loop is named the D-loop or “protruding loop” (residues V239-N254), according to previous convention (Chen et al., 2011). We shall refer to the second loop as the S-loop or “size determination loop” (residues L281-Q291), since mutations that affect capsid size such as A285T (Suhanovsky and Teschke, 2011) occur in this segment.Figure 3B shows that the S2 order parameters, which monitor flexibility on the ps-ns timescale, are correlated with the precision of the NMR structure. The regions that are least precisely defined in the NMR structure are colored in red in Fig. 3B, and correspond to segments that are most dynamic as determined by the 15N relaxation data (Fig. 3A). The dynamics of the I-domain were further explored using trypsin proteolysis (Fig. 3C). A time course of trypsin digestion of the I-domain was carried out and analyzed by tricine SDS-PAGE, followed by in-gel digestion of the tryptic peptides, separation of fragments using liquid chromatography, and peptide identification by mass spectroscopy. The arrows in Fig. 3B show the predicted trypsin cleavage sites in the I-domain. Black arrows indicate trypsin sites that were not cleaved, with all four falling in the rigid parts of the I-domain. Green arrows indicate trypsin sites that were cut (Fig. 3C). Five of the cut cleavage sites are in the flexible regions of the I-domain, including the D- and S-loop and G266 in the turn between the second and third of the β-barrel. A sixth trypsin site occurs at E323 at the beginning of the short α-helix, which corresponds to a site with intermediate flexibility (S2 = 0.89).The flexibility of the D- and S-loops could be intrinsic, or could result from studying the I-domain in isolation from the rest of the coat protein for the NMR studies. The solvent accessibility of the I-domain in the context of the full-length coat protein was investigated using the protein footprinting method Fast Photochemical Oxidation of Proteins (FPOP), coupled with mass spectrometry (Hambly and Gross, 2005). This method utilizes hydroxyl radicals to oxidatively modify solvent accessible side chains. Because the reactivity of the hydroxyl radical is residue-dependent, it is difficult to compare differences in oxidative modification between peptides that have different amino acid sequences. Thus to compare modification levels within the coat protein, we calculated a modifiable fraction. This fraction is determined from the extent of modification and scales with respect to the differences in amino acid reactivity (see Supplementary Methods). Three tryptic peptides, 256–268, 273–279, and 312–325 (Fig. 3D) showed minimal modification (modifiable fraction ≤ 1). Peptide 256–268 is part of the rigid β2 strand. Peptide 273–279 encompasses strands β3 from the barrel and βi from the short sheet, both of which are rigid. Similarly peptide 312–325 consists of the rigid strand βii and the short αhelix. By contrast, significant FPOP modification was observed for peptides that overlap with the dynamic D-loop (238–249 and 250–268) and S-loop (280–286), indicating that these I-domain segments are also flexible in the full-length coat protein monomers.Fitting of the I-domain Structure into the P22 Coat Protein CryoEM DensityIn Figure 4, the NMR structure of I-domain (Fig. 4A) is compared with the two previous structural models of the intact P22 coat protein from reconstructions of cryoEM data obtained at resolutions of 8.2 Å (PDB ID 3IYI; (Parent et al., 2010) (Fig. 4B) and 3.8 Å (PDB ID 2XYY; (Chen et al., 2011) (Fig. 4C). In the structural model based on the 8.2 Å-resolution data (Fig. 4B), a threading protocol was used to identify structural motifs with similar amino acid sequence profiles to the I-domain. The top candidate was the telokin-fold, an immunoglobulin-(Ig)-like β-sandwich (Fig. 4B). Ig-like motifs occur often as extra domains in virus and phage coat proteins (Pell et al., 2010), whereas the 6-stranded β-barrel fold evinced by the NMR I-domain structure, has to our knowledge not been previously observed in a coat protein. The 8.2 Å-resolution model captures the all-β-sheet character of the NMR structure, except the sequence positions of the β-strands differ, probably as an artifact of the threading protocol. In addition, rather than forming a closed β-barrel with orthogonal packing, the I-domain in the cryoEM model forms a β-sandwich with an aligned packing of the β-strands. The β-sandwich consists of two β-sheets, roughly corresponding to the NMR structure’s strands β1-β2-βi for the first β-sheet, and strands β3-β4-β5 for the second β-sheet. Residues beyond V313, the start of strand βii in the small sheet of the NMR structure, could not be modeled in the 8.2 Å-resolution cryoEM data. While there is some similarity in the first β-sheet, with the twelve residues F236-G247 in the hairpin between strand β1 and β2 giving a Cα RMSD of 3.1 Å between the two structures, the difference between the βbarrel fold in the NMR structure and the β-sandwich fold in the EM structure gives a global RMSD of 17.8 Å when all 84 equivalent atoms are compared with the program SuperPose (Maiti et al., 2004).A second structure for the I-domain (Fig. 4C) was determined directly from the 3.8 Å resolution cryoEM data (Chen et al., 2011). When aligned, the NMR derived I-domain structure had an RMSD of 20.7 Å over 82 equivalent Cα atoms in comparison to the Chen procapsid model. Like the lower resolution Parent model described above, the model based on the 3.8 Å resolution cryo-EM density map also shows considerable differences from the NMR structure of the I-domain, with an RMSD of 25.0 Å over all 114 equivalent Cα positions. While much of the secondary structure is captured in the 3.8 Å-resolution cryoEM model, the connectivity of the elements is considerably different in the NMR structure, likely due to the limited resolution of the cryoEM density map in this region. Despite their differences, there are some overall similarities. Strands β2-β4-β5 of the NMR structure match well with corresponding strands in the 3.8 Å resolution cryoEM model, when connectivity and sequence assignment are not considered. Both structures clearly show a long D-loop (Fig. 2C), although this corresponds to residues V239-N254 in the NMR structure, rather than T258-G277 in the 3.8 Å-resolution cryoEM model. Overall, the D-loop in the NMR structure is negatively charged with one basic (K249) and three acidic (D244, D246, D253) residues, while it was positively charged in the 3.8 Å-resolution cryoEM model (Chen et al., 2011). An α-helix is seen in both structures but in the NMR model it is formed by residues P322-Y327 rather than V278-L289 in the cryoEM modelTo obtain an improved model of the P22 coat protein, the new NMR structure of I-domain was fit into the 3.8 Å-resolution cryoEM density map for the P22 procapsid and the 4 Å mature virion map using Foldhunter (Jiang et al., 2001) (Fig. 4D, E). The overall correlation value for the I-domain fit was between 0.65 and 0.68, with ~50–56% of the residues encompassed by the density map depending on the fitted monomer from the NMR ensemble. In all of the fits, portions of the termini, as well as the D- and S-loops, appeared to be shifted outside of the density, probably as these are dynamically disordered based on the NMR, proteolysis, and FPOP data (Fig. 3). To best model these portions of the I-domain structure, the cryoEM model and the NMR structure were combined as described in the Materials and Methods. Once a single subunit model was constructed, it was transformed into all subunits in the asymmetric unit, and once again refined using Phenix (Fig. 4F, G). The I-domain component of the refined cryoEM model had a global RMSD of 4.2–5.4 Å when compared to the ensemble of NMR I-domain models, with the majority of the differences occurring at the flexible termini and the D- and S-loops. Model #28 of the ensemble had the highest correlation to the procapsid and mature virion density maps (0.7341, 0.3309, respectively). After refinement of this model, in the context of the entire capsid protein, correlation of the capsid protein models to the procapsid and mature virion density maps improved to 0.781 and 0.5646, respectively. Superposition of the NMR model with the density refined capsid protein showed a RMS deviation of 4.847 Å and 5.088 Å to the procapsid and mature virion models, respectively. It should be noted that the majority of the differences in the structure were locate in three regions on the NMR model: the D-loop, the S-loop and the termini. Coincidentally, these three regions also show the most flexibility in the NMR models. It should also be noted that the HK97-like core domain of the P22 capsid protein was modeled only as Cαs; bond length and bond geometry remained comparable to the previous Chen models. Except for the extension of the two loops, the HK97-like core domain of the P22 capsid protein remained nearly identical to the Parent and Chen models.Electrostatic Polarity of the I-domain StructureA key feature of the I-domain NMR structure is a markedly asymmetric distribution of positive and negative charges on the β-barrel. The anti-parallel pairing of strands β1 and β6 closes the β-barrel of the I-domain. This side of the βbarrel, which faces the environment in the assembled capsid, has a predominantly negatively charged character due to surface exposed acidic residues (Fig. 5A, B left). Outside the β-barrel, acidic residues are also present in the D-loop and on the surface of the α-helix on this side of the molecule (Fig. 5A). By contrast, the opposite side of the I-domain that packs against the rest of the coat protein core structure has a markedly positive character (Fig. 5A, B right). Positively charged basic residues are present on the surface of the β-barrel, as well as in the S-loop, while this side of the structure has few if any exposed acidic groups. In the cryoEM model that was refined based on the NMR structure (Fig. 5C), it is clear electrostatic interactions play a key role in docking the I-domain to the HK97-core of the coat protein, as the positive surface of the I-domain complements a negatively charged patch on the surface of the HK97-core of the coat protein.Inferences about the function of the I-domain from its structureBecause the β-barrel structure of the I-domain is unique among the viral coat proteins that share the HK97-fold, we examined the potential functional roles for this accessory domain. The I-domain contributes significantly to both coat protein stability and rapid folding (Suhanovsky and Teschke, 2013). The revised cryoEM model based on the I-domain NMR structure shows the domain may also play a role in capsid stabilization, consistent with previous suggestions (Chen et al., 2011). To investigate the role of the I-domain in capsid stabilization in both procapsids and mature capsids, the I-domain model was fit to the 4.0 Å resolution cryoEM density map of the P22 mature capsid (Fig. 4F), in addition to modeling the I-domain in the context of the 3.8 Å P22 procapsid, described above. Starting from the aforementioned procapsid model, a single subunit in the asymmetric unit was fit using Foldhunter, then iteratively refined using Phenix (Adams et al., 2011) and Coot (Emsley and Cowtan, 2004). As in the procapsid model, the single refined subunit was transformed into all positions in the asymmetric unit and refined with Phenix to remove potential clashes (Fig. 4G).During capsid maturation, the capsid proteins undergo a rather substantial rigid-body rearrangement, leading to hole closure (Fig. 4F, G) and an increase in internal capsid volume by 10% (Prasad et al., 1993). The overall structure of the I-domain in the refined procapsid and mature capsid models is very similar (Fig. 4D, E). The most significant differences are caused by changes in the orientation of neighboring coat protein subunits at a 2-fold axis of symmetry (Fig. 6A, B, Sup. Fig. S2). The subunit interface, particularly at the D-loop of the I-domain and F-loop (Chen et al., 2011) of the HK97-core, is altered during maturation. In the procapsid, the D-loop from the I-domain of one coat protein subunit is tucked in between the I-domain and P-domain of the neighboring subunit in the adjacent capsomer, whereas the D-loops in the mature capsid coat protein are located directly across from each other along the two-fold axis of symmetry. Thus, during maturation the tip of the D-loops (N245, D246, G247) are shifted ~12 Å apart at the two-fold axis. Likewise, the two F-loops (located in the P-domain at a subunit interface, indicated by the small arrows in Figure 4D and E) are closer together in the procapsid than the mature virion (Fig. 6A, Sup. Fig S2). Potential intermolecular salt bridges across the icosahedral 2-fold axis in the procapsid (given the resolution of the models) may occur between E81 of the F-loop in the P-domain and K249 of the I-domain D-loop, R299 of I-domain strand β4 and D244 of the I-domain D-loop, and between R269 of I-domain strand β2 and D246 of the I-domain D-loop. The salt bridges are likely to be important for both assembly and stability of the procapsid. Accordingly, uncontrolled assembly of coat protein, which occurs to a small extent in the absence of scaffolding protein, is inhibited by low concentrations of NaCl (~100 mM), corroborating the important role for electrostatic interactions during assembly. In the mature capsid, the coat protein subunits from adjacent capsomers move apart at the 2-fold axis. The only remaining I-domain contacts are located at the tip of the D-loops through residues D244, N245, and D246 (Figure 6B, Sup. Fig. S2).Another potential function for the I-domain is suggested by a search for structurally homologous folding motifs using the Dali server (Holm and Rosenstrom, 2010). The I-domain shows structural similarity to a fold classified in the SCOP database (Murzin et al., 1995) as a “reductase/isomerase/elongation factor” (RIEf) common domain. The top 100 hits in the Dali search were all to RIEf domains of translation factors, with the top match to a domain (residues 205–325) in chain A of translation initiation factor 2γ (IF2γ) from Sulfolobus solfataricus (PDB code 3P3M). MATERIALS AND METHODSNMR structure and dynamicsProtocols for the expression and purification of the I-Domain, together with its NMR assignments have been previously described (Rizzo et al., 2013). All NMR experiments were done on 1.5 mM I-domain samples in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) containing 0.02% (w/v) NaN3, at a temperature of 37 °C. Experiments to obtain restraints for structure calculations were recorded on 600 and 800 MHz Varian Inova NMR spectrometers equipped with cryogenic probes. Distance restraints were from 3D 15N- and 13C-editted NOESY experiments, together with a 2D 1H-NOESY spectrum for aromatic ring protons (Table 1). The mixing time for all NOESY experiments was 150 ms. Hydrogen bonds were identified from a long-range HNCO experiment (Cordier and Grzesiek, 1999) recorded in TROSY mode on a 2H,13C,15N sample at 800 MHz, deuterium isotope exchange experiments recorded on I-domain samples dissolved in 99.96% D2O, and NOE-patterns characteristic of secondary structure. Backbone φ and ψ dihedral angles were calculated from the assigned HN, Hα, N, Cα, Cβ and C’ chemical shifts using the program TALOS+ (Shen et al., 2009). Side-chain χ1 dihedral restraints were obtained from HNHB experiments as previously described (Rizzo et al., 2013). The NMR structure of the I-domain was calculated using the Aria (Bardiaux et al., 2012) and CNS (Brunger et al., 1998) programs. The 30 lowest energy NMR structures (Table 1) have been deposited in the PDB under accession code 2M5S. Backbone dynamics for the I-domain were characterized with 15N relaxation experiments and analyzed in terms of the Model Free formalism (Lipari, 1982). Details of the NMR relaxation experiments used to study the dynamics of the I-domain are described in the Supplementary Methods section.FPOP labelingCoat protein monomers were subjected to oxidative labeling by H2O2 for short time periods during photolysis with a laser. MATERIALS AND METHODS

NMR structure and dynamicsProtocols for the expression and purification of the I-Domain, together with its NMR assignments have been previously described (Rizzo et al., 2013). All NMR experiments were done on 1.5 mM I-domain samples in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) containing 0.02% (w/v) NaN3, at a temperature of 37 °C. Experiments to obtain restraints for structure calculations were recorded on 600 and 800 MHz Varian Inova NMR spectrometers equipped with cryogenic probes. Distance restraints were from 3D 15N- and 13C-editted NOESY experiments, together with a 2D 1H-NOESY spectrum for aromatic ring protons (Table 1). The mixing time for all NOESY experiments was 150 ms. Hydrogen bonds were identified from a long-range HNCO experiment (Cordier and Grzesiek, 1999) recorded in TROSY mode on a 2H,13C,15N sample at 800 MHz, deuterium isotope exchange experiments recorded on I-domain samples dissolved in 99.96% D2O, and NOE-patterns characteristic of secondary structure. Backbone φ and ψ dihedral angles were calculated from the assigned HN, Hα, N, Cα, Cβ and C’ chemical shifts using the program TALOS+ (Shen et al., 2009). Side-chain χ1 dihedral restraints were obtained from HNHB experiments as previously described (Rizzo et al., 2013). The NMR structure of the I-domain was calculated using the Aria (Bardiaux et al., 2012) and CNS (Brunger et al., 1998) programs. The 30 lowest energy NMR structures (Table 1) have been deposited in the PDB under accession code 2M5S. Backbone dynamics for the I-domain were characterized with 15N relaxation experiments and analyzed in terms of the Model Free formalism (Lipari, 1982). Details of the NMR relaxation experiments used to study the dynamics of the I-domain are described in the Supplementary Methods section.

Fitting the NMR structure into cryoEM mapsResidues corresponding to the I-domain NMR structure were removed from the cryoEM model. The best fit I-domain structure from the NMR ensemble was then concatenated with the truncated cryoEM model, followed by constrained refinement against the cryoEM density map (Baker et al., 2013) with the programs Phenix (Adams et al., 2011) and Coot (Emsley and Cowtan, 2004). The single subunit model was then transformed into all subunits in the asymmetric unit and refined using Phenix (Adams et al., 2011). Further details can be found in the Supplementary Methods.
