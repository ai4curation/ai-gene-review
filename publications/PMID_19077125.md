In abstinent opiate addicts, relapse can be triggered by exposure to
environmental cues associated with drug use; thus, the disruption of these
learned associations may be an effective approach for reducing relapse.
Interestingly, glutamatergic systems are thought to be involved in
opiate-induced behavioral plasticity. In this study, changes in expression and
phosphorylation levels of alpha-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic
acid (AMPA) glutamate receptor subunits (GluR1, GluR2) in the hippocampus were
investigated in rats showing a conditioned response (CR) to an opiate-paired
environment as well as in animals in which this conditioned behavior was
extinguished. Additionally, another set of animals went through a drug-unpaired
paradigm (without conditioning) in order to examine the effects of the
pharmacology of the drug itself. Subcellular fractionation techniques were used
to analyse the local distribution of AMPA glutamate subunits within the synapse,
especially at the postsynaptic density (PSD). Results showed that
morphine-dependent CRs did not alter expression or redistribution of GluR1 or
GluR2; however, the unpaired administration of morphine resulted in an increase
in the phosphorylation of the GluR1 subunit at extrasynaptic sites.
Interestingly, the extinction of the CR significantly increased phosphorylation
of the GluR1 subunit at the PSD. Therefore we propose that, within the synapse,
the phosphorylation of the GluR1 subunit at the PSD may be a key mechanism in
the extinction of opiate-associated CRs.

Introduction

Relapse in abstinent human opiate addicts can be triggered by the exposure to environmental cues previously associated with drug use (O’Brien et al., 1977). Learned associations that develop between the abused opiate and the environment in which it is consumed are engendered through Pavlovian conditioning processes (Sideroff & Jarvik, 1980). This conditioned response (CR) is long-lasting and can occur despite years of abstinence after drug use (O’Brien et al., 1992). However, little is known regarding the mechanisms by which the conditioned stimulus (CS) may loose its ability to elicit this CR; this process is called extinction. It has been suggested that the previously learned drug-environment association and its extinction are two distinct learning processes involving different molecular mechanisms (Bouton, 1988; Bouton, 2000; Bouton, 2002; Quirk & Mueller, 2008).

Learning and memory are primary components in the development of context-associated cues (Everitt & Wolf, 2002; Koob et al., 1998; Parker et al., 2006), likewise, they are key to extinction. The hippocampus plays a crucial role in associative memory networks and in the learning of relational information between environmental stimuli (Morris, 2003). Indeed, studies have implicated a role for the hippocampus in morphine-dependent conditioned behavior (Corrigall & Linseman, 1988; Ferbinteanu & McDonald, 2001).

Addictive processes involve a key element of neuronal plasticity (Nestler & Aghajanian, 1997; Robbins & Everitt, 1999). Neuronal plasticity is an enduring change in synaptic efficacy that is thought to underlie the capacity for learning and memory. Indeed, the neuroadaptations in hippocampal function that develop with repeated exposure to opiates may play a role in the modulation of opiate-associated cues (Frohardt et al., 2000; Maren, 2001; Wilson et al., 1995). Though not yet well characterized, glutamatergic systems are thought to be involved in this opiate-induced neuronal plasticity (Trujillo, 2000). In this regard, it has been recently suggested that the AMPA glutamate receptor may represent a key player in the regulation of the molecular mechanisms underlying reactivity to opiate-associated cues (Harris et al., 2004; Moron et al., 2007; Trujillo, 2000; Zhong et al., 2006). Indeed, it has been described that morphine administration alters levels of the GluR1 and GluR2 subunits of AMPA receptors in the hippocampus (Zhong et al., 2006). Therefore, we hypothesize that modulation of AMPA receptors may underlie synaptic plasticity in response to opiate-dependent behaviors (see rev. by (Malenka, 2003).

To test this hypothesis, the expression and synaptic localization of GluR1 and GluR2 subunits of AMPA receptors in the hippocampus were analyzed in rats showing a conditioned response to an opiate-paired environment as well as in animals in which this conditioned behavior was extinguished. To this end we employed the conditioned place preference (CPP) paradigm in combination with an extinction training protocol. Subcellular fractionation was performed to examine the expression and synaptic localization of AMPA receptor subunits in response to these morphine-associated behaviors. We found that extinction of morphine-CPP increases phosphorylation of the GluR1 subunit at the synapse and that this effect may be independent of the molecular pharmacology of the drug itself or drug withdrawal. Thus, these findings provide new evidence for the key role of the GluR1 subunit in the extinction of opiate-induced conditioned responses.

Materials and MethodsAnimals and drugA total of 50 male Sprague-Dawley rats (Harlan Sprague–Dawley, Inc., Houston, TX, USA) were used. Animals weighted 175–199 g at the beginning of the experiments and were housed 2 per cage with food and water ad libitum. Rats were allowed to acclimate for 10 days in a colony room at a constant temperature (21–23 °C) and humidity (45–50%) on a 12 h light–dark cycle (light 07:00–19:00 h). They were handled daily during the first week after arrival. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Texas Medical Branch. Morphine sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was diluted in sterile saline (0.9% NaCl) and administered subcutaneously (s.c.). All vehicle injections consisted of sterile saline (0.9% NaCl). One of the main compartments was black with flooring consisting of 0.4 cm stainless steel rods spaced 1.5 cm apart. The other main compartment was white with steel mesh floor spaced 1.2 cm apart (0.1 cm diameter). The center zone was gray with a sheet-metal floor. The system was automated (Med Associates, Inc., Georgia, VT) and used 12 infrared photocell beams in the two main compartments and 6 in the grey one (spaced 5 cm apart and 3 cm above the chamber floor) to allow assessment of side preference. The signal was then processed by the software to extract the “time spent” in seconds in each compartment of the apparatus. Chambers were illuminated with overhead fluorescent lights and lighting was adjusted to 7 lux in each conditioning environment and 30 lux in the grey chamber.Place preference paradigmFigure 1A shows the experimental design for the CPP paradigm. Briefly, animals (N=32) received a single preconditioning test (day 0) in which each rat was placed in the center box, and allowed to freely roam the entire CPP apparatus for 15 min in order to assess the unconditioned chamber preference. Absence of a strong preference or aversion for one of the compartments was verified. The next day, rats were assigned to receive saline or morphine paired with one of the two conditioning environments in a counterbalanced manner (unbiased procedure, (Bardo et al., 1995; Cunningham et al., 2003; Cunningham et al., 2006), such that half of the rats were conditioned in their preferred-chamber, and the other half were conditioned in their non-preferred chamber. During conditioning (days 1, 2, 3 and 4), twice-daily sessions were conducted, with animals receiving a 30 min conditioning session by confining them in one side of the CPP chamber in the morning and a second 30 min conditioning session by confining them in the opposite side of the CPP chamber 6 h later. Rats were divided into 2 treatment groups (n=16/group). The morphine-CPP group received morphine (10 mg/kg, s.c.) in one side of the CPP chamber (morphine-paired) and saline in the other side (saline-paired) of the chamber. The saline-CPP group received saline in both sides of the CPP chamber. On the expression test day (day 5), each animal was placed into the central compartment with free access to all three compartments; their activities were monitored over a period of 15 min. A CPP is defined as an increase in the time spent in the chamber formerly paired with morphine (calculated as time spent in the morphine-paired compartment minus time spent in the saline-paired compartment) as compared to saline-conditioned animals (see (Mueller et al., 2002; Mueller et al., 2008)). For subsequent biochemical analyses (see section below), half of the animals were sacrificed immediately following the expression test and their hippocampi were dissected and stored at −80°C. The extinction of morphine-CPP was performed with the remaining animals (N=16) using an injection-free paradigm (Biala & Budzynska, 2006; Shoblock et al., 2005). Following the initial CPP test, rats were given 15 min tests once a day until the extinction criteria was reached (no injections were given during this extinction period). Extinction criteria was reached when rats spent less than 55% of the total time in the morphine-paired chamber during 2 consecutive days. When a rat reached the criteria it was immediately sacrificed along with a randomly chosen saline control rat. Upon sacrifice, rats hippocampi were dissected and stored at −80°C for subsequent biochemical analyses (see section below). The homogenate was brought to a final concentration of 1.25 M sucrose by adding 2 M sucrose and 0.1 mM CaCl2. It has been reported that these antibodies do not show cross-reactivity with other AMPA receptor subunits (Gerges et al., 2004; King et al., 2006; Osten et al., 1998; Vissavajjhala et al., 1996). After incubating with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibody membranes were scanned directly by the Odyssey Infrared Fluorescent Imaging System (Li-Cor Biosciences, Lincoln, NE). Blots were reprobed with an anti-actin antibody (1:10,000, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) to ensure equal loading and transfer. Blots were also blotted with antibody to tubulin. Band densities were analyzed using Odyssey Software (Li-Cor Biosciences, Lincoln, NE). Quantification was performed by measuring the intensity of the band with protein specific antibodies and comparing it to that of actin since quantitative analysis did not show any changes in actin levels across conditions. In contrast, tubulin levels were altered upon morphine administration and therefore it was excluded as a housekeeper marker. Additionally, validation of the subcellular fractionation protocol was performed using antibodies to PSD95 (Upstake, Lake Placid, CA), CaMKII alpha subunit (Upstake, Lake Placid, CA), and syntaxin 1 (Chemicon, Temecula, CA).Data analysisMorphine-CPP values are reported as mean time ± SEM difference in time (sec) spent in the morphine-paired chamber versus saline-paired chamber for the morphine treated animals and time spent in the black chamber versus the white chamber for the saline group. During preconditioning, the individual baseline preference for each of the compartments was analyzed using a two-tailed paired Student’s t-test. The CPP level was evaluated during the expression test using a two way ANOVA, followed by a two-tailed paired Student’s t-test comparing the drug-paired chamber versus the saline-paired chambers for the morphine-CPP, and the black versus the white chambers for the saline-CPP. For the immunoblotting studies, measurement of pGluR1, GluR1 and GluR2 levels were normalized to actin levels in the lane, to control for variation in loading and transfer. Values were normalized to saline control values. Differences between groups were determined using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Significance was set at p < 0.05.

Subcellular fractionation and PSD isolation

Subcellular fractionation was performed essentially as described previously (Moron et al., 2007). Briefly, hippocampi (with approximately same wet weight) from individual animals were homogenized in 1.5 ml of 0.32 M sucrose, 0.1 mM CaCl2 containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). The homogenate was brought to a final concentration of 1.25 M sucrose by adding 2 M sucrose and 0.1 mM CaCl2. The homogenate was then placed in an ultracentrifuge tube and overlaid with 1 M sucrose and subjected to centrifugation at 100,000g for 3 h at 4°C. The synaptosomal fraction was collected at the 1.25 M/1 M sucrose interface. To obtain the synaptic junctions, the synaptosomal fraction was diluted with 20 mM Tris-Cl, pH 6, 0.1 mM CaCl2, containing 1% Triton X-100 (TX-100) and mixed for 20 min at 4 °C, and centrifuged at 40,000g for 20 min at 4 °C. The pellet containing the isolated synaptic junctions was collected. To separate presynaptic proteins from the PSD, the pellet was resuspended in 20 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8, 1% TX-100, 0.1 mM CaCl2. The mixture was again mixed for 20 min at 4 °C, and centrifuged at 40,000g for 20 min at 4 °C. The insoluble pellet containing the PSD fraction was collected and stored at −80 °C until use. The supernatant containing the presynaptic fraction (PAZ) was collected to exclude presence of AMPA receptors at the presynapse.

ImmunoblottingFor immunoblotting, 10 μg of protein from each synaptic fraction obtained from individual animals was resolved in 7.5% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Scheicher & Schuell, Bioscience, Keene, NH) by electroblotting. Membranes were incubated with selective antibodies to AMPA receptors subunits: anti-GluR1 C-terminal (1:1000; Chemicon, Temecula, CA), anti-GluR2 N-terminal (1:2000; Chemicon, Temecula,, CA), and anti-pGluR1 at Serine 845 (1:1000; Chemicon, Temecula, CA). It has been reported that these antibodies do not show cross-reactivity with other AMPA receptor subunits (Gerges et al., 2004; King et al., 2006; Osten et al., 1998; Vissavajjhala et al., 1996). After incubating with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibody membranes were scanned directly by the Odyssey Infrared Fluorescent Imaging System (Li-Cor Biosciences, Lincoln, NE). Blots were reprobed with an anti-actin antibody (1:10,000, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) to ensure equal loading and transfer. Blots were also blotted with antibody to tubulin. Band densities were analyzed using Odyssey Software (Li-Cor Biosciences, Lincoln, NE). Quantification was performed by measuring the intensity of the band with protein specific antibodies and comparing it to that of actin since quantitative analysis did not show any changes in actin levels across conditions. In contrast, tubulin levels were altered upon morphine administration and therefore it was excluded as a housekeeper marker.
