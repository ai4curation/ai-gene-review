The HK97-fold is an ancient, conserved protein structure that assembles protein
shells, forming the capsids of Duplodnaviria viruses and prokaryotic biochemical
compartments called encapsulsins. It has been proposed that encapsulins evolved
from HK97-fold viruses. But the higher complexity of viral capsids represents a
biophysical paradox in the evolution of HK97-fold protein shells. To resolve
this problem, we hypothesize the existence of simpler HK97-fold viral capsids
associated with smaller genomes than those of known HK97-fold viruses. Such
small viral genomes are searched in environmental metagenomes across public
databases, including IMG/M and MGnify. Using hidden Markov models and protein
language models, we identify and characterize small viral genomes encoding
HK97-fold proteins that exhibit molecular similarities to encapsulins yet lack
portal and tail genes. The phylogenetic analyses of HK97-fold proteins reveal
independent mixed groups of viruses and encapsulins, and structural analyses
identify similarities between encapsulins and procapsids, the immature state of
viral capsids. The observations support a bidirectional evolution between
encapsulins and HK97-fold viruses enabled by the viral procapsid state. Using
biophysical and genomic arguments, we introduce an evolutionary model of
HK97-fold protein shells, predicting that they emerged as lower-symmetry
biochemical compartments and that HK97-fold viral capsids may retain biochemical
functions.

Contemporary biological compartments can be interpreted as imprints or milestones in the evolution of life and are invaluable for extracting clues on the origin of life13. HK97-fold proteins are a highly diverse group of proteins in sequence space22, and they are characterized by displaying the conserved HK97-fold or Johnson fold23,24 (Fig. 1A). The subset of HK97-fold proteins with genomic functions forms the capsids protecting the genome of viruses in the Duplodnaviria realm15,25. This includes tailed prokaryotic viruses that infect Bacteria and Archaea17,19,26,27, related prokaryotic mobile elements, like phage inducible chromosomal islands (PICIs) and gene transfer agents (GTAs)28–30, and non-tailed eukaryotic viruses infecting plankton (mirusviruses)31 and animals (herpesviruses)32,33. The other subset of HK97-fold proteins forms encapsulins in Bacteria and Archaea, cellular compartments that carry metabolic functions including iron storage34,35, sulfur metabolism36, and oxidative stress response18,37. These functions have been proposed as ancient38–41.

Initial sequence and structural analyses of HK97-fold proteins were either inconclusive about their origin or favored viral HK97-fold proteins as ancestral, given their presence across more lineages15. A more recent and exhaustive phylogenetic and structural evolutionary study of HK97-fold proteins, using an expanded set of encapsulins, indicated that encapsulins are likely derived from HK97-fold viruses42. However, the evolutionary model of encapsulins emerging from viruses does not provide a rationale for how HK97-fold shells evolved metabolic functions from shells that carried genomic functions, or for how known HK97-fold viruses, which form far more complex structures than encapsulins43, would have emerged before encapsulins. Prior evolutionary models noted but did not explicitly incorporate the differences in structural complexity between viral capsids and encapsulins, which typically form small T = 1 or T = 3 shells15,42.

The HK97-fold proteins in encapsulins co-assemble around several copies of a protein called cargo, encoded in the same operon and responsible for catalyzing the biochemical reactions37,44. Most encapsulins form protein shells built from 60 HK97-fold proteins distributed in 12 pentamers (oligomers of five proteins) as the vertices of the simplest icosahedral architecture, T = 1, several form T = 3, and a few T = 4 icosahedral architectures combining pentamers at the vertices and hexamers on the faces42,44,45 (Fig. 1B). For some encapsulins, the presence of cargo proteins promotes the assembly of a larger icosahedral T-number shell than without them46. T is the triangulation number or T-number, which indicates the number of major capsid proteins (MCPs) in the asymmetric unit and indexes icosahedral capsid size and geometry, with larger T corresponding to larger HK97-fold shells47,48 (Fig. 1B).

Unlike encapsulin assembly, which often involves only the major capsid protein-like and the cargo protein, the maturation of HK97-fold viruses is more complex. In addition to HK97-fold proteins, it involves scaffold proteins (sometimes fused with the HK97-fold), portal proteins to facilitate DNA translocation, a terminal complex to ensure packaging, and often minor proteins that reinforce the structure29,49–53.

The HK97-fold proteins, guided by the scaffold proteins, assemble around a portal complex that is responsible for the translocation of the double-stranded (ds) DNA genome within the capsid54–57. This forms an empty shell called a procapsid, which, as the double-stranded (ds) DNA is packaged through the portal, expands, releases the scaffold proteins, and becomes more faceted24,58–62. Bacterial and archaeal viruses, along with prokaryotic mobile elements that build capsids with HK97-fold proteins, append to the portal a protein tail apparatus that enables the delivery of dsDNA into the host63–65. HK97-fold viral genomes display a wide range of lengths due to the versatility of HK97-fold proteins forming capsid architectures of different sizes17,26,43,66. Most Duplodnaviria viruses form primarily T = 7 structures followed by T = 13 and T = 16 structures17,32,43,67–69 (Fig. 1B). The largest HK97-fold viral capsid reconstructed corresponds to Lysinbacillus Phage G, forming a T = 52 architecture, containing more than 3,000 major capsid proteins and packaging a dsDNA genome that is 626 kbp (kilo base pairs) long26,70. The smallest reconstructions correspond to isometric T = 3 capsid variants of the Bacillus phage ϕ2971, which packages its genome in elongated T = 3, Q = 5 capsids72. Quasi-spherical T = 4 capsid reconstructions have been obtained for Streptococci C1 phage73, the satellite Escherichia phage P474, and the Staphylococcus aureus pathogenic island 1 or SaPI129. The smallest tailed phage genomes are 11.6 kbp long from the non-satellite Salmonella phage astrithr75 and the satellite phage P476. The smallest HK97-fold capsid characterized in association with genetic material packaging functions corresponds to the Rhodobacter capsulatus gene transfer agent (RcGTA), which is encoded in a 15 kbp genomic region but instead packages 4 to 4.5 dsDNA fragments derived from the host genome. This RcGTA assembles a shortened, oblate T = 3-like head28. To our knowledge, there is no documented evidence of Duplodnaviria viruses or HK97-fold gene transfer agents forming T = 1 capsids (Fig. 1B)43.

The greater structural complexity of HK97-fold viral capsids compared with encapsulins makes it tempting, under a parsimony argument, to consider the possibility of viruses evolving from encapsulins43, in apparent tension with the “viruses-to-encapsulins” scenario42. We hypothesize that if encapsulins emerged from viruses, smaller HK97-fold viruses may still be undiscovered. Regardless, finding such viruses or confirming their absence would help illuminate ancestral HK97-fold compartments. We built our approach on three key elements. First, genomes associated with HK97-fold viruses are prevalent when sequencing environmental samples and display a much higher diversity than the genomes from isolated Duplodnaviria viruses20,21,77–79. Second, the genome length of HK97-fold viruses follows a well-established allometric relationship with their capsid size and architecture22,43,80. Third, HK97-fold viruses can encode direct terminal repeats (DTRs) in their genomes, providing a bioinformatic proxy for identifying complete genomes among uncultured viruses81–83. A previous study combined these elements to search for simple HK97-fold viral genomes in human gut metagenomes43. It found 14 candidates encoding HK97-fold proteins in genomes below the isolated threshold of 11.6 kbp, which we defined here as the twilight zone of HK97-fold viral genomes. The genomes ranged from 6.9 kbp to 10.0 kbp, with no candidates in the range of 1 kbp to 5 kbp predicted to be compatible with T = 1 architectures.

Here, we expanded the analysis to include samples across ecosystems (Fig. 2A, B and S1), refined the HK97-fold detection strategies, introduced a new network-based method to analyze the co-occurrence of HK97-fold sequence profiles and generated large-scale structural and sequence-based phylogenetic analyses. Our investigation identified 523 genomes with HK97-fold proteins below the genome length associated with the shortest HK97-fold-encoding genome of a phage isolate. Among these small genomes, 93 encoded HK97-fold proteins with encapsulin hidden Markov protein profiles. The newly identified HK97-fold proteins helped to reveal at least three transition events between encapsulins and HK97-fold viruses, suggesting bidirectional evolution. Further molecular and structural comparisons led us to propose that viral procapsids are the evolutionary nexus of genomic and biochemical HK97-fold compartments, and we discuss that modern procapsids could carry both genomic and biochemical functions. ResultsKey terminologyThe following terms are summarized here to facilitate the interpretation of the results. Twilight zone: metagenomically assembled genomes with lengths < 11.6 kbp; the threshold is defined by the smallest genome from isolated HK97-fold viruses. Due to the biology of HK97-fold viruses and the prevalence of dsDNA data from standard sequencing methods, the investigated contigs were initially interpreted as dsDNA in origin, using kilobase pairs (kbp) units. Fringe zone: genomes with lengths between 11.6 kbp and 20 kbp, including both isolated and metagenomically assembled genomes; this region was used as a control. Micro genomes: twilight-zone genomes < 5 kbp. Mini genomes: twilight-zone genomes 5–11.6 kbp. Major capsid protein (MCP): the HK97-fold protein that builds the capsid shell in Duplodnaviria; in the context of viruses, we use MCP and HK97-fold protein equivalently. Structural domains: canonical structural regions of the HK97-fold defined from its three-dimensional configuration (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig.  S2). Key terminologyThe following terms are summarized here to facilitate the interpretation of the results. Twilight zone: metagenomically assembled genomes with lengths < 11.6 kbp; the threshold is defined by the smallest genome from isolated HK97-fold viruses. Due to the biology of HK97-fold viruses and the prevalence of dsDNA data from standard sequencing methods, the investigated contigs were initially interpreted as dsDNA in origin, using kilobase pairs (kbp) units. Fringe zone: genomes with lengths between 11.6 kbp and 20 kbp, including both isolated and metagenomically assembled genomes; this region was used as a control. Micro genomes: twilight-zone genomes < 5 kbp. Mini genomes: twilight-zone genomes 5–11.6 kbp. Major capsid protein (MCP): the HK97-fold protein that builds the capsid shell in Duplodnaviria; in the context of viruses, we use MCP and HK97-fold protein equivalently. Structural domains: canonical structural regions of the HK97-fold defined from its three-dimensional configuration (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig.  S2). Sequence domains: domains identified in protein sequences by matches to hidden Markov model (HMM) profiles in the Pfam database (Supplementary Fig. S2); encapsulin domains are Pfam domains built from encapsulin shell proteins and are used here as encapsulin-like sequence signatures within HK97-fold proteins.

The HK97-fold proteins from the NCBI Reference Sequence Database (RefSeq) group displayed the most extensive coverage, with a median of 95% (Fig. 3B). This was expected because the Phage-coat Pfam clan has been historically built from proteins of isolated viruses in RefSeq, whereas HK97-fold proteins from uncultured viruses are more divergent and therefore less likely to be fully captured by existing Pfam profiles. By contrast, the HK97-fold proteins that showed the greatest enrichment in encapsulin domains were from twilight-zone genomes, in agreement with our working hypothesis (Fig. 3C). Encapsulin domain hits concentrated in MCPs from mini genomes (5 kbp to 11.6 kbp). However, in micro genomes (<5 kbp), none of the 45 identified MCPs displayed encapsulin domains.

Nearly 19% of MCPs in mini genomes (93 out of 491) carried encapsulin domains, and in more than half of these cases (57 out of 93), encapsulin domains were their only Phage-coat profiles. These MCPs are encoded on contigs that pass CheckV viral filters, carry Duplodnaviria hallmark genes, and lack the operon context typical of encapsulins. We therefore treated them as viral MCPs with encapsulin-like domains, although some metagenomic elements may represent misclassified systems. The encapsulin-domain enrichment of MCPs from mini genomes was significantly higher than in the environmental control group (11.6 kbp to 20 kbp), where only 2.5% (20 out of 798) of the MCPs contained encapsulin domains, although 4 displayed an encapsulin domain fraction of 1 (Fig. 3C). In the RefSeq group, only 1.9% (17 out of 910) MCPs contained a trace of encapsulin domains. Their encapsulin-enriched MCPs, defined as those displaying one or more encapsulin domains, displayed encapsulin fractions below 0.75, and were found in genomes of lengths around ~ 50 kbp and ~ 100 kbp (Fig. 3D), except for one MCP with encapsulin fraction 1 from the deep-sea Asgard archaea virus VerdaV2 (NC_074645.1) with a genome length of 19.5 kbp belonging to the “fringe zone” (11.6–20 kbp)85. The RefSeq genomes encoded hallmark HK97-fold MCPs, portal proteins, and tail proteins. No HK97-fold proteins from the viral mobile elements (GTAs or PICIs) were found to contain encapsulin domains (Fig. 3C).

The prevalence of encapsulin domains across viral MCPs decreased with the viral genome length (Fig. 3D). The encapsulin-enriched twilight zone mini genomes (5 − 11.6 kbp) occupied the genome-length region between the projected genome capacities of the two largest encapsulin compartments, T = 3 and T = 4, which had projected genome capacities of ~ 5 kbp and ~ 10 kbp, respectively. The few encapsulin-enriched MCPs in the environmental control group had genome lengths ranging from 13 kbp to 19 kbp, which exceeded the maximum projected packaging capacity of known encapsulins. The even smaller fraction of viral genomes from RefSeq containing MCPs with encapsulin domains ranged from 19 kbp to 58 kbp, except for one virus with a 103 kbp genome (Fig. 3D).

The analysis revealed that viral procapsids are more structurally similar to encapsulins than to the mature capsid state (Fig. 6A). The comparison included the encapsulin from Bacillus thermotolerans (PDB 6NJ8)34 and the procapsid and capsid structures assembled by Staphylococcus aureus Pathogenicity Islands (PDB 7RWZ and 8VD4)29,88. The encapsulin and viral HK97-fold protein sequences were poorly conserved, displaying a 14.4% identity over 473 aligned residues (23.9% similarity), yet their quaternary structures (protein shells) are strikingly similar. Both entities assemble into a quasi-spherical structure, exhibiting comparable inner diameters: ~ 322 Å for the encapsulin and ~ 330 Å for the procapsid. Their shells enclose a comparable volume (~ 2.15·10⁴ nm³) with a projected capacity of ~10.75 kbp. This capacity is 60% smaller than the internal volume (~ 3.51·10⁴ nm³) and packaging capacity (~ 15.2 kbp) observed in the mature capsid (Fig. 6A). The expansion in volume from the procapsid to the mature capsid stage is common among Duplodnaviria viruses (Supplementary Fig. S7) and facilitates scaffold protein release26,61.

Since encapsulins contain cargo proteins and procapsids contain scaffold proteins, cargo and scaffold proteins with available structures were compared. This included encapsulin cargos from Bacillus thermotolerans and Myxococcus xanthus, and scaffold proteins from phage P22 and phage Φ29. Global pairwise alignments were computed with EMBOSS Needleman–Wunsch89, revealing low overall sequence identity (4.4–18.0%) and similarity (6.9–30.9%), with the highest similarity observed for the M. xanthus cargo versus the Φ29 scaffold (Fig. 6A). However, common structural traits were identified (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. S8). Both proteins are alpha-rich and contain an alpha-helical hairpin motif, although their orientation inside the capsid may differ (Fig. 6A). These results indicate structural parallels between cargo and scaffold proteins.

The DNA packaging possibilities in portal-less and non-tailed HK97-fold twilight viruses

The group of encapsulin-enriched mini genomes reported here lacked a recognizable portal, which is essential for the dsDNA genome packaging in all known Duplodnaviria viruses108 (Fig. 4). They also lacked the main proteins that assemble the tail apparatus, which is essential for translocating the dsDNA genome in prokaryotic Duplodnaviria and for associated HK97-fold mobile elements28,108. The lack of these genes raises the question of how these entities could replicate, pack, and deliver a dsDNA genome. One hypothesis is that these entities are satellites of other viral entities that carry the more common HK97-fold virus machinery.

The alternative hypothesis is that these twilight zone viruses deviate from the canonical replication and packaging strategy of Duplodnaviria. Several genomes encode proteins annotated as rolling-circle replication initiators (Rep/RepA-like). The proteins belong to the broader HUH endonuclease superfamily that includes plasmid and ssDNA viral replicases93,109, and RepA proteins participate in the plasmid-like phase of HK97-fold phages P4 and N15110. RepA proteins share the canonical HUH endonuclease mechanism of site‑specific nicking and subsequent strand transfer/ligation on ssDNA that can, in principle, couple rolling-circle replication to directed DNA translocation. In small ssDNA viruses, genome packaging occurs through 3-fold or 5-fold pores in the capsid, without a dedicated portal–tail complex111–114. The existence of small ssDNA viruses with capsids built from double jelly-roll proteins in lineages traditionally associated with dsDNA genomes113–116, together with chimeric viruses that combine circular ssDNA genomes with capsid proteins most similar to those of ssRNA viruses117, underscores that genome type and capsid fold can be decoupled. In this broader context, the co-occurrence of HK97-fold major capsid proteins and Rep/RepA-like initiators in portal-less, tail-less mini genomes supports the possibility that some of these entities might package circular ssDNA via a Rep-mediated mechanism. Thus, it is mechanically possible that some twilight-zone viruses could represent HK97-fold viruses with a ssDNA lifestyle. This hypothesis remains untested and will require experimental confirmation.

Either the need for helper viral elements or an alternative packaging and infection strategy could explain the lack of isolated twilight zone HK97-fold viruses described here. Among isolated HK97-fold viruses, satellite lifestyles seem more common in those with relatively short genomes (12 kbp to 20 kbp) and HK97-type mobile elements (11 kbp to 18 kbp)118–121. The presence of tail genes in a small fraction of twilight zone viruses may be a remnant of a more canonical tailed phage structure and replication, possibly in combination with helper viruses. Nonetheless, it is important to highlight that the characteristic small ssDNA viruses (< 6 kb) in the Microviridae family, which form tailless viruses, can generate a dynamic tail during infection122,123. Therefore, the few tail genes observed in the HK97-fold twilight viruses could operate similarly to dynamic tails instead of the canonical Duplodnaviria tails.

Genome annotation

The open reading frames (Data File S5) were annotated in three complementary steps (Fig. 2A). First, high-level functional categories were predicted using the viral protein families-protein language model, VPF-PLM87. The VPF-PLM had been trained on the prokaryotic virus remote homologous groups (PHROGs) database145. Functions were assigned when obtaining likelihood scores greater than 60%; the highest-ranked function was selected. The method achieves a weighted F1-score (harmonic mean of the precision and recall) of 85% at this threshold for assigning correct high-level functional categories to viral proteins validated on ocean virome sequences (see annotations in Supplementary Data File S6).

Second, the VIBRANT (Virus Identification By iteRative aNnoTation) tool, which combines neural networks and hidden Markov models (HMMs)146, was applied to predict specific functions for each open reading frame. Version 1.2.1 of VIBRANT was executed with default parameters, which included curated HMM profiles from three major databases: KEGG (March 2019 release), Pfam (v32), and Virus Orthologous Groups (VOG; release 94) (see annotations in Data File S7).

Third, all predicted open reading frames, regardless of prior functional assignments, were scanned with hmmscan against the Pfam database147, clan CL0373 (“Phage-coat”), which contains curated HMM profiles for domains characteristic of the HK97 fold. The clan comprised 17 HMM profiles, including viral capsid protein domains (14) and encapsulin shell domains (3). An additional viral capsid protein HMM profile (41798) from the efam database148 was included, as it represents a recently proposed HK97-fold MCP lineage identified using protein language models87. Hits were accepted at a cutoff of E-value < 10⁻³. The HMM profile list and annotations are provided in Data File S8.

Finally, HHpred149 was used for targeted manual curation and contextual annotation of selected genomes of interest. We examined 639 open reading frames from 60 genomes, focusing primarily on proteins that remained unannotated by the automated methods or on genes from genomes showcased in figures. These ORFs largely corresponded to accessory or hypothetical proteins. HHpred searches were run against Pfam-A (v37), NCBI Conserved Domains (v3.18), and PHROGs (v4) with default server parameters and a MAC realignment threshold of 0.3. The Protein Data Bank (PDB) library was included to enhance structural evolutionary insights. Hits below 20% probability were discarded. For each open reading frame, the top hit was selected unless a longer hit of comparable significance was present. Functions were labeled according to statistical confidence: very high (> 99%, ***), high (95–99%, **), moderate (70–95%, *), and low (50–70%,?). Annotations with a probability below 50% were considered hypothetical. Manual annotations took precedence over automated assignments when they conflicted. The HHpred annotations are provided in Supplementary Data File S9.

Sequence-based phylogeny

To provide an independent validation of the evolutionary patterns identified through structural comparisons, we conducted a standard amino-acid sequence phylogenetic analysis using the same set of HK97-fold proteins included in the structural tree. Protein sequences were aligned using MAFFT (v7.520) with the L-INS-i algorithm, which is suitable for aligning globally similar proteins that may harbor modular rearrangements, such as domain insertions or deletions. MAFFT internally applied automated gap penalties and iterative refinement procedures. The alignment was trimmed using TrimAl (v1.4.rev15) with the “gappyout” parameter to remove poorly aligned positions while preserving informative regions. Maximum-likelihood inference was performed in IQ-TREE (v2.3.6) using ModelFinder for substitution model selection under the Bayesian Information Criterion. Branch support was calculated using ultrafast bootstrap approximation with 1000 replicates. Because no clear external outgroup could be defined for the HK97-fold dataset, the maximum-likelihood tree was rooted using midpoint rooting. The resulting tree is available in Supplementary Fig. S4 and Supplementary Data File S16.
