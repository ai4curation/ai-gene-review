Mutations in the AARS2 gene are linked to infantile cardiomyopathy; however, the
underlying molecular mechanism remains unknown. Here we report that PCBP1, a
poly(rC) binding protein, interacts with the AARS2 transcript to mediate its
alternative splicing. Cardiomyocyte-specific deletion of Pcbp1 in mice impairs
normal splicing and causes premature termination of Aars2, leading to defects in
heart development and postnatal lethality. Similarly, mice with a deletion in
Aars2 that mimics a disease-causing splicing lesion display heart developmental
abnormalities, reminiscent of those in patients with infantile mitochondrial
cardiomyopathy. Mechanistically, loss of Pcbp1 or Aars2 in the heart reduces
oxidative phosphorylation, a hallmark of patients with AARS2 mutations. This
reduction in mitochondrial-encoded proteome activates mitonuclear communication
and the unfolded protein response pathway, thereby inducing a compensatory
nuclear-encoded mitochondrial gene program. Our findings provide insights into
the PCBP1-AARS2 regulatory axis in mitochondrial cardiomyopathy.

Congenital heart disease (CHD) remains the most common birth defect and the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in children worldwide. Although the genetics of many simple Mendelian forms of CHD have been identified, the molecular basis for most other types remains unknown. This may be due to disorders caused by multiple genes and/or posttranscriptional/posttranslational variations. RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) are key posttranscriptional regulators of RNA metabolism, controlling alternative splicing, RNA stability and translation. Although RBPs have been implicated in heart development and maturation1–3, the roles of many RBPs remain unknown.

After posttranscriptional modification, a key step in protein synthesis is the ‘charging’, or aminoacylation, of transfer RNA (tRNA) by tRNA synthetase. This process involves cytosolic and mitochondrial tRNA synthetases in separate cellular compartments, although all tRNA synthetase genes are encoded in the nucleus. Disruption of each mitochondrial tRNA synthetase gene is linked to an autosomal recessive disorder, all of which present with tissue-specific and cell-specific phenotypes despite widespread expression4. The recessively inherited AARS2 (alanyl-tRNA synthetase 2) mutation was first described in patients with infantile cardiomyopathy associated with defects in oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)5 (COXPD8, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM): 614096). However, the knock-in mice harboring Aars2 editing domain mutations cause early embryonic lethality before heart formation6, making it challenging to study AARS2 in the context of heart development in vivo.

PCBP1 belongs to the poly(rC)-binding protein (PCBP) family of RBPs; there is only one member in invertebrates but four in mammals (PCBP1, PCBP2, PCBP3 and PCBP4)7. The PCBP family plays important roles in posttranscriptional RNA processing, alternative splicing and translation regulation by interacting with pyrimidine-rich motifs in targeted RNAs8–10; they also have unconventional functions in transcription9 and iron trafficking through ferritin11–13. Germline deletion of either Pcbp1 or Pcbp2 in mice is embryonically lethal14, suggesting that they are independently essential during development. We previously generated a hypomorphic Pcbp1 mutant mouse line that exhibits abnormalities in skeletal muscle growth due to defects in the proliferation and differentiation of skeletal muscle myoblasts and satellite cells, through modulation of the microRNA processing pathway15. However, the function of PCBP1 in cardiomyocytes, especially during cardiac development, is unexplored.

In the present study, we sought to determine the functional and mechanistic roles of PCBP1 and AARS2 during heart development. Using genetic and biochemical approaches, we discovered an essential role for PCBP1 in heart development by regulating the alternative splicing of Aars2, which is required for its proper function. Genetic deletion of either Pcbp1 or Aars2 in cardiomyocytes leads to a non-compaction cardiomyopathy and a significant reduction in the proteins that constitute the OXPHOS complexes, reminiscent of COXPD8 patients with infantile mitochondrial cardiomyopathy. Our findings define a previously unrecognized function for PCBP1 in regulating Aars2 alternative splicing in cardiomyocytes during development, offering insights into the RBP-regulated mechanisms underlying CHD.

Because mitochondrial translation must be coordinated for proper assembly of the OXPHOS system46, the overall reduction in mitochondrial-encoded proteins observed in Aars2-cKOMyh6 hearts aligns with the OXPHOS defects seen in both Aars2-cKOCtnt and Pcbp1-cKOCtnt embryos. By intersecting genes significantly upregulated 4 hours after MycERT2 activation with MYC ChIP−seq peaks near promoters, we identified MYC-dependent transcriptional targets. This yielded 425 genes that were both upregulated in MycERT2 hearts and contained MYC ChIP−seq binding sites, termed the ‘Myc Activation Signature in Heart’ (Fig. 7j). GSEA of the P14 Aars2-cKOMyh6 transcriptome with this signature showed significant enrichment (Fig. 7k). Additional evidence of MYC activation is provided by the activated MYC pathway identified from the PPI network of P14 Aars2-cKOMyh6 hearts (Extended Data Fig. 10h), and the substantial enrichment of an independent human MYC signature, ‘HALLMARK_MYC_TARGETS_V1’, from the Molecular Signatures Database, observed in both P14 and P21 transcriptomes (Extended Data Fig. 10i−j).Because MYC activation has been reported to promote ribosome biogenesis48,49, we examined our data using the ribosome biogenesis gene signature and observed a robust enrichment of this gene set (Extended Data Fig. 10k). Interestingly, subsets of ribosomal biogenesis genes, such as Rrp12, Nhp2 and Fbl, are also MYC targets with direct MYC ChIP binding at their promoters (Fig. 7k, genes in magenta, and Fig. 7l). Ribosomal biogenesis primarily occurs at the nucleolus, and its activation leads to enlargement of the nucleolar structure50. Notably, MYC activity has been implicated in regulating nucleolar and ribosome biogenesis51,52. We observed a striking nucleolar enlargement in cardiomyocytes deficient in Aars2 (Fig. 7m,n), supporting that activated Myc transcription increased ribosomal biogenesis in the heart. Together, these data suggest that MYC is a key mediator of mitonuclear communication, promoting mitochondrial protein translation in the Aars2 mutant heart.

DiscussionMutations of the Aars2 gene, including those that affect its splicing, are linked to human congenital mitochondrial cardiomyopathy5,53. In the present study, we generated mouse models to demonstrate that cardiomyocyte-specific Aars2 mutations cause cardiac defects in embryonic and postnatal hearts, mimicking those observed in patients. Our studies implicate PCBP1, an RBP that directly interacts with Aars2 RNA to regulate its splicing, as a crucial molecule required for proper AARS2 function; furthermore, genetic deletion of Pcbp1 in cardiomyocytes phenocopies cardiac defects found in Aars2 mutant mice. We found that loss of PCBP1−AARS2 in cardiomyocytes reduces mitochondrial and OXPHOS function. Pcbp1 and Aars2 mutant mice also exhibit an activation of the UPR/ISR pathway and enhanced mitonuclear communication. Therefore, PCBP1 likely acts as a splicing modulator55, with future work aimed at identifying other ribonucleoprotein complexes involved.

AARS2-related infantile cardiomyopathy involves a recurrent pathogenic p.Arg592Trp mutation in the editing domain, found in homozygous or compound heterozygous patients17. Data from modeling the human AARS2 p.Arg592Trp and p.Arg580Trp mutations suggest that these mutations cause a reduction of AARS2 protein and are sufficient to cause a severe defect in mitochondrial protein synthesis. Additional AARS2 damaging mutations have recently been reported in the intronic splice donor site (c.2255+1 G > A) and acceptor site (c.2146-2 A > G) flanking exon-16 of the AARS2 gene56,57, and our experimental data suggest that such mutations cause AARS2 mRNA mis-splicing and loss of function. We speculate that our loss-of-function Aars2 mouse models, which also display mitochondrial translation defects, share a similar pathological mechanism with the human disease.

Our Aars2-cKOCtnt mice are embryonically lethal and exhibit LVNC, as do Pcbp1-cKOCtnt mice. Both display OXPHOS deficiency, indicating that they are in the same regulatory axis, linking AARS2-related infantile cardiomyopathy to exon-16 splicing in patients. Aars2-cKOCtnt has a more severe phenotype, likely due to earlier Aars2 deficiency (Cre recombination), compared to Pcbp1-cKOCtnt, where Pcbp1 deletion and Aars2 mis-splicing take longer and require more steps. Furthermore, there may be additional PCBP1-dependent, AARS2-independent pathways that oppose the damaging effects from Aars2 depletion. Future work should explore branching pathways from the PCBP1−AARS2 axis and test if Aars2 overexpression rescues cardiac defects in Pcbp1-cKOCtnt mice.

From late gestation, more than half of the ATP generated in the heart relies on OXPHOS, and this balance shifts toward further reliance on OXPHOS after birth58, which is essential for postnatal cardiomyocyte development and maturation59. Direct perturbation of OXPHOS in the embryonic heart can lead to severe heart defects and lethality60–62. The results of our genetic approach to delete the Aars2 gene in embryonic and postnatal mouse hearts confirm its function in heart development and cardiomyopathy. We also found that destabilization of C-I and C-IV occurred in Aars2-cKOMyh6 mice before the onset of cardiomyopathy, indicating that the assembly and turnover of C-I and C-IV are more sensitive to mitochondrial translational defects caused by Aars2 deficiency and are likely the primary causes of the mitochondrial catastrophe that follows. Our finding is consistent with the notion that the turnover of unassembled OXPHOS subunits occurs primarily at the protein level via mitochondrial proteases63,64, and the precise mechanism underlying the selective sensitivity warrants further exploration.

We identified MYC as a key transcription factor activated by Aars2 loss of function, promoting mitochondrial and ribosomal biogenesis. Because AARS2 regulates mitochondrial translation, the observed defects and nuclear activation of the UPR/ISR and MYC programs are likely linked as part of retrograde mitonuclear communication in response to cellular stress. Interestingly, MYC can upregulate ATF4 by activating GCN2 via uncharged tRNAs, and ATF4 and MYC co-occupy the promoter of some MYC targets, regulating amino acid and protein synthesis65. It remains to be seen if this transcriptional crosstalk occurred similarly in mitochondrial cardiomyopathy. Our data show that the PCBP1−AARS2 axis induces UPR/ISR, possibly by disrupting mitotranslation, and activates MYC and ATF4. Further studies are needed to identify the downstream targets and pathways regulated by this axis in cardiac development and CHD.

Our findings reveal how the RBP PCBP1 regulates splicing of the mitochondrial tRNA aminoacyl transferase AARS2, which is linked to mitochondrial cardiomyopathy. The PCBP1−AARS2 axis safeguards mitochondrial translation and stabilizes OXPHOS complexes in the heart. Given the lack of effective treatments for OXPHOS deficiency diseases, our study advances understanding of the tissue-specific regulatory mechanisms involved and may identify potential therapeutic targets.

eCLIP−seqeCLIP studies were performed according to the published single-end enhanced CLIP (seCLIP) protocol76 with the following modifications (Eclipse Bioinnovations). Approximately 80 mg of frozen embryonic mouse heart tissue was cryogrinded and ultraviolet crosslinked at 400 mJoules cm−2 with 254-nm radiation. The equivalence of 75 μg of RNA was lysed using 1 ml of eCLIP lysis mix and subjected to two rounds of sonication for 4 minutes with 30 seconds ON/OFF at 75% amplitude. A prevalidated PCBP1 antibody (Abcam, ab168377) was then precoupled to anti-rabbit IgG Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific), added to the lysate and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Before immunoprecipitation, 2% of the sample was taken as the paired input sample, with the remainder magnetically separated and washed with eCLIP high-stringency wash buffers. Immunoprecipitation and input samples were cut from the membrane at the relative band size of 40−75 kDa above that band size. RNA adapter ligation, immunoprecipitation−western blot, reverse transcription, DNA adapter ligation and PCR amplification were performed as previously described19.The eCLIP cDNA adapter contains a sequence of 10 random nucleotides at the 5′ end. This random sequence serves as a unique molecular identifier (UMI) after sequencing primers are ligated to the 3′ end of cDNA molecules77. Therefore, eCLIP reads begin with the UMI, and, in the first step of analysis, UMIs were pruned from read sequences using umi_tools (version 0.5.1)78. UMI sequences were saved by incorporating them into the read names in the FASTQ files to be used in subsequent analysis steps. Next, 3′ adapters were trimmed from reads using Cutadapt (version 2.7)79, and reads shorter than 18 bp were removed. Reads were then mapped to a database of human and mouse repetitive elements, and rRNA sequences were compiled from Dfam80 and GenBank81. All non-repeat mapped reads were mapped to the mouse genome (mm10) using STAR (version 2.6.0c)82. PCR duplicates were removed using umi_tools (version 0.5.1) by using UMI sequences from the read names and mapping positions. Peaks were identified within eCLIP samples using the peak caller CLIPper (https://github.com/YeoLab/clipper)83, using parameters consistent with ENCODE standards. For each peak, immunoprecipitation versus input fold enrichment values were calculated as a ratio of counts of reads overlapping the peak region in the immunoprecipitation and the input samples (read counts in each sample were normalized against the total number of reads in the sample after PCR duplicate removal). A P value was calculated for each peak by Yates’ χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test if the observed or expected read number was lower than 5. Comparison of different sample conditions was evaluated in the same manner as immunoprecipitation versus input enrichment; for each peak called in immunoprecipitation libraries of one sample type, we calculated enrichment and P values relative to normalized counts of reads overlapping these peaks in another sample type. Peaks were annotated using transcript information from GENCODE84 with the following priority hierarchy to define the final annotation of overlapping features: protein-coding transcripts (CDS, UTR and intron), followed by non-coding transcripts (exon and intron). De novo motif discovery for peaks detected was performed using HOMER (version 4.11), and the positionally enriched k-mer analysis (PEKA) in whole gene, 3′ UTR, intron or exon regions was performed using PEKA software (https://github.com/ulelab/peka)85.

Principal component analysis and enrichment analysisWe defined expressed genes as those that have expression in at least half of all samples. Expressed genes were subjected to principal component analysis (PCA); unit variance scaling was applied to rows; and singular value decomposition with imputation was used to calculate principal components. Principal components 1 and 2 were plotted in two-dimensional coordinates. GSEA was performed on expressed genes according to the software manual88. Gene sets with nominal P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25 were considered significant. All expressed genes were log2 transformed and centered, and unsupervised hierarchical clustering and visualization were performed with ClustVis89. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was performed using either the R package clusterProfiler90 with enrichGO and the compareCluster function or ShinyGO (0.80)91. For identifying putative upstream transcription factors, we performed the analysis with the ENCODE_and_ChEA_Consensus_TFs_from_ChIP-X library from Enrichr92, which is a database containing genes where transcription factor binds in the vicinity of their promoter regions. Gene regulatory network analysis was performed with Metascape93. In brief, all protein−protein interactions (PPIs) among input genes were extracted from an integrated PPI data source (STRING, BioGrid, OmniPath and InWeb_DB) and formed a PPI network. The MCODE algorithm was then applied to this network to identify neighborhoods where proteins are densely connected. Each MCODE network is assigned with a unique color, and Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was applied to each MCODE network to extract biological meaning, where the top three best P value terms were retained.
