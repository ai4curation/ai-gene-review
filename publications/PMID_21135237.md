## Full Text

Abstract

The accumulation of AMPA receptors (AMPARs) at synapses is essential for excitatory synaptic transmission. However, the mechanisms underlying synaptic targeting of AMPARs remain elusive. We have now used a molecular replacement approach on an AMPAR-null background to investigate the targeting mechanisms necessary for regulating AMPAR trafficking in the hippocampus. Although there is an extensive literature on the role of the GluA1 C-tail in AMPAR trafficking, there is no effect of overexpressing the C-tail on basal transmission. Instead, we found that the first intracellular loop domain (Loop1) of GluA1, a previously overlooked region within AMPARs, is critical for receptor targeting to synapses, but not for delivery of receptors to the plasma membrane. We also identified a CaMKII phosphorylation site (S567) in the GluA1 Loop1, which is phosphorylated in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, we show that S567 is a key residue that regulates Loop1-mediated AMPAR trafficking. Thus, our study reveals a unique mechanism for targeting AMPARs to synapses to mediate synaptic transmission.

Keywords: GluA2, GluR1, postsynaptic density, hippocampus, pyramidal neurons

AMPA receptors (AMPARs) are tetramers composed of GluA1-4 (GluR1-4 or GluRA-D) subunits, and mediate the majority of fast excitatory synaptic transmission in the brain ( 1 , 2 ). The dynamic regulation of AMPAR trafficking into and out of synapses is a major mechanism underlying synaptic plasticity ( 3 – 6 ). However, the mechanisms underlying AMPAR trafficking to synapses to mediate basal synaptic transmission remain largely elusive ( 3 – 6 ). In the hippocampus, the majority of AMPARs are either GluA1A2 or GluA2A3 heteromers ( 7 ), and associate with their accessory subunits, transmembrane AMPAR regulatory proteins (TARPs), which are critical for receptor trafficking ( 8 ). Furthermore, GluA1A2 heteromers are the major species at both synaptic and extrasynaptic membranes of hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neurons ( 9 ). Most studies to date have focused on the role of AMPAR C-termini in receptor trafficking ( 3 – 5 ). However, interference with GluA1 C-tail function has little effect on basal synaptic transmission ( 10 – 12 ). For example, basal synaptic transmission is not impaired upon overexpression of the GluA1 C-tail ( 12 ), nor in knockin mice in which GluA1 lacks its C-tail PDZ ligand ( 10 ) or key phosphorylation sites ( 11 ). These data suggest that regions other than the GluA1 C-tail may function in GluA1-containing AMPAR synaptic delivery to maintain synaptic transmission.

To study AMPAR trafficking, we have generated a triple floxed Gria1-3 fl/fl mouse line in which expression of the genes encoding GluA1, A2 and A3 are conditionally regulated ( 9 ). Expression of Cre recombinase in CA1 pyramidal neurons from Gria1-3 fl/fl mice leads to the loss of AMPARs ( 9 ), creating a null background for the reintroduction of WT or mutant AMPAR subunits that can be studied independent of native receptors. Using this molecular replacement approach, we found that delivery of AMPARs to synapses requires the first cytoplasmic domain (Loop1) of the AMPAR GluA1 subunit. In addition, we identified S567 of GluA1 Loop1 as a CaMKII phosphorylation substrate, and found that S567 is a critical residue for the regulation of the Loop1-dependent AMPAR trafficking to synapses.

Results

Expression of the GluA1 Loop1 Impairs Synaptic Transmission.

In agreement with previous studies ( 12 ), expression of the GluA1 C-tail in hippocampal slice cultures did not impair AMPAR-mediated synaptic transmission ( Fig. 1 C and H ). We thus explored the possibility that Loop1 ( Fig. 1 A and B ), an ∼30-aa cytoplasmic domain, plays a role in AMPAR trafficking. We first expressed GFP tagged GluA1 Loop1 in pyramidal neurons as a potential dominant negative. Next, 2–4 d after biolistic transfection in hippocampal slice cultures, we made simultaneous dual whole-cell recordings from GFP positive pyramidal neurons and untransfected neighboring neurons, and compared the amplitudes of AMPAR and NMDAR excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) evoked by a common input. Expression of the GluA1 Loop1 specifically reduced the amplitude of AMPAR EPSCs by ∼40%, without affecting either NMDAR EPSC amplitude ( Fig. 1 D and H ) or paired-pulse ratio ( Fig. S1 ), a measure of presynaptic release probability. Loop1 from GluA2, which is 72% identical to that of GluA1 ( Fig. 1 B ), also impaired synaptic transmission ( Fig. 1 E and H ). In contrast, expression of Loop1 from the kainate receptor subunit GluK2, which is 36% identical to GluA1 Loop1 ( Fig. 1 B ), did not affect synaptic transmission ( Fig. 1 F and H ). Furthermore, to control for potential nonspecific effects, we expressed GluA1 Loop1 in pyramidal neurons from GluA1 KO mice but observed no effect on AMPAR EPSCs ( Fig. 1 G and H ). Therefore the effect of GluA1 Loop1 expression on synaptic transmission depends on the presence of GluA1-containing receptors. Taken together, these data show that GluA1 Loop1 functions in AMPAR trafficking.

Fig. 1.

GluA1 Loop1 Is Required for Synaptic Delivery of GluA1 Homomers.

AMPAR trafficking involves two general steps: delivery of receptors to the surface and targeting of the receptors to the synapse. Which step might depend on GluA1 Loop1? To address this question, we compared the trafficking of GluA1 to that of GluA1/K2, a mutant of GluA1 in which the Loop1 region has been swapped with the loop domain from GluK2 ( Fig. 2 A ). In HEK293T cells, agonist-evoked currents from outside-out patches showed that GluA1/K2, similar to GluA1, traffics to the surface, forms homomeric receptors, as judged by the strong inward rectification ( Fig. S2 A and B ), and binds TARPs ( Fig. S3 ). Thus, swapping the Loop1 from GluK2 did not change basic biophysical and trafficking properties of GluA1 in heterologous cells.

Fig. 2.

To study GluA1/K2 trafficking in neurons, we used a molecular replacement approach. In hippocampal slice cultures from GRIA1-3 fl/fl mice, expression of mCherryCre (hereafter Cre) eliminated AMPAR-mediated synaptic transmission ( Fig. S4 ) and AMPAR currents from somatic outside-out patches in ∼2–3 wk ( 9 ). We next biolistically transfected neurons with two expression constructs: Cre and GluA1-IRES-EGFP or Cre and GluA1/K2-IRES-EGFP (identified by both red and green fluorescence). Both GluA1 and GluA1/K2 fully rescued surface AMPAR currents as revealed by recordings from somatic outside-out patches ( Fig. 2 B ). The glutamate-evoked currents were strongly inwardly rectifying, indicating that native receptors have been replaced with the expressed subunits ( Fig. 2 B ). In addition, GluA1 rescued the majority of synaptic AMPARs (∼70%, Fig. 2 C ), and the synaptic currents were as rectifying as those from the GluA2 KO ( Fig. 2 D ), as expected for GluA1 homomers. In striking contrast, very little rescue occurred with GluA1/K2 ( Fig. 2 E and F ). Taken together, these findings indicate that, although GluA1 Loop1 is not necessary for the delivery of GluA1 to the neuronal surface, it is essential for its targeting to the synapse.

GluA1 Loop1 Is Required for Synaptic Delivery of GluA1A2 Heteromers.

As GluA1A2 heteromers are the major receptor complexes at synapses in CA1 pyramidal neurons ( 9 ), we examined the role of GluA1 Loop1 in synaptic trafficking of GluA1A2 heteromers. In HEK293T cells, GluA1/K2, similar to GluA1, could assemble with GluA2 to form heteromeric receptors ( Fig. S2 C ). To study GluA1A2 heteromer trafficking in neurons, we cotransfected pyramidal neurons in hippocampal slice cultures from GRIA1-3 fl/fl with Cre-IRES-GluA1 or Cre-IRES-GluA1/K2 together with GluA2-IRES-EGFP. Outside-out patch recordings from pyramidal neurons expressing either Cre, GluA1, and GluA2 or Cre, GluA1/K2, and GluA2 showed similar glutamate-evoked currents from control cells, demonstrating that we rescued surface expression of AMPARs ( Fig. 3 A ). The I/V recordings in both conditions confirmed that the majority of AMPARs on the surface in the rescued cells were heteromers containing GluA2 ( Fig. 3 A ). These data suggest that trafficking of GluA1/K2-GluA2 heteromers to the surface is not impaired. We next tested the ability of the heteromers to rescue synaptic transmission. In contrast to the full rescue of synaptic transmission by wt GluA1 and GluA2 ( Fig. 3 B ), expression of GluA1/K2 and GluA2 showed significantly smaller AMPAR EPSCs, compared with that in control cells ( Fig. 3 D ). In addition, rectification indices in neurons expressing GluA1 and GluA2 ( Fig. 3 C ) and GluA1/K2 and GluA2 ( Fig. 3 E ) are identical to that in control neurons, indicating that receptors trafficked to synapses are heteromers. These data indicate that GluA1A2 heteromers traffic to synapses through a GluA1 Loop1-dependent mechanism.

Fig. 3.

GluA1 Is Phosphorylated on S567 by CaMKII both in Vitro and in Vivo.

What is the molecular basis for the Loop1-dependent receptor targeting? We noted that GluA1 Loop1 contains several serine (S) /threonine (T) residues that could potentially be phosphorylated. We reasoned that, if GluA1 Loop1 was subject to phosphorylation in neurons, this posttranslational modification could be critical for the regulation of Loop1-dependent trafficking. Using in vitro phosphorylation assays, we found that GluA1 Loop1 was effectively phosphorylated by CaMKII ( Fig. 4 A ), but not PKA or PKC ( Fig. 4 B ). In contrast, GluA2 Loop1, although sharing a high similarity with GluA1 Loop1, was not as efficiently phosphorylated by CaMKII ( Fig. 4 A ), indicating substrate specificity for GluA1 Loop1 phosphorylation. To identify the specific residue(s) phosphorylated by CaMKII in vitro, we generated point mutations of each S/T residue within GluA1 Loop1 and discovered that mutation of S567 to alanine (A) completely eliminated CaMKII phosphorylation of this region ( Fig. 4 C ). Therefore, GluA1 S567 is the CaMKII phosphorylation site in GluA1 Loop1.

Fig. 4.

To evaluate the phosphorylation of GluA1 Loop1 in vivo, we developed a phosphospecific antibody (pS567-Ab) that recognized GluA1 Loop1 phosphorylated at S567. Using an in vitro phosphorylation assay of purified GST-GluA1Loop1, we found that CaMKII, but not PKA or PKC, phosphorylated GluA1 S567 ( Fig. 5 A ). In addition, the phosphospecific antibody clearly recognized only the phosphorylated fusion protein and point mutations of S567 eliminated any immunoreactivity ( Fig. 5 B ). We next analyzed phosphorylation of immunoprecipitated full-length GluA1 expressed in HEK293T cells and observed specific phosphorylation of GluA1, but not GluA2 ( Fig. 5 C ). Importantly, in detergent extracts of adult rat hippocampi, pS567-Ab recognized a single ∼100-kDa protein in GluA1 immunoprecipitates ( Fig. 5 D and Fig. S5 ), demonstrating that endogenous GluA1 is phosphorylated at S567 in native brain tissue.

Fig. 5.

S567 of GluA1 Loop1 Negatively Regulates AMPAR Trafficking.

Does S567 in GluA1 Loop1 function in AMPAR trafficking? For instance, phosphorylation of S567 could act as a triggering signal for forward trafficking of AMPARs to synapses. Alternatively, phosphorylation at S567 may hold receptors away from synapses, and thus function as a negative regulator for synaptic delivery of AMPARs. To test these possibilities, we used the molecular replacement approach to study trafficking of GluA1 S567A, the nonphosphorylatable mutant, and GluA1 S567D, the phospho-mimetic mutant in hippocampal slice cultures. We transfected neurons with gold particles that were coated with two expression constructs: Cre and GluA1 S567A-IRES-EGFP or Cre and GluA1 S567D-IRES-EGFP. Both mutants fully rescued surface AMPAR currents as revealed by recordings from somatic outside-out patches, indicating that S567 is not involved in AMPAR trafficking to neuronal surface ( Fig. 6 A ). Interestingly, whereas GluA1 S567A rescued the majority of AMPAR-mediated synaptic transmission (∼82%), synaptic transmission in neurons expressing GluA1 S567D was significantly smaller than that in nearby controls (∼45%), showing that the phosphomimetic mutation at S567 impaired receptor trafficking to synapses ( Fig. 6 B ). Taken together, these data show that S567 is a critical residue that regulates Loop1-dependent AMPAR trafficking. Furthermore, we identify S567 as a unique CaMKII site on GluA1, and phosphorylation of GluA1 S567 in neurons inhibits receptor trafficking to synapses and acts as a negative regulator for synaptic trafficking of AMPARs.

Fig. 6.

We also evaluated synaptic targeting of GluA1 using immunofluorescence microscopy and showed that the surface expression of GluA1 S567A and GluA1 S567D was indistinguishable from WT GluA1 when expressed in cultured hippocampal neurons ( Fig. 6 C ), In contrast, the synaptic localization of GluA1 S567A was significantly enhanced, whereas the synaptic expression of GluA1 S567D was decreased ( Fig. 6 D ), as determined by colocalization with endogenous PSD-95. Importantly, these imaging data support the physiology data in demonstrating that a phosphomimetic mutation at S567 specifically inhibits trafficking of AMPARs to synapses. Indeed, the enhanced synaptic localization of GluA1 S567A in Fig. 6 D is reminiscent of the data from our molecular replacement assay in which synaptic rescue of the GluA1 S567A mutant (∼82% of control, Fig. 6 B ) is higher than that of WT GluA1 (∼70% of control, Fig. 2 C ). Furthermore, we reasoned that trafficking of the GluA1/A2-Loop1 chimera would be similar to GluA1 S567A, as GluA2 Loop1 was not an efficient phosphorylation substrate of CaMKII. Indeed, a molecular replacement experiment showed that synaptic delivery of GluA1/A2-Loop1 was as efficient as GluA1 S567A ( Fig. S6 ).

Discussion

Using a molecular replacement approach, we now show that a cytoplasmic domain, Loop1, of the GluA1 subunit is critical for AMPAR trafficking. Specifically, Loop1 is critical for the targeting of AMPARs to the synapse, but not for the delivery of receptors to extrasynaptic membranes. In addition, we identified a unique CaMKII phosphorylation site within Loop1 and showed that a phospho-mimetic mutation of S567 limits unregulated trafficking of AMPARs to synapses. Therefore, regulation of GluA1 Loop1 contributes to the maintenance of the stability of basal synaptic transmission.

Synaptic trafficking of AMPARs is thought to involve two steps: ( i ) an extrasynaptic insertion of receptors on the surface, and ( ii ) a subsequent synaptic insertion of AMPARs from extrasynaptic pools ( 13 – 20 ). The GluA1 subunit has been shown to play a critical role in both steps. Indeed, genetic deletion of the GluA1 subunit leads to a profound loss of excitatory synaptic transmission onto CA1 pyramidal neurons ( 9 , 21 , 22 ), indicating the importance of GluA1 in the maintenance of basal synaptic transmission. In addition, GluA1 plays a key role in synaptic plasticity, including both LTP ( 12 , 18 , 21 , 23 , 24 ) and LTD ( 11 ), suggesting an important role for GluA1 in activity-dependent AMPAR trafficking. Furthermore, the maintenance of extrasynaptic AMPARs is essentially dependent on the GluA1 subunit ( 9 , 21 , 22 ). Previous studies have shown that the GluA1 C-tail is critical for synaptic plasticity, but not for basal synaptic transmission ( 12 ). More recently, a membrane proximal region of the GluA1 C-tail that mediates the interaction with 4.1N protein was found to function in surface delivery of GluA1, but not in synaptic trafficking in the absence of activity ( 18 ). Therefore, the mechanisms regulating synaptic trafficking of GluA1-containing AMPARs to mediate basal synaptic trafficking are unclear. Our study now shows that the GluA1 Loop1 is critical for synaptic trafficking, but not for surface delivery of GluA1, thus providing a linkage between extrasynaptic and synaptic trafficking of AMPARs.

Our findings are reminiscent of the role of TARPs in regulating AMPAR synaptic expression in a two-step process, with surface expression being PDZ ligand independent, but synaptic expression requiring an intact PDZ ligand on TARPs ( 15 , 25 – 27 ). However, our studies on Loop1-dependent trafficking also show that the interaction of TARPs is not the only determining factor. Specifically, we find that the GluA1/K2 chimera has decreased synaptic expression, yet retains TARP binding ( Fig. S3 ), Therefore, further studies will be necessary to understand the additional mechanisms underlying this unique synaptic targeting process.

Previous studies have reported that GluA1 homomers overexpressed in rat hippocampal slice cultures failed to traffic to synapses ( 12 , 24 , 28 ). In contrast, our molecular replacement assay reveals that GluA1 homomers can effectively traffic to synapses. It is unclear what accounts for this discrepancy, although different expression strategies (overexpression vs. molecular replacement), different expression constructs (GFP fusion to GluA1 vs. wt GluA1 in our study) and different slice culture preparations (rat vs. mouse) may underlie the differences. Nevertheless, the robust rescue of synaptic transmission by expression of GluA1 homomers on an AMPAR-null background allowed the efficient quantitative analysis of trafficking of expressed receptors ( Figs. 2 and 3 ), which revealed Loop1-dependent AMPAR trafficking.

Phosphorylation of GluA1 by protein kinases is critical for AMPAR trafficking ( 11 , 24 , 29 – 31 ). For example, genetic evidence demonstrates that regulation of GluA1 S831, a CaMKII/PKC site, and S845, a PKA/PKG site, is important for activity-dependent AMPAR trafficking ( 11 , 30 , 32 – 34 ). In addition, PKC phosphorylation of S818 in the GluA1 C-tail regulates the interaction between GluA1 and 4.1N and promotes GluA1 trafficking to extrasynaptic membranes ( 18 ). Furthermore, phosphorylation of S818 works synergistically with S831 and S845 phosphorylation to facilitate synaptic trafficking of AMPARs in response to neuronal activity ( 24 ). However, until now, all of the characterized phosphorylation sites have been located within the GluA1 C-tail and none of these sites are critical for the maintenance of basal synaptic transmission ( 11 , 18 , 24 , 33 ).

As a multifunctional protein kinase, CaMKII functions both positively and negatively in AMPAR trafficking as a consequence of phosphorylating its many substrates. For example, CaMKII phosphorylation of TARPs is critical for activity-dependent trafficking of AMPARs to synapses during LTP ( 35 ). However, CaMKII phosphorylation of PSD-95 inhibits LTP-induced synaptic potentiation ( 36 ). Furthermore, CaMKII phosphorylation of synGAP negatively regulates synaptic strength ( 37 ). Interestingly, CaMKII can phosphorylate itself at two separate sites, one enhancing kinase activity and the other inhibiting activity ( 38 ). This is analogous to CaMKII phosphorylation of GluA1 in which C-tail phosphorylation enhances receptor trafficking in response to neuronal activity, whereas phosphorylation of S567 in Loop1 inhibits delivery of AMPARs to synapses.

Although overexpression of the CaMKII catalytic domain promotes the trafficking of recombinant GluA1 homomers to synapses ( 28 ), the underlying mechanism is unknown. Complex intracellular calcium dynamics and differential subcellular localization of CaMKII isoforms may allow its substrates to be phosphorylated in a temporally and spatially controlled manner to regulate AMPAR trafficking. Alternatively, overexpression of the CaMKII catalytic domain may lead to simultaneous phosphorylation of many proteins that function differentially in AMPAR trafficking, resulting in phosphorylation of proteins (such as TARPs or GluA1 C-tail) that promote synaptic trafficking that can override phosphorylation of other substrates that negatively regulate synaptic trafficking of AMPARs (such as GluA1 S567). A relevant example is the activation of PKC by phorbol esters ( 39 ) and postsynaptic loading of catalytic fragment of PKC ( 40 ), which enhance AMPAR-mediated mEPSC amplitude in CA1 pyramidal neurons. However, PKC phosphorylation of GluA2 S880 excludes AMPARs from synapses, and thus negatively regulates AMPAR trafficking ( 41 ). Thus, it is important in the future to determine neuronal activity patterns in which individual substrates of protein kinases are phosphorylated in a temporally and spatially controlled manner to fulfill specific trafficking events.

In hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neurons, AMPARs at extrasynaptic membranes are highly abundant ( 9 , 22 ) and have been proposed to serve as a reserve pool of receptors, which not only supply synaptic pools to maintain synaptic transmission but are also trafficked to synapses during synaptic plasticity ( 13 – 20 ). Our results suggest that the exchange of receptors between extrasynaptic and synaptic sites is a highly regulated step in AMPAR synaptic trafficking and that the GluA1 Loop1 domain provides a unique mechanism for such regulation of AMPAR trafficking in neurons.

Materials and Methods

Organotypical hippocampal slice cultures were prepared from P6-8 rat or P5–P8 GRIA1-3 fl/fl mice and transfected biolistically with various plasmids after 2–3 d in culture. Phosphorylation of the Loop1 domain of AMPAR subunits was characterized using purified GST fusion proteins, recombinant receptors expressed in HEK293T cells and receptors in native brain tissues. Immunocytochemistry was used to study trafficking of expressed AMPAR subunits in dissociated hippocampal cultures. Detailed protocols for subcloning constructs, antibody production, electrophysiological recordings, biochemical experiments, and immunostaining are provided in SI Materials and Methods .
