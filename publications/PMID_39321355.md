CCCH-type tandem zinc finger (TZF) motifs are found in many RNA-binding proteins
involved in regulating mRNA stability, translation, and splicing. In
Caenorhabditis elegans, several RNA-binding proteins that regulate embryonic
development and cell fate determination contain CCCH TZF domains, including
POS-1. Previous biochemical studies have shown that despite high levels of
sequence conservation, POS-1 recognizes a broader set of RNA sequences compared
to the human homologue tristetraprolin. However, the molecular basis of these
differences remains unknown. In this study, we refined the consensus RNA
sequence and determined the differing binding specificities of the two zinc
fingers of POS-1. We also determined the solution structure and characterized
the internal dynamics of the TZF domain of POS-1. From the structure, we
identified unique features that define the RNA binding specificity of POS-1. We
also observed that the TZF domain of POS-1 is in equilibrium between
interconverting conformations. Transitions between these conformations require
internal motions involving many residues with correlated dynamics in each ZF. We
propose that the correlated dynamics are necessary to allow allosteric
communication between the nucleotide-binding pockets observed in the N-terminal
ZF. Our study shows that both the structure and conformational plasticity of
POS-1 are important in ensuring recognition of its RNA binding targets.

INTRODUCTION

Posttranscriptional regulation is an important part of spatial and temporal regulation of gene expression (1–4). Regulation of mRNA localization, stability and translation is carried out by the interplay of a variety of proteins and their interactions with target mRNAs (5–8). The tristetraprolin (TTP) family of proteins binds to the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of mRNAs through a CCCH-type tandem zinc finger (TZF) domain to regulate transcript stability and localization in the cell (9). The TZF domain is composed of two highly conserved zinc finger (ZF) motifs connected by a linker, and it is flanked by low complexity N- and C- terminal regions, which are predicted to be unstructured (Table S1).

In C. elegans, a set of CCCH-type TZF RNA-binding proteins function specifically in oocyte maturation and in early embryonic development (10). One such protein is POS-1 (UniProtKB G5EF15), which accumulates in the posterior of the zygote after fertilization and is asymmetrically distributed to the posterior daughter cell after each cell division for four cycles (11, 12). Worms with a pos-1 null mutation produce embryos with abnormal cell divisions and cell-specification early in development resulting in lethality (13). Several genes have been identified to have different expression levels in pos-1 mutants (glp-1, apx-1 and nos-2) (13–15) because their translation is either repressed or activated by the interaction of POS-1 with their 3′ UTRs. It has also been shown that the TZF domain of POS-1 is important in regulating the spatial distribution of POS-1 during early development, with each zinc finger playing a different role in a specific pathway leading to the asymmetric localization of POS-1 in the embryo. The N-terminal zinc finger (ZF1) of POS-1 was shown to be recognized by the protein degradation machinery in anterior somatic cells, while the C-terminal zinc finger (ZF2) interacts with the RNA-rich P granules leading to POS-1 segregation into germ lineage. Both mechanisms contribute to the spatial localization of POS-1 to the posterior of the embryo (16).

Accurate recognition of mRNA targets is essential for the proper regulatory function of these RNA binding proteins. POS-1 human homologs, TTP (UniProtKB P26651), TIS11b (UniProtKB Q07352) and TIS11d (UniProtKB P47974) (Figure 1), target the sequence 5’-UAUUUAUU-3’ (17, 18). In contrast, CCCH-type TZF proteins from C. elegans (Figure 1) have been shown to recognize different RNA sequences. For example, OMA-1 (UniProtKB G5EC86) recognizes UA(A/U) repeats (19) and MEX-5 (UniProtKB Q9XUB2) targets promiscuously any U-rich sequence (20). The consensus sequence for POS-1 was determined to be UA(U2–3)RD(N1–3)G, where R is any purine, D is A, G, or U, and N is any base (21). Given the high sequence conservation of these CCCH-type TZF domains, how can POS-1, MEX-5 and OMA-1 have different RNA-binding specificities from each other and the human TTP proteins? To fully understand the function of these proteins in vivo it is necessary to characterize the mechanisms that allow these relatively conserved small domains to precisely recognize their RNA sequences.

So far, the only structural information about how this family of proteins engages target RNA comes from the structure of TIS11d bound to its target RNA, 5′-UUAUUUAUU-3′ (22). Although the structure of TIS11d with its target RNA represented a breakthrough for the field when it became available two decades ago, it cannot explain the different binding specificities observed for other members of the CCCH-type TZF family. The low amount of secondary structure and the intrinsic flexibility has made the structural characterization of these TZF domains particularly challenging. At the same time, it has highlighted how RNA recognition can be impacted by both their structure and their conformational dynamics (23, 24). To expand our understanding of how these TZF proteins recognize their target RNAs, in this work, we characterized the structure and dynamics of the TZF domain of POS-1. We further refined the consensus sequence of POS-1 and showed that structural plasticity plays a key role in the RNA binding specificity, including a novel allosteric communication between binding pockets in ZF1.

RESULTSSolution structure of the CCCH-type TZF domain of POS-1The chemical shift assignments for the CCCH-type TZF domain of POS-1 (referred to here as POS-1[97–173]) was carried out using standard triple resonance experiments (68). We determined 93% and 85% of the backbone and sidechain assignments, respectively (BMRB accession code 30571). Complete chemical shift assignment of the protein could not be achieved because some residues were broadened beyond detection due to chemical exchange.The solution structure of POS-1[97–173] was determined using distance constraints based on 13C- and 15N-edited 3D-NOESY spectra which yielded a total of 1054 distance constraints, of which 233 were long-range constraints. In addition, based on the backbone chemical shift data, 143 backbone dihedral constraints obtained from TALOS (35) were included in the structure calculations. Additional constraints were included to ensure a tetrahedral Zn+2 coordination in each zinc finger domain (22). The Zn2+ to POS-1 ratio was determined to be 1.8 using Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-OES) (69), indicating a 2:1 [Zn+2]:[POS-1] binding stoichiometry. The structural statistics are summarized in Figure 2, calculated over an ensemble of 20 structures (PDB accession code 6NZL).The NMR ensemble of structures shows that in the absence of RNA each individual zinc finger folds into a well-defined structure with a backbone RMSD of ~ 0.4 Å (Figure 3). The linker residues between the zinc fingers of POS-1 are highly flexible, as indicated by the lack of long-range interactions between the individual zinc fingers, by the backbone dihedral angle information derived from the backbone resonance data and by the relaxation parameters, R1, R2 and NOE, discussed below.Each zinc finger is characterized by a CX8CX5CX3H motif, where the Zn+2 ion is coordinated by three cysteine residues via the Sγ group and a histidine residue via Nε2. These residues are C104, C113, C119, H123, and C147, C156, C162, H166 in ZF1 and ZF2, respectively. The Zn+2 coordination stabilizes the folding of each zinc finger, which otherwise have few secondary or tertiary structural elements (Figure 3). In fact, removal of the Zn+2 ions from the TZF domain causes all the cross peaks in the 2D 15N-1H heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) spectrum to collapse in the middle, indicating that POS-1 is fully unfolded.The structures of the zinc fingers are similar to one another, with a backbone RMSD of 0.53 Å between the lowest energy structures for residues 104–123 and 147–166, of ZF1 and ZF2, respectively. There are also several notable structural differences. In each zinc finger, a short α-helix is present between the first and second zinc-coordinating cysteine residues (residues 105–110 and 148–153, in ZF1 and ZF2, respectively). In ZF1, the α-helix is further stabilized by electrostatic interactions between the sidechains of the negatively charged D105 and the positively charged K108 and/or R109 (Figure 4), which were observed in 8 and 11 out of 20 lowest energy structures, respectively. In ZF2, however, these stabilizing salt bridges are missing, and the α-helix forms long-range interactions with the C-terminal residues, as indicated by the observation of NOE contacts between residues F150 and I168, and S151 and V169 (Figure 4D). In addition, in ZF1 the conformation of the coil following the α-helix is stabilized by a set of H-bonds formed between the backbone S110-O and A112-HN groups, and between the sidechain S110-Oγ and the backbone A112-O, where the latter is observed in 6 out of 20 structures in the final ensemble. Instead in ZF2, for the corresponding region, H-bond formation is observed between the T153 sidechain and the N155 backbone, where T153-Oγ, acts either as proton donor or acceptor to the backbone N155-O or N155-HN, respectively. An additional H-bond is also observed between N155-Hδ2 and T153-O in 6 out of 20 structures (Figure 4A, B).In both zinc fingers, a three-residue turn between the second and third coordinating cysteines is followed by a short 310-helix (residues 116–118 and 159–161 in ZF1 and ZF2, respectively). This maintains the third cysteine residue in the appropriate orientation for Zn+2 coordination. The overall structure of each zinc finger is stabilized by long-range interactions between residues located at the N- and C-terminal end of each zinc finger, bringing the ends in proximity. This network of interactions is centered around A122 and I165, in ZF1 and ZF2, respectively, where A122-HN (I165-HN) forms a H-bond with A102-O (V145-O), and A122-O (I165-O) forms a H-bond with C104-HN (C147-HN) (Figure 4C). The structure of ZF1 is further stabilized by a salt bridge between the side chains of K100 and E127 and an H-bonding network involving the backbone atoms of R120, R129, G124, L128, and K100, where K100 is sandwiched between R120 and R129. These stabilizing interactions are facilitated by the presence of a third 310-turn at the N-terminus of the linker, formed by residues 125–127.Overall, the solution NMR structure of POS-1[97–173] shows that this domain is highly flexible in regions outside the two ZFs. For ZF1 and ZF2, Zn+2 coordination serves to hold the structure together, resulting in overall highly similar folds, with some minor differences discussed in detail above.Comparison of the structure of free POS-1[97–173] with the structure of TIS11d in the RNA-bound stateTIS11d and POS-1 share 69% and 62% sequence similarity over their first and second zinc finger motifs, respectively. It has been established that TIS11d recognizes AU-rich elements (ARE); more specifically each zinc finger binds to a UAUU motif, with ZF1 binding the 3’ half-site and ZF2 binding the 5’ half-site. The previously published structure of TIS11d bound to the RNA sequence 5’-UUAUUUAUU-3’ (22) shows that, aside from the stacking interactions between the aromatic side chains and the RNA bases, most of the interactions between the protein and the RNA are H-bonds between the protein backbone and RNA bases. These data would suggest that the difference in RNA binding specificities observed between POS-1 and TIS11d may originate from differences in the backbone structure of the proteins or differences in surface volume.Upon the addition of RNA to POS-1, we observed sample precipitation and line broadening in the 2D NMR spectrum (Figure S1), likely due to aggregation. Therefore, we were unable to use solution NMR to solve the structure of the complex. In our previous work, molecular dynamics simulations of TIS11d revealed that the structures of the individual zinc finger domains remained conserved between the bound and free states (70). Therefore, in the absence of a POS-1:RNA structure, we turned to direct structural comparison with TIS11d as a way to frame our understanding of POS-1:RNA binding. We compared the structure of TIS11d in the bound state with that of POS-1 in the free state to identify the structural differences that may account for the differential RNA binding specificities observed for these two proteins. Comparison of the structures of the individual zinc fingers of POS-1 in the free-state to those of TIS11d in its RNA-bound state indicates that the structures of the zinc fingers in the two proteins are very similar, with a backbone RMSD of 0.64 Å and 0.44 Å for ZF1 and ZF2, respectively. Furthermore, the structure at the RNA binding interface is surprisingly highly conserved in these homologous proteins. Figure 5 shows the binding interface and highlights the interactions observed in the TIS11d:RNA complex that are superimposed with the corresponding region of POS-1. The residues which form the interface of TIS11d:RNA were observed to be in the same conformations, with the exception of the E157 side chain, and almost perfectly superimpose with the corresponding residues in free POS-1. This finding indicates that differences in RNA-binding specificities of these two proteins are not due to differences in the backbone structure of the zinc fingers.The RNA consensus sequence of POS-1 was reported to be UA(U2–3)RD(N1–3)G, where R is any purine, D is A, G, or U, and N is any base (21). Comparison with the cognate sequence of TIS11d (UAUUUAUU) shows that the 5’-half of the target sequence is the same for the two proteins (UAUU), but the 3’-half is different. Assuming a similar binding mode between POS-1 and TIS11d, with ZF1 binding to the 3’-half of the RNA target and ZF2 binding to the 5’ half-site, we expect ZF2 of POS-1 to interact with the same nucleotides as ZF2 of TIS11d (UAUU). The difference between the RNA binding specificity of ZF1 observed for POS-1 and TIS11d (RD(N1–3)G vs. UAUU, respectively) is perhaps also reflected in a greater primary sequence variation observed for the residues located at the protein-RNA interface of ZF1 (Figure 5 and Figure 1).The most striking difference is that three charged residues, R153, E157 and R160 in TIS11d are replaced by A98, A102 and D105 in POS-1 (Figure 5C). In the complex of TIS11d with the RNA nonamer UUAUUUAUU (22), the long-range interactions formed by the sidechains of R153, E157 and R160 stabilize the RNA-binding interface of ZF1 and, at the same time, enable coupling between ZF1 and ZF2 in the RNA-bound state of TIS11d. TIS11d and POS-1 share 69% and 62% sequence similarity over their first and second zinc finger motifs, respectively. The previously published structure of TIS11d bound to the RNA sequence 5’-UUAUUUAUU-3’ (22) shows that, aside from the stacking interactions between the aromatic side chains and the RNA bases, most of the interactions between the protein and the RNA are H-bonds between the protein backbone and RNA bases. These data would suggest that the difference in RNA binding specificities observed between POS-1 and TIS11d may originate from differences in the backbone structure of the proteins or differences in surface volume.

Upon the addition of RNA to POS-1, we observed sample precipitation and line broadening in the 2D NMR spectrum (Figure S1), likely due to aggregation. Therefore, we were unable to use solution NMR to solve the structure of the complex. In our previous work, molecular dynamics simulations of TIS11d revealed that the structures of the individual zinc finger domains remained conserved between the bound and free states (70). Therefore, in the absence of a POS-1:RNA structure, we turned to direct structural comparison with TIS11d as a way to frame our understanding of POS-1:RNA binding. We compared the structure of TIS11d in the bound state with that of POS-1 in the free state to identify the structural differences that may account for the differential RNA binding specificities observed for these two proteins. Comparison of the structures of the individual zinc fingers of POS-1 in the free-state to those of TIS11d in its RNA-bound state indicates that the structures of the zinc fingers in the two proteins are very similar, with a backbone RMSD of 0.64 Å and 0.44 Å for ZF1 and ZF2, respectively. Furthermore, the structure at the RNA binding interface is surprisingly highly conserved in these homologous proteins. Figure 5 shows the binding interface and highlights the interactions observed in the TIS11d:RNA complex that are superimposed with the corresponding region of POS-1. The residues which form the interface of TIS11d:RNA were observed to be in the same conformations, with the exception of the E157 side chain, and almost perfectly superimpose with the corresponding residues in free POS-1. This finding indicates that differences in RNA-binding specificities of these two proteins are not due to differences in the backbone structure of the zinc fingers.

The RNA consensus sequence of POS-1 was reported to be UA(U2–3)RD(N1–3)G, where R is any purine, D is A, G, or U, and N is any base (21). Comparison with the cognate sequence of TIS11d (UAUUUAUU) shows that the 5’-half of the target sequence is the same for the two proteins (UAUU), but the 3’-half is different. Assuming a similar binding mode between POS-1 and TIS11d, with ZF1 binding to the 3’-half of the RNA target and ZF2 binding to the 5’ half-site, we expect ZF2 of POS-1 to interact with the same nucleotides as ZF2 of TIS11d (UAUU). The difference between the RNA binding specificity of ZF1 observed for POS-1 and TIS11d (RD(N1–3)G vs. UAUU, respectively) is perhaps also reflected in a greater primary sequence variation observed for the residues located at the protein-RNA interface of ZF1 (Figure 5 and Figure 1).

The most striking difference is that three charged residues, R153, E157 and R160 in TIS11d are replaced by A98, A102 and D105 in POS-1 (Figure 5C). In the complex of TIS11d with the RNA nonamer UUAUUUAUU (22), the long-range interactions formed by the sidechains of R153, E157 and R160 stabilize the RNA-binding interface of ZF1 and, at the same time, enable coupling between ZF1 and ZF2 in the RNA-bound state of TIS11d. In POS-1 the smaller, nonpolar sidechains of A98 and A102 are incapable of forming the same hydrogen bonds as R153 and E157 in TIS11d that are important for RNA binding and for coupling ZF1 with ZF2. Additionally, the smaller side chain of D105 in POS-1 faces away from the RNA interface and interacts with the sidechains of K107 and/or R108 in the α-helix (Figure 5C). These structural differences permit the formation of a larger and shallower binding groove for the 6th and 7th nucleotides (Figure 6, binding pockets 5 and 6) and explain why POS-1 can bind to both purine and pyrimidine with high affinity at these sites, whereas TIS11d is highly specific for U6 and A7 at the same sites (Figure S2).

Another difference in the primary sequence observed at the protein-RNA interface of ZF1 is located around the third Zn2+-coordinating cysteine: glutamine, cysteine, arginine (QCR) in POS-1 instead of lysine, cysteine, glutamine (KCQ) in TIS11d (Figure 1 and Figure 5C). Q175 in TIS11d is replaced by R120 in POS-1, which is a much bulkier residue. To accommodate this bulkier sidechain in the POS-1:RNA complex, the RNA is likely to have a different conformation than what is observed in the TIS11d:RNA complex. In addition, due to its multivalent character, R120 may interact with the bound RNA to provide additional stability and/or an alternative binding mode.

In summary, structural comparison between TIS11d:RNA and POS-1[97–173] suggests how POS-1 may accommodate the same mRNA sequence motif in ZF2, while broadening specificity via ZF1. We identified several key differences between TIS11d and POS-1 that have important implications for altered specificity.

POS-1 dynamics in the free stateThe results of the RNA binding studies discussed above along with the structural similarities of POS-1 and TIS11d suggest that the plasticity of POS-1 may play a role in differentiating its RNA-binding specificity from TIS11d. To shed light on the fundamental mechanisms that enable POS-1 to bind a variety of targets we characterized the backbone dynamics of POS-1 using NMR relaxation measurements.To probe the fast timescale dynamics of POS-1[97–173] (ps-ns timescale), we collected 15N-spin relaxation experiments to measure R1, R2 and heteronuclear NOE at two magnetic fields (Figure 9, black dots). The 15N-1H-NOE profile shows a dip for the residues in the linker region and at the ends of the domain, supporting our previous observation that the residues located in the linker region and at N- and C-terminal end of the domain are highly dynamic. The highly variable transverse relaxation rate constants, R2, observed for both zinc fingers indicate that they may be experiencing conformational fluctuations in μs-ms range.Model-free analysis of the NMR relaxation parameters was used to deconvolute the different timescale dynamics contributing to the observed NMR relaxation data. Model-free assuming isotropic tumbling did not fit the relaxation parameters and indicated that an isotropic model is not sufficient to characterize the overall diffusion of the protein and that diffusion of POS-1[97–173] might be more accurately described by an axially symmetric model. However, we could not use an axially symmetric model because POS-1 does not have a single unique structure in solution due to continuous reorientation of the zinc fingers with respect to each other. To gain insight on the dynamics of each zinc finger, we instead studied them individually.We expressed and purified the individual ZF1 and ZF2 domains (residues 97–133 and 141–173, respectively). The residues included in each individual zinc finger construct were determined based on the structure of the tandem zinc finger domain. The structure of each isolated zinc finger was verified to be the same as in the tandem zinc finger domain by 15N-1H-HSQC (Figure S6). For ZF1, we observe very good agreement for residues 102–123, with slight chemical shift perturbations for residues 99–101. As expected, we observe more significant perturbations for residues at the C-terminus (residues 125–133) due to the loss of the linker residues in this shorter construct. For the same reason, in ZF2 we also observe more significant chemical shift differences for residues at the N-terminus of the construct (residues 141–146). These observations indicate that the structure of the individual zinc fingers is minimally perturbed compared to the full TZF domain and therefore, they fold independently from each other without the need for long-range interactions between the zinc fingers.The NMR relaxation parameters measured for each individual zinc finger (Figure 9, red dots) also show excellent agreement with the data collected for the full TZF domain (black dots), further supporting that each zinc finger is structurally and dynamically independent from the other and that the internal dynamics of the protein can be investigated using the individual zinc finger constructs.The results of the Model-free analysis for the NMR relaxation data measured for each individual zinc finger are shown in Figure 10. For both zinc fingers, the relaxation data is best fitted by an isotropic diffusion model. From this analysis we determined an order parameter, S2, for each residue, which provides information on the amplitude of motions occurring on a timescale faster than the molecular tumbling (ps-ns). S2 values range from zero (for highly flexible regions) to one (for rigid regions). The S2 profile is similar for both ZF domains: for most of the residues we observe low mobility, with the exclusion of the second Zn+2 coordinating cysteine in each ZF (C113 and C156) and the terminal residues. POS-1 dynamics in the free state

The results of the RNA binding studies discussed above along with the structural similarities of POS-1 and TIS11d suggest that the plasticity of POS-1 may play a role in differentiating its RNA-binding specificity from TIS11d. To shed light on the fundamental mechanisms that enable POS-1 to bind a variety of targets we characterized the backbone dynamics of POS-1 using NMR relaxation measurements.

To probe the fast timescale dynamics of POS-1[97–173] (ps-ns timescale), we collected 15N-spin relaxation experiments to measure R1, R2 and heteronuclear NOE at two magnetic fields (Figure 9, black dots). The 15N-1H-NOE profile shows a dip for the residues in the linker region and at the ends of the domain, supporting our previous observation that the residues located in the linker region and at N- and C-terminal end of the domain are highly dynamic. The highly variable transverse relaxation rate constants, R2, observed for both zinc fingers indicate that they may be experiencing conformational fluctuations in μs-ms range.

Model-free analysis of the NMR relaxation parameters was used to deconvolute the different timescale dynamics contributing to the observed NMR relaxation data. Model-free assuming isotropic tumbling did not fit the relaxation parameters and indicated that an isotropic model is not sufficient to characterize the overall diffusion of the protein and that diffusion of POS-1[97–173] might be more accurately described by an axially symmetric model. However, we could not use an axially symmetric model because POS-1 does not have a single unique structure in solution due to continuous reorientation of the zinc fingers with respect to each other. To gain insight on the dynamics of each zinc finger, we instead studied them individually.

We expressed and purified the individual ZF1 and ZF2 domains (residues 97–133 and 141–173, respectively). The residues included in each individual zinc finger construct were determined based on the structure of the tandem zinc finger domain. The structure of each isolated zinc finger was verified to be the same as in the tandem zinc finger domain by 15N-1H-HSQC (Figure S6). For ZF1, we observe very good agreement for residues 102–123, with slight chemical shift perturbations for residues 99–101. As expected, we observe more significant perturbations for residues at the C-terminus (residues 125–133) due to the loss of the linker residues in this shorter construct. For the same reason, in ZF2 we also observe more significant chemical shift differences for residues at the N-terminus of the construct (residues 141–146). These observations indicate that the structure of the individual zinc fingers is minimally perturbed compared to the full TZF domain and therefore, they fold independently from each other without the need for long-range interactions between the zinc fingers.

The NMR relaxation parameters measured for each individual zinc finger (Figure 9, red dots) also show excellent agreement with the data collected for the full TZF domain (black dots), further supporting that each zinc finger is structurally and dynamically independent from the other and that the internal dynamics of the protein can be investigated using the individual zinc finger constructs.

The results of the Model-free analysis for the NMR relaxation data measured for each individual zinc finger are shown in Figure 10. For both zinc fingers, the relaxation data is best fitted by an isotropic diffusion model. From this analysis we determined an order parameter, S2, for each residue, which provides information on the amplitude of motions occurring on a timescale faster than the molecular tumbling (ps-ns). S2 values range from zero (for highly flexible regions) to one (for rigid regions). The S2 profile is similar for both ZF domains: for most of the residues we observe low mobility, with the exclusion of the second Zn+2 coordinating cysteine in each ZF (C113 and C156) and the terminal residues. The C-terminal region of ZF2 shows higher flexibility compared to that of ZF1. This difference is likely due to the long-range interactions that we observe between K100, at the N-terminus of ZF1, and E127/R129 at the beginning of linker region (Figure 4C), which are not present in ZF2.

Overall, the MD simulations of the POS-1 ZFs highlight the conformational plasticity of POS-1, in agreement with the NMR data, and provide mechanistic insights into the motions observed experimentally.
