AcrIE3 (DMS3-30, A0SMP0) host discrimination: can the two Cas8e proteins explain it?

Hypothesis slug: kgap-acrie3-cas8e-discrimination Target: Pseudomonas phage DMS3 gene DMS3-30, UniProt A0SMP0, NCBITaxon:2913973 Source: genes/9CAUD/DMS3-30/DMS3-30-ai-review.yaml, focus type free_text

Summary

Verdict: partially supported. The seed hypothesis proposes that the reason AcrIE3 inhibits the Pseudomonas aeruginosa type I-E system but not the Escherichia coli K-12 one is readable from the two Cas8e proteins. Half of that proposal holds. The Cas8e PAM recognition site is rebuilt between the two hosts, and the rebuild is fixed within each species, so this is a species boundary and not a strain accident. The other half fails. The specific electrostatic reasoning the hypothesis rests on, that an acidic inhibitor surface docking at a DNA-phosphate-reading site is sensitive to charge, predicts the wrong host when tested. Sequence and apo structural models cannot separate a residue-identity explanation from a conformational one, and this report states that plainly.

The computed core is as follows. E. coli K-12 Cse1/Cas8e (UniProt Q46901, 502 aa) and P. aeruginosa Cas8e (UniProt A0A3S4PYN6 / WP_057392414, 522 aa) are 21.7% identical over the whole protein. Across the 21 residues that contact the PAM duplex in the E. coli Cascade crystal structure 5H9F, only 3 are identical, which is 14.3%, below the whole-protein figure. Nine of the 21 substitutions change formal charge. The largest single local change is the PAM-reading L1 loop, which is the glycine hinge GFGGGFK at E. coli positions 157-163 and the six-residue-longer PGGLVKTVRDSDK at P. aeruginosa positions 171-183. That loop string is present in 16 of 16 E. coli K-12-like Cse1 sequences and in 65 of 66 P. aeruginosa sequences, so both versions are species-fixed. Forward and reciprocal structural mappings, which make different errors, converge on the same patch and nominate K176 and K183 as the host-side basic residues at the site, flanked by D180 and D182, with L174 and V175 adding roughly 100 Å³ of side-chain volume where E. coli has the glycines G159 and G160.

Three results push against the hypothesis as written. AlphaFold DB has no model for A0SMP0 and no P. aeruginosa type I-E Cascade structure is deposited, so the occlusion question the hypothesis asks cannot be answered from deposited coordinates; the modelling substitute fails its own same-protein control. AcrIE3 computes to net charge −13 at pH 7 and pI 3.94, yet it inhibits the less basic Cas8e (pI 5.48) and fails against the more basic Cse1 (pI 8.55), so bulk and patch charge complementarity point the wrong way. The published precedent of AcrIC8 and AcrIC9, which block PAM recognition allosterically and competitively respectively, shows that inhibiting PAM read-out does not require touching PAM residues, so an allosteric or assembly-level difference stays open as a competing explanation. On the inhibitor side the analysis does produce a concrete, experimentally grounded output: solvent accessibility on the 1.33 Å AcrIE3 crystal structure 8HEL gives one dominant acidic surface patch, E35, D53, E54, E61, E64, D66, E67, spanning 27.9 Å with 723 Å² of exposed area.

For curation, the usable action is narrow and specific. The evidence supports a molecular function term describing inhibition of a host defence complex through binding the Cascade large subunit, with the species restriction recorded as part of the evidence rather than as part of the term. The report avoids recommending plain "protein binding", since a more informative description is supported by direct structural and mutational work.

Key findings

Finding 1: the two Cas8e proteins are twilight-zone homologs at 21.7% identity

Global Needleman-Wunsch alignment with BLOSUM62 and affine gaps (open 11, extend 1) gives 21.7% identity between Q46901 (502 aa) and A0A3S4PYN6 (522 aa). This is not an artefact of the particular pair. All cross-clade Cas8e comparisons sampled fall between 20% and 27%: the median identity of 54 unique non-E. coli Enterobacteriaceae Cas8e sequences against Q46901 is 23.1%, with a maximum of 24.9%, and 0 of 54 pass a 40% identity filter. Within-clade identity is an order of magnitude more informative: 98.5% among 67 unique P. aeruginosa Cas8e sequences and a median of 97.8% among 19 E. coli K-12-like Cse1 sequences.

The practical consequence showed up directly. phenix.superpose_pdbs, which seeds superposition on a sequence alignment, reported 19.8% identity for this pair and fitted 3 atoms, producing a meaningless result. A TM-align-style structural superposition of the two AlphaFold models gave TM-score 0.495, against 0.834 for the same-protein control (AlphaFold Q46901 onto PDB 5H9F chain A). Any residue-level claim about this pair therefore has to be anchored on structure. The sequence alignment alone cannot place the PAM site.

This produces an incidental hazard for curators. Most NCBI entries annotated "type I-E CRISPR-associated protein Cse1/CasA" in E. coli (520 aa), Klebsiella (507 aa) and P. aeruginosa (522 aa) sit in Cas8e clades only about 21-25% identical to E. coli K-12 CasA. Transferring residue-level or mechanistic annotation across the "Cas8e" label is unsafe at that identity.

Finding 2: the PAM site is more divergent than the protein as a whole, with 9 charge changes in 21 positions

PAM contact residues were taken from the deposited coordinates of PDB 5H9F (E. coli Cascade bound to dsDNA, 2.45 Å, PMID: 26863189) rather than quoted from text, because the available abstracts describe the PAM read-out structurally without naming residues. The PAM is the non-target-strand 5'-ATG at chain M residues 16-18, paired with target-strand chain N residues 33-35. Twenty-one Cse1 (chain A) residues lie within 4.0 Å of that duplex.

The abstract of Hayes et al. states: "The 5'-ATG PAM is recognized in duplex form, from the minor groove side, by three structural features in the Cascade Cse1 subunit", and "Optimal PAM recognition coincides with wedge insertion, initiating directional target DNA strand unwinding to allow segmented base-pairing with crRNA". The 21 extracted residues are the atomic realisation of those structural features.

Mapping them onto P. aeruginosa Cas8e by structural superposition gives the table below. Three of 21 are identical (14.3%), which is lower than the 21.7% whole-protein identity. Nine substitutions change formal charge. Fourteen of 21 mappings have a Cα distance below 3.5 Å and are treated as reliable; the other seven are flagged.

The three preserved positions are the ones that do chemistry on the DNA: the glutamine wedge Q354/Q355, the alanine A355/A356 beside it, and the lysine K163/K183. Everything around them has been rebuilt.

Electrostatics of the patch show the same thing from a different angle. Within 6 Å of the PAM duplex E. coli has 4 Lys and no Asp or Glu, a net of +4, while P. aeruginosa has 2 basic and 2 acidic residues, a net of 0. Within 12 Å both reach net +3, and identity across the 12 Å patch is 12%. The inner shell loses its uniform positive charge while the outer shell is matched.

Finding 3: the differences are species-wide on both sides

Cas8e sequences were collected from NCBI and grouped into 67 unique P. aeruginosa, 43 unique non-aeruginosa Pseudomonas, 19 unique E. coli K-12-like, and 54 unique non-E. coli Enterobacteriaceae. At all 21 mapped PAM-contact positions the P. aeruginosa consensus residue occurs in at least 97% of the 67 sequences, with 17 of 21 positions at 99-100%. All 21 E. coli positions are 100% conserved across the 16 K-12-like sequences that pass a 40% identity filter.

One position needs a correction to the reference. At 268, 65 of 67 P. aeruginosa sequences carry Phe where the chosen reference A0A3S4PYN6 carries Tyr. The consensus is F268, still a bulky aromatic replacing the E. coli lysine K266, so the physicochemical statement survives the correction.

Across the wider Pseudomonas genus the same positions are 53-93% conserved, with a median identity of 58.4% to the P. aeruginosa reference. This generates a testable prediction: AcrIE3 sensitivity should track P. aeruginosa-like Cas8e specifically and should weaken across the genus as those positions drift. The 54 non-E. coli Enterobacteriaceae sequences are all below 25% identity to K-12 Cse1 and therefore do not constrain the E. coli side at all.

Finding 4: the PAM-reading L1 loop is the largest local difference

The structural mapping places E. coli Cse1 157-163 (GFGGGFK) opposite P. aeruginosa Cas8e 171-183 (PGGLVKTVRDSDK). The P. aeruginosa loop is 6 residues longer. The E. coli version has 4 Gly, 2 bulky aromatics, 1 Lys and no acidic residue. The P. aeruginosa version has 2 Gly, 3 bulky aliphatics, 3 Lys and 2 Asp. Mean side-chain volume rises from 113 to 125 Å³.

Within-clade conservation of the exact string is close to absolute: 16 of 16 E. coli K-12-like sequences carry GFGGGFK, and 65 of 66 P. aeruginosa sequences carry PGGLVKTVRDSDK, with the 66th carrying PGGLVKAVRDSDK, a single T177A change.

This loop is the one the structural literature assigns to PAM binding. Tay et al. report that "Structure-based mutagenesis analysis demonstrates that the well-defined flexible loop and a partially conserved structural motif ([FW]-X-[TH]) are involved in PAM binding and recognition, respectively" (PMID: 25420472). A regular-expression scan found no [FW]-X-[TH] match in the PAM loop of either Q46901 or A0A3S4PYN6, so that motif cannot be used to transfer residue assignments between these two proteins. Whole-protein matches exist elsewhere (FRH67, WPH277, FTT300 in E. coli; FDH143 in P. aeruginosa) and are not at the PAM site. Motif-level transfer fails here for the same reason the pairwise alignment does.

For an acidic all-helical inhibitor, a glycine hinge with almost no charge and a loop carrying three lysines and two aspartates are different docking surfaces. Koo et al. state that "Through extensive mutational analyses, we show that AcrIE3 interacts with the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) recognition site in Cas8e through its negatively charged surface residues" (PMID: 39541974), which makes the charge composition of this loop the directly relevant variable.

Finding 5: reciprocal mapping narrows the host-side candidates and removes R179

The forward analysis mapped E. coli contact residues onto P. aeruginosa. The reciprocal test asks the opposite question: after superposition, which P. aeruginosa residues land within 4.0 Å of the PAM duplex in its crystallographic position. Two methods that fail in different ways agreeing is better evidence than either alone.

The first attempt, using the global transform from the whole-protein structural alignment (TM 0.495), produced a meaningless answer: residues 135-144 with a 0.28 Å closest approach, intersecting the forward map at only 3 positions. The global transform is not usable for contact assignment and is reported here as a failed step. Rebuilding the superposition locally by Kabsch fit over the 14 high-confidence PAM-site anchors (Cα RMSD 1.96 Å) gives a usable answer: N138 (3.3 Å), T139 (3.6), G172 (2.8), G173 (1.4), L174 (1.2), V175 (1.5), K176 (2.0), D180 (2.5), S181 (2.0), D182 (2.3), K183 (2.2), Q355 (3.9), A356 (2.5), K357 (3.9). The matching E. coli crystal set at the same 4.0 Å cutoff against the PAM duplex alone is 17 residues: K116, G123, A124, T125, F158, G159, G160, G161, F162, K163, K266, K268, T270, N353, Q354, A355, S356.

Ten of the 14 reciprocal positions are already in the forward map. Four are new (T139, G172, K176, D180). One forward candidate, R179, is absent from the reciprocal set and is dropped: it points away from the duplex. The basic residues surviving both tests are K176 and K183. The reciprocal test also places two aspartates, D180 and D182, directly at the site, where E. coli has no acidic residue within 6 Å at all.

Finding 6: no structure distinguishes an occluded from an exposed PAM site

Searches of the RCSB file, search and data APIs returned no deposited P. aeruginosa type I-E Cascade structure. The only type I-E Cascade coordinates with bound dsDNA are from E. coli, for example 5H9F. The AcrIE5 study used an AlphaFold model of P. aeruginosa Cascade (PMID: 41161091), which is consistent with no experimental structure existing.

A direct occlusion test was run anyway, by superposing each apo AlphaFold model onto DNA-bound 5H9F chain A and counting clashes with the DNA. The control failed. The E. coli AlphaFold model clashes with its own protein's DNA (27 atoms within 3 Å, 17 at the PAM duplex, minimum distance 1.38 Å), and the P. aeruginosa model clashed more under the global transform (100 atoms, 42 at the PAM duplex, minimum 0.28 Å). Because the same-protein control clashes, that excess cannot be separated from apo-to-bound conformational change plus superposition error.

Redoing the comparison with the locally refined superposition removes the apparent effect entirely: the P. aeruginosa closest approach is 1.22 Å against 1.38 Å for the E. coli control, with 15 control residues within 4 Å. There is no detectable differential occlusion. This supersedes the earlier global-transform clash counts, which are reported here only as a method that did not work.

Stated plainly, as the hypothesis asks: sequence and apo models establish that the two PAM sites differ in residue identity, charge and bulk. They cannot establish that the AcrIE3 host range is caused by those residues rather than by a conformational or dynamic difference in how the PAM-reading loops move. Sequence alone cannot tell a residue-level explanation from a conformational one here.

Finding 7: bulk charge complementarity predicts the wrong host

AcrIE3 (A0SMP0, DMS3-30) is 68 aa with 6 Lys/Arg and 19 Asp/Glu, a net formal charge of −13 at pH 7, an acidic fraction of 27.9%, and a computed isoelectric point of 3.94. This confirms from sequence the negatively charged character reported for the crystal structure: "The crystal structure of AcrIE3 exhibits an all-helical fold with a negatively charged surface" (PMID: 39541974).

The target it inhibits is the less basic of the two. Whole-protein pI is 5.48 for P. aeruginosa Cas8e and 8.55 for E. coli K-12 Cse1. The same reversal holds locally: the 6 Å shell is +4 in E. coli and 0 in P. aeruginosa. Charge complementarity alone predicts that an inhibitor at −13 should prefer the E. coli site, which is the opposite of the observed host range. Outer shells are matched (12 Å: net +3 in both), so this is not an artefact of shell choice.

This is a negative result against the electrostatic reading the seed hypothesis proposes, and it matters because that reading is the main motivation for looking at charge at all. If charge is part of the explanation it cannot be acting through net or patch complementarity. It would have to act through a small number of specific paired contacts, for example K176 and K183 engaging particular carboxylates on AcrIE3, with D180 and D182 contributing shape and desolvation. The alternative reading is that charge is secondary and the discrimination is set by loop shape, length and conformation, which the 6-residue insertion would support.

Finding 8: the PAM site is a convergent anti-CRISPR target, and PAM blockade can be allosteric

Three structural studies place type I Acr proteins at the Cas8e PAM recognition site of P. aeruginosa Cascade. AcrIE3 contacts it through acidic surface residues (PMID: 39541974). For AcrIE5, Hong et al. report that "The mobile loop and α-helices of AcrIE5 mediated key interactions with Cas8e at the PAM recognition site, as well as with adjacent Cas7e and Cas5e" (PMID: 41161091).

Other AcrIE proteins bind elsewhere: AcrIE9 targets Cas7e (PMID: 41458398), AcrIE8.1 binds Cas11 (PMID: 41137488), and AcrIE7 either binds Cas3 (PMID: 40318042) or the R-loop single-stranded DNA (PMID: 40178896). The PAM site is therefore a repeatedly chosen target, which makes a PAM-site explanation for AcrIE3 host range mechanistically reasonable.

Two observations from the same literature set open competing explanations. Hu et al. report for type I-C that "Structural analysis showed that AcrIC8 inhibits PAM recognition through allosteric inhibition, whereas AcrIC9 achieves so through direct competition." (PMID: 38242128), so blocking PAM recognition does not require contacting the PAM residues. Kang et al. report that "In vitro binding assays using individually purified type I-E Cas subunits from P. aeruginosa did not detect direct interaction with AcrIE9, including with Cas7e." (PMID: 41458398), so AcrIE binding can require the assembled Cascade. Both the AcrIE5 and AcrIE7 studies report the same dependence. Cas8e sequence comparison alone cannot address either possibility.

Finding 9: AcrIE3 carries one dominant acidic surface patch of seven residues

Every residue-level candidate above sits on the host protein. The gene under review is A0SMP0, so the analysis was moved onto it.

AlphaFold DB has no model for A0SMP0: v2, v3, v4, v6 and the prediction API all return 404. This is reported as a plain resource-access fact. Two experimental AcrIE3 crystal structures exist instead: 8HEL at 1.33 Å (Koo et al., PMID: 39541974) and 8ZEY at 1.73 Å (Kim et al., PMID: 38797150).

Shrake-Rupley solvent accessibility on 8HEL chain A (68 residues, all modelled, total SASA 4820 Å²) gives 17 solvent-exposed acidic residues at RSA above 0.25 (D6, E11, E14, E19, D21, E22, E35, D41, D42, E45, D46, D53, E54, E61, E64, D66, E67) against 5 exposed basic residues (K2, R26, R34, K57, R60). Exposed acidic residues cover 1802 Å², 37.4% of the surface; exposed basic residues cover 695 Å², 14.4%. Linking carboxylate centroids below 10 Å gives patch sizes 7, 2, 2, 2, 2, 1, 1. The dominant patch is E35, D53, E54, E61, E64, D66, E67, spanning 27.9 Å with 723 Å² of exposed area, concentrated toward the C-terminal helix. Patches 2 and 3 are D6/E11 (8.4 Å, 204 Å²) and D21/E22 (7.2 Å, 149 Å²).

Kim et al. independently report that "Additionally, surface analysis of AcrIE3 revealed the presence of three acidic regions, which potentially play a crucial role in the inhibitory function of this protein.", on a separate crystal structure, and describe the fold: "Our structural examination showed that AcrIE3 adopts a helical bundle fold comprising four α-helices, with a notably extended loop at the N-terminus." (PMID: 38797150). The patch decomposition computed here on 8HEL is consistent with that independent description.

A geometric compatibility check was run and should be read as permissive only. K176 and K183, the two basic residues surviving both mappings at the P. aeruginosa PAM site, are 17.9 Å apart measured NZ to NZ. The closest-matching carboxylate pair on AcrIE3 patch 1, E54 to D66, is 17.5 Å apart. That match is not evidence: the 21 carboxylate pairs within patch 1 span 7.0 to 27.7 Å, so some pair matches almost any target distance. The usable statement is the negative one, that no spacing on the patch is incompatible with the loop, so geometry does not exclude the proposed contact. The D180 to D182 carboxylate spacing on the host loop is 10.7 Å.

Mechanistic model

The model the evidence supports is a species-level rebuild of the Cas8e PAM-reading surface, with AcrIE3 tuned to one version of it. The causal step from "the surface differs" to "the inhibitor fails" is not demonstrated, and the diagram marks that link as uncertain.

Three branches reach the observed phenotype and the available data cannot choose between them. The residue-identity branch has named candidates on both sides and geometry that does not exclude it. The conformational branch cannot be tested without a P. aeruginosa Cascade structure, and the occlusion test that would have constrained it returned no differential signal once the superposition was done fairly. The allosteric or assembly branch is supported by direct precedent in type I-C and by the repeated finding that AcrIE proteins need assembled Cascade.

What the model does exclude is the simplest version of the seed reasoning. Bulk electrostatic attraction between an acidic inhibitor and a basic PAM pocket predicts E. coli as the preferred host, which is wrong. Charge, if it matters here, acts through a small number of specific pairings and through desolvation, not through overall complementarity.

GO curation implications

All items below are leads requiring curator verification.

The evidence supports a molecular function annotation for A0SMP0 describing inhibition of a host CRISPR-Cas defence complex by binding its large subunit. Two independent crystal structures of the protein exist (8HEL at 1.33 Å, PMID: 39541974; 8ZEY at 1.73 Å, PMID: 38797150), and the first reports extensive mutational analysis placing the interaction at the Cas8e PAM recognition site through negatively charged surface residues. That is a direct, assayed molecular interaction with a named partner and a named site, so a more informative term than "protein binding" is supported and plain "protein binding" should not be the final recommendation.

The sensible shape of the annotation is a molecular function term for the inhibitory activity against the host complex, with the interaction partner recorded through the appropriate extension or with field naming P. aeruginosa Cas8e, and a biological process term covering evasion or suppression of host defence by a viral gene product. A cellular component term is not supported by anything in this analysis: no localisation experiment appears in the retrieved literature.

The species restriction belongs in the evidence and qualifier layer, not in the term. The finding that the inhibition is specific to the P. aeruginosa type I-E system is an experimental fact about the assay context (PMID: 24736222), and the present analysis adds that the structural basis for that restriction sits in a species-fixed difference in the target. A curator should record the organism context of the assay and leave the GO term itself unnarrowed.

The incidental finding from the Cas8e survey is worth passing to whoever curates the host side. Entries annotated "type I-E CRISPR-associated protein Cse1/CasA" across E. coli, Klebsiella and P. aeruginosa belong to clades 21-25% identical to E. coli K-12 CasA. Residue-level or mechanism-level annotation transferred across that label by homology is not safe, and the PAM-contact positions in particular are 14% identical.

Mechanistic scope

The immediate molecular activity being tested is binding of a 68-residue phage protein to the large subunit of a multi-subunit host surveillance complex, at the surface that reads the protospacer adjacent motif, with the consequence that the complex cannot engage target DNA productively. That is one binding event and one blocked step.

Everything else in the chain is downstream. Phage survival in a CRISPR-positive host is a phenotype of the blocked step, not an activity of A0SMP0. The failure against E. coli K-12 is a property of the interaction, established by the observation that all four Pseudomonas type I-E anti-CRISPRs fail against an induced E. coli system while being expressed to Coomassie-visible levels; that fact is part of the supplied context and was not re-derived here.

Two scope boundaries matter for curation. The PAM recognition site assignment comes from mutational analysis on the P. aeruginosa system (PMID: 39541974), so the direct activity is documented against one host system only. Several AcrIE proteins fail to bind individually purified Cas subunits and need assembled Cascade (PMID: 41458398), which leaves open whether the functional unit AcrIE3 recognises is Cas8e alone or a composite Cas8e-Cas7e-Cas5e surface. The AcrIE5 contact map, which extends onto Cas7e and Cas5e (PMID: 41161091), makes the composite possibility concrete.

Conflicts and alternatives

The strongest conflict is internal to the hypothesis. Its stated motivation is that "an acidic inhibitor surface docking at a DNA-phosphate-reading site is sensitive to exactly that", meaning charge and bulk. The charge test runs the wrong way: AcrIE3 at net −13 and pI 3.94 inhibits the Cas8e with pI 5.48 and a net-0 inner shell, and fails against the Cse1 with pI 8.55 and a net +4 inner shell. The bulk half of the reasoning survives, since the P. aeruginosa loop adds about 100 Å³ at the base-contacting positions and is 6 residues longer.

The allosteric alternative is backed by direct precedent. AcrIC8 inhibits PAM recognition without competing for the site, while AcrIC9 competes (PMID: 38242128). If AcrIE3 works even partly allosterically, the residues at the PAM site are not necessarily the ones that determine host range, and the whole comparison would be aimed at the wrong surface.

The assembly alternative is backed by repeated negative binding data. AcrIE9 shows no detected interaction with individually purified type I-E Cas subunits (PMID: 41458398), and the AcrIE5 and AcrIE7 studies report similar behaviour. If AcrIE3 recognises a composite interface, Cas8e sequence comparison is incomplete by construction, since Cas7e and Cas5e differences are not in the comparison at all.

A methodological conflict sits inside the analysis and is reported rather than smoothed over. The forward and reciprocal mappings disagree at four positions out of fourteen, and the reciprocal mapping removed R179 from the candidate list after the forward mapping had nominated it. The global-transform version of the reciprocal test gave an answer that was clearly wrong (residues 135-144, 0.28 Å closest approach), which is why the superposition was refit locally. Any residue-level candidate here carries that method dependence.

The AcrIE family itself argues against assuming a single mechanism. AcrIE8.1 binds Cas11 (PMID: 41137488), AcrIE9 binds Cas7e (PMID: 41458398), and the two AcrIE7 papers disagree about whether its target is Cas3 (PMID: 40318042) or R-loop single-stranded DNA (PMID: 40178896). Family membership carries no mechanistic information.

Limitations and knowledge gaps

No P. aeruginosa type I-E Cascade structure exists. Checked: the RCSB file, search and data APIs; the AcrIE5 study used an AlphaFold model of the complex, consistent with none existing. This is the single limitation that caps the whole analysis, because it is what prevents separating a residue-identity explanation from a conformational one. A cryo-EM structure of P. aeruginosa Cascade, with DNA and with AcrIE3, would resolve it.

The structural mapping is model-to-model across the twilight zone. Checked: TM 0.495 for the cross-species superposition against 0.834 for the same-protein control; 14 of 21 forward mappings have Cα distance below 3.5 Å. Seven mappings are flagged unreliable and no conclusion rests on them alone. The residue correspondences are candidates, not assignments.

The occlusion test has no clean positive control. Checked: superposing the E. coli apo AlphaFold model onto its own DNA-bound structure produces 27 clashing atoms and a 1.38 Å closest approach, so apo-to-bound conformational change alone generates a clash signal of the size being measured. The locally refined comparison shows no differential occlusion (1.22 against 1.38 Å), which is the honest reading, and it means the conformational branch is untested rather than excluded.

Whether AcrIE3 binds isolated Cas8e or needs assembled Cascade. Checked: the literature reports that AcrIE9, AcrIE5 and AcrIE7 show weak or undetectable binding to individual purified subunits. The AcrIE3 study reports interaction with the Cas8e PAM recognition site through mutational analysis, and the retrieved abstract does not state the binding format. This matters because the answer decides whether a Cas8e-only comparison is the right experiment.

The original host-range paper could not be retrieved in full. Checked: search_pubmed returned the abstract of Pawluk et al. 2014 (PMID: 24736222) but repeated targeted queries failed to return more, and the full text was not accessible through the available tools. The abstract states that the four type I-E anti-CRISPR families do not inhibit the E. coli type I-E system, which is the fact the seed hypothesis supplies. No residue-level or expression-level detail is quoted from that paper here, and none should be inferred.

Sequence sampling is NCBI-biased. Checked: 67 unique P. aeruginosa sequences drawn from more than 515 entries, 19 E. coli K-12-like, 43 other Pseudomonas, 54 other Enterobacteriaceae. Clinical isolates dominate public P. aeruginosa sequencing, so the 98.5% within-species identity may overstate conservation in the wider environmental population.

The E. coli side is constrained only within K-12-like strains. Checked: 0 of 54 non-E. coli Enterobacteriaceae Cas8e sequences pass a 40% identity filter against Q46901 (median 23.1%, maximum 24.9%). The claim that the E. coli PAM-site residues are 100% conserved applies to 16 K-12-like sequences, which is a narrow base.

No model exists for A0SMP0 in AlphaFold DB. Checked: v2, v3, v4, v6 and api/prediction/A0SMP0 all return 404. Two experimental structures cover the gap, so this limits only automated pipelines that expect an AlphaFold entry, which is worth noting for curators who rely on them.

Discriminating tests

  1. Reciprocal L1 loop swap. Replace E. coli Cse1 157-163 (GFGGGFK) with P. aeruginosa 171-183 (PGGLVKTVRDSDK) and the reverse, then assay AcrIE3 inhibition of each chimeric Cascade. Gain of AcrIE3 sensitivity in the E. coli chimera would make the residue-identity explanation causal. No change in either direction would push the explanation toward conformation or toward the composite interface. This is the single most informative experiment available and it tests the central claim directly.

  2. Point mutants at the host candidates. Mutate P. aeruginosa K176 and K183 individually and together, plus D180 and D182, and measure AcrIE3 binding and inhibition. The prediction from the specific-pairing model is that K176A and K183A reduce binding while D180A and D182A do less, since the model assigns them shape and desolvation roles.

  3. Bulk against charge separation. Mutate P. aeruginosa L174 and V175 to glycine, recreating the E. coli hinge composition without changing charge. If inhibition is lost, bulk and loop rigidity carry the discrimination. If inhibition persists, charge-mediated pairing is the better model.

  4. Binding format test. Measure AcrIE3 affinity for purified P. aeruginosa Cas8e alone and for assembled Cascade, by isothermal titration calorimetry or surface plasmon resonance. A large difference would show that the functional target is a composite interface, which would mean a Cas8e-only sequence comparison cannot answer the host-range question.

  5. Cryo-EM of P. aeruginosa Cascade. Apo, DNA-bound and AcrIE3-bound. This is the only route to a direct answer on whether the PAM site is occluded in one organism and exposed in the other, and it would convert the candidate residue list into an observed contact map.

  6. Inhibitor-side alanine scan. Mutate E35, D53, E54, E61, E64, D66 and E67 on AcrIE3, individually and as the full patch, and assay inhibition of P. aeruginosa Cascade. This tests the inhibitor half of the model with residues taken from a 1.33 Å experimental structure, and it is independent of every structural mapping assumption in this report.

  7. Hydrogen-deuterium exchange on both Cas8e proteins. Compare exchange rates across the L1 loop in each protein, apo and DNA-bound. Different loop dynamics in the absence of residue-contact differences would favour the conformational branch, which no static comparison can reach.

  8. Test the clade prediction. Assay AcrIE3 against type I-E systems from Pseudomonas species outside P. aeruginosa, where the PAM-site positions are 53-93% conserved rather than ≥97%. Graded loss of inhibition tracking PAM-site divergence would support the residue-identity model across a natural series.
