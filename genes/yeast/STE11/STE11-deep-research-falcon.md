# Research report: *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* STE11 (UniProt P23561)

## Executive conclusion

**Identity is verified.** The requested protein is the budding-yeast **Ste11 MAP kinase kinase kinase (MAP3K/MEKK)** encoded by **STE11/YLR362W** in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* strain S288c, consistent with UniProt **P23561**. The literature identifies this protein as a shared upstream kinase in three principal MAPK outputs: pheromone-induced mating, filamentous/invasive growth, and the Sho1 branch of the high-osmolarity glycerol (HOG) response. This target must not be confused with the unrelated *Schizosaccharomyces pombe* protein called Ste11 or with Ste11 homologs from other fungi.

Ste11’s primary biochemical role is to transfer phosphate from ATP to serine/threonine residues in pathway-specific MAPK kinases. Its best-established physiological substrates are **Ste7**, in mating and filamentous-growth signaling, and **Pbs2**, in Sho1-dependent HOG signaling. Its decisive biological role is therefore not simply “a stress kinase,” but a **shared MAP3K whose localization into distinct scaffolded complexes routes information to different MAPKs and cellular responses**. Direct kinetic constants and a comprehensive physiological substrate map remain limited.

## 1. Identity and domain verification

The S. cerevisiae literature explicitly identifies **STE11/YLR362W** as encoding a MEKK-family MAP3K that regulates mating, invasive growth, and osmotic-stress signaling. Its catalytic domain is homologous to mammalian MEKK-family kinases, matching the supplied protein-kinase-superfamily and STE Ser/Thr-family annotation (wang2011ste11pmekksignals pages 1-3).

The experimentally characterized architecture also agrees with the supplied domain annotation. Ste11 has a multifunctional **N-terminal regulatory region** and a **C-terminal kinase domain**. Residues **26–129** have SAM-domain similarity and are required for binding the Ste50 adaptor; a neighboring region, approximately residues **133–335**, contributes to Ste5 association and negative regulation of catalytic activity. Deletion of this inhibitory region or activating mutations can bypass normal Ste50 dependence (wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 12-14, wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 10-10). Interaction between the N-terminal regulatory and C-terminal catalytic regions supports an autoinhibitory model, while Ste50 binding can disrupt this interaction and promote Ste11 activation (wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 1-2).

The supplied Ras-binding-like domain annotation should be interpreted cautiously. The strongest experimental recruitment mechanism involves the **RA domain of Ste50**, which links the Ste11–Ste50 complex to membrane-associated proteins, rather than establishing Ste11 itself as a conventional Ras effector (sharmeen2020molecularroleofa pages 38-42, michal2011coordinationofmap pages 117-119).

## 2. Molecular function, reaction, and substrate specificity

Ste11 is an ATP-dependent protein serine/threonine kinase classified as a MAP3K. Its generic reaction is:

**ATP + protein serine/threonine-OH → ADP + phosphorylated protein.**

### Canonical substrates

1. **Ste7 MAPKK.** Ste11 phosphorylates and activates Ste7 in the mating and filamentous-growth cascades. In-vitro reconstitution and genetic ordering place Ste11 above Ste7 and the downstream MAPKs Fus3/Kss1 (herskowitz1995mapkinasepathways pages 11-11).  
2. **Pbs2 MAPKK.** In the Sho1 HOG branch, activated Ste11 phosphorylates Pbs2, which activates Hog1. This branch is distinct from the Sln1 branch, in which Ssk2/Ssk22 substitute for Ste11 upstream of Pbs2 (wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 1-2, wang2011ste11pmekksignals pages 1-3).

These are the appropriate substrates for a primary functional annotation. Ste50 can also be phosphorylated by Ste11, but its dominant role is as an adaptor and regulator, not as the principal MAPKK output (wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 1-2).

A 2011 study connected activated Ste11 to Pbs2, Ste7, Cmk2, Ptk2, and separate stimulation of Mpk1/Slt2 in regulation of the cell-wall gene **FKS2**. However, Cmk2 and Ptk2 were described as putative/context-dependent targets, while Mpk1 activation was a pathway-level effect. These should not be treated as having the same direct biochemical status as Ste7 and Pbs2 (wang2011ste11pmekksignals pages 1-3, wang2011ste11pmekksignals pages 13-15).

## 3. Activation and regulation

### Ste20 and Cdc42

Ste11 is activated principally by **Ste20**, a p21-activated kinase associated with active Cdc42. In mating, filamentation, and Sho1-HOG signaling, assembly near the plasma membrane brings Ste11 into proximity with Ste20, enabling activating phosphorylation. Cdc42 is therefore an upstream spatial organizer and Ste20 regulator, not a direct phosphodonor to Ste11 (wang2011ste11pmekksignals pages 1-3, sharmeen2020molecularroleofa pages 38-42).

### Ste50 and autoinhibition

Ste50 binds Ste11 through SAM-containing N-terminal regions. Biochemical experiments found that Ste50 can relieve the inhibitory interaction between Ste11’s regulatory and catalytic portions and modulate Ste11 autophosphorylation. The Ste11–Ste50 interaction appeared constitutive in pull-down experiments and did not detectably change following α-factor or **0.4 M NaCl**, suggesting that regulation depends more on localization, conformation, and partner assembly than on simple inducible binding (wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 1-2, wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 12-14).

Ste50 is essential for normal Sho1-branch signaling but less absolutely required for mating, where the Ste5 scaffold provides another strong recruitment route. Constitutively active Ste11 alleles can bypass Ste50 requirements, supporting a regulatory rather than downstream catalytic role for Ste50 (wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 1-2, wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 12-14).

## 4. Pathway-specific functions

### 4.1 Pheromone response and mating

The core kinase sequence is **Ste20 → Ste11 → Ste7 → Fus3/Kss1**. The Ste5 scaffold binds pathway components and recruits the module to Gβγ-associated plasma-membrane sites following pheromone receptor activation. This assembly positions Ste11 near Ste20 and channels its activity toward Ste7, ultimately producing mating-specific transcription, cell-cycle arrest, polarized “shmoo” growth, and cell fusion (wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 1-2, shu2024extrabutnot pages 23-28, sharmeen2020molecularroleof pages 38-42).

Ste5 does more than increase reaction efficiency: it contributes to specificity. It preferentially organizes Ste7 and Fus3, whereas Kss1 has different scaffold dependence. This architecture helps a shared Ste11–Ste7 module produce mating rather than filamentation output (shu2024extrabutnot pages 23-28).

### 4.2 Filamentous and invasive growth

Under appropriate nutrient and surface conditions, Ste11 functions in the filamentous-growth pathway, principally **Cdc42/Ste20 → Ste11 → Ste7 → Kss1**. Kss1-dependent transcription drives polarized growth, adhesion, haploid invasion, or diploid pseudohyphal development, depending on cell type and genetic background. Ste11 and its activators are shared with other pathways; specificity arises from upstream sensors and spatially organized complexes rather than a filamentation-exclusive Ste11 isoform (wang2011ste11pmekksignals pages 1-3, shu2024extrabutnot pages 23-28).

Ste50’s RA domain is important for delivering Ste11-containing complexes to the plasma membrane during filamentous-growth signaling. This places Ste11 near Cdc42-associated Ste20 and pathway-specific surface inputs (michal2011coordinationofmap pages 117-119). A key qualification is that filamentation is strongly strain- and condition-dependent; S288c phenotypes should not automatically be extrapolated to highly filamentous laboratory backgrounds.

### 4.3 Sho1 branch of the HOG pathway

Hyperosmotic stress activates Hog1 through two upstream branches. The **Sho1 branch** employs a membrane-associated Ste20/Ste11/Pbs2 module, whereas the parallel **Sln1 branch** uses Ssk1 and the MAP3Ks Ssk2/Ssk22. Both converge on Pbs2 and Hog1 (wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 1-2).

In the Sho1 branch, Sho1 and Pbs2 operate as organizing elements, while Opy2–Ste50 recruits Ste11 to the membrane. Juxtaposition of Ste11 with Pbs2 favors selective Pbs2 phosphorylation and subsequent Hog1 activation. This organization explains why Ste11 is essential for Sho1 signaling but dispensable for the Sln1 route when that route remains intact (wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 12-14, michal2011coordinationofmap pages 117-119, shu2024extrabutnot pages 23-28).

In backgrounds lacking the parallel two-component branch, Ste11 kinase activity becomes essential for hyperosmotic survival, and deletion of STE50 phenocopies deletion of STE11. Constitutively activated Ste11 bypasses this Ste50 requirement, reinforcing the conclusion that Ste50 recruits and unlocks Ste11 rather than replacing its kinase function (wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 12-14).

## 5. Subcellular localization

Ste11 is best viewed as a **soluble signaling enzyme that is transiently recruited to plasma-membrane/cortical complexes**, rather than as a constitutive membrane or organelle protein.

* During mating, membrane-recruited Ste5 positions Ste11 near Cdc42/Ste20 and the Ste7–Fus3 module. Ste50–Cdc42 interactions may provide an additional recruitment route (sharmeen2020molecularroleofa pages 38-42, sharmeen2020molecularroleof pages 38-42).
* During filamentous growth, Ste50-mediated delivery places Ste11 at polarized plasma-membrane sites where upstream sensors, Cdc42, and Ste20 are organized (michal2011coordinationofmap pages 117-119).
* During Sho1-HOG signaling, Opy2–Ste50 recruits Ste11, while Sho1-associated Pbs2 brings the MAPKK substrate into the same membrane complex (sharmeen2020molecularroleofa pages 193-198, michal2011coordinationofmap pages 117-119).

Thus, the location where Ste11 carries out its primary physiological function is the **cytoplasmic face of stimulus-specific plasma-membrane signaling complexes**, followed by propagation through cytoplasmic kinase cascades. Nuclear accumulation is characteristic of downstream MAPKs such as Hog1, Fus3, and Kss1, not the defining localization of Ste11.

## 6. Specificity, feedback, and crosstalk

The central conceptual issue is how one MAP3K can serve several pathways without indiscriminately activating all outputs. The current model combines:

* **scaffold selection:** Ste5 routes Ste11 toward Ste7/Fus3 in mating; Pbs2/Sho1 routes it toward Pbs2/Hog1 in HOG signaling;
* **membrane recruitment:** distinct receptor, G-protein, Opy2, Sho1, Cdc42, and Ste50 assemblies position the kinase;
* **downstream MAPK preferences and phosphatases:** Fus3, Kss1, and Hog1 have different scaffold relationships and negative regulators;
* **feedback insulation:** activated Hog1 helps prevent Sho1-derived signals from spilling into mating outputs (shu2024extrabutnot pages 23-28).

Classic experiments showed that loss of **HOG1** or **PBS2** allowed osmotic stress—tested with **1 M sorbitol**—to activate pheromone-pathway outputs. This crosstalk required Sho1, Ste20, Ste50, Ste11, Ste7, Fus3 or Kss1, and Ste12, but not the pheromone Gβ component Ste4 or the Ste5 scaffold. These findings demonstrate that Ste11 is intrinsically capable of transmitting a Sho1-originating signal toward mating machinery when normal Hog1-dependent insulation is lost (o’rourke1998thehog1mapk pages 1-2).

Broader crosstalk extends to cell-wall regulation. Activated Ste11 stimulated FKS2 expression through contributions from HOG, mating, calcineurin/Crz1, and PKC-associated routes and increased Mpk1/Slt2 signaling independently of the canonical mating output. In a **ste7Δ pbs2Δ** background, activated Ste11-driven FKS2 expression was approximately **threefold lower** than in relevant single-mutant comparisons, illustrating redundancy and compensatory routing. These results are informative but secondary to Ste11’s canonical MAP3K annotation (wang2011ste11pmekksignals pages 1-3, wang2011ste11pmekksignals pages 13-15).

## 7. Recent research: 2024 update

The most relevant recent study is Zhang et al., published in *Molecular Biology of the Cell* in **October 2024**: [doi:10.1091/mbc.e24-06-0270](https://doi.org/10.1091/mbc.e24-06-0270). It examined concurrent salt and pheromone inputs through phospho-MAPK measurements, nuclear translocation, reporters, and transcriptomics. Importantly, the study treated Ste11 as shared upstream pathway architecture but **did not directly perturb Ste11**, so its results refine the operating context of Ste11 rather than its catalytic mechanism (zhang2024sharedandredundant pages 1-2).

Key findings were:

* Sho1 supported the **duration** of Hog1 activation, while Sln1 strongly influenced activation **amplitude**; Sln1 also restrained aspects of Hog1-dependent transcription (zhang2024sharedandredundant pages 1-2, shu2024extrabutnot pages 34-37).
* When signaling was forced predominantly through Sho1 in **ssk1Δ** cells, salt activated Kss1 and partially induced mating transcription. Kss1 phosphorylation peaked at approximately **5–10 minutes**, whereas pheromone-induced Fus3 activation peaked within about **5 minutes** and was comparatively insensitive to salt or branch genotype (shu2024extrabutnot pages 37-41).
* Transcriptomics used **350 mM KCl for 15 minutes**. More than **5,920** salt-responsive transcripts were unaffected by deletion of either HOG branch, showing substantial redundancy, but **48 genes** were selectively induced in ssk1Δ cells and enriched for mating functions; seven were selectively induced in Sho1-deficient cells and enriched for iron/siderophore transport (shu2024extrabutnot pages 34-37, shu2024extrabutnot pages 28-34).
* The Kss1-dependent program contained **108 differentially expressed genes**. Under Sho1-dominant signaling, representative mating genes included **FUS1** (log2 fold change 2.56; adjusted P=1.48×10−27), **PRM1** (2.18; 1.78×10−21), and **BAR1** (1.14; 4.95×10−20) (shu2024extrabutnot pages 37-41, shu2024extrabutnot pages 55-58).

Expert interpretation is that pathway “redundancy” is only partial: the two HOG inputs converge on Hog1 but shape different dynamics and crosstalk. Since the Sho1 branch uses Ste11 whereas the Sln1 branch does not, these findings strengthen the view that Ste11-containing membrane assemblies are a major locus of signal coordination. They do not, however, show a new Ste11 substrate or a changed catalytic reaction.

No directly relevant 2023–2024 study retrieved here superseded the classic biochemical evidence for Ste11 activation, substrate choice, or localization. For those questions, older focused experiments remain more authoritative than newer broad surveys.

## 8. Applications and real-world relevance

Ste11 has no established clinical use and is not itself a validated therapeutic target. Its practical importance is primarily as an experimentally tractable platform for:

1. **MAPK systems biology:** dissecting scaffold-mediated specificity, feedback, dynamic encoding, and simultaneous-stimulus processing;
2. **synthetic biology:** rewiring yeast MAPK inputs and outputs, constructing signal-responsive transcription systems, and designing biosensors;
3. **industrial yeast stress research:** understanding how osmotic adaptation and morphogenesis affect fermentation robustness, although Ste11-specific engineering must be balanced against mating and filamentation crosstalk;
4. **comparative fungal biology:** providing a reference architecture for homologous stress and pathogenicity pathways in fungi.

These applications rely on the conservation and modularity of the cascade, but results from constitutively active STE11 alleles, other *S. cerevisiae* backgrounds, or Ste11 homologs in pathogenic fungi cannot be assigned directly to P23561 without species- and strain-specific validation.

## 9. Evidence-weighted annotation

**Primary annotation:** Ste11 is a regulated serine/threonine MAP3K that phosphorylates the MAPKKs Ste7 and Pbs2 after recruitment to plasma-membrane signaling complexes. It transmits pheromone, filamentation, and Sho1-dependent osmotic signals into the Fus3/Kss1 and Hog1 MAPK pathways.

**Strong direct evidence:** genetic ordering, biochemical activation of Ste7, Ste11–Ste50 binding and regulatory-domain experiments, deletion/constitutive-activation epistasis, and pathway-specific phospho-MAPK and transcriptional readouts (wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 1-2, herskowitz1995mapkinasepathways pages 11-11, wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 12-14, wu1999functionalcharacterizationof pages 10-10).

**Strong pathway-level inference:** Opy2/Ste50- and Ste5-dependent cortical recruitment, Pbs2 scaffold routing, and Hog1-mediated insulation (o’rourke1998thehog1mapk pages 1-2, sharmeen2020molecularroleof pages 38-42, michal2011coordinationofmap pages 117-119, shu2024extrabutnot pages 23-28).

**Secondary or qualified assignments:** Cmk2 and Ptk2 as possible context-dependent targets; Mpk1/Slt2 and FKS2 regulation as crosstalk outputs rather than canonical direct substrate relationships (wang2011ste11pmekksignals pages 1-3, wang2011ste11pmekksignals pages 13-15).

**Principal knowledge gaps:** quantitative full-length enzyme kinetics, a high-resolution structure of full-length regulated Ste11, stimulus-resolved endogenous Ste11 localization at native abundance, and a definitive proteome-wide catalogue separating direct physiological substrates from indirect pathway effects.
