Gene Description

TAS2R38 (T2R38) encodes a class-A-like, seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled bitter taste receptor of the Pan troglodytes TAS2R (taste receptor type 2) family. It is a single-exon gene whose product is expected to reside in the plasma membrane of bitter-responsive taste receptor cells of the tongue, where it detects phenylthiocarbamide (PTC) and structurally related thiourea/thiocarbamide (N-C=S-bearing) bitter compounds, initiating gustducin-linked signaling: coupling to a heterotrimeric G protein, activation of phospholipase C-beta-2, IP3-mediated Ca2+ release from the endoplasmic reticulum, and TRPM5-dependent depolarization, culminating in neurotransmitter release onto gustatory afferents. As in humans, chimpanzee populations show genetically determined variation in PTC-type bitter sensitivity. A start-codon-loss allele (ATG to AGG) that would force downstream translation initiation at Met97 and delete the first 96 residues is common in western chimpanzee populations but essentially absent from central, eastern, and Nigeria-Cameroon populations, and this reduction in PTC sensitivity arose through mutations distinct from those underlying the analogous human PAV/AVI polymorphism, indicating independent evolutionary origins in the two lineages.

Functional annotation report: chimpanzee TAS2R38 (UniProt Q697L5)

Executive summary

The requested identity is internally consistent: TAS2R38 in Pan troglodytes encodes taste receptor type 2 member 38, a membrane bitter-taste receptor of the T2R/TAS2R family. The supplied InterPro/Pfam assignments—TAS2R (IPR007960), TAS2R38 (IPR030050), and PF05296—agree with current classification of T2Rs as a distinct class-T family of seven-transmembrane G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). No conflicting gene with the same symbol was identified. The accession-to-protein mapping itself is accepted from the supplied UniProt record because the available search tools did not independently query UniProt.

The best-supported primary function is recognition of phenylthiocarbamide (PTC) and related thiourea/N–C=S bitter chemicals, initiating an aversive gustatory signal. Direct chimpanzee evidence links TAS2R38 variation to a bimodal PTC-tasting phenotype. Particularly strong population-genetic evidence identifies a start-codon-loss allele, ATG→AGG, enriched in western chimpanzees and predicted or experimentally supported to abolish normal receptor function. Reduced PTC sensitivity evolved independently in humans and chimpanzees through different mutations after their lineages separated, so human TAS2R38 haplotypes must not be transferred to Q697L5 (meyerhof2011molecularbiologyof pages 8-8, campbell2012evolutionoffunctionally pages 10-11).

The receptor is expected to act at the plasma membrane of bitter-responsive taste cells and couple to the canonical T2R calcium-signaling pathway. However, native localization, complete signaling, natural ligand spectrum, and extraoral roles have not been directly established for chimpanzee Q697L5. These are orthology- or family-based annotations rather than chimpanzee-specific experimental findings.

1. Identity verification and molecular classification

Gene/protein match. Chimpanzee studies explicitly designate this locus cTAS2R38, one of the TAS2R genes in Pan troglodytes. The description “taste receptor type 2 member 38” and membership in the T2R GPCR family therefore agree with the supplied gene symbol, species, accession, and domains (hayakawa2012ecogeographicaldiversificationof pages 2-3).

Protein architecture. Current T2R-family understanding describes these receptors as class-T, seven-transmembrane GPCRs with an extracellular N-terminus and intracellular C-terminus. T2Rs generally have very short N-terminal regions, lack the class-A-like disulfide bond between transmembrane helix III and extracellular loop 2, and contain activation-associated motifs including F3.49Y3.50xxK3.53 and H7.49S/P7.50xxL7.53. The relatively unconstrained extracellular region may support a flexible ligand-binding pocket. These are family-level structural features, not a solved experimental structure of chimpanzee TAS2R38 (itoigawa2024theremarkablediversity pages 11-12).

Accordingly, Q697L5 is not an enzyme or transporter. It is a chemosensory signaling receptor: extracellular bitter-ligand recognition is transduced across the membrane into intracellular G-protein signaling.

2. Primary molecular function and ligand specificity

The strongest chimpanzee-specific assignment is detection of PTC-class bitterness. Chimpanzees display genetically determined bimodal sensitivity to compounds containing an N–C=S group, and mutations in chimpanzee TAS2R38 account for this variation. The mutations differ from those underlying human TAS2R38 variation, demonstrating independent origins of reduced sensitivity (meyerhof2011molecularbiologyof pages 8-8).

PTC is principally an experimental probe rather than a demonstrated ecological ligand. Across primate TAS2R38 orthologs, recognized or candidate ligands include PTC, 6-n-propylthiouracil (PROP), sinigrin, allyl isothiocyanate, and goitrin. Nevertheless, the available comparative study used chimpanzee mainly as a sequence reference and did not report potency values for Q697L5. These broader compounds should therefore be treated as candidate chimpanzee ligands, not a fully validated Q697L5 pharmacological profile (purba2020evolutionofthe pages 1-2).

No reliable chimpanzee-specific EC50, binding affinity, Hill coefficient, or complete agonist panel was recovered. Functional assays relevant to chimpanzee constructs used heterologous HEK293T cells, the chimeric G protein Gα16gust44, Fluo-4 calcium imaging at two-second intervals, and concentration–response modeling. Such assays establish receptor-dependent signaling capability but do not reproduce native taste-cell physiology (suzukihashido2015rapidexpansionof pages 16-18).

3. Cellular localization and biological process

Probable native site of action

As a seven-transmembrane chemoreceptor, functional TAS2R38 is expected to reside in the plasma membrane of bitter-responsive taste-receptor cells, with its ligand-facing surface exposed to the oral environment. Its biological role is to convert contact with selected bitter chemicals into a cellular signal that contributes to bitter perception and potentially avoidance of unsuitable foods. General mammalian T2R evidence supports membrane receptor function and taste-cell chemical-to-neural transduction, but direct immunolocalization, in situ hybridization, or single-cell expression data for chimpanzee TAS2R38 were not found (meyerhof2011molecularbiologyof pages 2-4).

Thus, “taste-bud cell plasma membrane” is a strong orthology-based localization, but it should not be represented as experimentally demonstrated for Q697L5. Evidence that macaque TAS2R38 occurs in intestinal cells, or that human TAS2R38 occurs in airway and other extraoral tissues, does not prove corresponding expression in chimpanzees.

Canonical signaling pathway

The expected pathway is:

bitter agonist → TAS2R38 activation → heterotrimeric G-protein signaling, commonly gustducin-associated → PLCβ2 activation → IP3 production → Ca²⁺ release from endoplasmic-reticulum stores through IP3 receptors → TRPM5 activation and membrane depolarization → transmitter output to gustatory afferents.

The recovered mammalian review directly supports ER calcium release through IP3 receptor type 3 and subsequent TRPM5 activation, while classifying T2Rs as heptahelical GPCRs (meyerhof2011molecularbiologyof pages 2-4). The full chain, including gustducin, PLCβ2, and ATP-mediated afferent signaling, is accepted canonical mammalian bitter-taste biology but was not directly demonstrated in native chimpanzee TAS2R38-expressing cells. The most defensible annotation is therefore that Q697L5 probably uses the conserved T2R gustatory pathway, with species-specific verification outstanding.

4. Allelic variation, functional loss, and evolutionary evidence

The most informative chimpanzee variant changes the normal initiation codon from ATG to AGG. Translation beginning at a downstream Met97 would produce a protein missing the first 96 residues, a major truncation expected to impair membrane topology, trafficking, or signaling. Heterologous calcium-assay work examined this downstream-initiation construct and an engineered rescued control, supporting the interpretation that the start-codon mutation is functionally consequential (suzukihashido2015rapidexpansionof pages 16-18).

In the 2012 subspecies survey, the start-loss allele occurred in 70 of 92 western-chimpanzee alleles (76%). It was absent from the sampled eastern (0/20), central (0/4), and Nigerian–Cameroonian (0/2) alleles. West–east differentiation was high, with FST = 0.614, and the derived allele was restricted to western chimpanzees in that dataset (hayakawa2012ecogeographicaldiversificationof pages 2-3). These figures are compelling but should be interpreted in light of uneven and very small samples for several subspecies.

The nonfunctional allele was inferred to have arisen approximately 0.5 million years ago. Authors have discussed drift, balancing selection, and environmental selection associated with dietary or floral change, but the precise selective agent remains unresolved; the age and adaptive narrative are evolutionary inferences rather than direct functional demonstrations (hayakawa2012ecogeographicaldiversificationof pages 6-7).

Comparative work indicates that PTC tasting was ancestral in humans and chimpanzees, while reduced sensitivity evolved independently after the lineages separated about 5–6 million years ago. Human PAV/AVI-associated residues and human allele-age estimates therefore do not describe chimpanzee Q697L5 (campbell2012evolutionoffunctionally pages 10-11).

5. Current understanding and recent developments

The most relevant recent synthesis is Itoigawa, Nakagita, and Toda, published November 2024, International Journal of Molecular Sciences 25:12654, DOI/URL: https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms252312654. It reinforces three conclusions important for annotation: T2Rs are structurally distinctive class-T GPCRs; receptor repertoires and ligand sensitivities change rapidly among vertebrates; and species-specific functional assays are essential because human results cannot automatically be assigned to nonhuman orthologs (itoigawa2024theremarkablediversity pages 17-19, itoigawa2024theremarkablediversity pages 11-12).

The 2024 review did not add a direct chimpanzee TAS2R38 localization study, native-tissue signaling experiment, solved structure, or quantitative ligand-response dataset. Consequently, the foundational chimpanzee studies remain more informative for Q697L5 than recent human-centered TAS2R38 literature. This is an important negative result: the annotation is secure at the level of receptor class and PTC-related taste function, but considerably less complete at the levels of native cell biology and pharmacology.

A related 2024 primate study showed that intact TAS2R repertoires vary substantially even among relatively close taxa and cautioned against simple diet-to-gene-number explanations. In sampled cercopithecids, targeted capture recovered 25–28 intact TAS2Rs in colobines and 27–36 in cercopithecines. Although these are not chimpanzee statistics, they reinforce expert concern that genome assemblies and cross-species extrapolation can mischaracterize this rapidly evolving gene family.

6. Applications and real-world relevance

For this chimpanzee protein, current applications are primarily research applications, not clinical implementations:

  1. Sensory ecology: TAS2R38 genotyping provides a molecular marker for studying how bitter perception may shape food choice among chimpanzee populations.
  2. Evolutionary genetics: Independent human and chimpanzee loss-of-sensitivity variants provide a strong natural example of convergent phenotypic evolution through different molecular changes.
  3. Comparative receptor pharmacology: Wild-type and start-loss constructs can be compared in heterologous calcium assays to test receptor trafficking, activation, and ligand specificity.
  4. Conservation genomics: The strong geographic differentiation in western chimpanzees illustrates why subspecies identity and population structure matter when interpreting functional genetic variation.

Human TAS2R38 is being explored in nutrition and mucosal innate immunity, but such medical applications cannot presently be assigned to chimpanzee Q697L5. There is no direct evidence that Q697L5 has a clinically implemented diagnostic or therapeutic use.

7. Recommended functional annotation

Molecular function: Bitter-compound-activated, class-T GPCR; experimentally and genetically associated with PTC/N–C=S compound perception in chimpanzees.

Biological process: Bitter taste detection and gustatory signal transduction, likely contributing to behavioral discrimination or avoidance of selected bitter compounds.

Cellular component: Integral component of the plasma membrane; probably the apical membrane of bitter-responsive taste cells. This localization is inferred from conserved T2R biology rather than directly demonstrated in Pan troglodytes.

Pathway: Probable gustducin/G-protein–PLCβ2–IP3–Ca²⁺–TRPM5 gustatory pathway. Direct chimpanzee-native-cell confirmation is lacking.

Functional variants: A start-codon-loss ATG→AGG allele can force downstream initiation at Met97, deleting 96 N-terminal residues and strongly reducing or abolishing normal receptor function. It was observed at 76% in sampled western-chimpanzee alleles and was absent from the other sampled subspecies (suzukihashido2015rapidexpansionof pages 16-18, hayakawa2012ecogeographicaldiversificationof pages 2-3).

Confidence: High for identity, family, membrane-receptor class, and PTC-related bitter detection; moderate for the complete chimpanzee ligand spectrum and canonical signaling pathway; low or presently unsupported for native extraoral expression, immune roles, detailed subcellular trafficking, and ecological natural agonists.

8. Principal evidence gaps

The highest-priority missing experiments are: native chimpanzee tongue single-cell expression or spatial localization; surface-trafficking measurements for wild-type and start-loss proteins; standardized dose–response testing against PTC, PROP, glucosinolate products, and ecologically relevant plant metabolites; direct coupling measurements with chimpanzee gustducin/PLCβ2 machinery; and genotype-linked behavioral testing across adequately sampled subspecies. Until such data exist, human and macaque extraoral functions should remain explicitly labeled as comparative hypotheses rather than annotations of Q697L5.
