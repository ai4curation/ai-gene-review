Summary

The gene sucC of Escherichia coli K-12 encodes the β-subunit of succinyl-CoA synthetase (SCS), also called succinate–CoA ligase [ADP-forming] (UniProt P0A836, EC 6.2.1.5). SCS catalyzes the only substrate-level phosphorylation of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle: the reversible, Mg²⁺-dependent reaction succinyl-CoA + Pᵢ + NDP ⇌ succinate + CoA + NTP, where N is adenosine (preferred in E. coli) or guanosine. In the oxidative direction this conserves the energy of the succinyl-CoA thioester bond as a nucleoside triphosphate (ATP in E. coli); in the reverse direction it regenerates succinyl-CoA for biosynthesis.

The functional enzyme is an α₂β₂ heterotetramer assembled from the sucC-encoded β-subunit and the sucD-encoded α-subunit. Catalysis proceeds through a phosphohistidine intermediate (His246α) that physically swings ~29–35 Å between two spatially separated active sites, coupled to strict half-sites reactivity (alternating-site cooperativity). The β-subunit (sucC) is the substrate-specificity engine of the enzyme: its N-terminal ATP-grasp fold binds the nucleotide and Mg²⁺ (and a single residue, Pro20β, sets the ADP-vs-GDP preference), while its C-terminal domain binds the dicarboxylate substrate succinate with strict selectivity over the other C4 acids of the cycle.

Physiologically, SCS is a soluble cytoplasmic enzyme (experimentally confirmed cytosolic localization, GO:0005829, IDA) sitting at a central metabolic branch point. It is encoded in the sdhCDAB-sucABCD operon downstream of 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase. In the oxidative TCA cycle it generates ATP; running in reverse, it supplies succinyl-CoA — the acyl donor for essential biosynthetic pathways including methionine biosynthesis (homoserine O-succinyltransferase) and heme/tetrapyrrole biosynthesis (5-aminolevulinate synthase). This anabolic role is particularly important during anaerobic growth, when the oxidative route from 2-oxoglutarate is repressed.

Key Findings

Primary function: the TCA cycle's unique substrate-level phosphorylation enzyme

The sucC gene product is one of two subunits of succinyl-CoA synthetase, the enzyme that carries out the sole substrate-level phosphorylation reaction of the citric acid cycle. SCS catalyzes a reversible reaction: succinyl-CoA + Pᵢ + NDP ⇌ succinate + CoA + NTP, where N denotes adenosine or guanosine. The reaction conserves the energy stored in the thioester bond of succinyl-CoA (produced by the preceding 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase step) by forming a nucleoside triphosphate, and it does so via a transiently phosphorylated histidine residue. The E. coli enzyme is structurally and kinetically well characterized, with X-ray structures to 2.3 Å resolution.

The uniqueness and reversibility of this reaction are explicitly documented: "Succinyl-CoA synthetase (SCS) carries out the substrate-level phosphorylation of GDP or ADP in the citric acid cycle" (PMID: 9917402), and "Succinyl-CoA synthetase (SCS) catalyzes a reversible reaction that is the only substrate-level phosphorylation in the citric acid cycle" (PMID: 33645539). This is the defining biochemical function of the gene product and the reason it is classified as EC 6.2.1.5 (a CoA ligase forming ADP).

Quaternary structure: an α₂β₂ heterotetramer with shared active sites

The functional enzyme in E. coli is a non-dissociating α₂β₂ heterotetramer, assembled from the sucD-encoded α-subunit and the sucC-encoded β-subunit. "The enzyme, as isolated from Escherichia coli, has an alpha 2 beta 2 subunit structure" and "the active enzyme is the nondissociating tetramer" (PMID: 3304349). Crystallography independently confirms this: "E. coli SCS is an (alphabeta)2-tetramer; there are two copies of each subunit in the asymmetric unit of the crystals" (PMID: 9917402). Renaturation experiments show fully active enzyme can be reconstituted from separately denatured α and β subunits, with a bimolecular rate-determining assembly step, confirming that neither subunit is catalytically competent alone and that the two form an obligate partnership. Each αβ dimer contributes one active site, where the catalytic phosphohistidine and the CoA thiol are located.

The β-subunit houses the nucleotide-binding site in an ATP-grasp fold

A central, specific role of the sucC product is to bind the nucleotide substrate. X-ray diffraction of phosphorylated E. coli SCS soaked with ADP/Mg²⁺ "revealed an ADP-Mg(2+) complex bound in the ATP-grasp fold of the N-terminal domain of each beta-subunit" (PMID: 10625475). This was corroborated biochemically by photoaffinity labeling: "Two tryptic peptides from the N-terminal domain of the beta-subunit were labeled with 8-N3-ATP" (PMID: 10353839), with the labeled peptides (residues 107–119β and 121–146β) lying along one side of the ATP-grasp fold. The G53βV/R54βE mutant could not be phosphorylated by ATP or GTP but could still be phosphorylated by succinyl-CoA + Pᵢ, definitively localizing the nucleotide active-site chemistry to the β-subunit N-terminal domain.

Catalysis via a swinging phosphohistidine intermediate

The reaction mechanism requires a phosphohistidine intermediate and large-scale conformational movement. A histidine of the α-subunit (His246α in E. coli) is transiently phosphorylated, and the phosphoryl group sits at the N-termini of two "power helices," one contributed by the α-subunit C-terminal domain and one by the β-subunit C-terminal domain. Because the nucleotide binds in the β N-terminal domain while the phosphate on the histidine is generated near the succinate/CoA site, "the catalytic histidine residue would have to move about 35 A to react with the nucleotide" (PMID: 9917402). Structures that captured the phosphohistidine loop in two distinct conformations confirmed the swinging-loop model: "The phosphoryl transfer bridges a distance of 29 Å between the binding sites for phosphohistidine in site I and site II" (PMID: 33645539). Two glutamate residues — one from each subunit — are required to stabilize the histidine during phosphoryl transfer at the two sites; critically for sucC, "Glu 197 beta, a residue in the nucleotide-binding domain, would provide similar stabilization of the histidine residue during the actual phosphorylation/dephosphorylation by nucleotide at site II" (PMID: 11781092). Thus the β-subunit contributes directly to the chemistry at the nucleotide site, not merely to binding.

Nucleotide specificity is set by a single β-subunit residue

Unlike the GTP-preferring SCS isoforms of pig heart and Thermus aquaticus, "Escherichia coli SCS … uses GDP/GTP but prefers ADP/ATP" (PMID: 22751660). This preference is governed by residue 20 of the β-subunit: a "mutant protein designed to probe nucleotide specificity (P20betaQ) had a Km(app) for GTP that was more than 5 times lower than that of wild-type SCS" (PMID: 10353839), with no change to other substrate parameters. The Pro→Gln substitution shifts the enzyme toward GTP usage, pinpointing Pro20β as the molecular determinant of purine-nucleotide selectivity. This is a clear example of the β-subunit (sucC) directly encoding substrate specificity.

The β-subunit C-terminal domain binds succinate with strict specificity

The second substrate-binding role of the β-subunit lies in its C-terminal domain, which binds the dicarboxylate substrate succinate (and participates in CoA binding). Mutations disrupting a loop of the Rossmann-type fold in this domain — I322βA and R324βN/D326βA — "had the greatest effect on the binding of succinate and CoA" without disrupting nucleotide phosphorylation (PMID: 10353839). A dedicated structural study confirmed that "Succinate binds in the carboxy-terminal domain of the β-subunit" and explained the enzyme's selectivity: "The structure shows why succinyl-CoA synthetase is specific for succinate and does not react appreciably with citrate nor with the other C4-dicarboxylic acids of the citric acid cycle, fumarate and oxaloacetate, but shows some activity with L-malate" (PMID: 27487822). This strict substrate specificity ensures SCS operates cleanly on succinate/succinyl-CoA within the dense pool of TCA intermediates.

Half-sites reactivity and alternating-site cooperativity

Despite having two active sites, the E. coli enzyme phosphorylates only one histidine at a time. "The enzyme shows strict half-sites reactivity with respect to the phosphorylation of a histidine residue in the alpha subunit that represents a step in catalysis" (PMID: 3311148). Positional ¹⁸O-isotope exchange experiments on a mono-thiophosphorylated enzyme showed no scrambling of [β,γ-¹⁸O]ATP into the non-bridge position, "clearly indicating that the enzyme does not undergo even transient bisphosphorylation" (PMID: 3311148). These data support a model of cooperative, alternating active sites in which catalysis at one αβ half promotes phosphoryl transfer in the neighboring half — an elegant coupling of the two catalytic sites of the heterotetramer.

Operon context and metabolic flux

sucC is encoded in the sdhCDAB-sucABCD operon, immediately downstream of the 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase genes (sucAB) and alongside its partner α-subunit gene sucD. "the tricarboxylic acid cycle enzymes encoded by the sdhCDAB-sucABCD operon" (PMID: 30497599) places sucC within the oxidative TCA cycle gene cluster, co-regulated with succinate dehydrogenase and 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase. Deletion phenotypes confirm the functional assignment: "disruption of sucC and sucD (encoding subunits of the succinyl-CoA synthetase) increased HHP [high hydrostatic pressure] resistance" via elevated basal RpoS activity (PMID: 30497599). Flux analysis shows SCS carries real, adjustable metabolic flux: deleting upstream isocitrate dehydrogenase "diminishes flux through succinyl CoA synthetase" (PMID: 31504493), consistent with SCS lying downstream of 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase in the oxidative arm.

Metabolic branch point feeding biosynthesis

The succinyl-CoA that SCS consumes (oxidative direction) or produces (anabolic direction) is a key acyl donor for biosynthesis. Homoserine O-succinyltransferase uses succinyl-CoA for the committed step of methionine biosynthesis: "Homoserine acyltransferases catalyze the commitment step to methionine and other important biological precursors" (PMID: 17442255). In tetrapyrrole/heme biosynthesis, 5-aminolevulinate synthase "catalyzes the condensation of glycine with succinyl-coenzyme A to yield aminolevulinate, carbon dioxide and CoA" (PMID: 11929042). Because SCS reversibly interconverts succinyl-CoA + Pᵢ + ADP and succinate + CoA + ATP, it both consumes succinyl-CoA (oxidative TCA, generating ATP) and regenerates it from succinate (anabolic direction). The anabolic role is "particularly important for providing succinyl-CoA during anaerobic growth when the oxidative route from 2-oxoglutarate is severely repressed" (UniProt P0A836 annotation).

Cofactor requirement and cytoplasmic localization

UniProt P0A836 annotates the β-subunit as 388 amino acids (~41.4 kDa) and the enzyme as requiring Mg²⁺. Crystallographic analysis supports the Mg²⁺ requirement: "the 'true' nucleotide substrate is probably an ADP-Mg(2+) complex" (PMID: 10625475). The enzyme is a soluble, cytoplasmic protein. Experimental GO annotations assign cytosol (GO:0005829) with evidence code IDA (Inferred from Direct Assay) and the succinate-CoA ligase (ADP-forming) complex (GO:0009361, IDA). Molecular-function annotations include succinate-CoA ligase (ADP-forming) activity (GO:0004775, IMP), succinate-CoA ligase (GDP-forming) activity (GO:0004776, EXP), plus ATP binding and Mg²⁺ binding; process annotations include the tricarboxylic acid cycle (GO:0006099, IMP) and succinyl-CoA metabolic process (GO:0006104).

Structural classification in the ATP-grasp superfamily

InterPro/UniProt feature mapping of P0A836 shows a two-domain architecture: an ATP-grasp domain spanning residues 9–244 (N-terminal), carrying ATP-binding residues (46, 53–55, 99, 102, 107) and Mg²⁺-binding residues (199, 213), with substrate (succinate) binding residues in the C-terminal domain (264, 321–323). Pfam classifications are PF08442 (ATP-grasp_2) plus PF00549 (Ligase_CoA); PROSITE PS50975 (ATP_GRASP). These database-derived residues coincide precisely with the experimentally identified sites (the 8-N₃-ATP-labeled 107–119β peptide, the G53β/R54β phosphorylation-null mutant, and the C-terminal I322β/R324β/D326β succinate/CoA-binding mutants). The ATP-grasp fold is defined as "two α+β domains that 'grasp' a molecule of ATP between them", and the superfamily enzymes "catalyze the ATP-assisted reaction of a carboxylic acid with a nucleophile via the formation of an acylphosphate intermediate" (PMID: 21920581), consistent with the succinyl-phosphate-type intermediate chemistry of SCS.

The swinging phosphohistidine couples two spatially separated half-reactions

SCS chemistry occurs in two half-reactions at two distinct sites, bridged by a mobile phosphohistidine loop:

  Site I (succinate/CoA end)                      Site II (nucleotide end)
  ─────────────────────────                       ──────────────────────────
  succinyl-CoA + Pi                                 His246α~P  +  NDP
        │                                                 │
        ▼  (succinyl-phosphate; CoA released)             ▼  (phosphoryl transfer to nucleotide)
   His246α  +  succinate                              His246α  +  NTP
        └──────────  phospho-His loop swings ~29–35 Å  ──────────┘
            (Glu208α stabilizes at site I; Glu197β stabilizes at site II)

A phosphoryl group derived from Pᵢ and succinyl-CoA (generating a succinyl-phosphate intermediate and releasing CoA) is placed onto His246α at site I. The phosphohistidine loop then swings ~29–35 Å to site II, where the phosphoryl group is transferred to the nucleotide bound in the β-subunit ATP-grasp fold, producing NTP. The two glutamates (Glu208α at site I, Glu197β at site II) stabilize the histidine for phosphorylation/dephosphorylation at each location. Strict half-sites reactivity means only one of the two catalytic halves carries the phosphoryl group at any moment, with the two sites functioning in an alternating, cooperative fashion.

In the clockwise (oxidative) direction, SCS converts the high-energy thioester of succinyl-CoA into ATP (or GTP) — the TCA cycle's only substrate-level phosphorylation. In the reverse (anabolic) direction, it regenerates succinyl-CoA from succinate, replenishing the acyl donor needed for methionine and heme biosynthesis, which is physiologically critical when anaerobic conditions repress the oxidative 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase route. Its position in the sdhCDAB-sucABCD operon co-regulates it with the flanking TCA enzymes, and flux experiments confirm it carries adjustable metabolic flux downstream of 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase.

X-ray crystallography (PMIDs 9917402, 10625475, 27487822, 33645539) and photoaffinity/mutagenesis studies (PMIDs 10353839, 11781092) independently place the nucleotide site in the β N-terminal ATP-grasp fold and the succinate site in the β C-terminal domain. Comparative orthologous structures (pig heart GTP-specific SCS, 10873456; T. aquaticus, 22751660) confirm the conserved mechanism and highlight the species-specific nucleotide preference controlled by residue 20β.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  • Localization granularity. Cytosolic localization is experimentally supported (IDA, GO:0005829), consistent with SCS being a soluble enzyme. However, formal demonstration of whether it is exclusively free in the cytoplasm versus transiently associated with other TCA enzymes in a "metabolon" in E. coli is not established by the reviewed literature.
  • Residue-numbering conventions. Some catalytic residues are cited from orthologs or UniProt feature tables (e.g., Glu197β, Pro20β, succinate-binding residues 264/321–323). While cross-validated against experimental E. coli studies, exact numbering can differ between mature-protein and precursor schemes; specific E. coli numbering should be confirmed against the current UniProt entry when precision matters.
  • Quantitative kinetics. The report establishes directionality, specificity, and mechanism qualitatively and semi-quantitatively (e.g., >5-fold Km shift for P20βQ). A consolidated table of absolute kinetic constants (kcat; Km for succinate, CoA, Pᵢ, ADP/GDP) for wild-type E. coli SCS was not assembled and would strengthen the metabolic-flux interpretation.
  • Regulation. Transcriptional regulation of the sdhCDAB-sucABCD operon (e.g., by ArcA, FNR, Crp) and any post-translational modification of SCS in E. coli were not examined in depth; these govern when the enzyme runs oxidatively versus anabolically.
  • In vivo directionality. The precise in vivo balance between ATP-generating (oxidative) and succinyl-CoA-supplying (anabolic) flux under defined growth conditions was inferred from annotation and flux studies rather than measured directly here.

  2. Confirm E. coli-specific residue numbering. Map Pro20β, Glu197β, His246α, and the succinate-binding C-terminal residues onto the current P0A836/sucD sequences and a deposited E. coli SCS structure (e.g., PDB entries from PMIDs 9917402/10625475/27487822) to lock in exact numbering.
  3. Directionality under defined conditions. Use ¹³C stable-isotope-resolved metabolomics in aerobic versus anaerobic chemostat cultures to measure net SCS flux direction and quantify anabolic succinyl-CoA supply during anaerobic growth.
