Functional Annotation Report: pncB (Nicotinate Phosphoribosyltransferase), Escherichia coli K-12

UniProt: P18133 | Gene: pncB (b0931, JW0914) | EC: 6.3.4.21 (also historically EC 2.4.2.11) | Organism: Escherichia coli strain K-12

Summary

The E. coli K-12 gene pncB (UniProt P18133) encodes nicotinate phosphoribosyltransferase (NAPRTase, NAPRT), a soluble cytoplasmic enzyme that catalyzes the committed, rate-limiting entry step of the Preiss–Handler NAD⁺ salvage pathway. The enzyme transfers a phosphoribosyl group from 5-phospho-α-D-ribose 1-diphosphate (PRPP) onto nicotinate (nicotinic acid, NA), producing nicotinate mononucleotide (NaMN) plus pyrophosphate (PPi). NaMN is a direct precursor of NAD⁺, feeding into the downstream, essential and conserved NadD→NadE segment shared with the de novo pathway. The gene identity is unambiguous: the UniProt annotation (NAPRTase family; NAPRTase/Nic_PRibTrfase domains), the catalyzed reaction, the organism, and the primary literature all converge on the same well-characterized E. coli enzyme.

The most distinctive mechanistic feature of NAPRTase is that it is a facultative ATPase: it couples the hydrolysis of ATP (1:1 with NaMN synthesis) to the phosphoribosyl-transfer reaction, thereby driving the reaction thermodynamically far past its chemical equilibrium. This ATP coupling proceeds through a covalent phospho-histidine intermediate at His-219, which transiently phosphorylates the enzyme and dramatically increases its affinity for substrates — the structural basis for its classification as a ligase (EC 6.3.4.21) rather than a simple glycosyltransferase. This energy-coupling mechanism was described as "a new paradigm for biochemical energy coupling" and distinguishes NAPRTase from most other phosphoribosyltransferases.

Functionally, pncB/NAPRTase sits at a key metabolic and regulatory node. Its product NaMN converges on the same downstream chemistry as the de novo quinolinate route, making it the branch-point enzyme of the deamidating nicotinamide/nicotinate salvage route. Its expression is feedback-controlled by the NAD-sensing bifunctional regulator NadR, which uses NAD⁺ as a corepressor to repress pncB coordinately with the de novo genes nadA/nadB, tying nicotinate salvage to intracellular NAD⁺ status. Because the enzyme controls NAD(H) pool size, pncB overexpression has become a widely used metabolic-engineering lever for boosting NAD(H) availability in industrial E. coli strains.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — pncB encodes NAPRTase, the committed, rate-limiting entry enzyme of the Preiss–Handler NAD⁺ salvage pathway

NAPRTase catalyzes the formation of nicotinate mononucleotide (NaMN) and pyrophosphate from nicotinate and PRPP:

Nicotinate (NA) + PRPP  →  Nicotinate mononucleotide (NaMN) + PPi

The pncB gene of E. coli was cloned on a 1.5-kb TaqI–EcoRI fragment and shown to encode nicotinic acid phosphoribosyltransferase (PMID: 2211655): "The pncB gene from Escherichia coli, which encodes nicotinic acid phosphoribosyltransferase (EC 2.4.2.11), was cloned on a 1.5-kilobase TaqI-EcoRI fragment." Overexpression of the cloned gene produced a 25-fold increase in enzyme activity and a 5-fold increase in intracellular NAD⁺ — direct evidence that NAPRTase activity controls the size of the cellular NAD pool: "increased nicotinic acid phosphoribosyltransferase activity effects a 5-fold increase in the intracellular concentration of NAD" (Wubbolts et al., 1990; PMID: 2211655).

The reaction product and the enzyme's position in the pathway are explicitly defined in the metabolic-engineering literature (PMID: 12616693): NAPRTase "catalyzes the formation of nicotinate mononucleotide (NAMN), a direct precursor of NAD, from NA. This reaction is believed to be the rate-limiting step in the NAD salvage pathway." The description of NAPRTase as the rate-limiting enzyme of the NAD(H) salvage pathway is corroborated repeatedly across independent metabolic-engineering studies that use pncB overexpression to raise NAD pools (PMID: 22975127, PMID: 23740313, PMID: 12009797).

Substrate specificity: The enzyme is specific for nicotinate (the deamidated pyridine, nicotinic acid) as the pyridine-base substrate and PRPP as the phosphoribosyl donor. It does not act on nicotinamide directly; in the deamidating salvage route nicotinamide must first be converted to nicotinate by nicotinamidase (PncA) before NAPRTase can act (see Finding 5).

Finding 2 — NAPRTase is an ATP-dependent enzyme that couples ATP hydrolysis to NaMN synthesis via a covalent phospho-His-219 intermediate (basis for EC 6.3.4.21)

Although the chemical transglycosylation (NA + PRPP ⇌ NaMN + PPi) is near thermodynamic equilibrium, NAPRTase is unusual among phosphoribosyltransferases in that it consumes ATP to drive the reaction strongly forward. Vinitsky & Grubmeyer (1993) showed that NAPRTase hydrolyzes ATP in 1:1 molar stoichiometry with NaMN synthesis, shifting the products/substrates ratio from the equilibrium value of 0.67 to a steady-state value of ~1100 — an ~1000-fold thermodynamic enhancement (PMID: 7503993): "NAPRTase hydrolyzes ATP in 1:1 molar stoichiometry to NAMN synthesis. Hydrolysis of ATP alters the ratio of products/substrates for the reaction nicotinate + PRPP <--> NAMN + PPi from its equilibrium value of 0.67 to a steady-state value of 1100." This paper framed the behavior as "a new paradigm for biochemical energy coupling."

The mechanism of this coupling is a covalent phosphoenzyme intermediate. ATP phosphorylates a specific active-site histidine, His-219, forming an acid-labile/base-stable phosphoramidate (phosphohistidine). Gross et al. (1996) identified His-219 as the site of phosphorylation and demonstrated that the phospho-His-219 species is a genuine catalytic intermediate shared by both the ATPase and NaMN-synthesis functions (PMID: 8672422): "NAPRTase phosphorylated at His-219 is an intermediate in the energy transduction mechanism of NAPRTase."

Mechanistically, phosphorylation of the enzyme dramatically tightens substrate binding. Gross et al. (1998) showed that the phosphoenzyme (E-P) binds PRPP with a K_D of 0.6 µM — ~2000-fold tighter than the unphosphorylated enzyme — and lowers the K_m for both nicotinate and PRPP by roughly 200-fold (PMID: 9521741): "The phosphoenzyme (E-P) binds PRPP with a KD of 0.6 microM, a value 2000-fold lower than that measured for the nonphosphorylated enzyme." Thus the energy of ATP hydrolysis is transduced into a high-affinity, catalytically poised phosphoenzyme that is subsequently discharged during product formation. This ATP-coupling behavior is the biochemical justification for reclassifying NAPRTase from a transferase (old EC 2.4.2.11) to a ligase, EC 6.3.4.21.

Finding 3 — pncB transcription is feedback-repressed by the NAD-sensing bifunctional regulator NadR, coordinately with de novo NAD genes

NAPRTase is embedded in a NAD-responsive regulatory network. In Salmonella typhimurium — the close enteric relative in which much of this regulation was dissected — the pyridine-nucleotide salvage pathway is controlled at pncB, and pncB, nadA, and nadB are coordinately regulated by the product of the nadR gene (Foster et al., 1990; PMID: 2198247): "the pyridine nucleotide salvage pathway is controlled at pncB. The transcriptional expression of these three loci is coordinately regulated by the product of nadR."

NadR is a NAD-dependent DNA-binding repressor that binds conserved "NAD box" operator sequences as a multimer, using NAD⁺ itself as the physiological corepressor. Penfound & Foster (1999) demonstrated that NAD is the relevant in vivo corepressor (PMID: 9882682): "NAD proved to be the relevant in vivo corepressor." When intracellular NAD⁺ is abundant, NadR–NAD represses the salvage and de novo genes; when NAD⁺ is scarce, repression is relieved and salvage flux increases. Consistent with this feedback logic, growth of E. coli in the presence of nicotinic acid (which raises NAD) strongly represses NAPRTase activity (PMID: 2211655): "Growth of E. coli in the presence of nicotinic acid leads to strong repression of nicotinic acid phosphoribosyltransferase activity."

NadR is itself a remarkable multifunctional/trifunctional protein: an N-terminal DNA-binding (repressor) domain, a central NMN adenylyltransferase domain that senses NAD, and a C-terminal ribosylnicotinamide kinase domain (PMID: 15805524, PMID: 12446641). It also modulates the activity of the PnuC NMN transport system (PMID: 1991724). More recently, NadR has been shown to be a c-di-GMP effector, linking the second messenger c-di-GMP to NAD biosynthesis by de-repressing NadR-controlled genes (PMID: 40823837) — placing pncB within an even broader signaling context.

Finding 4 — NAPRTase adopts a type II PRTase (α/β) fold and functions as a soluble cytoplasmic enzyme

The crystal structure of a NAPRTase ortholog from Thermoplasma acidophilum revealed a multidomain architecture whose central functional domain has a type II phosphoribosyltransferase fold (PMID: 15753098): "the functional domain has a type II phosphoribosyltransferase fold that may be a common architecture for both nicotinic acid and quinolinic acid (QA) phosphoribosyltransferases (PRTase)." This fold is shared with quinolinate PRTase despite low primary sequence similarity, reflecting deep evolutionary convergence on a common catalytic architecture for pyridine-mononucleotide biosynthesis.

The structure also defines the molecular basis of nicotinate recognition: the active-site pocket forms at a dimer interface, and the nicotinate moiety of the product NaMN is intercalated between the highly conserved aromatic residues Tyr21 and Phe138 (PMID: 15753098): "the nicotinate moiety of NAMN is intercalated between highly conserved aromatic residues Tyr(21) and Phe(138)." These aromatic clamps provide π-stacking interactions that confer specificity for the planar pyridine ring.

Localization: The E. coli enzyme (~401 aa; the S. typhimurium ortholog is 399 aa) is purified and assayed as a soluble protein, consistent with a cytoplasmic location where all three of its substrates — PRPP, nicotinate, and ATP — reside. UniProt P18133 annotates no signal peptide and no transmembrane segment, supporting an exclusively cytosolic function. There is no evidence of membrane association or secretion; the enzyme operates within the cytoplasm as part of soluble central metabolism.

Finding 5 — pncB is the branch-point enzyme of the deamidating nicotinamide salvage route, converging on the shared, essential NadD→NadE segment

NAPRTase occupies the convergence point between salvage and de novo NAD⁺ synthesis. The salvage pathway recycles both intracellular NAD breakdown products and preformed pyridine compounds taken up from the environment, such as nicotinic acid (PMID: 12616693): "The salvage pathway recycles intracellular NAD breakdown products and preformed pyridine compounds from the environment, such as nicotinic acid (NA)." In the deamidating route, exogenous or recycled nicotinamide is first deamidated to nicotinate by nicotinamidase (PncA), and PncB/NAPRTase then converts nicotinate to NaMN.

Crucially, NaMN is the same product generated by the de novo pathway's quinolinate phosphoribosyltransferase (NadC). Both routes therefore funnel into the shared terminal two steps: NadD (nicotinate/nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferase), which adenylylates NaMN to nicotinate adenine dinucleotide (NaAD), and NadE (NAD synthetase), which amidates NaAD to NAD⁺. Sorci et al. (2010) emphasize that these last two steps are conserved and essential in most bacteria (PMID: 20926389): "Enzymes involved in the last steps of NAD biogenesis, nicotinate mononucleotide adenylyltransferase (NadD) and NAD synthetase (NadE), are conserved and essential in most bacterial species." Thus pncB supplies NaMN to a conserved, essential downstream segment, making it the metabolic gateway for nicotinate utilization.

This is the "new paradigm" of biochemical energy coupling — a phosphoribosyltransferase behaving as a ligase.

Synthesis

pncB/NAPRTase is best understood as the energy-invested, feedback-controlled gatekeeper of nicotinate salvage. It commits scavenged nicotinate to NAD⁺ synthesis, spends an ATP to make this commitment thermodynamically decisive, and is transcriptionally throttled by the cell's NAD⁺ status via NadR. Because it is rate-limiting, it functions as the principal control point that sets NAD(H) pool size — which is precisely why metabolic engineers exploit it (see below). Its product NaMN is the common currency into which both de novo and salvage routes convert, feeding the essential NadD→NadE finishing steps.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Structural data are from an ortholog. The type II PRTase fold and the Tyr21/Phe138 nicotinate-binding residues (F4) were determined for the Thermoplasma acidophilum NAPRTase, not E. coli P18133. Residue numbering and precise active-site geometry in the E. coli enzyme are inferred by homology.

  2. Energy-coupling mechanism characterized largely in Salmonella. The phospho-His-219 intermediate and kinetic coupling parameters (F2) were elucidated primarily in S. typhimurium NAPRTase. Given the very high identity between the enterobacterial orthologs, these conclusions almost certainly transfer to E. coli, but the His-219 numbering should be mapped onto the E. coli sequence for certainty.

  3. Regulation dissected mainly in Salmonella. The NadR corepressor mechanism and coordinate control of pncB/nadA/nadB (F3) were established in S. typhimurium. E. coli NadR is homologous and the NA-repression phenotype is reproduced in E. coli (PMID: 2211655), but quantitative operator/affinity data specific to E. coli pncB are less complete.

  4. No direct E. coli subcellular-localization experiment. The cytoplasmic assignment (F4) rests on solubility during purification, the absence of a signal peptide/TM segment in P18133, and the cytosolic location of its substrates — strong but inferential rather than a direct localization assay.

  5. ATP coupling is "facultative." NAPRTase can run without ATP (at the unfavorable 0.67 equilibrium). The physiological conditions under which the cell toggles between ATP-coupled and uncoupled operation, and the in vivo flux consequences, are not fully quantified.

  6. mRNA-level regulation. Translational enhancement by downstream adenine-rich motifs in the pncB coding sequence (PMID: 17085569) adds a layer of expression control that was noted but not integrated into a quantitative model here.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Solve or model the E. coli NAPRTase structure. Determine a crystal/cryo-EM structure of E. coli P18133 (or build a validated AlphaFold model) to confirm the type II PRTase fold, map the His-219 equivalent, and verify the Tyr/Phe nicotinate-clamp residues directly in the E. coli enzyme.

  2. Site-directed mutagenesis of the phospho-acceptor His. Mutate the E. coli His-219 equivalent (H→A, H→Q) and measure: (a) loss of ATPase activity, (b) loss of the ~1000-fold thermodynamic enhancement, and (c) in vivo NAD pool size — confirming the energy-coupling residue in E. coli.

  3. Quantify in vivo ATP coupling. Use ¹³C/isotope flux and metabolomics under energy-limited vs. energy-replete conditions to determine when NAPRTase runs in ATP-coupled vs. uncoupled mode and how this sets NaMN/NAD flux.

  4. Direct localization assay. Confirm cytoplasmic localization with a functional fluorescent fusion or cell-fractionation + immunoblot in E. coli.

  5. Map E. coli NadR operators on pncB. Perform ChIP-seq / DNase footprinting and EMSA with purified E. coli NadR ± NAD to quantify operator affinity and define the NAD-dependent repression threshold for pncB specifically.

  6. Probe the c-di-GMP–NadR–pncB axis in E. coli. Test whether manipulating c-di-GMP levels de-represses pncB and raises NAD⁺ in E. coli, extending the recently reported c-di-GMP/NadR link (PMID: 40823837).

Conclusion

E. coli K-12 pncB (P18133) encodes nicotinate phosphoribosyltransferase (NAPRTase), the soluble cytoplasmic, rate-limiting entry enzyme of the Preiss–Handler NAD⁺ salvage pathway. It transfers a phosphoribosyl group from PRPP to nicotinate to yield NaMN + PPi, uniquely coupling ATP hydrolysis (via a phospho-His-219 intermediate) to drive the reaction ~1000-fold past equilibrium — the basis of its ligase classification (EC 6.3.4.21). Its product NaMN feeds the conserved, essential NadD→NadE steps that complete NAD⁺ synthesis, and its expression is feedback-repressed by the NAD-sensing regulator NadR, embedding pncB in the cell's central NAD⁺ homeostatic circuitry.
