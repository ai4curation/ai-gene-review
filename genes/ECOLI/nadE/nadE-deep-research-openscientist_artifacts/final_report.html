NadE (P18843) — NH₃-dependent NAD⁺ synthetase of Escherichia coli K-12

Functional Annotation Report

UniProt: P18843 · Gene: nadE (synonyms efg, ntrL; ordered locus b1740 / JW1729) · EC 6.3.1.5 · Organism: Escherichia coli strain K-12

Summary

NadE (UniProt P18843; gene nadE, synonyms efg, ntrL; ordered locus b1740/JW1729) encodes the ammonia-dependent NAD⁺ synthetase of Escherichia coli K-12 (EC 6.3.1.5). It is the enzyme that catalyzes the final, obligatory step of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) biosynthesis: the ATP-dependent amidation of nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide (NaAD, also called deamido-NAD⁺) to yield NAD⁺. The reaction uses free ammonia as the nitrogen donor and requires Mg²⁺, consuming ATP and releasing AMP and pyrophosphate (PPi). This is the terminal chemistry of NAD⁺ production in the cell, and because NAD⁺/NADP⁺ participate in roughly 12% of all biochemical reactions in the E. coli K-12 metabolic model, NadE sits at a metabolic chokepoint of exceptional importance.

The enzyme is a soluble, cytoplasmic homodimer with an α/β (Rossmann-like) single-domain fold that places it in the "N-type" ATP pyrophosphatase (adenine-nucleotide α-hydrolase) superfamily, alongside GMP synthetase, asparagine synthetase, and argininosuccinate synthetase. Crystal structures of the E. coli enzyme — captured alone, with natural substrates, and with the product NAD⁺ — reveal two NaAD/NAD binding sites at the dimer interface and one ATP site within each subunit, and they document a two-state conformational cycle ("catalysis" and "substrate/product exchange" states) driven by Mg²⁺ coordination. Mechanistically, NadE operates by two chemical steps: (1) ATP activates the carboxylate of NaAD to form a covalent NAD-adenylate intermediate (releasing PPi), and (2) ammonia performs a nucleophilic attack on this intermediate to form the amide, releasing AMP. The E. coli enzyme, unlike the glutamine-dependent NAD synthetases of eukaryotes and some bacteria (e.g., Mycobacterium tuberculosis), lacks an N-terminal glutamine amidotransferase (nitrilase-superfamily) domain and is strictly ammonia-dependent.

NadE is the single convergence point of all NAD⁺ biosynthetic routes in E. coli: both the de novo pathway (nadA/nadB/nadC) and the Preiss–Handler salvage pathway (pncA/pncB) funnel into nicotinate mononucleotide (NaMN), which NadD adenylylates to NaAD, which NadE then amidates to NAD⁺. Loss of NadE function is lethal — temperature-sensitive nadE mutants accumulate large internal pools of NaAD and cannot make NAD⁺ at the non-permissive temperature — establishing the gene as essential and validating the enzyme as an antibacterial drug target with exploitable species-specific active-site differences.

Key Findings

1. NadE catalyzes the terminal amidation step of NAD⁺ biosynthesis

NadE is the structural gene for NAD⁺ synthetase, the enzyme that performs the last metabolic step of NAD⁺ biosynthesis. The reaction is the amidation of nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide (NaAD/deamido-NAD⁺) to produce NAD⁺. This was established both biochemically and genetically. Structural studies of NAD synthetases describe the core chemistry directly: "Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide synthetases (NADS) catalyze the amidation of nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide (NAAD) to yield the enzyme cofactor nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD)" (PMID: 15699042).

The genetic identification came from the orthologous system in Salmonella typhimurium, where the gene "designated nadE" was shown to encode "NAD synthetase, which catalyzes the final metabolic step in NAD biosynthesis" (PMID: 2834324). Crucially, that same study showed that loss-of-function strains "accumulate large internal pools of nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide, the substrate for NAD synthetase" (PMID: 2834324) — a direct in vivo demonstration that NaAD is the substrate and that NadE is responsible for its conversion to NAD⁺.

Overall reaction:

NaAD (deamido-NAD⁺) + ATP + NH₃  ──Mg²⁺──▶  NAD⁺ + AMP + PPi

2. NadE is a homodimer with substrate sites at the dimer interface and an ATP site per subunit

Crystal structures of the ammonia-dependent E. coli enzyme — solved alone, with natural substrates, and with the product NAD⁺ — established its quaternary architecture. "The structures disclosed two NAAD/NAD binding sites at the dimer interface and an adenosine triphosphate (ATP) binding site within each subunit" (PMID: 15699042). The pyridine (NaAD/NAD) sites are therefore built from both subunits, consistent with the obligate dimeric organization of the enzyme.

These structures also captured the enzyme in motion. "The E. coli NADS structures revealed unexpected dynamical rearrangements in the NAAD/NAD binding pocket upon NAAD-to-NAD conversion, which define a catalysis state and a substrate/product exchange state" (PMID: 15699042). The conversion is driven by concerted movement of the nicotinysyl moiety together with Phe-170 and residues 224–228, triggered by differential Mg²⁺ coordination — explaining how the active site reconfigures between performing chemistry and exchanging substrate for product.

3. A two-step mechanism: ATP-driven adenylation of NaAD, then ammonia attack

The catalytic chemistry of NH₃-dependent NAD⁺ synthetases proceeds via a covalent reaction intermediate. An atomic-resolution (1 Å) structure of the homologous Bacillus subtilis enzyme co-crystallized with NaAD and ATP showed that "Both binding sites are occupied by the NAD-adenylate intermediate, pyrophosphate and two magnesium ions" (PMID: 12077433). This captures the key intermediate: ATP activates the carboxylate of NaAD to form NAD-adenylate, releasing pyrophosphate.

The second step is amide-bond formation. The same study proposed "a mechanism... for the second catalytic step, which includes a nucleophilic attack by the ammonia molecule on the intermediate" (PMID: 12077433). Ammonia attacks the activated acyl-adenylate carbon, displacing AMP and forming the carboxamide that defines NAD⁺. This two-step chemistry (adenylation then amidation) is conserved across NH₃-dependent bacterial NAD synthetases, including E. coli NadE.

4. Membership in the ATP pyrophosphatase family and strict ammonia dependence

NadE belongs to the ATP pyrophosphatase (adenine-nucleotide α-hydrolase, "N-type") family. The defining structural work on GMP synthetase noted the conserved nucleotide-binding fingerprint "common to a family of ATP pyrophosphatases, including NAD synthetase, asparagine synthetase and argininosuccinate synthetase" (PMID: 8548458). These enzymes share a nucleotide-binding fold and the mechanistic logic of adenylating a substrate carboxylate before nucleophilic substitution.

A central distinction defines the E. coli enzyme's substrate specificity for the nitrogen donor. Many bacterial NAD synthetases, including E. coli's, are ammonia-dependent: "many purified prokaryotic NAD+ synthetases are ammonia-dependent" (PMID: 12771147). In contrast, the M. tuberculosis enzyme "utilizes both glutamine and ammonia to catalyze NAD(+) production, in contrast to the corresponding NH(3)-dependent enzymes from other prokaryotes" (PMID: 15748981). The glutamine-utilizing enzymes carry an N-terminal nitrilase-superfamily glutamine amidotransferase (GAT) domain that generates ammonia in situ from glutamine; the single-domain E. coli NadE lacks this domain and therefore depends on exogenous free ammonium as its nitrogen source.

5. Biochemical requirements: Mg²⁺ dependence and alkaline pH optimum

Recombinant E. coli NAD synthetase purified to homogeneity was characterized enzymatically. The enzyme "required approximately 1.5 mM magnesium for optimal activity. The pH optimum was found to be 8.0-8.5" (PMID: 10544053). The Mg²⁺ requirement is consistent with the two-metal coordination seen in the atomic-resolution intermediate structure (two Mg²⁺ ions at the active site), and the alkaline pH optimum is consistent with a mechanism that relies on the neutral/unprotonated ammonia species as the nucleophile. The protein was crystallized with substrates NaAD and ATP, diffracting to 2.0 Å in space group P3₁21.

6. NadE is the common terminal convergence point of all NAD⁺ biosynthetic routes

In E. coli (and Salmonella), NAD⁺ can be made by two upstream routes that converge on a single shared terminal sequence. The de novo pathway (nadA, nadB, nadC) and the salvage/Preiss–Handler pathway both produce nicotinate mononucleotide (NaMN); NaMN is adenylylated by NadD to NaAD; and NadE then amidates NaAD to NAD⁺. Thus every route to NAD⁺ passes through the NadE step.

The conserved de novo gene set is well established: "the de novo pathway to nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide and the genes governing the enzymes involved in this pathway in S. typhimurium are very similar to those in E. coli" (PMID: 203571). The salvage contribution is described in the context of NAD homeostasis: NadE, "together with nicotinamide deamidase (PncA), compose the major pathway for utilization of the pyridine ring in the form of amidated (Nm) or deamidated (Na) precursors" (PMID: 26443758). The broad metabolic weight of the product underscores why this chokepoint matters: NAD and NADP "collectively contribute to approximately 12% of all biochemical reactions included in the metabolic model of Escherichia coli K-12" (PMID: 26443758).

7. Subcellular localization: a soluble cytoplasmic enzyme

NadE functions in the bacterial cytoplasm. It was "overexpressed and purified to homogeneity. The recombinant protein was active in an in vitro enzyme assay" (PMID: 10544053), behaving as a soluble protein crystallized from aqueous conditions. Its substrates and product (NaAD, ATP, Mg²⁺, ammonium, NAD⁺) are exclusively cytoplasmic small molecules, and the protein is a single-domain soluble α/β enzyme with no signal peptide or transmembrane segment. The combined evidence places NadE firmly in the cytosol, where it carries out its reaction as part of soluble central metabolism.

8. Catalytic site at a β-sheet switch point with a conserved P-loop serine

The founding crystallographic analysis of an NH₃-dependent NAD⁺ synthetase (the B. subtilis ortholog, as the free form and ATP complex at 2.6/2.0 Å) defined the architectural basis of catalysis. "The enzyme consists of a tight homodimer with alpha/beta subunit topology. The catalytic site is located at the parallel beta-sheet topological switch point, where one AMP molecule, one pyrophosphate and one Mg2+ ion are observed" (PMID: 8895556).

A conserved active-site serine from the "P-loop" was implicated in the first chemical step: "Residue Ser46, part of the neighboring 'P-loop', is hydrogen bonded to the pyrophosphate group, and may play a role in promoting the adenylation of deamido-NAD+ during the first step of the catalyzed reaction" (PMID: 8895556). On the basis of its conserved catalytic fingerprint, the study concluded that "NAD+ synthetase belongs to the newly discovered family of 'N-type' ATP pyrophosphatases" (PMID: 8895556). This architecture — interfacial NaAD/NAD sites, per-subunit ATP sites, and a P-loop serine — is shared by the E. coli enzyme, whose own structures confirmed the same arrangement.

9. NadE is essential and a validated antibacterial drug target

Because NAD⁺ is indispensable and NadE catalyzes its sole terminal biosynthetic step, loss of the enzyme is lethal. "Mutants of nadE include those with a temperature-sensitive lethal phenotype" (PMID: 2834324); at the non-permissive temperature these cells accumulate NaAD and cannot complete NAD⁺ synthesis. This essentiality, combined with structural knowledge, makes NadE an attractive drug target. The E. coli structural study emphasized that cross-species active-site differences are "relevant for designing species-specific antibiotics" (PMID: 15699042), and work on the M. tuberculosis ortholog independently concluded that "the enzyme represents a valid target for the development of new antimycobacterial agents" (PMID: 12182838).

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

The reaction and its two chemical steps

NadE converts the metabolic precursor NaAD into the ubiquitous redox cofactor NAD⁺ by installing an amide nitrogen on the pyridine carboxylate. The transformation is energetically coupled to ATP hydrolysis and proceeds through a covalent acyl-adenylate intermediate:

Step 1 — Adenylation (activation)
   NaAD–COO⁻ + ATP·Mg²⁺  ─▶  NaAD–CO–O–AMP (NAD-adenylate) + PPi
        │                                   (P-loop Ser stabilizes PPi;
        │                                    two Mg²⁺ coordinate phosphates)
Step 2 — Amidation (nucleophilic substitution)
   NaAD–CO–O–AMP + NH₃   ─▶  NAD⁺ (NaAD–CO–NH₂) + AMP

The first step activates the otherwise unreactive carboxylate by converting it into a high-energy mixed anhydride with AMP; the second step uses ammonia as the nucleophile to displace AMP and form the amide. This logic is the shared signature of the N-type ATP pyrophosphatase family (GMP synthetase, asparagine synthetase, argininosuccinate synthetase), all of which adenylate a substrate before nucleophilic substitution.

The E. coli structures add a dynamic dimension: as NaAD is converted to NAD⁺, the pocket toggles between a catalysis state and a substrate/product exchange state, with the nicotinysyl moiety, Phe-170, and residues 224–228 moving in concert under the control of Mg²⁺ coordination. This conformational cycling reconciles the need for a tightly organized active site during chemistry with the need to release product and load fresh substrate.

Every biosynthetic route to NAD⁺ in E. coli — whether built de novo from aspartate or salvaged from pyridine precursors — must pass through the NadE-catalyzed amidation of NaAD. This single-point convergence explains both the strict essentiality of nadE and why its inhibition is lethal regardless of nutrient availability.

  • Organism: All primary functional and structural data for the E. coli enzyme (PMIDs 15699042, 10544053) are from E. coli K-12; genetic identification is from the closely related, essentially identical Salmonella ortholog (2834324), with mechanistic detail from conserved Bacillus orthologs.

  • No ambiguity encountered. The efg/ntrL synonyms reflect historical naming (the "nitrogen regulatory protein" AltName), but the biochemical identity as the terminal NAD⁺ synthetase is unambiguous and well supported.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Direct enzymological parameters for the E. coli enzyme are sparse in the reviewed set. Precise kinetic constants (kcat, Km for NaAD, ATP, and ammonium) and the quantitative preference for ammonia over glutamine were not captured in the cited abstracts. While ammonia dependence is well established for prokaryotic NadE as a class, quantitative substrate-affinity data specific to P18843 would strengthen the annotation.

  2. Atomic-resolution intermediate capture is from orthologs, not E. coli itself. The NAD-adenylate intermediate was visualized at 1 Å in B. subtilis (12077433). The E. coli structures (15699042) show substrate/product states and conformational dynamics, but the covalent intermediate structure cited is from the ortholog. Family-wide conservation makes the extrapolation sound, but an E. coli-specific intermediate structure would close the gap.

  3. The ntrL/"nitrogen regulatory protein" alternative name is unexplained by the reviewed literature. Whether NadE has any genuine regulatory/moonlighting role connected to nitrogen metabolism (beyond consuming ammonia as a substrate) was not resolved. This historical annotation deserves scrutiny.

  4. Regulation of nadE expression and NAD⁺ pool homeostasis at the physiological level was addressed only broadly (26443758). How NadE activity is tuned to NAD⁺ demand in E. coli is not detailed here.

  5. In vivo flux and metabolic control analysis quantifying how much NadE actually limits NAD⁺ flux under various growth conditions was not examined.

Conclusion

E. coli NadE (P18843) is the ammonia-dependent NAD⁺ synthetase (EC 6.3.1.5), a soluble cytoplasmic homodimer that catalyzes the final, essential step of NAD⁺ biosynthesis — the ATP- and Mg²⁺-dependent amidation of nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide (NaAD) to NAD⁺, using free ammonia as the nitrogen donor and proceeding through an NAD-adenylate intermediate. As a member of the N-type ATP pyrophosphatase family with substrate sites at the dimer interface and a per-subunit ATP site, it functions as the single convergence point of both the de novo (nadA/B/C) and salvage/Preiss–Handler (pncA/pncB) pathways. Its essentiality, demonstrated by lethal nadE mutants that accumulate NaAD, makes it a validated antibacterial drug target with exploitable species-specific active-site features.
