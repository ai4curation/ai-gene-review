Functional Annotation Report: sucD (P0AGE9) — Succinyl-CoA Synthetase α-Subunit of Escherichia coli K-12

Gene: sucD (OrderedLocusNames b0729, JW0718) · Organism: Escherichia coli K-12 Protein: Succinate–CoA ligase [ADP-forming] subunit alpha (SCS-α); EC 6.2.1.5 Family: Succinate/malate CoA-ligase α-subunit (ATP-grasp / CoA-binding superfamily)

Summary

The gene sucD (ordered locus b0729 / JW0718; UniProt P0AGE9) of Escherichia coli K-12 encodes the α-subunit of succinyl-CoA synthetase (SCS), also called succinate–CoA ligase [ADP-forming] or succinate thiokinase, EC 6.2.1.5. This enzyme catalyzes the only substrate-level phosphorylation step of the citric acid (TCA) cycle, carrying out the reversible reaction:

succinyl-CoA + Pᵢ + NDP ⇌ succinate + CoA + NTP (N = adenosine or guanosine)

The identity, organism, protein family, and catalytic residues given in the UniProt record were all independently verified against the primary literature and against the P0AGE9 sequence itself. There is no gene-symbol ambiguity: all authoritative E. coli literature using the symbol sucD refers to this exact, well-characterized protein.

The α-subunit (sucD) is the catalytic heart of the enzyme. It carries the active-site histidine (His246α) that is transiently phosphorylated during catalysis (forming a 3-phosphohistidine intermediate) and provides the binding site for coenzyme A / succinyl-CoA. The companion β-subunit (sucC) supplies the nucleotide-binding site, which lies in an ATP-grasp fold roughly 35 Å away from His246α. Consequently, the phosphoryl group is relayed across the enzyme by a large-scale conformational movement of the His246α loop — the "swinging histidine." The functional enzyme is a soluble, cytoplasmic (αβ)₂ heterotetramer in which each α-subunit contacts only β-subunits, and the two active sites are built at α/β interfaces.

Physiologically, sucD is the distal gene of the nine-gene sdhCDAB–sucABCD operon encoding core aerobic TCA-cycle enzymes. Its expression is coordinately regulated with the 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase genes (sucAB) and is repressed anaerobically through ArcA and FNR, matching its role in aerobic energy metabolism. The enzyme conserves the thioester-bond energy of succinyl-CoA as a nucleoside triphosphate; in E. coli it can use both GDP/GTP and ADP/ATP but prefers ADP/ATP.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — sucD encodes the catalytic α-subunit of succinyl-CoA synthetase (EC 6.2.1.5)

The foundational identification comes from Buck, Spencer & Guest (1985), who sequenced the E. coli sucABCD gene cluster and showed that "The sucD gene comprises 864 base pairs (288 codons, excluding the start and stop codons), and it encodes a product of Mr 29 644, corresponding to the alpha subunit of succinyl-CoA synthetase" (PMID: 3002435). This directly ties the gene symbol to the α-subunit protein of calculated mass ~29.6 kDa.

The catalytic role in central metabolism is stated plainly by Fraser, James, Bridger & Wolodko (1999): "Succinyl-CoA synthetase (SCS) carries out the substrate-level phosphorylation of GDP or ADP in the citric acid cycle" (PMID: 9917402). SCS is the only TCA-cycle enzyme that generates a nucleoside triphosphate directly (rather than a reduced cofactor such as NADH/FADH₂), making it the sole point of net substrate-level ATP/GTP conservation in the cycle. The full reversible reaction, succinyl-CoA + Pᵢ + NDP ⇌ succinate + CoA + NTP, is given by Fraser et al. 2000 (PMID: 10873456). (Finding F001)

Finding 2 — The α-subunit carries the catalytic phosphohistidine (His246α) and the CoA-binding site

The 2.5 Å crystal structure of E. coli SCS by Wolodko, Fraser, James & Bridger (1994) localized both key chemical functions to the α-subunit: "One molecule of coenzyme A is bound to each alpha-subunit at a typical nucleotide-binding motif, and His-246 of each alpha-subunit is phosphorylated. This phosphohistidine, a catalytic intermediate, is stabilized by two helix dipoles (the 'power' helices), one from each of the two subunit types" (PMID: 8144675).

This establishes two cardinal facts: (1) CoA/succinyl-CoA binds the α-subunit, and (2) His246α is the residue transiently phosphorylated during catalysis. The "power helix" dipoles that stabilize the labile phosphohistidine are contributed one each by α and β — which is why the chemistry requires the intact heterodimer. Buck et al. 1985 had already noted that the α-subunit contains a 12-residue stretch identical to a previously isolated active-site histidine peptide, foreshadowing this structural result. The phosphorylated-His246α intermediate was later confirmed in the ADP complex structure (Joyce et al. 2000, PMID: 10625475). (Finding F002)

Finding 3 — SCS is an (αβ)₂ heterotetramer; α contacts only β, and active sites lie at α/β interfaces

Wolodko et al. 1994 also defined the quaternary architecture: "The quaternary organization of the E. coli enzyme is an alpha 2 beta 2 heterotetramer. In this tetramer, the alpha-subunits interact only with the beta-subunits, whereas the beta-subunits interact to form the dimer of alpha beta-dimers. The two active site pockets are located at regions of contact between alpha- and beta-subunits" (PMID: 8144675).

The structural implication is that the α-subunit cannot function alone — its active site is completed by the β-subunit, and the tetramer is held together by β–β contacts. Fraser et al. 1999 refined the (αβ)₂ tetramer at 2.3 Å with two αβ copies per asymmetric unit (PMID: 9917402). This explains why sucC and sucD are co-expressed and translationally coupled (the sucC stop codon overlaps the sucD start by one nucleotide; Buck et al. 1985), ensuring stoichiometric 1:1 subunit production. (Finding F003)

Finding 4 — The phosphoryl relay requires a ~35 Å movement of His246α ("swinging histidine")

A defining mechanistic feature of SCS is that its two chemical sub-sites are far apart. Fraser et al. 1999 reasoned: "the binding site for the nucleotide may reside in the N-terminal domain of the beta-subunit. If this is so, the catalytic histidine residue would have to move about 35 A to react with the nucleotide" (PMID: 9917402).

This was confirmed experimentally. Joyce et al. (2000) solved the structure with "an ADP-Mg(2+) complex bound in the ATP-grasp fold of the N-terminal domain of each beta-subunit" (PMID: 10625475), proving the nucleotide binds on β, distant from His246α on α. Earlier, Joyce et al. (1999) had localized the nucleotide site to the N-terminal ATP-grasp domain of β by 8-azido-ATP photoaffinity labeling and site-directed mutagenesis, concluding the H246α loop moves between the two sites during catalysis (PMID: 10353839). The mechanism therefore proceeds in discrete chemical steps linked by a large conformational excursion of the His246α loop — the "swinging histidine" model. (Finding F004)

Finding 5 — E. coli SCS uses both GDP/GTP and ADP/ATP, preferring ADP/ATP; Cys123α tunes but is not a covalent intermediate

Nucleotide specificity is a property of the β-subunit site, not α. Joyce et al. (2012) contrast organisms: "...Escherichia coli SCS, which uses GDP/GTP but prefers ADP/ATP" (PMID: 22751660). Specificity is governed by residues around the β-site; e.g., the P20βQ mutation lowered the apparent Kₘ for GTP more than 5-fold (Joyce et al. 1999, PMID: 10353839). The true nucleotide substrate is an ADP·Mg²⁺ complex (Joyce et al. 2000, PMID: 10625475), so Mg²⁺ is required.

A conserved α-subunit residue, Cys123α, lies near both the CoA-binding site and His246α, raising the possibility of a covalent succinyl-thioester intermediate. Hidber et al. (2007) tested this directly by mutating Cys123α to Ala/Ser/Thr/Val; all mutants remained catalytically active, and the authors concluded: "This proved that the specific formation of a thioester bond with Cys123alpha is not part of the catalytic mechanism" (PMID: 17642514). The residue's size nonetheless matters for optimal activity, so it plays a tuning/structural role near the active site rather than a direct chemical one. (Finding F005)

Finding 6 — His246α is catalytically essential; the enzyme is a soluble cytoplasmic protein

The essentiality of His246α was demonstrated by loss-of-function genetics. Buck & Guest (1989) replaced the phosphorylation-site histidine with aspartate and found: "No activity was detected after the histidine residue at the phosphorylation site of the alpha-subunit was replaced by aspartate (His-246 alpha----Asp)" (PMID: 2548486). This is direct causal proof that His246α of the sucD product is the indispensable catalytic residue.

The same study established subcellular localization by biochemical fractionation: upon overexpression of sucCD, "the alpha and beta subunits accounted for almost 30% of the protein in supernatant fractions of the cell-free extracts" (PMID: 2548486). Recovery in the soluble supernatant (not the membrane/pellet fraction) identifies SCS as a soluble, cytoplasmic enzyme, consistent with its role in the cytosolic TCA cycle of E. coli and with the absence of any membrane-spanning or targeting sequence. The study further showed that the β-subunit CoA-site residues Trp-43β/His-50β are essential, while Cys-47β is not. (Finding F006)

Finding 7 — sucD is the distal gene of the sdhCDAB–sucABCD operon, co-regulated with aerobic TCA genes and repressed anaerobically by ArcA/FNR

Buck et al. 1985/1986 established the genetic organization: sucC/sucD are the distal genes of the sucABCD operon within the nine-gene citric-acid-cycle cluster gltA–sdhCDAB–sucABCD, and "expression of succinyl-CoA synthetase is coordinately regulated with 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase" (PMID: 3543212).

Regulation was characterized by Park, Chao & Gunsalus (1997), who showed sucABCD is primarily transcribed from the upstream sdhC promoter, varies ~14-fold with carbon source and ~4-fold with oxygen, and is anaerobically repressed: "The negative control of sucABCD gene expression seen under anaerobic conditions, like that for the sdhCDAB genes, is provided by the arcA and fnr gene products" (PMID: 9209026). Cunningham & Guest (1998) added that the weaker internal suc promoter is repressed by IHF and partially activated by σ³⁸ (RpoS), with primary regulation occurring at the upstream sdh promoter and extensive RNase III processing of the long readthrough transcript (PMID: 9720032). Together these place sucD firmly in the aerobic TCA-cycle regulon, expressed when a complete oxidative cycle operates. (Finding F007)

Finding 8 — Sequence-level confirmation of catalytic residues in P0AGE9

As an internal consistency check, the P0AGE9 canonical sequence was inspected directly. The catalytic His246α appears in the motif KGKRMGHAGAI (full-length His247 = mature His246); the conserved Cys123α appears as RMIGPNCPGVI (full-length Cys124 = mature Cys123); and the N-terminal CoA-ligase α signature ...GFTGSQ... is present. These map exactly to the InterPro domain assignments (IPR003781 CoA-binding; IPR005810 CoA-ligase α; IPR017440 active-site signature), independently corroborating the structural and mutagenesis evidence. All literature-reported functional positions are confirmed at the expected places in the actual sequence, closing the loop between annotation, structure, and sequence. (Finding F008)

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

SCS catalyzes a three-step, ping-pong mechanism that couples the free energy of the succinyl-CoA thioester bond to phosphoanhydride-bond (NTP) synthesis. The α-subunit (sucD) is central to the first two steps; the β-subunit (sucC) performs the third.

STEP 1 (α-site):   succinyl-CoA + Pi  →  succinyl-phosphate + CoA
                   (CoA and succinyl-CoA bound at the α-subunit)

STEP 2 (α-site):   succinyl-phosphate + His246α  →  P~His246α + succinate
                   (His246α becomes 3-phosphohistidine; phosphoryl captured)

   ── His246α loop swings ~35 Å from the α CoA/succinate site
      to the β nucleotide (ATP-grasp) site ──

STEP 3 (β-site):   P~His246α + NDP  →  His246α + NTP
                   (phosphoryl transferred to ADP/GDP → ATP/GTP)

Division of labor. The α-subunit (sucD) supplies the chemistry of CoA/succinyl handling and the mobile phosphocarrier histidine; the β-subunit supplies the nucleotide site and determines nucleotide specificity. Neither subunit is catalytically competent alone — the active site is built at the α/β interface, and both "power helix" dipoles (one per subunit type) are needed to stabilize the otherwise highly labile phosphohistidine intermediate. This explains the strict genetic coupling of sucC and sucD.

Physiological context. Within the TCA cycle, SCS acts between 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase (which produces succinyl-CoA) and succinate dehydrogenase (which oxidizes succinate). It is the single cycle step that conserves thioester energy as a nucleoside triphosphate (substrate-level phosphorylation). In E. coli, which prefers ADP/ATP, this yields ATP directly. Succinyl-CoA is also a biosynthetic branch-point metabolite (e.g., for lysine/methionine and tetrapyrrole/heme pathways), so SCS activity helps set the cellular succinyl-CoA/CoA balance. The operon-level regulation (aerobic induction, ArcA/FNR anaerobic repression) matches the aerobic functioning of the full oxidative TCA cycle; a reported phenotype in which ΔsucC/ΔsucD mutants show altered high-hydrostatic-pressure resistance via elevated RpoS activity (PMID: 30497599) is a pleiotropic consequence rather than the primary function.

Comparative perspective. Across species the enzyme family shows diversity in nucleotide specificity and quaternary structure, but the catalytic logic is conserved. Pig GTP-specific SCS is an αβ-heterodimer with CoA bound to the α-subunit N-terminal domain, its free thiol extending toward the catalytic histidine (PMID: 26249701); the pig enzyme's phosphorylated/dephosphorylated structures confirm the power-helix stabilization motif (PMID: 10873456). The same swinging-phosphohistidine chemistry recurs in related enzymes such as ATP-citrate lyase, where His273α is the phosphorylated residue (PMID: 12135479), and the phosphohistidine strategy is shared with nucleoside diphosphate kinase (PMID: 7669763). These cross-family comparisons reinforce, rather than challenge, the E. coli sucD assignment.

Strength of the evidence. The core annotation rests on direct, precise experimental evidence: an X-ray crystal structure visualizing CoA and phospho-His246 on the α-subunit (PMID: 8144675); loss-of-function mutagenesis proving His246α is essential (PMID: 2548486); and photoaffinity labeling plus additional crystal structures placing the nucleotide on β (PMID: 10353839, PMID: 10625475). These are gold-standard, low-throughput biochemical/structural studies specific to E. coli — exactly the type of evidence the research question prioritizes. High-throughput/physiological data (e.g., the HHP-resistance transposon screen, PMID: 30497599) are consistent but peripheral, providing context rather than mechanistic definition.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Static structural snapshots. The ~35 Å "swinging histidine" motion is inferred from multiple crystal structures of distinct catalytic states (free, phosphorylated, ADP-bound) rather than from a single continuous dynamic measurement. Time-resolved, cryo-EM, or computational studies of the loop trajectory in E. coli SCS are not available in the reviewed literature.

  2. In vivo nucleotide usage. Although E. coli SCS can use GDP/GTP and prefers ADP/ATP in vitro (PMID: 22751660), the actual in vivo partitioning between ADP/ATP and GDP/GTP under different growth conditions was not quantified here, and precise steady-state kinetic constants (Kₘ, k_cat) were not compiled.

  3. Role of Cys123α. Its size affects optimal activity (PMID: 17642514), but the precise structural reason (steric gating of CoA/succinyl positioning vs. His246α mobility) is not fully resolved.

  4. Localization inference. Localization evidence is biochemical (solubility, absence of targeting signals) rather than from direct imaging.

  5. Regulatory fine structure. While ArcA/FNR anaerobic repression and the dominant upstream sdh promoter are established, the quantitative contribution of each promoter/processing site to steady-state sucD protein levels across growth phases is not precisely delineated.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments / Actions

  1. Time-resolved / cryo-EM capture of the swinging histidine. Use time-resolved crystallography or cryo-EM of E. coli SCS trapped in intermediate states to directly visualize the His246α loop trajectory between the α and β sub-sites.

  2. Quantify in vivo NTP output. Use ¹³C/³¹P metabolic flux or in-cell assays to measure whether SCS produces predominantly ATP vs. GTP under aerobic vs. microaerobic growth, and compile Kₘ/k_cat for ADP vs. GDP.

  3. Dissect Cys123α geometry. Combine the existing Cys123α mutant panel with structural determination to clarify how side-chain volume at position 123α influences substrate positioning and catalytic rate.

  4. Promoter contribution mapping. Build chromosomal reporter fusions isolating each promoter/RNase III processing site in sdhCDAB–sucABCD to quantify their individual contributions to sucD expression across carbon sources and oxygen tensions.

  5. Mechanism of the RpoS/HHP link. Test whether altered succinyl-CoA/succinate pools in ΔsucD cells causally drive elevated basal RpoS activity, clarifying the connection to stress resistance.
