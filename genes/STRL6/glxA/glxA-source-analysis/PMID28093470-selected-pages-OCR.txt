INTRODUCTION

GlxA from the Gram-positive soil dwelling bacteria Streptomyces is a structurally
distinct member of the mononuclear copper-radical oxidase family [1]. Members
contain a copper-radical active site in which the radical resides on a post-
translationally formed 3’-(S-cysteinyl) tyrosine created through the covalent cross-
linking of a Cys and Tyr residues [2-4]. This Cys-Tyr redox cofactor together with the
one-electron acceptor copper (Cu) ion facilitates the two-electron stereospecific
oxidation of a range of primary alcohols to the corresponding aldehyde, with the
concomitant production of H2O: as a result of reduction of the co-substrate oxygen [5,
6]. In the CAZy (Carbohydrate Active enZyme) database, mononuclear Cu-radical
oxidases are classed into the auxiliary activity family 5 (AAS) [7, 8].
Phylogenetically, the AA5 family can be divided into two sub-families; subfamily 1
includes known (methyl)glyoxal oxidases and subfamily 2 encompasses galactose 6-
oxidases [9]. GIxA however, is not associated with either subfamily.

The gixA gene is widespread in actinobacteria, where recent studies have
brought to the foreground the central role GIxA has in the Cu-dependent
morphological development of the biotechnology important strain Streptomyces
lividans. GlxA is transcriptionally coupled to CslA, which belongs to the
GlycosylTransferase Family 2, and includes cellulose and chitin synthases, amongst
others [7]. Together CslA and GlxA produce/modify a B-(1,4)-glycan, of unknown
chemical composition. This glycan has been demonstrated to be critical for
development of aerial hyphae as the microbe switches from vegetative to aerial
growth coupled with the secretion of secondary metabolites such as antibiotics [1, 10,
11]. Deletion of cs/A blocks the formation of reproductive aerial hyphae on solid
medium [10, 11]. Likewise, a g/xA null mutant blocks development, coinciding with
the loss of glycan deposition at hyphal tips; the same phenotype as observed with the
cslA deletion [1, 12]. This is consistent with a model whereby CslA and GlxA
cooperatively function in glycan deposition at the hyphal tip and are jointly
responsible for the formation of reproductive aerial structures [1].

The W288A and W288F variants do not turnover glycolaldehyde
The physiological substrate for GIxA is presently undetermined. Unlike Gox, which
has a broad substrate specificity in vitro [5], the diose glycolaldehyde is the only
known compound to have activity with GlxA [1]. However, the catalytic efficiency at
pH 7.4 is low with a kea/Km = 1.22 M' s™ [1]. A coupled peroxidase assay as
previously reported for WT GIxA was carried out with the Cu(II)-loaded apo-WT
GIxA and the Cu(I])-loaded W288A and W288F variants. For the WT sample activity
in line with what has been previously reported was detected [1], but in contrast no
guaiacol oxidation product was detected for the W288A and W288F variants despite a
myriad of assay conditions sampled (i.e. increased variant concentrations, bubbling
samples with O>, addition of oxidants). This indicates that neither the W288A or
W288F variants are capable of producing H2O2 with the in vitro substrate
glycolaldehyde.
The Cys-Tyr radical is not formed in the Trp288 variants
EPR spectroscopy has previously been used to detect the 3’-(S-cysteinyl) tyrosine
radical in both Gox and GIxA [1, 2, 4]. Addition of either K3[Fe(CN)s] or K2[Ir(Cl)6]
to the mature WT GIxA or Gox enzymes results in a decrease in the Cu(II) signal and
the concomitant appearance of a three component free radical EPR spectrum [1, 28],
assigned to a Cys-Tyr radical [2, 4] (Fig. 7). Experimental and computational studies
on holo-Gox have shown that the cross-link allows electron delocalization onto the
Sy(Cys), making the Cys-Tyr radical less strongly coupled than a Tyr radical to the
Cu(II) centre [29]. As a result of this weaker antiferromagnetic exchange coupling
between the radical and Cu(II) site two paramagnetic species may be observed in the
EPR spectrum [28]. However, for the W288A and W288F variants, addition of either
oxidant does not yield the intense EPR signature seen for WT GlxA (Fig. 7). Instead,
a residual signal that is consistent with it being the same as the radical triplet assigned
to the 3’-(S-cysteinyl) tyrosine is detected (inset Fig. 7). This indicates that Trp288 is
important for stabilising the Cys-Tyr radical. Interestingly, the residual triplet EPR
