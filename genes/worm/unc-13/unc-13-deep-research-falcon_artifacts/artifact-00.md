| Identity and isoforms | The target is *C. elegans unc-13*, ORF **ZK524.2**, represented by UniProt **P27715**. Literature describes long and short products, **UNC-13L** and **UNC-13S**, sharing a C-terminal release-machinery region; UNC-13L has an extended N-terminus that supports active-zone positioning. A study explicitly used ZK524.2-derived UNC-13L/P27715 material. (pqac-00000003, pqac-00000006) | Identity, organism, isoforms, and domain organization agree with an UNC-13/Munc13-family presynaptic release factor; this is not an unrelated similarly named gene. | Kohn et al., October 2000, [doi:10.1091/mbc.11.10.3441](https://doi.org/10.1091/mbc.11.10.3441); Hu et al., August 2013, [doi:10.7554/eLife.00967](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.00967) |
| Primary function and null phenotype | *unc-13* null mutants retain broadly normal nervous-system architecture, synapse density, and postsynaptic receptor density, but accumulate approximately **2–3-fold more synaptic vesicles** and show almost no evoked cholinergic or GABAergic release. Morphologically docked vesicles are not fusion competent in Ca²⁺-free spontaneous-release or hyperosmotic-saline assays. (pqac-00000001, pqac-00000004) | UNC-13 is not an enzyme or transporter. It is an essential presynaptic organizer and vesicle-priming factor that converts docked synaptic vesicles into a fusion-competent state and also contributes to subsequent exocytosis. | Richmond et al., November 1999, [doi:10.1038/14755](https://doi.org/10.1038/14755) |
| Presynaptic/active-zone localization | Immunogold and morphometric analysis localized UNC-13 to the presynaptic plasma-membrane region within approximately **100 nm** of dense projections. Few vesicles contact this membrane domain in *unc-13* null animals, although a population within **30 nm** of the dense projection remains. (pqac-00000004) | UNC-13 acts intracellularly at presynaptic active zones, where it helps establish or maintain vesicle contacts in a spatially restricted priming domain rather than functioning extracellularly. | Weimer et al., August 2006, [doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2350-06.2006](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2350-06.2006) |
| C2A, UNC-10/RIM, and release timing | The UNC-13L N-terminus containing C2A is punctate at synapses, whereas isolated C2A is diffusely axonal; deleting C2A or the larger N-terminal region disrupts precise localization. Wild-type evoked EPSCs had a **18.56 ± 2.22 ms** 90–10% decay time, fast and slow constants of **5.29 ± 0.20 ms** and **40.30 ± 2.61 ms**, and a **77.21 ± 4.33%** fast fraction. N-terminal disruption prolonged and desynchronized release. (pqac-00000002) | C2A plus adjacent UNC-13L sequences recruit the protein near UNC-10/RIM and Ca²⁺-entry sites, increasing release probability and synchrony. Direct worm evidence supports UNC-13–RIM functional/positional coupling; detailed atomic interaction models draw partly on ortholog studies. | Zhou et al., November 2013, [doi:10.7554/eLife.01180](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.01180); Hu et al., August 2013, [doi:10.7554/eLife.00967](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.00967) |
| UNC-13L/S division of release | UNC-13L preferentially supports fast release close to dense projections, whereas slower release involves UNC-13L and UNC-13S at more distal or diffuse sites. The UNC-13L C2A region promotes active-zone anchoring and short release latency. (pqac-00000006, pqac-00000009) | Isoform-specific nanoscale positioning creates kinetically distinct vesicle-release pathways: UNC-13L favors tightly coupled fast exocytosis, while UNC-13S contributes to a more loosely coupled slow component. | Hu et al., August 2013, [doi:10.7554/eLife.00967](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.00967) |
| C1–C2B regulatory module | Deleting C2B increased stimulus-evoked fusion and tonic EPSC frequency by approximately **30%** without changing unitary EPSC amplitude; electrical silencing reduced this hypersecretion. Tonic fusion was absent without UNC-13. (pqac-00000003) | The C1–C2B region is not merely activating: in the tested state it restrains Ca²⁺-dependent release, consistent with an intramolecular regulatory switch. C1 recognition of DAG/phorbol ester and detailed lipid/Ca²⁺ coordination are strongly supported across Munc13 proteins, but specific molecular contacts should not be assigned to worm UNC-13 solely from ortholog structures. | Michelassi et al., August 2017, [doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2017.07.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2017.07.015) |
| MUN–syntaxin–SNARE role | A C-terminal UNC-13 fragment containing Munc-homology regions partially rescued *unc-13*. MHD2 substitutions F1000A/K1002A disrupted syntaxin interaction and reduced spontaneous event rate and evoked EPSC amplitude, despite a normal hyperosmotic-release pool. Open-syntaxin knock-in partially bypassed *unc-13* exocytosis defects. (pqac-00000004, pqac-00000008, pqac-00000010) | The MUN region promotes productive UNC-64/syntaxin engagement and neuronal SNARE assembly. Direct worm evidence shows that UNC-13–syntaxin coupling is required for efficient evoked fusion; the detailed model in which MUN opens closed syntaxin is additionally supported by reconstitution and ortholog structures. | Madison et al., December 2005, [doi:10.1016/j.cub.2005.10.049](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2005.10.049); Tien et al., November 2020, [doi:10.1038/s41467-020-19178-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-19178-x) |
| HC2M/C2C vesicle adaptor | Removing the C-terminal HC2M/C2C region strongly impaired transmission. Replacing it with RAB-3- or synaptogyrin-based synaptic-vesicle targeting restored about **70% of wild-type aldicarb sensitivity**; rescue required the RAB-3 membrane anchor, whereas plasma-membrane or generic organelle targeting was ineffective. (pqac-00000000, pqac-00000005) | HC2M is best interpreted as a synaptic-vesicle/endosomal adaptor that positions the MUN-containing priming machinery across the vesicle–plasma-membrane interface. The experiments do not fully distinguish release-site nucleation, docking, and biochemical priming. | Padmanarayana et al., March 2021, [doi:10.1073/pnas.2016276118](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2016276118) |
| TOM-1/tomosyn and UNC-18 pathway | UNC-18(P334A) partially bypassed the requirement for UNC-13; deleting *tom-1* enhanced this suppression and improved locomotion and acetylcholine release. Corresponding mammalian Munc18-1(P335A) enhanced SNARE assembly and partially bypassed Munc13 in liposome fusion. (pqac-00000007) | Genetic evidence places UNC-18 as a positive SNARE-assembly/priming effector downstream of or parallel to UNC-13, opposed by TOM-1/tomosyn. The liposome mechanism is **ortholog-based inference**, while suppression and neurotransmission phenotypes are direct worm evidence. | Park et al., September 2017, [doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0338-17.2017](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0338-17.2017) |
| 2024 applications and cautions | Contemporary studies use *unc-13* loss as a strong perturbation of synaptic-vesicle output in cell-nonautonomous stress, immunity, and longevity experiments. In cilia/CNG-channel work, UNC-13 dependence was contextual evidence for neuronal secretion rather than evidence of a new biochemical activity; no unc-13-specific effect size was supplied in the available passage. (pqac-00000011) | These experiments validate *unc-13* mutants as circuit-output tools, but downstream UPR, immune, lifespan, or anatomical phenotypes should not be annotated as UNC-13’s primary molecular function. Recent reports of altered neuronal positioning also caution that severe release mutants may not always be anatomically neutral. | Li et al., June 2024, [doi:10.1073/pnas.2321228121](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2321228121); Bokman et al., December 2024, [doi:10.3390/ijms252312956](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms252312956) |
