Gene Description

UNC-13 is the single Caenorhabditis elegans Munc13 ortholog, a large presynaptic protein of the active zone built from a DAG/phorbol-ester-binding C1 domain, calcium- and phospholipid-responsive C2 domains, a calmodulin-binding region and a central MUN domain. It is the priming factor of the synaptic vesicle cycle - by engaging syntaxin and promoting productive SNARE-complex assembly it converts docked vesicles into a fusion-competent, release-ready state. Animals lacking all UNC-13 products hatch but die as paralyzed larvae, with normal synapse architecture, an accumulation of synaptic vesicles and almost no evoked release at cholinergic or GABAergic synapses, while vesicles still dock but cannot fuse. The locus produces several N-terminally distinct isoforms whose different positioning relative to the active zone underlies fast and slow components of release. UNC-13 additionally augments dense-core vesicle exocytosis and is the point at which diacylglycerol and calcium signals set release probability.

Research report: Caenorhabditis elegans unc-13 (UniProt P27715)

Executive conclusion

The requested target is correctly identified: gene unc-13, ORF ZK524.2, in Caenorhabditis elegans, encoding the conserved presynaptic UNC-13/Munc13-family protein represented by UniProt P27715. Its reported C1-like diacylglycerol/phorbol-ester-binding region, multiple C2 domains, and MUN domain agree with the literature. This report does not substitute mammalian MUNC13 genes, Drosophila Unc13A/B, or unrelated similarly named genes for the worm target. One study explicitly identifies the analyzed long protein as ZK524.2/UniProt P27715 material. (michelassi2017ac1c2module pages 17-19)

UNC-13 is not an enzyme or transporter. It is a large intracellular active-zone protein whose primary function is to make synaptic vesicles fusion competent and to couple them spatially and biochemically to the neuronal SNARE fusion machinery. The strongest integrated model is that UNC-13 (1) positions release-ready vesicles at presynaptic membrane domains, (2) promotes priming through its MUN-containing C-terminal machinery and productive UNC-64/syntaxin–SNARE assembly, and (3) uses its C1/C2 and N-terminal modules to integrate lipid, Ca²⁺-dependent, calmodulin-associated, and active-zone positional signals. Direct worm experiments establish the localization and physiological roles; some atomic details of syntaxin opening and lipid/Ca²⁺ recognition remain informed partly by mammalian Munc13 structures and reconstitutions. (padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 1-2, zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 6-8, michelassi2017ac1c2module pages 17-19)

Identity, isoforms, and domain architecture

The unc-13 locus produces multiple nervous-system proteins, conventionally discussed as UNC-13L and UNC-13S. They share the conserved C-terminal exocytotic machinery, whereas UNC-13L possesses an extended N-terminal region that includes C2A and additional localization determinants. This difference is functionally important: UNC-13L is concentrated close to active-zone dense projections and preferentially supports rapid release, while UNC-13S is more distal or diffuse and contributes to slower release. (zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 22-23, hu2013unc13lunc13sand pages 19-20)

The experimentally supported modular organization is:

  • C2A-containing UNC-13L N-terminus: active-zone positioning and interaction/function with UNC-10/RIM; it affects release probability, synchrony, and spontaneous release.
  • C1 domain: the expected DAG/phorbol-ester-responsive module of the UNC-13 family. Its biochemical assignment is strongly conserved, although individual worm-specific lipid contacts should not be inferred solely from mammalian structures.
  • C2B and adjacent C1–C2B module: a Ca²⁺/lipid-responsive regulatory unit that can restrain release under basal conditions rather than acting only as an accelerator.
  • MUN domain: central exocytotic module involved in syntaxin engagement and SNARE-complex assembly.
  • C-terminal C2C/HC2M module: a Ca²⁺-insensitive vesicle/endosomal adaptor comprising the C2C region and neighboring helices; it helps connect the MUN machinery to synaptic vesicles. (padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 9-9, padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 1-2, michelassi2017ac1c2module pages 17-19)

Primary molecular function

Synaptic-vesicle priming rather than catalysis

The foundational electrophysiological study found that unc-13 mutants retained broadly normal nervous-system architecture, synapse density, and postsynaptic receptor density, but accumulated approximately two- to threefold more synaptic vesicles at neuromuscular junctions. Evoked release at both cholinergic and GABAergic synapses was nearly absent. Vesicles that appeared morphologically docked were nevertheless incompetent for spontaneous release in Ca²⁺-free conditions or for hyperosmotic-saline-evoked release. These findings identify UNC-13 primarily as an essential vesicle-priming/fusion-competence factor, not as a vesicle-production protein or neurotransmitter-metabolizing enzyme. Richmond et al., published November 1999: https://doi.org/10.1038/14755. (padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 1-2, zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 23-24)

Later work refined this conclusion. UNC-13 acts at several closely connected steps: establishing vesicle contact at release domains, priming vesicles, organizing their distance from Ca²⁺ entry, and promoting productive syntaxin/SNARE-dependent fusion. Consequently, “priming factor” is the best concise annotation, but it should not be interpreted as a single isolated biochemical reaction. (padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 9-9, padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 1-2, zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 23-24)

Syntaxin and SNARE assembly

A C-terminal UNC-13 fragment containing Munc-homology regions and the terminal C2 region partially rescued behavioral and secretion defects. Mutations F1000A/K1002A in MHD2 disrupted interaction with syntaxin and reduced both endogenous synaptic-event rate and nerve-evoked EPSCs, while leaving the hyperosmotic-release pool approximately normal. Thus, direct worm evidence shows that UNC-13–syntaxin engagement is needed for efficient evoked fusion and suggests UNC-13 regulates more than the initial formation of a primed pool. Madison et al., published December 2005: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2005.10.049. (zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 23-24, zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 22-23)

Open-syntaxin knock-in experiments subsequently showed partial bypass of unc-13 exocytosis defects, supporting the model that UNC-13 helps convert UNC-64/syntaxin into a productive SNARE-assembly state. Open syntaxin also enhanced release in other exocytosis mutants, so this suppression is mechanistically informative but not uniquely diagnostic of UNC-13 action. Tien et al., published November 2020: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-19178-x. (tien2020opensyntaxinovercomesa pages 15-16)

The expert consensus model is therefore that the MUN region helps open or position syntaxin within the UNC-18/Munc18-bound template and thereby promotes neuronal SNARE assembly. The genetic and interaction evidence is direct in worms; the detailed structural trajectory of “closed syntaxin → open syntaxin → SNARE complex” is partly based on ortholog structures and in-vitro reconstitution. (padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 1-2, park2017unc18andtomosyn pages 1-2)

Cellular and subcellular localization

UNC-13 functions inside presynaptic neuronal terminals, at or adjacent to the plasma membrane of active zones. Immunogold electron microscopy placed UNC-13 in a membrane domain extending approximately 100 nm from the presynaptic dense projection. In null animals, few vesicles contacted this domain, although a population within about 30 nm of the dense projection persisted. UNC-10/RIM localizes at dense projections and contributes independently to the nearest membrane-contacting vesicle population. Weimer et al., published August 2006: https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2350-06.2006. (zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 23-24)

UNC-13L’s C2A-containing N-terminal region is required for precise punctate localization. Isolated C2A was diffusely axonal; deleting C2A or the broader N-terminal region displaced UNC-13 from its normal active-zone position. Thus, C2A is necessary but not sufficient—the surrounding N-terminal sequences also contribute. (zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 6-8)

The C-terminal HC2M module supplies a complementary spatial function at the vesicle side. Artificially targeting the MUN-containing protein to synaptic vesicles through RAB-3 or synaptogyrin restored approximately 70% of wild-type aldicarb sensitivity, whereas plasma-membrane or generic organelle targeting did not. Rescue by RAB-3 required its prenylated membrane anchor. These findings favor a trans-vesicle adaptor model in which HC2M connects UNC-13 to the synaptic-vesicle/endosomal membrane, while other parts engage the target membrane and fusion apparatus. Padmanarayana et al., published March 2021: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2016276118. (padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 9-9, padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 9-11)

The authors appropriately caution that these experiments cannot fully separate release-site nucleation, morphological docking, and biochemical priming. HC2M should therefore be annotated as promoting vesicle association and efficient priming, not as a proven standalone tether with a fully identified binding partner. (padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 9-11)

Isoform-specific release kinetics

UNC-13L and UNC-13S constitute a spatial “code” for release kinetics. UNC-13L positioned close to the dense projection supports the rapid component, whereas UNC-13 proteins at more distal or diffuse sites contribute to slower release; TOM-1/tomosyn preferentially inhibits the latter pathway. Hu et al., published August 2013: https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.00967. (zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 22-23, hu2013unc13lunc13sand pages 19-20)

Quantitative recordings in wild type yielded an evoked-EPSC 90–10% decay time of 18.56 ± 2.22 ms, fast and slow time constants of 5.29 ± 0.20 ms and 40.30 ± 2.61 ms, and a fast fraction of 77.21 ± 4.33%. Removing C2A altered release kinetics, while deleting the larger UNC-13L N-terminus caused still more prolonged, desynchronized release. A diffuse N-terminally deleted construct could rescue evoked-EPSC amplitude and increase sucrose-evoked release, indicating that vesicles can be primed away from optimally positioned active-zone sites but then fuse more slowly. Zhou et al., published November 2013: https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.01180. (zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 6-8)

This is important mechanistically: UNC-13 abundance alone does not determine output. Its nanoscale distance from Ca²⁺ channels and dense projections controls release probability and latency.

Regulatory domains and signaling inputs

C1–C2B regulation

Deletion of C2B enhanced stimulus-evoked fusion and increased tonic EPSC frequency by approximately 30%, without changing unitary EPSC amplitude. Silencing the nervous system with histamine reduced this hypersecretion, demonstrating that much of it was activity/Ca²⁺ dependent. Complete absence of UNC-13 abolished tonic cholinergic fusion. Michelassi et al., published August 2017: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2017.07.015. (michelassi2017ac1c2module pages 17-19)

These data overturned an overly simple view that every C1/C2 input merely activates UNC-13. Instead, the C1–C2B segment can form an autoinhibitory regulatory module, with ligand binding or conformational changes shifting UNC-13 between restrained and release-promoting states. C1 recognition of DAG/phorbol esters, C2-domain Ca²⁺/phospholipid sensing, and calmodulin-dependent acceleration are consistent with the family architecture; however, exact residue-level assignments derived from mammalian Munc13 should be labeled ortholog-based unless independently tested in the worm protein. (padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 1-2, michelassi2017ac1c2module pages 17-19, hu2013unc13lunc13sand pages 19-20)

UNC-10/RIM and Ca²⁺-channel coupling

UNC-10/RIM helps organize dense projections and contributes to positioning UNC-13L near Ca²⁺ entry sites. C2A-containing UNC-13L supports higher release probability, shorter latency, and synchronized exocytosis. The worm data establish functional and spatial coupling; some claims of direct molecular linkage to specific CaV channel subunits derive from broader RIM literature rather than a purified worm UNC-13–channel complex. (zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 6-8, zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 23-24)

UNC-18/Munc18 and TOM-1/tomosyn

UNC-18 and TOM-1 exert opposing effects downstream of or parallel to UNC-13. The gain-of-function UNC-18(P334A) mutation partially bypassed UNC-13 loss; removing tom-1 enhanced this suppression and improved locomotion and acetylcholine release. Corresponding mammalian Munc18-1(P335A) promoted SNARE-complex formation and partially bypassed Munc13 in liposome-fusion assays. Park et al., published September 2017: https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0338-17.2017. (park2017unc18andtomosyn pages 1-2)

The evidence supports a pathway in which UNC-13 enables productive syntaxin/SNARE assembly, UNC-18 stabilizes or templates that assembly, and TOM-1/tomosyn competes with productive SNARE formation. Genetic ordering in worms is direct; the detailed biochemical template mechanism is reinforced by mammalian reconstitution.

Biological processes and organismal consequences

The immediate biological process is chemical synaptic transmission, especially synaptic-vesicle docking/priming, active-zone organization, and Ca²⁺-evoked exocytosis at cholinergic and GABAergic neuromuscular junctions. Severe loss produces uncoordinated movement because motor-neuron output is nearly abolished, not because muscle receptors or gross synapse numbers are initially absent. (padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 1-2, zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 23-24)

Broad behavioral, developmental, immune, metabolic, and stress phenotypes are usually downstream consequences of blocking neuronal secretion rather than separate biochemical functions of UNC-13. This distinction is essential for functional annotation.

Developments in 2023–2024 and current applications

The 2023–2024 literature primarily expands the experimental use and organism-level consequences of unc-13 perturbation rather than revising its core molecular mechanism.

  1. Circuit-output control: Modern studies use unc-13 null or strong loss-of-function alleles as a genetic method to suppress conventional synaptic-vesicle release while comparing them with unc-31/CAPS mutants, which predominantly impair dense-core-vesicle/neuropeptide release. This allows investigators to distinguish classical neurotransmitter signaling from peptide secretion.

  2. Cell-nonautonomous stress and longevity: A June 2024 PNAS study of sensory-cilia/CNG-channel inhibition reported that intestinal UPRER activation and longevity signaling involved neuronal secretory machinery, including UNC-13. This demonstrates dependence on neuronal output but does not establish UPRER regulation as UNC-13’s primary biochemical function; the available passage contained no unc-13-specific effect size. Li et al.: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2321228121. (li2024inhibitionofa pages 10-11)

  3. Anatomical caveat for circuit experiments: A December 2024 study reported aberrant positioning of head chemosensory neurons in an unc-13 neurotransmitter-release mutant, whereas the tested unc-31 neuropeptide-release mutant did not show the same pattern. This challenges the frequent assumption that unc-13 mutants are functionally silent but anatomically normal. Researchers should therefore verify neuronal morphology when attributing behavioral phenotypes solely to acute loss of synaptic transmission. Bokman et al.: https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms252312956.

  4. Presynaptic homeostasis: A 2024 PNAS study used UNC-13 blockade as evidence that reduced vesicle exocytosis feeds back to increase presynaptic CaV2-channel abundance, revealing inverse coupling between release efficiency and channel abundance. This places UNC-13 perturbation within active-zone homeostatic experiments, but the feedback response is downstream of UNC-13’s priming function rather than a direct UNC-13 catalytic activity.

  5. Neuroimmune and inter-tissue signaling: Recent worm studies have used unc-13 dependence to implicate synaptic neurotransmission in microbiota–brain control of innate immunity and in neuronal regulation of systemic mitochondrial or ER stress responses. These applications illustrate UNC-13’s value as a selective genetic gate for conventional synaptic output; they should not be converted into annotations that UNC-13 directly senses pathogens, mitochondria, or unfolded proteins.

No 2023–2024 direct mechanistic paper retrieved here overturned the established model. The most recent decisive domain-level advance in the reviewed evidence remains the 2021 identification of HC2M/C2C as a conserved vesicle-targeting module. (padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 9-9, padmanarayana2021auniquec2 pages 9-11)

Evidence-weighted functional annotation

Recommended primary annotation:

UNC-13 is a multidomain presynaptic active-zone protein essential for synaptic-vesicle priming and efficient Ca²⁺-evoked neurotransmitter release. Its MUN-containing C-terminal machinery promotes productive syntaxin/SNARE assembly; its N-terminal/C2A region controls positioning near UNC-10/RIM and Ca²⁺ entry sites; its C1–C2 modules integrate lipid and Ca²⁺-dependent regulation; and its C-terminal HC2M/C2C module associates the priming machinery with synaptic vesicles.

Location: cytoplasmic face of presynaptic active zones and associated synaptic-vesicle/plasma-membrane interfaces, enriched within roughly 100 nm of dense projections. (zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 6-8, zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 23-24)

Pathway: active-zone assembly and spatial coupling → vesicle contact/docking-associated organization → UNC-13/UNC-18-dependent syntaxin and SNARE assembly → priming → Ca²⁺-triggered membrane fusion; negatively regulated by TOM-1/tomosyn and modulated by UNC-10/RIM, DAG/phorbol-ester-responsive C1 signaling, C2-domain regulation, and calmodulin-associated inputs. (michelassi2017ac1c2module pages 17-19, zhou2013positionofunc13 pages 23-24, park2017unc18andtomosyn pages 1-2)

Evidence confidence: very high for essential presynaptic priming, active-zone localization, isoform-specific release kinetics, and functional interaction with syntaxin/RIM/tomosyn pathways; moderate for the exact physical tethering partner of HC2M and for residue-level lipid/Ca²⁺ mechanisms transferred from ortholog structures.

Key limitations

  • UniProt domain labels correctly identify domain classes but do not by themselves establish every domain’s direction of regulation in vivo.
  • “Docking,” “membrane contact,” and “priming” are experimentally related but not interchangeable. Fixation-dependent ultrastructure can classify vesicle contact, whereas electrophysiology and hyperosmotic assays assess fusion competence.
  • Mammalian Munc13 biochemical and structural findings are highly informative because the machinery is conserved, but they should remain explicitly labeled as ortholog-based inference when direct C. elegans validation is absent.
  • Severe unc-13 mutants are powerful tools for blocking synaptic output, but the 2024 neuronal-positioning result indicates that developmental anatomy should be checked in long-term mutant experiments.

unc-13 (C. elegans Munc13, UniProt P27715) - curation notes

Identity

  • Swiss-Prot UNC13_CAEEL, P27715, 2155 aa, ORF ZK524.2; sole worm Munc13 ortholog (vertebrates:
    UNC13A/B/C). Multiple transcripts from alternative splicing and promoters share the C-terminal
    release machinery (C1, C2B, MUN, C2C) but differ N-terminally (UNC-13L carries C2A)
    PMID:11029047.
  • The null allele deleting all products hatches but dies as a paralyzed L1
    PMID:11029047.

Core biology: active-zone priming factor that makes docked vesicles fusion-competent

  • Electrophysiology of unc-13 nulls: normal nervous-system architecture, normal synapse and
    postsynaptic receptor densities, a two- to threefold accumulation of synaptic vesicles, and
    near-complete loss of evoked release at both cholinergic and GABAergic synapses
    [PMID:10526333 "Mutants of unc-13 had normal nervous system architecture"; "evoked release at both GABAergic and cholinergic synapses was almost absent in unc-13 null alleles"].
  • Crucially, vesicles still dock morphologically but are not release-competent, assayed by
    calcium-free spontaneous release and hyperosmotic challenge, placing UNC-13 at priming/fusion
    rather than docking
    PMID:10526333.
  • Independent EM work confirms the reciprocal relationship to UNC-18: unc-13 mutants accumulate
    tethered vesicles while losing docked ones, so priming (UNC-13) is separable from
    UNC-18-dependent tethering
    PMID:21423527.
  • Localization: the most abundant UNC-13 form is present at most or all synapses
    PMID:11029047, and UNC-13
    is an active-zone protein whose targeting is independent of ELKS-1
    PMID:15976086.
  • Molecular function: the family acts through syntaxin engagement; a WormBase production GO-CAM
    models the worm activity as syntaxin-1 binding at the presynaptic active zone, part of synaptic
    vesicle exocytosis [file:gocams/5b528b1100000489/5b528b1100000489-src.yaml].
  • Domain-level regulation: C1 (DAG/phorbol ester), C2B (Ca2+/phospholipid) and a calmodulin-binding
    region make UNC-13 the integration point for second messengers controlling release probability
    [file:worm/unc-13/unc-13-deep-research-falcon.md].

Dense-core vesicles and secondary/behavioural roles

  • UNC-13 also augments dense-core vesicle exocytosis, in a UNC-31/CAPS-dependent manner
    PMID:18031683.
  • unc-13 mutants were used to show that small clear vesicle release is required for the
    cell-nonautonomous longevity signal downstream of neuronal XBP-1s
    PMID:23791175.
  • unc-13 interacts genetically with egl-30/Galphaq in the control of egg laying and pharyngeal
    pumping, which are behavioural outputs of DAG-regulated release
    PMID:14704167.

Annotations traceable to a linked marker mutation

  • GO:0007283 spermatogenesis and GO:0060282 positive regulation of oocyte development (both IGI,
    PMID:25261697) cite fog-3 and rnp-1 as interactors. In that paper unc-13(e1091) appears only as the
    cis-linked LGI marker used to follow fog-3
    PMID:25261697;
    the germline phenotypes are attributed to fog-3 and rnp-1, and no unc-13 germline experiment is
    reported. These two rows are therefore removed.

GO decisions (summary)

  • Core: syntaxin-1 binding (GO:0017075) at the presynaptic active zone (GO:0048786), driving
    synaptic vesicle priming (GO:0016082) within synaptic vesicle exocytosis (GO:0016079).
  • Non-core: dense-core granule priming, calmodulin binding, behavioural (pumping, egg-laying)
    and glutamatergic-transmission terms.
  • Synaptic vesicle membrane (IBA) over-annotates the location: worm UNC-13 acts from the
    presynaptic membrane/active zone, not from the vesicle surface.

  C2 domains, a calmodulin-binding region and a central MUN domain. It is the priming factor of the synaptic
  vesicle cycle - by engaging syntaxin and promoting productive SNARE-complex assembly it converts docked
  vesicles into a fusion-competent, release-ready state. Animals lacking all UNC-13 products hatch but
  die as paralyzed larvae, with normal synapse architecture, an accumulation of synaptic vesicles and
  almost no evoked release at cholinergic or GABAergic synapses, while vesicles still dock but cannot
  fuse. The locus produces several N-terminally distinct isoforms whose different positioning relative
  to the active zone underlies fast and slow components of release. UNC-13 additionally augments dense-core
  vesicle exocytosis and is the point at which diacylglycerol and calcium signals set release probability.
