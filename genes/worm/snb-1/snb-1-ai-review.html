Gene Description

SNB-1 is the Caenorhabditis elegans synaptobrevin (VAMP1/2) orthologue, a small tail-anchored R-SNARE of synaptic vesicles. Its cytoplasmic coiled-coil helix zippers with the plasma-membrane Q-SNAREs UNC-64/syntaxin-1 and RIC-4/SNAP-25 to form the four-helix SNARE complex that drives fusion of synaptic vesicles with the presynaptic membrane, releasing acetylcholine, GABA and glutamate. Animals lacking snb-1 hatch but arrest as uncoordinated L1 larvae, while hypomorphs with lesions on the t-SNARE-binding face of the helix are lethargic, aldicarb-resistant, and have defective pharyngeal and enteric neurotransmission; a residual release in nulls suggests partial substitution by another R-SNARE. SNB-1 colocalises with RAB-3 and synaptotagmin on synaptic vesicles, is delivered to synapses by the UNC-104 kinesin, and after fusion forms a plasma-membrane reservoir (about 30% of the protein) that is retrieved by AP180/UNC-11-dependent endocytosis; SNB-1::GFP is the standard presynaptic marker in the worm. The protein is also expressed in the germline, where it resides mainly on the plasma membrane and on secretory granules of oocytes.

Functional annotation report: C. elegans snb-1 / SNB-1

Executive conclusion

The requested identity is verified. snb-1 is the Caenorhabditis elegans neuronal synaptobrevin gene on genomic clone T10H9, corresponding to the supplied locus T10H9.4 and UniProt O02495. Its product is a synaptic-vesicle membrane protein of the synaptobrevin/VAMP family, not an enzyme or solute transporter. The primary function of SNB-1 is to act as the vesicular SNARE that couples a neurotransmitter-filled vesicle to the presynaptic fusion machinery. Its SNARE helix associates with the plasma-membrane SNAREs UNC-64/syntaxin and RIC-4/SNAP-25, and assembly of this trans-SNARE complex supplies force for fusion of the vesicle and presynaptic membranes. Direct worm genetics establish that SNB-1 is essential for efficient neurotransmission and organismal viability; the molecular description of SNARE “zippering” is supported additionally by the exceptionally conserved synaptobrevin mechanism across animals. (nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 2-2, nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 1-2)

No evidence was found that the search had inadvertently switched to an unrelated “SNB1” gene in another organism. The identity, organism, family, and supplied InterPro/Pfam annotations are mutually consistent.

1. Identity, family, and molecular architecture

The foundational study cloned the full-length C. elegans snb-1 cDNA, mapped the gene to genomic clone T10H9, sequenced mutant alleles, and rescued strong mutant phenotypes with a wild-type genomic fragment. Predicted SNB-1 was 68% identical to human synaptobrevin-2 and 66% identical to rat cellubrevin, firmly placing it in the synaptobrevin/VAMP family. This agrees with the supplied Synaptobrevin-like, Synaptobrevin/VAMP, V_SNARE_CC, and PF00957 annotations for UniProt O02495. (nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 2-4, nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 2-2)

Functionally, the architecture is that of a compact tail-anchored vesicle protein: a cytoplasmic synaptobrevin/V-SNARE coiled-coil motif is followed by a C-terminal membrane anchor. The membrane anchor retains SNB-1 in the synaptic-vesicle membrane, whereas the cytoplasmic SNARE motif contributes one helix to the four-helix neuronal SNARE bundle. Thus, SNB-1 has no catalytic reaction and no transported substrate. Its relevant “substrate” in a broad physical sense is the vesicle–plasma-membrane pair that is brought into fusion through protein-complex assembly.

The worm genetic data strongly support this assignment. Viable missense alleles L62F and A66G alter the hydrophobic face of the proposed amphipathic SNARE-interaction helix, precisely where disruption would be expected to compromise association with syntaxin and SNAP-25. The exact ternary-complex mechanism is therefore a synthesis of direct worm genetics and conserved SNARE biochemistry rather than a claim that purified O02495 itself has been reconstituted in every mechanistic step. (nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 2-4, nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 1-2)

2. Cellular location and site of action

Endogenous antibody staining and translational reporters detected SNB-1 predominantly in the nervous system, with strong labeling in the nerve ring and dorsal and ventral nerve cords. Most neuronal somata showed weaker labeling, while commissural and dendritic processes generally lacked prominent signal. SNB-1 colocalized with the synaptic-vesicle proteins RAB-3 and synaptotagmin, supporting its residence on synaptic vesicles clustered at presynaptic regions. (nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 2-4)

The protein’s operative site is therefore the cytoplasmic face of neurotransmitter-containing synaptic vesicles at presynaptic boutons and active zones. Before fusion, vesicle-associated SNB-1 engages UNC-64/syntaxin and RIC-4/SNAP-25 in the presynaptic plasma membrane. After fusion, SNB-1 temporarily enters the plasma membrane and must be retrieved and resorted into recycled vesicles. Accumulation of SNB-1 in neuronal somata when UNC-104-dependent transport is perturbed further connects it to kinesin-mediated delivery of synaptic-vesicle cargo from the cell body to axonal synapses. (nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 2-4)

SNB-1 is not itself an active-zone scaffold or calcium sensor. UNC-13-dependent priming, UNC-2/CaV2-mediated calcium entry, synaptotagmin calcium sensing, and active-zone scaffolds position and regulate the vesicle, whereas SNB-1 is a core membrane-fusion effector. This distinction explains why loss of SNB-1 broadly compromises transmitter release across neuronal classes rather than selectively altering one neurotransmitter-signaling pathway.

3. Biological process and pathway placement

SNB-1 functions in the synaptic-vesicle cycle:

  1. Biosynthesis and polarized transport: newly synthesized SNB-1 enters precursor vesicles in the neuronal soma and is transported toward axons and synapses, principally in the UNC-104/KIF1A-dependent synaptic-vesicle cargo system.
  2. Vesicle clustering, docking, and priming: SNB-1-bearing vesicles accumulate near active zones and become competent for release through interactions among UNC-13, UNC-18, syntaxin, SNAP-25, complexin, and other presynaptic proteins.
  3. Calcium-triggered exocytosis: calcium entry and synaptotagmin activation permit SNARE zippering; SNB-1 supplies the vesicular SNARE helix that links the vesicle physically to the target membrane.
  4. Endocytosis and recycling: after fusion, synaptic-vesicle membrane and SNB-1 are recovered, sorted, and reused. AP180/UNC-11 and other endocytic adaptors regulate this process; defects in upstream trafficking proteins can mislocalize SNB-1 without implying that SNB-1 itself is the trafficking regulator.

Recent trafficking work has refined the first step. A peer-reviewed May 9, 2024 PLOS Genetics study showed that LRK-1/LRRK2, AP-3, SYD-2/Liprin-α, and UNC-104 organize polarized transport and sorting of heterogeneous synaptic-vesicle-protein carriers, with SNB-1::GFP used among the cargo reporters. This places SNB-1 within a contemporary model in which synaptic-vesicle proteins can travel in compositionally heterogeneous carriers, some associated with lysosomal proteins. Importantly, the result concerns machinery that transports SNB-1; it does not show that SNB-1 regulates LRK-1 or AP-3. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1011253. (nadiminti2024lrk1lrrk2andap3 pages 31-32, nadiminti2024lrk1lrrk2andap3 pages 33-34)

An emerging 2026 bioRxiv preprint—not peer reviewed and later than the requested 2023–2024 priority window—used in vivo RUSH release in PLM touch neurons to track newly synthesized SNB-1. An early, predominantly anterograde population appeared in axons within 20 minutes without lysosomal overlap; by 45 minutes, another population overlapped endolysosomal compartments in soma and axon. Early carriers frequently co-migrated with SNG-1 and RAB-27 but less with RAB-3. Loss of SAM-4/Myrlysin, a BORC subunit, accelerated vesicle–lysosome overlap and reduced early SNG-1/SNB-1 co-transport. These findings suggest at least two temporally distinct biosynthetic routes and heterogeneous maturation of SNB-1 carriers. DOI: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.04.29.721573. (chauhan2026temporaltrackingof pages 1-6)

4. Experimental evidence for physiological function

Strong loss of function

Animals lacking effective SNB-1 function arrest shortly after completing embryogenesis. They retain limited movement but are extremely uncoordinated and unable to feed. Rescue by a wild-type genomic snb-1 fragment establishes that these outcomes are attributable to the locus rather than background mutations. SNB-1 is therefore essential for viability under standard laboratory conditions and for effective neuronal control of movement and feeding. (nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 2-4, nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 1-2)

Residual movement in putative null animals is scientifically important: it indicates that removal of SNB-1 severely reduces but may not abolish every form of membrane fusion or transmitter release. Possible explanations include another vesicular SNARE, SNB-1-independent spontaneous fusion, maternal protein, or residual activity not detected by the genetic lesion. Accordingly, SNB-1 should be annotated as the principal neuronal synaptobrevin required for efficient synaptic transmission, not as evidence that absolutely every synaptic-vesicle fusion event is SNB-1-dependent. (nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 6-8)

Hypomorphic alleles and pharmacology

Viable alleles md247, js17, js43, and js44 are resistant to aldicarb, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor. In worms, resistance generally indicates reduced presynaptic acetylcholine release because less transmitter accumulates when degradation is blocked. These mutants also show broad behavioral abnormalities consistent with reduced synaptic efficacy. The md247 allele contains a 20-base-pair duplication and frameshift, whereas js17/js43 and js44 carry the L62F and A66G substitutions, respectively. (nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 2-4)

Quantitative electrophysiology

Extracellular pharyngeal recordings provide the most precise organism-specific functional evidence. In snb-1(md247), pump duration increased from 134 ± 2 ms in wild type to 320 ± 5 ms. M3 inhibitory-neuron transients declined from 4.04 ± 0.08 to 0.65 ± 0.10 per pump, while normalized M3 amplitude fell from 0.354 ± 0.005 to 0.169 ± 0.004. These large effects demonstrate impaired glutamatergic input to pharyngeal muscle and show that the behavioral phenotype reflects a severe synaptic-release defect, not simply abnormal muscle responsiveness. (nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 6-8)

The combined aldicarb and electropharyngeogram findings indicate that SNB-1 supports transmission at multiple synapse types, including cholinergic and glutamatergic connections. Its role is therefore general vesicle fusion rather than neurotransmitter synthesis, receptor signaling, or transmitter-specific vesicular loading.

6. Current applications and real-world implementation

Live synapse and vesicle-cargo reporter

SNB-1::GFP is one of the standard live presynaptic reporters in C. elegans. Cell-specific constructs generate fluorescent puncta along axons, enabling quantification of synapse number, punctum intensity, vesicle distribution, axonal transport, and cargo mislocalization in intact transparent animals. For example, a 2013 study expressed SNB-1::GFP from a cholinergic promoter and quantified dorsal-cord puncta by confocal microscopy and automated image analysis. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0057842. (yu2013differentialrolesfor pages 2-3)

SNB-1 localization is also used as a downstream readout of trafficking-gene function. Loss of lnp-1, for example, mislocalized both SNB-1 and RAB-3; the accompanying aldicarb resistance, locomotor defects, and normal levamisole response supported a presynaptic trafficking defect. That experiment informs how LNP-1 controls vesicle cargo; it should not be interpreted as showing that SNB-1 directly controls LNP-1. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-9568.2008.06049.x. (mihaela2008analysisoflnp1 pages 60-64)

Reporter interpretation nevertheless requires caution. Overexpressed or tagged SNB-1 can differ from endogenous protein in abundance and trafficking, and changes in puncta can reflect altered transport, clustering, synapse number, vesicle turnover, or expression. Whenever possible, reporter phenotypes should be paired with endogenous staining, functional rescue, electrophysiology, or independent active-zone markers.

Pan-neuronal promoter

The snb-1 promoter is widely implemented as a pan-neuronal driver for transgenic disease models and mechanistic screens. In recent 2023–2024 research it has been used to express amyloid-β, tau, SOD1, TDP-43-related constructs, and neuronal proteostasis reporters. This is a practical application of snb-1’s broad neuronal expression, not evidence that endogenous SNB-1 causes Alzheimer disease, ALS, or tauopathy. The distinction is essential for functional annotation.

Translational relevance

SNB-1 itself is not an established worm drug target or clinical biomarker. Its principal real-world value is as a conserved in vivo model of neuronal SNARE function and as a reporter/promoter platform for studying synapse development, axonal transport, vesicle recycling, anesthetic action, neurodegeneration, and candidate therapeutic modifiers. Because core SNARE machinery is deeply conserved, precise worm genetics can reveal general principles of presynaptic secretion, but disease or drug conclusions require validation in mammalian neurons.

7. Expert assessment and remaining uncertainties

The strongest annotation is: SNB-1 is the broadly neuronal, synaptic-vesicle-localized v-SNARE required for efficient neurotransmitter exocytosis at presynaptic terminals. This conclusion rests on convergent evidence—sequence conservation, vesicle colocalization, transport behavior, allele-specific lesions in the SNARE helix, genomic rescue, pharmacology, severe organismal phenotypes, and quantitative electrophysiology. (nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 2-4, nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 2-2, nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 6-8)

Several boundaries should be retained:

  • The available evidence supports a structural/mechanochemical fusion role, not enzymatic catalysis or substrate transport.
  • Syntaxin and SNAP-25 are the expected and genetically supported target-SNARE partners, but some atomic and biochemical details derive from conserved-family studies rather than SNB-1-specific purified-protein experiments.
  • SNB-1 puncta mark synaptic-vesicle-rich presynaptic regions but are not identical to active zones; an active-zone scaffold marker is preferable when the question is specifically active-zone number.
  • Recent 2024 work mainly advances understanding of the trafficking machinery carrying SNB-1 rather than revising its core fusion function.
  • The 2026 RUSH study is mechanistically informative but remains a preprint and should be treated as emerging evidence. (chauhan2026temporaltrackingof pages 1-6, nadiminti2024lrk1lrrk2andap3 pages 31-32)

Final functional annotation

Gene/protein: snb-1 / Synaptobrevin-1, T10H9.4, UniProt O02495, Caenorhabditis elegans.

Primary function: Vesicular SNARE and core structural/mechanochemical component of the presynaptic membrane-fusion apparatus.

Site of action: Cytoplasmic face of synaptic vesicles at presynaptic terminals, transiently entering the presynaptic plasma membrane after fusion and subsequently undergoing endocytic retrieval and recycling.

Core partners/pathway: UNC-64/syntaxin and RIC-4/SNAP-25 in the neuronal SNARE complex; function coordinated with UNC-13/UNC-18 priming, synaptotagmin-mediated calcium triggering, active-zone machinery, UNC-104/KIF1A transport, and endocytic adaptors such as UNC-11/AP180.

Biological consequences: Required for efficient cholinergic, glutamatergic, and likely broadly neuronal transmission; strong loss causes postembryonic arrest, profound uncoordination, and failure to feed, while hypomorphs produce severe synaptic and behavioral defects. (nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 1-2, nonet1998synaptictransmissiondeficits pages 6-8)

Confidence: High for identity, family, neuronal expression, synaptic-vesicle localization, and requirement in neurotransmission; moderate-to-high for detailed partner-level mechanism because it combines direct worm genetics with conserved SNARE biochemistry; emerging for the precise composition and temporal routing of newly synthesized SNB-1 precursor carriers.

Identity and structure

  • Reviewed entry SNB1_CAEEL, 109 aa, WormBase T10H9.4. N-terminal cytoplasmic v-SNARE coiled-coil (domain 23-83) and a single C-terminal transmembrane anchor (87-107); type IV membrane protein of synaptic vesicles (from snb-1-uniprot.txt). Orthologue of vertebrate VAMP1/2.

  • First metazoan synaptobrevin mutants: nulls die just after hatching, hypomorphs are lethargic, jerky, aldicarb-resistant and have pharyngeal transmission defects [PMID:9412487 "Mutants lacking snb-1 die just after completing embryogenesis."; "The viable mutants are resistant to the acetylcholinesterase inhibitor aldicarb, indicating that cholinergic transmission is impaired."].
  • Postsynaptic responses are intact (levamisole sensitivity normal), so the defect is presynaptic PMID:9412487.
