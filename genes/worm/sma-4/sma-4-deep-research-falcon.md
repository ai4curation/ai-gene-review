# Functional annotation of *sma-4* in *Caenorhabditis elegans*

## Identity and principal function

**The research target is the worm gene *sma-4* (ORF R12B2.1; UniProt P45897), not a similarly named gene in another organism.** The accession and ORF designation are those supplied in the query; the retrieved primary literature independently establishes that *C. elegans sma-4* encodes a conserved dwarfin/Mad-family protein. Modern pathway analyses identify SMA-4 specifically as the **co-mediator Smad (co-Smad)** of the DBL-1/BMP-like **Sma/Mab** pathway. Its nonredundant partners SMA-2 and SMA-3 are receptor-regulated Smads (R-Smads). These findings agree with the supplied SMAD/Mad-homology domain annotation. SMA-4 should not be confused with the worm dauer-pathway Smads DAF-8, DAF-14, and DAF-3, or treated as experimental evidence about human SMAD4 merely because it is homologous to it. (savage1996caenorhabditiselegansgenes pages 3-4, savagedunn2017thetgfβfamily pages 2-4)

**Primary functional annotation:** SMA-4 is an intracellular, DNA-binding transcriptional co-mediator that converts reception of an extracellular BMP-like signal into changes in gene expression. It is **not an enzyme, transporter, or structural constituent of the cuticle**; its direct biochemical activity demonstrated here is sequence-dependent DNA binding by its MH1 domain. The physiological effects on body size and the cuticle follow, at least in part, from regulated transcription of extracellular-matrix genes. (savage1996caenorhabditiselegansgenes pages 3-4, madaan2018bmpsignalingdetermines pages 5-7, savagedunn2017thetgfβfamily pages 2-4)

## Signaling mechanism and molecular evidence

In the established pathway model, neuronally secreted DBL-1 engages the receiving cell’s type II receptor DAF-4 and type I receptor SMA-6. Receptor signaling activates R-Smads SMA-2 and SMA-3, which act with SMA-4 to regulate transcription. DAF-4 is also used by the distinct DAF-7 pathway; the type I receptors and Smad sets confer much of the pathway distinction. The commonly depicted SMA-2–SMA-3–SMA-4 heterotrimer is supported by genetics and conserved Smad mechanisms, but **the cited worm experiments should not be read as a direct measurement of every proposed complex interaction or stoichiometry**. (wang2005cterminalmutantsof pages 7-8, savagedunn2017thetgfβfamily pages 2-4, gumienny2013tgfβsignalingin pages 8-10)

The strongest SMA-4-specific molecular experiment is Madaan and colleagues’ 2018 study. Recombinant **SMA-4 MH1** bound GTCT-containing Smad-binding elements in regulatory DNA between the cuticle-collagen genes *col-141* and *col-142*. Changing GTCT to ATCT prevented effective competition for binding. By contrast, the isolated SMA-3 MH1 domain did not bind the tested probes. SMA-3 ChIP-seq associated SMA-3 with the same regulatory region **in vivo**, while reporter experiments established a requirement for its Smad-binding elements in epidermal expression. Together, these experiments support a transcriptional complex in which SMA-4 can provide sequence-specific DNA contact; they do **not** constitute SMA-4 ChIP-seq or prove that SMA-4 alone specifies every target in the living worm. (madaan2018bmpsignalingdetermines pages 3-5, madaan2018bmpsignalingdetermines pages 5-7)

Structure–function tests distinguish SMA-4 from receptor-regulated Smads. Altering its two terminal serines to nonphosphorylatable alanines or negatively charged aspartates did not detectably compromise rescue of *sma-4* mutant body length: the unrescued *sma-4(e729)* animals averaged **0.79 ± 0.05 mm** (*n*=40), versus **1.19 ± 0.07 mm** with wild-type SMA-4 (*n*=42), **1.18 ± 0.07 mm** with the AA variant (*n*=38), and **1.19 ± 0.08 mm** with the DD variant (*n*=32). Thus SMA-4 does not require an intact native C-terminal SS pair for this output; this is **not** a direct phosphoproteomic demonstration that no SMA-4 residue is phosphorylated. The contrasting C-terminal requirements of SMA-3 reinforce the co-Smad/R-Smad distinction. (wang2005cterminalmutantsof pages 3-4, wang2005cterminalmutantsof pages 6-7)

## Where SMA-4 acts

The supported compartmental annotation is **intracellular, with a nuclear transcription-regulatory function**. SMA-4 lacks the signal sequence and transmembrane features expected of a secreted ligand or membrane receptor; direct binding of its MH1 domain to regulatory DNA places a key action at chromatin. Conserved Smad MH1/MH2 architecture also supports nuclear access and protein-complex formation. Importantly, the frequently cited observations of nuclear accumulation and receptor-dependent nuclear/cytoplasmic partitioning were made using **SMA-3::GFP**, not directly imaged SMA-4 protein. The studies examined here do not resolve endogenous SMA-4’s precise nuclear-to-cytoplasmic distribution or demonstrate an SMA-4-specific change in nuclear localization after DBL-1 stimulation. (savage1996caenorhabditiselegansgenes pages 3-4, madaan2018bmpsignalingdetermines pages 5-7, savagedunn2017thetgfβfamily pages 2-4, gumienny2013tgfβsignalingin pages 8-10)

**The epidermis/hypodermis is an experimentally established site of SMA-4 function in growth control.** Expressing *sma-4* cDNA from a hypodermis-specific promoter increased mean body length of *sma-4* mutants from **0.72 ± 0.06 mm to 1.21 ± 0.09 mm**. Extra SMA-4 also suppressed the body-length effect of depleting the extracellular pathway regulator CRM-1, but did not restore wild-type length to animals lacking DBL-1. This genetic ordering supports action in ligand-responsive cells downstream of extracellular signaling, without establishing direct physical contact between SMA-4 and CRM-1 or the receptor. Pathway components are also reported in the pharynx and intestine, but the published pharynx-specific pathogen-rescue and GFP localization experiments predominantly test **SMA-3**; they should not be presented as tissue-specific rescue or direct imaging of SMA-4. (fung2007crm1facilitatesbmp pages 11-15, ciccarelli2024bmpsignalingto pages 6-10, wang2005cterminalmutantsof pages 2-3, gumienny2013tgfβsignalingin pages 8-10)

## Biological outputs tied to its signaling role

**Growth and extracellular-matrix gene expression.** Classical *sma-4* loss-of-function mutants are small and have abnormal male-tail patterning, closely resembling mutants in the shared receptor DAF-4; the original cloning and rescue experiments established SMA-4 as a necessary, nonredundant pathway component. Cuticle collagens supply a more precise connection between transcription and body length: DBL-1-pathway signaling controls *col-41*, *rol-6*, *col-141*, and *col-142* expression, and perturbing collagen dosage changes animal size. Regulation is developmentally contingent. At the L2 stage, *col-141* and *col-142* transcripts increased in DBL-1-pathway mutants; in adults, *col-141* expression decreased in *sma-3* mutants, consistent with adult epidermal reporters. The direct SMA-4 evidence is binding to regulatory DNA, whereas these stage-dependent endogenous transcript comparisons were conducted chiefly in *dbl-1* or *sma-3* mutants. (savage1996caenorhabditiselegansgenes pages 2-3, madaan2018bmpsignalingdetermines pages 3-5, madaan2018bmpsignalingdetermines pages 5-7, madaan2018bmpsignalingdetermines pages 7-9)

**Bacteria-specific host responses: 2023–2024 developments.** A 2023 *eLife* study tested two *sma-4* alleles: the Q246Stop *e727* allele and a CRISPR deletion, *jj278*. Both increased avoidance of the tested Gram-negative bacteria *Enterobacter cloacae*, *Klebsiella oxytoca*, and *Serratia marcescens*, comparably to other canonical DBL-1-pathway mutants. Both also increased avoidance of *Staphylococcus epidermidis* under conditions in which loss of DBL-1, SMA-2, or SMA-3 did not produce the same response. The authors therefore propose an additional, potentially DBL-1-independent SMA-4 contribution; its activating signal and direct transcriptional targets remain unresolved. Consistent with context-dependent regulation, *sma-4* mRNA increased after exposure to the tested Gram-positive bacteria in both wild-type and *dbl-1* mutant backgrounds. The hypodermal RAD-SMAD reporter used in the study reports **pathway output**, not SMA-4 protein localization. (madhu2023thedbl1tgfβsignaling pages 8-10, madhu2023thedbl1tgfβsignaling pages 6-8, madhu2023thedbl1tgfβsignaling pages 17-18)

A separate **14 June 2024** *PLOS Genetics* study provided direct SMA-4 loss-of-function evidence in bacterial infection: *sma-4(jj278)* worms had reduced survival on *Photorhabdus luminescens* compared with controls (**72 mutants and 78 controls** in the reported survival comparison). SMA-2, SMA-3, and SMA-6 mutants likewise showed reduced survival, supporting a requirement for the canonical BMP-pathway machinery in this setting. Those numbers are **sample sizes, not survival percentages or effect sizes**. The study contrasts with earlier evidence that a DBL-1 response to epidermal fungal infection can require SMA-3 **without** SMA-2 or SMA-4: the co-Smad requirement is therefore dependent on the pathogen response being studied, not a property to assume for every DBL-1 output. (ciccarelli2024tgfβligandcrosssubfamily pages 6-7, ciccarelli2024tgfβligandcrosssubfamily pages 7-10, gumienny2013tgfβsignalingin pages 8-10)

Recent pharyngeal experiments further clarify possible infection mechanisms—pharyngeal SMA-3 restored mutant survival, antimicrobial-peptide expression, and pumping during bacterial challenge—but **they did not establish these outcomes by manipulating pharyngeal SMA-4**. Consequently, impaired pumping or altered *abf-2*/*cnc-2* expression should not yet be assigned as a demonstrated SMA-4-specific mechanism for its survival phenotype. There is no clinical implementation of SMA-4 itself described by these worm studies; its current application is chiefly as an experimentally tractable, conserved co-Smad for dissecting BMP-dependent transcription, tissue-specific growth, and host defense. (ciccarelli2024bmpsignalingto pages 6-10, ciccarelli2024bmpsignalingto pages 16-20, savagedunn2017thetgfβfamily pages 2-4)
