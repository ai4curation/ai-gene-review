Gene Description

lis-1 (pnm-1, T03F6.5) encodes the C. elegans ortholog of LIS1/NudF, a WD40 beta-propeller protein with an N-terminal LisH dimerization motif that regulates cytoplasmic dynein. LIS-1 is distributed throughout the cytoplasm and enriched at the cell cortex, near microtubule asters, at kinetochores and at the nuclear periphery; its perinuclear targeting depends on dynein and dynactin. With the NudE homolog NUD-2 it forms a dynein-regulatory complex that the KASH protein UNC-83 recruits to the outer nuclear envelope. In the one-cell embryo lis-1 loss phenocopies dynein heavy chain (dhc-1) loss, abolishing pronuclear migration, centrosome separation and movement from the posterior cortex, spindle assembly and positioning; lis-1 also contributes to hyp7 nuclear migration, bipolar spindle formation in germline mitoses, and GABAergic synaptic vesicle trafficking in ventral cord neurons. Loss of lis-1 causes maternal-effect embryonic lethality, germline mitotic arrest and apoptosis, sterility, uncoordinated movement and increased convulsion susceptibility.

Core Functions

LIS-1 regulates cytoplasmic dynein (with NUD-2), acting at the nuclear envelope, cortex, asters and kinetochores to drive dynein-dependent pronuclear migration, centrosome separation and positioning, spindle assembly and orientation, and nuclear migration in hyp7 precursors.

1. Identity and domain verification

Correct locus

Cockell and colleagues explicitly determined that C. elegans pnm-1 and lis-1 are the same gene and thereafter used the name lis-1. Two severe alleles, t1550 and t1698, were predicted to truncate the protein, had almost no detectable LIS-1, and were not further worsened by RNAi, supporting their interpretation as likely null alleles. Antibodies raised against residues 1–141 recognized a unique approximately 45-kDa embryonic band; residual immunoreactivity was 5.2% after RNAi and 2.9% in t1550/t1698 mutants relative to wild type. (cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 3-4)

Independent work also identifies pnm-1(t1550) as a nonsense allele of worm lis-1. Thus, the symbol is unambiguous in the specified organism and experimental context. (williams2004epilepticlikeconvulsionsassociated pages 2-3)

Family and architecture

The supplied UniProt annotation places Q9NDC9 in the WD-repeat LIS1/nudF family and assigns LisH, LIS1-N/PAC1-like LisH-like, dynein-regulator LIS1, and WD40/YVTN-repeat-like domains. This architecture fits the literature’s classification of worm LIS-1 as a WD40-repeat protein. Functionally, the N-terminal LisH/coiled-coil region is expected to mediate dimerization and regulatory contacts, while the C-terminal WD40 β-propeller provides interaction surfaces for dynein. The detailed residue-level mechanism has not been solved directly for Q9NDC9, however; it is inferred from conserved LIS1 structures. (williams2004epilepticlikeconvulsionsassociated pages 2-3, singh2024molecularmechanismof pages 6-8)

2. Primary molecular function

A dynein regulator rather than a motor

Dynein itself hydrolyzes ATP and moves cargo toward microtubule minus ends. LIS-1 has no known catalytic reaction or transported substrate of its own. Instead, worm genetics and imaging show that it is required for efficient dynein function. Severe lis-1 loss phenocopies inactivation of the dynein heavy-chain gene dhc-1, including failed centrosome separation, pronuclear migration, and bipolar-spindle formation. Combining homozygous lis-1 mutation with dhc-1 RNAi causes near sterility, providing strong genetic evidence that the two participate in the same functional system. (cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 1-2)

An especially direct functional assay tracked embryonic yolk granules. Their minus-end-directed movements occur at approximately 1.5 μm/s in wild type but were essentially abolished after lis-1 inactivation. LIS-1 is therefore required in vivo for dynein-driven cargo motility, not merely for spindle morphology. The granules are dynein cargo in this assay; they are not substrates transported by LIS-1 itself. (cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 4-5)

Current mechanistic model

The leading modern model is that LIS1 promotes formation of active dynein–dynactin–adaptor complexes and regulates motor behavior under load. In a 29 March 2024 Science study, cryo-EM of a roughly 4-MDa dynein–dynactin–JIP3–LIS1 assembly showed LIS1 contacting both the dynein motor and dynactin p150. Its N-terminal region contacted p150, while its WD40 domains engaged the dynein AAA3–AAA4/stalk-base region. The reconstructed intermediate contained two dynein dimers and two LIS1 dimers; LIS1-bound dynein-A occupied a bent-linker, pre-powerstroke state, whereas dynein-B contacted the microtubule. The authors interpret LIS1 and p150 as constraining and orienting dynein–dynactin for productive adaptor binding, followed by LIS1 release as motility begins. (singh2024molecularmechanismof pages 6-8, singh2024molecularmechanismof pages 1-3)

This 2024 work used reconstituted mammalian proteins and HeLa-cell assays, not worm Q9NDC9. It is therefore a strong structural explanation for the conserved LIS1 family but remains an inference for the exact molecular contacts of C. elegans LIS-1. (singh2024molecularmechanismof pages 37-40)

A complementary 2024 optical-trapping study with yeast dynein found that Lis1 did not increase maximum force production. Rather, AAA-ring binding slowed motility, while stalk interaction prolonged stalls and slowed force-induced detachment from microtubules. Thus, LIS1 may help dynein remain engaged under opposing load while also promoting assembly of active motor complexes. This, too, is conserved mechanistic inference rather than direct worm evidence. (kusakci2024lis1slowsforceinduced pages 1-3, kusakci2024lis1slowsforceinduced pages 22-23)

3. Cellular localization

Functional GFP–LIS-1 and anti-LIS-1 imaging found the protein broadly distributed through the embryonic cytoplasm, with regulated enrichment at:

  • the cell cortex;
  • the vicinity of microtubule asters;
  • pronuclei and the nuclear periphery in late prophase;
  • chromosomes during prometaphase;
  • the spindle during metaphase and early anaphase; and
  • kinetochores. (cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 9-10, cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 1-2, cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 4-5)

Localization is not governed by one simple recruitment pathway. DHC-1 and the dynactin components DNC-1/p150Glued and DNC-2/p50/dynamitin are necessary for LIS-1 enrichment at the nuclear periphery, but not for kinetochore localization and only partly for cortical enrichment. Conversely, LIS-1 contributes to DHC-1 enrichment at the cortex and nuclear periphery but is not absolutely required for it. These reciprocal, compartment-specific dependencies support transient or partially independent recruitment rather than a constitutive LIS-1–dynein complex everywhere in the cell. (cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 9-10, cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 10-11)

Microtubule depolymerization does not eliminate LIS-1 from the nuclear envelope, cortex, or kinetochores. Moreover, worm LIS-1 was not detectably concentrated specifically at microtubule plus ends relative to the lattice, cautioning against simply importing the classic fungal plus-end-targeting model into the worm embryo. (cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 9-10, cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 10-11)

4. Biological processes and pathways

Pronuclear migration, centrosomes, and spindle assembly

In the one-cell embryo, lis-1 loss prevents male and female pronuclear migration and disrupts centrosome separation and bipolar-spindle assembly. Partial depletion also impairs spindle orientation. Centrosome–male-pronucleus association was lost in 19% of RNAi embryos examined (n=21), and some embryos contained multiple female pronuclei. These are nearly the same processes disrupted by dhc-1 inactivation, placing LIS-1 in the dynein-dependent cell-division machinery. (cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 3-4, cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 4-5)

The likely physical pathway is:

LIS-1 → dynein–dynactin activation/retention on microtubules → minus-end-directed forces and transport → pronuclear movement, centrosome separation/positioning, spindle organization and orientation.

This is a cytoskeletal motor pathway, not a conventional receptor-mediated signaling cascade.

Germline mitosis, spindle checkpoint, and apoptosis

Loss of lis-1 produces missing, abnormal, mispositioned, or multipolar germline spindles, polyploid nuclei, and mitotic arrest. Distal gonad arms contained 521.3±18.1 nuclei in a lis-1 mutant versus 711.1±12.4 in wild type (P<0.0001). Mitotic-region nuclei increased from 3.3±0.4 to 6.1±0.4, consistent with delayed exit or arrest. (buttner2007progressionfrommitotic pages 3-5)

The arrest-to-death pathway was resolved genetically. lis-1 RNAi increased phosphohistone-H3-positive nuclei from 4.8±0.4 to 8.2±0.9 in controls (P<0.005), but not in animals lacking the spindle-checkpoint component MDF-1. MDF-1 loss also suppressed excess corpses, placing spindle-checkpoint activation upstream of death. Death required the caspase CED-3 but not the CEP-1/p53 DNA-damage pathway. The most precise pathway is therefore:

LIS-1 deficiency → dynein/spindle failure → MDF-1 spindle-checkpoint arrest → CED-3-dependent germ-cell death. (buttner2007progressionfrommitotic pages 5-9, buttner2007progressionfrommitotic pages 10-12)

LIS-1 also contributes to corpse clearance. Corpse persistence averaged 10.9±0.9 hours in lis-1 mutants versus 2.3±0.2 hours in wild type; dhc-1 mutants showed a similar 9.4±1.7-hour delay. This likely reflects dynein-dependent intracellular trafficking during engulfment, although the exact cargo and cell-autonomous step were not resolved. (buttner2007progressionfrommitotic pages 9-10)

Neuronal vesicle organization

LIS-1 is expressed in proliferating cells and neurons. Adult mutant escapers displayed abnormal distribution of synaptic vesicles throughout GABAergic neuronal processes and seizure-like anterior movements after exposure to 10 mg/ml pentylenetetrazole, a GABA antagonist. Gross neuronal nuclear placement was normal: mutant and control ventral cords both had approximately 73±3 nuclei, arguing against defective neuronal nuclear migration as the immediate cause. The parsimonious interpretation is a presynaptic trafficking or vesicle-positioning defect associated with the dynein cytoskeleton. (williams2004epilepticlikeconvulsionsassociated pages 2-3, williams2004epilepticlikeconvulsionsassociated pages 6-7)

This neuronal result is biologically informative but less mechanistically complete than the embryo work: direct biochemical coupling of worm LIS-1 to a defined synaptic-vesicle adaptor was not shown.

Actin and broader cytoskeletal organization

Both LIS-1 and DHC-1 are required for normal actin-cytoskeleton integrity in the worm. Genetic suppression in a dhc-1/lis-1 double-mutant context indicates that the relationship can be antagonistic under some sensitized conditions rather than a simple linear loss of motor activity. These findings show cytoskeletal cross-talk but do not establish LIS-1 as an actin-binding protein; its primary molecular assignment remains dynein regulation. (gilkrzewska2010regulatorsofthe pages 1-2)

2024: dynein–dynactin assembly

Singh et al., published 29 March 2024, supplied the strongest recent structural advance. The study combined cryo-EM, AlphaFold2-assisted modeling, pull-downs, reconstituted motility, and cellular relocation assays. It extended the older “LIS1 merely opens autoinhibited dynein” model by identifying a direct LIS1–dynactin-p150 interface that helps orient dynein and dynactin for adaptor capture. Structural data include PDB 8PTK and EMDB 17873. DOI: 10.1126/science.adk8544. Kusakci et al. used single-molecule microscopy and optical trapping to show that yeast Lis1 slows force-induced dynein detachment and prolongs stalls without increasing force generation. Assays used 300 nM Lis1 for wild-type dynein and 900 nM for a dynein mutant at 2 mM ATP, with roughly 14–124 and 11–66 observations per force point. DOI: 10.1038/s41589-023-01464-6. The recent literature substantially refines the conserved LIS1 mechanism, but direct 2023–2024 experiments on Q9NDC9 itself are sparse. A 2022 live-embryo study quantified the sequential accumulation of dynein regulators, including LIS-1, around nuclear-envelope breakdown, supporting temporally controlled recruitment to the spindle region. It used normalized nuclear-to-cytoplasmic fluorescence and formal statistical comparisons, but did not establish a new LIS-1 biochemical mechanism. DOI: 10.1038/s41598-022-15042-8. (torisawa2022sequentialaccumulationof pages 13-14)

Consequently, the best current annotation combines direct worm genetics and imaging with cautiously transferred structural mechanisms from highly conserved mammalian and yeast systems.

6. Current applications and real-world relevance

There is no clinical or industrial application of worm LIS-1 itself. Its current implementations are research uses:

  1. Live-cell assay of dynein function. The transparent one-cell embryo permits direct imaging of pronuclear movement, centrosome separation, spindle positioning, and yolk-granule transport.
  2. Genetic dissection of mitotic surveillance. lis-1 mutants connect motor/spindle failure to MDF-1-dependent arrest and CED-3-dependent apoptosis.
  3. Neuronal-transport disease modeling. Worm neuronal phenotypes provide a tractable system for separating vesicle-trafficking defects from nuclear-migration defects.
  4. Interpretation of human LIS1 disease mechanisms. Because human LIS1 haploinsufficiency causes lissencephaly, conserved worm phenotypes help identify fundamental dynein-dependent processes that may fail in development. However, worm embryonic and germline phenotypes should not be equated directly with human cortical neuronal migration.
  5. Testing dynein-regulator interactions. Genetic combinations with dhc-1, dynactin, checkpoint, and cytoskeletal genes offer in-vivo validation of mechanisms suggested by purified structural systems.

7. Expert assessment and confidence

The strongest annotation is: LIS-1 is a spatially and temporally regulated dynein cofactor that promotes active motor-complex formation and productive microtubule engagement, thereby enabling force-dependent nuclear, centrosomal, spindle, chromosome-associated, and vesicular movements. This conclusion is supported directly in worms by phenocopy, genetic interaction, localization dependency, and cargo-transport experiments, and mechanistically by conserved 2024 structural and optical-trapping studies. (cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 4-5, singh2024molecularmechanismof pages 6-8, cockell2004lis1isrequired pages 1-2)

Confidence is high for the gene identity, nonenzymatic dynein-regulator assignment, intracellular localization, and roles in early embryonic division and germline mitosis. Confidence is moderate for the exact Q9NDC9 interfaces and force-response mechanism because those details derive from mammalian or yeast proteins. Confidence is lower for specific synaptic cargo/adaptor relationships and proposed direct effects on actin, which remain incompletely resolved.

8. Recommended functional annotation

Molecular function: nonenzymatic cytoplasmic-dynein regulatory and assembly cofactor; promotes dynein-dependent microtubule-minus-end transport and force production.

Biological processes: pronuclear and nuclear migration; centrosome separation and positioning; bipolar-spindle assembly and orientation; chromosome/kinetochore-associated mitotic function; germline proliferation and spindle-checkpoint-linked apoptosis; dynein-dependent vesicle and yolk-granule transport.

Cellular component/localization: cytoplasm, cell cortex, microtubule asters/lattice, pronuclei and nuclear envelope, mitotic spindle, chromosome vicinity, kinetochores, germline, and neuronal processes.

Key partners/pathways: DHC-1 cytoplasmic dynein; dynactin DNC-1 and DNC-2; MDF-1 spindle checkpoint; CED-3 apoptosis. NUD-2/NudE-family regulation is biologically relevant to dynein but is not required for all LIS-1-dependent embryonic functions.

Important limitation: no catalytic reaction, ligand substrate, or transporter specificity should be assigned to LIS-1. Its “substrates” are not molecules transformed or transported by LIS-1; rather, LIS-1 regulates dynein complexes that move diverse cellular cargoes.

2026-09-27 — initial review (claude-code)

Sources: UniProt Q9NDC9, cached GOA-cited publications (full text for PMID:17376425, 20005871, 20599902;
abstract-only for 11685578, 15331665, 16996038, 17922003). Deep research (falcon) not yet available when written.

Core biology

  • lis-1 null alleles phenocopy dhc-1 PMID:15331665.
  • Localization: cytoplasm, cortex, asters, nuclear periphery, kinetochores; perinuclear targeting requires dhc-1/dnc-1/dnc-2 PMID:15331665.
  • NUD-2/LIS-1 complex recruited to the nuclear envelope by the KASH protein UNC-83; hyp7 nuclear migration defects after lis-1(RNAi) PMID:20005871.
  • Centrosome movement from the posterior cortex requires DHC-1 and LIS-1 PMID:20599902.
  • Germline: bipolar spindle defects, checkpoint-dependent arrest and apoptosis, slowed corpse engulfment, univalents PMID:17376425.
  • Neurons: GABA synaptic vesicle trafficking defects and convulsion susceptibility PMID:16996038.

Decisions

  • NEW GO:0140659 cytoskeletal motor regulator activity (consistent with human/fly/module).
  • GO:0031022 nuclear migration along microfilament -> MODIFY to GO:0030473 (hyp7 nuclear migration is kinesin-1/dynein, microtubule-based).
  • GO:0051661 maintenance of centrosome location IMP -> MODIFY to GO:0051660 establishment of centrosome localization (phenotype is failure of centrosome movement); IBA of same term accepted as PAINT judgment (validator warns about inconsistent actions; intentional).
  • cytoplasmic dynein complex / dynein complex -> MODIFY to GO:0005875.
  • protein binding (NUD-2) -> REMOVE (no informative MF term; interaction not disputed).
  • ARBA signaling / cell communication -> REMOVE; ARBA phagocytosis and IEA synapse, IMP locomotion -> MARK_AS_OVER_ANNOTATED.
  • Deep research (falcon) arrived 18:11 and agrees: LIS-1 non-catalytic dynein regulator; yolk-granule minus-end transport abolished; not enriched at MT plus ends (supports non-core microtubule sliding). Incorporated as supporting text.
