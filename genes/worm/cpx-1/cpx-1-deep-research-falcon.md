# Functional Annotation Report: *C. elegans cpx-1* / CPX-1 (UniProt Q9GUM7)

## Executive summary

The target is correctly identified as ***Caenorhabditis elegans cpx-1***, encoding CPX-1, a small cytoplasmic presynaptic protein of the complexin/synaphin family. The supplied UniProt accession Q9GUM7, description “putative complexin-1,” and ORF name Y73E7A.4 are consistent with the biological identity studied in the worm literature. Direct studies identify CPX-1 as one of two predicted *C. elegans* complexins and the closer worm ortholog of fly and mammalian complexins. Its conserved central α-helix and surrounding domain architecture agree with the supplied Synaphin InterPro/Pfam annotations. The alias Y73E7A.4 was not independently recovered in the article text examined and is therefore accepted specifically from the supplied UniProt record rather than used as an independent literature cross-check. No evidence from a similarly named gene in another organism was substituted. (hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 1-2, radoff2014theaccessoryhelix pages 2-4, hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 2-3)

CPX-1 is **not an enzyme or transporter**. It is a soluble presynaptic SNARE regulator and membrane-associated adaptor whose primary role is to control synaptic-vesicle exocytosis. It has two experimentally separable activities: it suppresses spontaneous, calcium-independent or tonic vesicle fusion, while promoting efficient stimulus-evoked release and maintaining docked/primed vesicles. Its central helix binds the assembled neuronal SNARE bundle, whereas its C-terminal region binds highly curved synaptic-vesicle membranes and positions the protein to regulate SNARE assembly. (martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 7-8, hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 1-2, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 3-4, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 6-7)

## 1. Identity verification and family assignment

Two predicted complexin genes occur in *C. elegans*, **cpx-1** and **cpx-2**. CPX-1 is more similar to fly and mouse complexins: one analysis reported 39% overall identity to mouse complexin-1, versus 32% for CPX-2, while the central SNARE-binding region is much more strongly conserved. The canonical architecture comprises an N-terminal region, accessory helix, central SNARE-binding helix, and a comparatively divergent C-terminal domain. This is precisely the architecture expected for a complexin/synaphin-family protein and is compatible with the supplied IPR008849 and PF05835 annotations. (hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 1-2, martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 2-3, hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 2-3)

The principal loss-of-function allele used in foundational work, **cpx-1(ok1552)**, is a roughly 1.3-kb deletion removing one of the two exons; descriptions indicate loss of the N-terminal, accessory, central-helical, and part of the C-terminal coding region. Homozygotes are viable but neurologically impaired, while heterozygotes resemble wild type. The distinct phenotype of *cpx-2* mutants and the inability of available expression evidence to support simple redundancy establish that the literature is specifically examining **CPX-1**, not an interchangeable second complexin. (martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 2-3, hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 2-3)

## 2. Primary molecular function

### 2.1 Regulation of the neuronal SNARE fusion machinery

Complexin is a small, soluble cytoplasmic protein rather than an integral membrane protein. Its conserved central α-helix binds a groove in the assembled ternary SNARE bundle formed between syntaxin and synaptobrevin. In synaptic exocytosis, this places CPX-1 downstream of vesicle docking and near the final membrane-fusion machinery comprising syntaxin, SNAP-25, and synaptobrevin/VAMP. (hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 1-2, martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 1-2, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 1-2)

Deleting the central helix prevents rescue of evoked release and broadly disrupts CPX-1 function. Nevertheless, a K71A/Y72A substitution that weakens SNARE binding can retain appreciable evoked-release support. Thus, engagement of the SNARE bundle is essential, but maximal binding affinity is not necessarily required for every positive effect on exocytosis. The evidence favors multiple conformational or kinetic roles rather than a simple static lock on the SNARE complex. (martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 7-8)

### 2.2 Bidirectional control: clamp plus facilitator

At the worm neuromuscular junction, CPX-1 has opposite effects on two release modes:

* **Spontaneous/tonic release:** CPX-1 suppresses unregulated vesicle fusion. Loss of CPX-1 increased tonic transmission approximately threefold in one study and caused a 12-fold increase in spontaneous fusion without external calcium in another assay. Across worm preparations summarized in a 2023 review, reported increases span approximately 2- to 20-fold, reflecting differences in preparation and event definition. (radoff2014theaccessoryhelix pages 2-4, martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 1-2, lottermoser2023complexinmembraneinteractions pages 3-4)
* **Stimulus-evoked release:** CPX-1 promotes synchronous release. Null mutants showed an approximately tenfold reduction in evoked fusion in one study and a 94% reduction in evoked-current amplitude in another. (hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 1-2, martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 1-2)
* **Priming/docking:** Ultrastructural work found about a 70% decrease in docked vesicles in mutants, supporting the conclusion that CPX-1 maintains a release-ready, primed vesicle population. Some loss of evoked output may therefore be secondary to depletion of release-ready vesicles, although domain-rescue experiments also support a direct facilitatory action. (martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 7-8, hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 1-2)

A useful mechanistic interpretation is that CPX-1 acts as a **temporal filter**: it reduces low-level fusion noise while preserving or enhancing high-probability, stimulus-coupled output. This interpretation reconciles the apparently opposing clamp and facilitator phenotypes and is favored by the authors of the direct worm studies. (martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 7-8)

## 3. Domain-level mechanism

### N-terminal region

The N terminus contributes to positive regulation of fusion, consistent with complexin-family studies across species. In worm domain-rescue experiments, positive support of evoked release can be partially separated from the C-terminal clamping mechanism. This separation is demonstrated by C-terminal truncations that fail to suppress spontaneous release yet preserve approximately 50% of normal evoked responses. (martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 7-8, wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 1-2)

### Accessory helix

The accessory helix lies immediately N-terminal to the central helix. Worm–mouse chimeras, helix-breaking mutations, non-native helical substitutions, and NMR analyses indicate that its key property is **helical stability**, not a unique sequence-specific protein or lipid interaction. A stable helix extending approximately from P37 to G66 appears to nucleate or propagate structure into the central SNARE-binding helix. Full-length rescue restored basal fusion to 2.7 ± 0.5 Hz, close to wild type at 3.3 ± 0.9 Hz, whereas disruptions of helix propagation impaired clamping. (radoff2014theaccessoryhelix pages 2-4)

### Central helix

This is the most conserved region and the core SNARE-interacting element. Reported conservation values vary with alignment boundaries—approximately 75% conservation in one analysis and 78% identity to mouse Cplx1 and 91% to fly complexin in another—but all support strong evolutionary constraint. The central helix is required for both inhibitory and facilitatory functions. (hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 1-2, martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 2-3)

### C-terminal domain and membrane-curvature sensing

The C-terminal domain is relatively disordered in solution but contains amphipathic and terminal hydrophobic motifs that bind phospholipid membranes, particularly highly curved membranes resembling synaptic vesicles. NMR showed strong vesicle-dependent engagement across approximately residues 110–143; the amphipathic region has also been mapped near L117–K136. The isolated C-terminal domain reproduces much of the lipid-binding behavior of full-length CPX-1. (snead2017uniquestructuralfeatures pages 4-6, wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 9-11)

Deleting the terminal 12 residues nearly abolishes lipid binding. Mutations such as LV/EE, helix-breaking K123P, deletion of the final 50 residues, or alteration of the terminal phenylalanine-rich motif impair inhibitory rescue to differing degrees. Studies with 35–45-nm synthetic vesicles—and the approximately 30-nm diameter of worm synaptic vesicles—support a curvature-sensitive interaction. Spectroscopy indicates that a largely unstructured C terminus becomes partly helical on curved membranes, resembling an amphipathic lipid-packing-sensor mechanism. (wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 11-13, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 3-4, wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 9-11, lottermoser2023complexinmembraneinteractions pages 4-6)

Membrane binding is **necessary but insufficient**. Several variants retain measurable lipid binding yet fail to restore clamping, showing that motif geometry, terminal residues, and orientation on the membrane matter in addition to bulk affinity. Cross-species replacement illustrates this point: mouse Cpx1 reaches worm synapses at near-normal abundance but poorly inhibits release; replacing worm C-terminal segments with mouse Cpx1 sequences similarly impairs function, whereas a mouse Cpx3 amphipathic region performs better. (wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 1-2, wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 7-9, wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 11-13)

## 4. Cellular and anatomical localization

CPX-1 is expressed broadly in the nervous system, including ventral-cord motor neurons, head-ganglion neurons, and the pharyngeal MC and M4 motor neurons. GFP-tagged CPX-1 exhibits punctate localization along nerve cords and colocalizes with the synaptic-vesicle marker RAB-3, identifying presynaptic boutons as its principal functional compartment. Panneuronal expression rescues mutant defects, confirming that the primary requirement is neuronal. (martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 2-3, hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 2-3)

Within presynaptic terminals, CPX-1 is dynamically associated with synaptic vesicles rather than permanently membrane-anchored. CPX-1::GFP is approximately 40% enriched at boutons relative to a soluble cytoplasmic marker, and its abundance tracks vesicle abundance more closely than SNARE abundance. Photoactivation measurements show fast and slow exchange components: soluble pGFP escapes in seconds, whereas complete exchange of the synaptic CPX-1 pool takes minutes. Axonal diffusion coefficients were approximately 2 µm²/s for CPX-pGFP and 4 µm²/s for free pGFP. Both the central SNARE-binding helix and C-terminal vesicle-binding region slow CPX-1 exchange. (wragg2015synapticactivityregulates pages 1-2, wragg2015synapticactivityregulates pages 4-5, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 3-4)

Artificial-targeting experiments are especially informative. Fusion of CPX-1 to the vesicular Rab RAB-3 restores aldicarb sensitivity, locomotion, and suppression of spontaneous release. Conversely, forcing CPX-1 onto the presynaptic plasma membrane with CAAX-, syntaxin-, or RIM-derived anchors gives poor or partial rescue. RAB-3 targeting can restore much of the inhibitory function of a C-terminal deletion, indicating that the CTD’s principal role is to position CPX-1 on vesicles where it can intercept assembling SNARE complexes. (wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 5-6, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 6-7)

## 5. Pathway placement and interaction partners

The most directly supported pathway is **synaptic-vesicle exocytosis**:

1. Synaptic vesicles are docked and primed at the presynaptic active zone.
2. Vesicular synaptobrevin and plasma-membrane syntaxin/SNAP-25 begin assembling into the neuronal SNARE bundle.
3. CPX-1 is locally concentrated by reversible CTD binding to the curved vesicle membrane.
4. Its central helix engages the assembled or assembling SNARE bundle.
5. In the absence of an appropriate stimulus, this configuration suppresses premature fusion; during stimulation, CPX-1 also supports a primed pool and efficient evoked release. (hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 1-2, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 1-2, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 3-4, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 6-7)

Directly supported molecular partners or physical contexts are therefore the **ternary SNARE complex**, especially syntaxin and synaptobrevin at the central-helix binding groove, and **curved synaptic-vesicle phospholipid membranes** through the CTD. RAB-3 is best interpreted as a vesicle marker and experimentally successful artificial anchor, not necessarily a demonstrated native CPX-1 receptor. Other exocytic proteins—including UNC-13/Munc13, UNC-18/Munc18, synaptotagmin, and UNC-31/CAPS—operate in the same release machinery, but the retrieved evidence does not establish each as a direct CPX-1-binding partner. (wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 1-2, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 6-7)

## 6. Organismal phenotypes and quantitative evidence

The *cpx-1(ok1552)* phenotype connects the molecular mechanism to nervous-system output. Mutants move at 40% ± 2.2% of wild-type speed; panneuronal CPX-1::GFP restores movement to 104% ± 5.7%. On 1.0 mM aldicarb, mutants paralyze within approximately 20 minutes, compared with roughly 2 hours for wild type, and neuronal rescue normalizes sensitivity. Because aldicarb inhibits acetylcholinesterase, hypersensitivity is consistent with excessive tonic acetylcholine release. GABA-neuron-specific expression does not rescue these broad phenotypes, arguing against a defect restricted to inhibitory neurons. (martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 2-3)

Aldicarb assays are useful integrated physiological readouts, but they are indirect. Patch-clamp recordings provide the stronger evidence that CPX-1 simultaneously restrains spontaneous fusion and supports evoked output, while electron microscopy supplies independent evidence for loss of docked vesicles. The convergence of behavior, pharmacology, electrophysiology, microscopy, biochemistry, and structural spectroscopy makes the core annotation unusually strong. (hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 1-2, martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 1-2, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 3-4)

## 7. Recent developments, 2023–2024

A January 2023 *Journal of Molecular Biology* review by Lottermoser and Dittman synthesized complexin membrane-interaction work and emphasized an updated model in which C-terminal membrane binding is an evolutionarily tuned determinant of synaptic localization and function. For worm CPX-1, the review highlights curvature-dependent folding of the C terminus and a lipid-packing-sensor-like mechanism rather than nonspecific membrane adsorption. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmb.2022.167774. (lottermoser2023complexinmembraneinteractions pages 3-4, lottermoser2023complexinmembraneinteractions pages 4-6)

A study posted to bioRxiv on **25 August 2023** examined differential regulation of excitatory and inhibitory worm neuromuscular-junction release by CPX-1 and CAPS/UNC-31. The retrieved version explicitly states that it was not peer reviewed. It is relevant to the emerging view that exocytic regulators can act differently at cholinergic and GABAergic synapses, but its claims should be considered preliminary, and reliable CPX-1-specific quantitative values were not recoverable from the available extracted text. No 2024 publication directly focused on worm *cpx-1* was identified in the searched corpus. Consequently, the strongest current functional annotation still rests on the detailed 2011–2017 primary studies, interpreted through the 2023 review. This is a search limitation rather than proof that no relevant 2024 report exists.

## 8. Research applications and real-world implementation

CPX-1 has no established enzymatic, therapeutic, or commercial application. Its principal application is as a **genetically tractable in-vivo model of presynaptic fusion control**. The worm system supports:

* structure–function tests of SNARE-binding and membrane-binding domains;
* live measurement of presynaptic protein mobility in an intact nervous system;
* separation of spontaneous from evoked vesicle fusion;
* correlation of electrophysiology with docked-vesicle ultrastructure;
* rapid organismal assays using locomotion and aldicarb sensitivity;
* cross-species domain replacement to test evolutionary conservation;
* investigation of synapse-type differences in excitatory versus inhibitory transmission. (radoff2014theaccessoryhelix pages 2-4, wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 7-9, wragg2015synapticactivityregulates pages 1-2, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 6-7)

These implementations are relevant to general neurobiology because complexin-family proteins are conserved regulators of SNARE-mediated release. However, conclusions about mammalian synapses cannot be transferred uncritically: the worm C-terminal domain and mammalian Cpx1 C terminus differ substantially, and mouse Cpx1 does not fully substitute for worm CPX-1’s inhibitory activity despite reaching synapses. (wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 1-2, wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 7-9)

## 9. Expert assessment and annotation confidence

**High-confidence annotation:** CPX-1 is a presynaptic complexin/synaphin-family regulator of neuronal SNARE-dependent synaptic-vesicle exocytosis. It suppresses spontaneous release, promotes efficient evoked release, and helps maintain docked/primed vesicles. It acts in the presynaptic cytoplasm at synaptic-vesicle-rich boutons, where its CTD reversibly recognizes curved vesicle membranes and its central helix binds SNARE complexes. (hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 1-2, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 1-2, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 3-4)

**Mechanistic model with strong but not complete resolution:** Vesicle binding concentrates and orients CPX-1 so that it can rapidly engage partially assembled SNAREs and prevent premature fusion, while preserving a state competent for stimulus-coupled release. The necessity of both vesicle and SNARE interactions is experimentally strong; the exact conformational sequence by which CPX-1 switches from clamp to facilitator remains unresolved. (martin2011complexinhasopposite pages 7-8, wragg2015synapticactivityregulates pages 1-2, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 6-7)

**Important caveats:** GFP reporters and overexpression may alter protein abundance; aldicarb is an indirect measure of cholinergic output; membrane binding is necessary but not by itself sufficient for clamping; and evolutionary divergence of the CTD limits one-to-one extrapolation between worm CPX-1 and vertebrate complexin-1. The 2023 excitatory/inhibitory comparison is a preprint and should not outweigh the peer-reviewed mechanistic literature. (wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 1-2, wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 7-9, wragg2017evolutionarydivergenceof pages 11-13)

## Conclusion

The most defensible functional annotation for **Q9GUM7 / CPX-1** is: **a soluble presynaptic complexin/synaphin adaptor that couples curved synaptic-vesicle membranes to the neuronal SNARE fusion machinery, clamps spontaneous exocytosis, supports stimulus-evoked fusion, and maintains a docked/primed vesicle pool**. Its functional site is the presynaptic bouton, especially the synaptic-vesicle surface and assembling SNARE complex. This conclusion is supported by convergent worm genetics, cell-specific rescue, electrophysiology, electron microscopy, live imaging, membrane biochemistry, NMR, and evolutionary domain-swapping experiments. (hobson2011complexinmaintainsvesicles pages 1-2, radoff2014theaccessoryhelix pages 2-4, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 3-4, wragg2013synapticvesiclesposition pages 6-7)
