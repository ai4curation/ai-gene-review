Gene Description

EAT-4 is the sole vesicular glutamate transporter (VGLUT) of Caenorhabditis elegans, a major facilitator superfamily protein orthologous to mammalian SLC17A6/7/8. It sits in the synaptic vesicle membrane of glutamatergic neurons and concentrates cytosolic L-glutamate into the vesicle lumen, using the proton electrochemical gradient generated by the vesicular V-ATPase, so that the vesicles release glutamate on exocytosis. eat-4 is expressed in a defined subset of neurons (including the pharyngeal motor neuron M3, the touch receptor neurons ALM and AVM, the polymodal nociceptor ASH and the male-repurposed PHC) and its expression defines glutamatergic neuron identity. Loss of eat-4 abolishes glutamatergic transmission from these neurons without otherwise impairing them, producing delayed pharyngeal muscle relaxation, loss of ASH-mediated nose-touch and osmotic avoidance, reduced spontaneous reversals, defective touch habituation, and abnormal male turning and vulva-location behaviour during mating.

Functional annotation report: C. elegans eat-4 / EAT-4 (UniProt P34644)

Executive conclusion

The requested identity is unambiguous: eat-4 is the Caenorhabditis elegans gene ZK512.6 encoding EAT-4/P34644, the worm’s canonical vesicular glutamate transporter (VGLUT). Positional cloning localized rescuing activity to ZK512, identified the corresponding cDNA, and showed rescue of independent eat-4 mutant phenotypes; later comparative analysis placed EAT-4 securely in the conserved SLC17A6/7/8 VGLUT lineage found across nematodes. This matches the supplied organism, protein description, MFS-related domains, and sodium/anion-transporter-family annotation. No literature on a different same-symbol gene was used. (lee1999eat4ahomolog pages 3-3, serranosaiz2020slc17a678vesicularglutamate pages 4-5)

The primary physiological substrate is L-glutamate, which EAT-4 accumulates in synaptic vesicles so that exocytosis can produce glutamatergic transmission. It is therefore not a plasma-membrane glutamate-clearance transporter and not primarily an enzyme. Its principal site of action is the presynaptic synaptic-vesicle membrane, especially in axonal terminals. The strongest functional evidence includes selective presynaptic defects in mutants, absence of depolarization-evoked glutamate signals from eat-4 mutant BAG neurons, and functional EAT-4 fluorescent puncta coincident with anatomically mapped presynaptic sites. (lee1999eat4ahomolog pages 1-3, choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 4-5, cuentascondori2026synaptotagmeatoolkit pages 6-7)

1. Identity, nomenclature, family, and historical correction

The original 1999 study described EAT-4 as a 563-residue homolog of rat BNPI, then called a brain-specific sodium-dependent inorganic-phosphate cotransporter. EAT-4 showed 48% amino-acid identity to BNPI and approximately 29% identity to mammalian renal Na–Pi transporters. That historical assignment was based on sequence similarity and the contemporary annotation of BNPI; the study did not directly demonstrate phosphate transport by purified EAT-4. (lee1999eat4ahomolog pages 3-5, lee1999eat4ahomolog pages 7-8)

Subsequent discovery that BNPI and its relatives are vesicular glutamate transporters changed the functional interpretation. Modern phylogenetic analysis identifies EAT-4 as the bona fide nematode ortholog of the vertebrate SLC17A6/SLC17A7/SLC17A8 VGLUTs; every examined nematode genome contained an EAT-4 ortholog. Two additional C. elegans relatives, VGLU-2 and VGLU-3, are more divergent and should not be conflated with canonical EAT-4. (serranosaiz2020slc17a678vesicularglutamate pages 4-5)

The supplied UniProt/InterPro/Pfam description—major facilitator superfamily-related MFS, MFS_Na/anion cotransporter, and MFS_1 domains—is therefore concordant with the literature. Contemporary topology models describe a multipass, approximately 12-transmembrane protein; early hydropathy analysis predicted only six to eight spans, illustrating improvement in membrane-protein topology prediction rather than a distinct protein identity. A recent structure-guided tagging study treated EAT-4 as a predicted 12-transmembrane protein and placed a fluorophore at the cytoplasmic C terminus without disrupting function. (cuentascondori2026synaptotagmeatoolkit pages 3-6, lee1999eat4ahomolog pages 3-5)

2. Primary molecular function and substrate

Accepted function

EAT-4 transports cytosolic glutamate into acidic synaptic vesicles, thereby establishing vesicular glutamate content and enabling quantal glutamate release. Vesicular neurotransmitter transport generally uses the electrochemical proton gradient generated by the vesicular H⁺-ATPase. Worm experiments are fully consistent with that conserved VGLUT mechanism, but purified EAT-4 kinetics, ion coupling, transport stoichiometry, and a comprehensive substrate-selectivity profile have not been directly reported in the retrieved primary literature. Consequently, claims about exact H⁺/Cl⁻ coupling should be regarded as inference from the SLC17/VGLUT family rather than worm-specific biochemical measurement. (choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 1-2, choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 6-7)

Direct in-vivo evidence is especially strong in BAG CO₂-sensory neurons. A 5-s, 100-mM KCl pulse evoked calcium-dependent extracellular iGluSnFR responses in cultured wild-type BAG neurons, whereas eat-4 mutants produced no evoked signal. This shows that EAT-4 is essential for detectable regulated glutamate release in this cell type and argues against a substantial EAT-4-independent vesicular pathway there. (choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 4-5, choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 6-7)

Why phosphate is not the current primary-substrate annotation

The early phosphate hypothesis was explicitly inferential: it followed BNPI’s then-current designation and proposed that phosphate uptake might indirectly affect glutamate synthesis. The same study found normal responses to externally applied glutamate but defective neuronal glutamatergic output, localizing the critical lesion presynaptically. Later VGLUT phylogeny, localization, functional complementation, and direct glutamate-release assays superseded the phosphate-centered interpretation. Thus, phosphate transport should not be presented as EAT-4’s established physiological role, although ancillary ion or phosphate transport has not been exhaustively excluded biochemically. (lee1999eat4ahomolog pages 7-8, lee1999eat4ahomolog pages 1-3, serranosaiz2020slc17a678vesicularglutamate pages 4-5)

3. Cellular and subcellular localization

EAT-4 acts predominantly in glutamatergic neurons, at presynaptic terminals and synaptic vesicles. Older transcriptional reporters showed neuronal expression and nerve-ring signal but could not by themselves prove protein localization. More direct evidence includes EAT-4 and VST-1 double-label electron microscopy on the same vesicles and endogenous EAT-4::GFP puncta along ASE axons at presynaptic positions identified by serial electron microscopy. Endogenous tagged alleles retained normal EAT-4-dependent salt chemotaxis, supporting the physiological relevance of the localization. (choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 4-5, cuentascondori2026synaptotagmeatoolkit pages 6-7)

The functional tagging study measured salt-gradient positions of 13.15 ± 4 mm from the peak for eat-4(ky5) mutants versus 7.76 ± 5.2 mm for wild type; tagged strains remained near wild-type performance, including 7.6 ± 3.6 mm with broad flippase activation. This is useful validation that observed puncta were not merely aggregates of a disabled protein. (cuentascondori2026synaptotagmeatoolkit pages 6-7)

Expression is broad but cell-selective. A detailed map includes sensory neurons such as ADL, AFD, ASE, ASH, ASK, AWC, BAG, FLP, OLQ, PVD and mechanosensory neurons; interneurons including AIB, AIZ, AUA, DVC, PVQ and RIA; motor/intermotor neurons such as RIM; and pharyngeal neurons including M3, I2, MI and I5. Some EAT-4-positive neurons coexpress another classical transmitter—for example serotonin in AIM/I5 and tyramine in RIM—so EAT-4 expression does not imply exclusive transmitter identity. (serranosaiz2013modularcontrolof pages 3-5, serranosaiz2013modularcontrolof pages 2-3)

A 2024 atlas used an endogenous eat-4 T2A–GFP–H2B knock-in, NeuroPAL cell identification, earlier reporters, and CeNGEN single-cell RNA sequencing. CeNGEN detected eat-4 in 37 of 38 previously mapped classes at all tested stringencies, with PVD detected at three of four, and suggested RIC, PVN and DVA as additional classes; endogenous CRISPR labeling independently confirmed RIC. Endogenous reporters reduce missing-cis-element problems associated with older multicopy constructs, although weak expression and detection thresholds remain important caveats. (wang2024aneurotransmitteratlas pages 3-5, wang2024aneurotransmitteratlas pages 7-8, wang2024aneurotransmitteratlas pages 5-6)

4. Mechanism and regulation of vesicle filling

The 2021 BAG-neuron study revealed that EAT-4 does not act in isolation. VST-1 occupies vesicles with EAT-4 and opposes EAT-4-dependent glutamate packaging. Loss of vst-1 increased glutamate release without increasing depolarization-evoked vesicle fusion, indicating increased glutamate content per released vesicle rather than more exocytosis. Wild-type vesicular lumenal pH was approximately 6.2. The proposed mechanisms—competition for vesicular electrochemical energy or VST-1-mediated glutamate efflux—remain alternatives rather than resolved molecular models. (choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 4-5, choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 6-7, choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 11-11)

This finding is conceptually important: synaptic strength can be tuned at the vesicle-loading step, not only through vesicle number, release probability, or postsynaptic receptor abundance. In the BAG circuit, excessive EAT-4-dependent signaling after VST-1 loss recruits inappropriate GLR-1-dependent excitation of RIA and disrupts CO₂ avoidance. (choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 1-2, choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 11-11)

5. Biological pathways and representative circuit functions

Pharyngeal pumping

The original eat-4 phenotype reflects loss of M3 glutamatergic transmission. M3-derived glutamate acts through the AVR-15 glutamate receptor on pharyngeal muscle to accelerate relaxation and limit contraction duration. Mutants retain sensitivity to iontophoretically applied glutamate, supporting a presynaptic packaging/release defect rather than postsynaptic receptor failure. This mechanistically explains the alternative name “abnormal pharyngeal pumping EAT-4.” (lee1999eat4ahomolog pages 1-3)

Mechanosensation and nociceptive signaling

EAT-4 contributes to chemical branches of touch circuitry while leaving residual gap-junction transmission. Anterior-touch response fidelity was 94.0 ± 1.4% in wild type, 76.3 ± 3.1% in eat-4(ky5), and 87.4 ± 4.1% in eat-4(n2474). After AVD ablation it fell to 23.9 ± 4.3% and 19.8 ± 7.4% in the mutant backgrounds, versus 65.3 ± 7.0% in wild type, resolving separable glutamatergic and electrical contributions. (lee1999eat4ahomolog pages 6-7)

ASH, IL1V and OLQV expression is associated with nose-touch and foraging phenotypes, while the 2024 rapid-cooling study supplied finer circuit resolution. ASH glutamate, packaged through EAT-4, activates GLR-1 in AIB; AIB then suppresses RIM, forming a disinhibitory route that promotes reversal. In parallel, AIZ-derived glutamate activates GLR-3 and GLR-6 in RIA. Neuron-specific eat-4 RNAi or rescue, receptor-specific rescue, behavior and calcium imaging collectively localized these pathways. (lee1999eat4ahomolog pages 5-6, lin2024molecularandcircuit pages 6-7, lin2024molecularandcircuit pages 7-8)

Thermotaxis

Thermotaxis uses glutamate in directionally different ways. AFD-derived EAT-4-dependent glutamate inhibits AIY through GLC-3/GluCl and favors colder-directed movement, whereas AWC glutamatergic input stimulates AIY and favors warmer-directed movement; RIA-derived glutamate provides another downstream contribution. These opposite signs arise from postsynaptic receptor/circuit context, not from any change in EAT-4’s transport chemistry. (ohnishi2011bidirectionalregulationof pages 3-5, ohnishi2011bidirectionalregulationof pages 1-2)

Quantitatively, wild type showed 55 ± 8% isothermal tracking after cultivation at 20°C, eat-4(ky5) showed 0%, and broad neuronal expression restored 50 ± 2%. Simultaneous AFD-plus-RIA rescue restored 39 ± 1%, whereas RIA alone gave only 10 ± 2%. At 23°C and 17°C, mutant thermotaxis indices were 0.47 ± 0.14 and −1.46 ± 0.08, compared with 2.27 ± 0.19 and 2.96 ± 0.22 in wild type. (ohnishi2011bidirectionalregulationof pages 1-2, ohnishi2011bidirectionalregulationof pages 2-3)

Salt learning and chemotaxis

EAT-4-mediated vesicular release from ASE is a standard experimental readout of learned salt navigation. Recent endogenous EAT-4 tags were validated with this behavior and produced presynaptic puncta suitable for cell-specific vesicle mapping. Earlier work further showed that PKC-1-dependent phosphorylation of presynaptic syntaxin changes basal ASER glutamate transmission, allowing excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic receptors to decode experienced versus current salt concentration. EAT-4 is therefore the packaging element in a plastic synapse; regulation can occur downstream at release machinery and receptors rather than by changing substrate identity. (cuentascondori2026synaptotagmeatoolkit pages 6-7)

GABA–glutamate cotransmission in the defecation circuit: 2023 advance

Li et al. showed that the nominally GABAergic DVB motor neuron also expresses EAT-4. DVB-derived glutamate activates inhibitory GLC-3 feedback in DVB and GLC-2/GLC-4 in enteric muscle. UBR-1 normally restrains this glutamatergic influence; ubr-1 mutants had 7.55 ± 0.34 expulsions per 10 min versus 12.9 ± 0.21 in wild type, and DVB-specific EAT-4 depletion corrected impaired calcium activity and expulsion. Receptor loss restored approximately 0.87 ± 0.03 expulsions/cycle for glc-3, 0.75 ± 0.03 for glc-2, and 0.83 ± 0.02 for glc-4. This is strong evidence that EAT-4 can participate in genuine cotransmission and that glutamate can be inhibitory in worms through chloride channels. Published online 9 October 2023, EMBO Reports, DOI: https://doi.org/10.15252/embr.202357014. (li2023ubr‐1ubiquitinligase pages 6-7, li2023ubr‐1ubiquitinligase pages 1-2)

Social feeding: 2024 advance

In aggregating npr-1 animals, loss of eat-4 reduced social aggregation. Re-expression in ADL and ASH together restored aggregation, whereas expression in either neuron alone was insufficient. EAT-4::pHluorin FRAP in ASH showed faster recovery in social npr-1 animals: slope 95% CI 0.001175–0.001322 versus 0.0008085–0.000926 in solitary controls, with global p < 0.0001. Faster release was independent of NRX-1, whereas increased presynaptic-site number depended on NRX-1, identifying parallel mechanisms that tune social feeding. Published October 2024, Nature Communications 15:9301, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-53590-x. (cowen2024conservedautismassociatedgenes pages 4-5, cowen2024conservedautismassociatedgenes pages 9-10, cowen2024conservedautismassociatedgenes pages 1-2)

6. Transcriptional control of glutamatergic identity

There is no single universal “master regulator” of eat-4. Instead, the locus contains modular cis-regulatory elements activated by neuron-class-specific terminal selectors. Identified regulators include UNC-86/MEC-3, CEH-36, TTX-1/CEH-14, LIN-11, CEH-37, UNC-42, CEH-6, ETS-5 and VAB-3. These factors often regulate eat-4 together with other terminal features, indicating coordinated specification of neuronal identity rather than isolated transporter expression. For example, CEH-14 is required for eat-4 in PHA, PHB and PHC, and VAB-3 controls it in OLL and ventral URY. (serranosaiz2013modularcontrolof pages 6-7, serranosaiz2013modularcontrolof pages 3-5)

This modular architecture explains how one transporter supports many circuits without implying identical upstream regulation or postsynaptic action in every EAT-4-positive neuron. It also makes the eat-4 promoter and its class-specific elements valuable tools for targeting glutamatergic populations, although whole-promoter expression should not be assumed to cover every endogenous site equally. (serranosaiz2013modularcontrolof pages 3-5, serranosaiz2013modularcontrolof pages 2-3)

7. Current applications and real-world implementation

EAT-4 is used primarily as an experimental genetic handle, not a therapeutic target in worms. Current implementations include:

  1. Defining glutamatergic identity: endogenous eat-4 reporters, NeuroPAL and single-cell transcriptomes map transmitter usage across the nervous system. (wang2024aneurotransmitteratlas pages 3-5, wang2024aneurotransmitteratlas pages 7-8)
  2. Cell-specific circuit dissection: rescue, RNAi and conditional deletion identify which neuron supplies glutamate in a behavior, as shown for thermotaxis, rapid cooling, DVB motor output and social feeding. (ohnishi2011bidirectionalregulationof pages 2-3, li2023ubr‐1ubiquitinligase pages 6-7, lin2024molecularandcircuit pages 6-7, lin2024molecularandcircuit pages 7-8)
  3. Monitoring presynaptic physiology: EAT-4::pHluorin reports vesicle cycling, while endogenous fluorescent EAT-4 identifies glutamatergic presynaptic puncta. (cuentascondori2026synaptotagmeatoolkit pages 6-7, cowen2024conservedautismassociatedgenes pages 9-10)
  4. Modeling conserved synaptic principles: EAT-4 studies reveal regulation of vesicular filling, excitatory/inhibitory transmitter balance, cotransmission and the behavioral consequences of altered glutamate output. These mechanisms have conceptual relevance to mammalian VGLUT biology, but worm behavioral findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence of human disease causality. (choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 4-5, li2023ubr‐1ubiquitinligase pages 6-7)

8. Evidence-weighted expert assessment and remaining uncertainties

The evidence for the core annotation is exceptionally strong: independent alleles, genomic rescue, presynaptic physiology, cell-specific rescue/knockdown, endogenous localization, glutamate imaging, phylogeny and circuit-level receptor genetics all converge on EAT-4 as the principal vesicular glutamate transporter. The most defensible concise annotation is:

EAT-4/P34644 is a multipass SLC17-family vesicular membrane transporter that packages cytosolic L-glutamate into neuronal synaptic vesicles, enabling and regulating glutamatergic chemical transmission at presynaptic terminals throughout the C. elegans nervous system.

Important limitations remain. Direct biochemical measurements of purified EAT-4’s transport kinetics, exact ion dependence, stoichiometry and full substrate spectrum are lacking in the retrieved literature. VST-1’s molecular action on vesicle filling remains unresolved, and expression of EAT-4 establishes capacity for glutamate release but does not reveal whether a circuit effect will be excitatory or inhibitory; that depends on postsynaptic receptor identity. Finally, older promoter reporters underestimated the expression map, so endogenous knock-ins and multimodal validation should be preferred for future annotation. (choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 4-5, choi2021opponentvesiculartransporters pages 6-7, wang2024aneurotransmitteratlas pages 7-8, wang2024aneurotransmitteratlas pages 5-6)

eat-4 (P34644) — vesicular glutamate transporter EAT-4 — curation notes

Identity

  • UniProt P34644 (EAT4_CAEEL, reviewed), 576 aa, major facilitator superfamily,
    sodium/anion cotransporter family, VGLUT subfamily (IPR011701 MFS; IPR020846 MFS domain).
    Orthologue of mammalian SLC17A6/7/8 (VGLUT1-3); the worm has a single VGLUT gene, so
    eat-4 expression defines glutamatergic neuron identity.
  • Historical note: eat-4 was cloned as a homologue of rat BNPI (brain-specific Na+/Pi
    cotransporter I) before BNPI was shown to be VGLUT1; the Na-Pi cotransporter interpretation
    in the 1999 paper is superseded and later worm papers uniformly call EAT-4 the VGLUT.

Assessment

  • Core: L-glutamate transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0005313) on the synaptic vesicle
    membrane, loading glutamate into synaptic vesicles (GO:0098700) for glutamatergic synaptic
    transmission (GO:0035249). All supported by IBA and by the worm loss-of-function data.
  • The ISS row GO:0015501 glutamate:sodium symporter activity (template human SLC17A1/NPT1)
    is biologically wrong for a VGLUT: vesicular glutamate uptake is driven by the membrane
    potential component of the V-ATPase proton gradient, not by Na+ symport (Na+/glutamate
    symport is the EAAT/SLC1 mechanism).
  • Plasma membrane (IEA from UniProt "Cell membrane") is a mis-placement; VGLUTs reside on
    synaptic vesicles, visiting the plasma membrane only transiently during exocytosis.
  • Behavioural rows (osmotic avoidance, nose touch, reversal, pumping, male turning and
    mating) are downstream consequences of loss of glutamatergic transmission from specific
    neurons; kept as non-core.
  • GO:0060076 excitatory synapse: in C. elegans several EAT-4-dependent glutamatergic synapses
    are inhibitory via glutamate-gated chloride channels (e.g. M3 -> pharyngeal muscle IPSPs
    via AVR-15), so "excitatory" is not universally correct for the worm VGLUT.

Deep research

  • eat-4-deep-research-falcon.md agrees on every point used here: EAT-4 is the worm VGLUT in
    the SLC17A6/7/8 lineage, its substrate is L-glutamate "which EAT-4 accumulates in synaptic
    vesicles so that exocytosis can produce glutamatergic transmission", its site of action is
    the presynaptic synaptic-vesicle membrane, and the 1999 Na-Pi cotransporter description is
    a historical mis-assignment that was never backed by a phosphate-transport assay.
