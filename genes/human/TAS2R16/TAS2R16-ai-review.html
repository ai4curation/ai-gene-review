Gene Description

Taste receptor type 2 member 16 (T2R16) is a human member of the TAS2R family of class-A-like, single-exon (intronless), seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptors that detect bitter-tasting compounds. It functions as a plasma-membrane chemoreceptor specialized for beta-D-glycopyranosides: it is activated by salicin and structurally related aryl and alkyl beta-glucosides, beta-mannosides, and plant cyanogenic/defense glycosides such as amygdalin and arbutin, while glucose alone is inactive. Ligand recognition occurs in a broad "two-faced" binding pocket formed mainly by transmembrane helices 3 and 7, which accommodates a conserved sugar moiety together with a chemically diverse aglycone. TAS2R16 is expressed in a subset of gustducin-positive taste receptor cells of the tongue (circumvallate and foliate papillae) and also in testis; upon agonist binding it couples to the heterotrimeric G protein alpha-subunit gustducin (GNAT3) and, through the TAS2R-family signaling cascade, activates phospholipase C-beta2 (PLCB2), generating IP3-dependent release of intracellular calcium that opens the cation channel TRPM5 and depolarizes the taste cell, triggering bitter-taste perception and behavioral rejection responses. Efficient trafficking of TAS2R16 to the plasma membrane is facilitated by the receptor-transporting proteins RTP3 and RTP4, with which it physically interacts; ectopic receptor accumulates transiently in the ER/Golgi compartment en route to the cell surface. A common coding polymorphism at residue 172 (Asn172/Lys172) alters cell-surface expression and beta-glycopyranoside sensitivity, and has been studied for its relationship to individual differences in bitter-taste perception and, in some population studies, to alcohol-dependence risk. Extra-oral expression of TAS2R16 in tissues such as sinonasal epithelium and gingiva has also been reported, with proposed but not yet clinically validated roles in innate mucosal defense and local anti-inflammatory signaling.

Core Functions

Bitter chemoreceptor for beta-D-glycopyranosides. TAS2R16 is a seven-transmembrane GPCR that binds salicin and structurally related beta-glucosides/beta-mannosides (including plant cyanogenic and defense glycosides such as amygdalin and arbutin) via a broad TM3/TM7 pocket that recognizes a conserved sugar group while tolerating a diverse aglycone; glucose alone does not activate the receptor. Upon agonist binding at the plasma membrane of gustducin-positive taste receptor cells, TAS2R16 couples to gustducin/G-protein signaling, contributing to the perception of bitter taste and to chemical-surveillance/avoidance of ingested beta-glycosides, including some toxic cyanogenic glycosides.

Human TAS2R16 (UniProt Q9NYV7): Functional-Annotation Research Report

Executive summary

The identity is verified: TAS2R16 denotes the human (Homo sapiens) taste receptor type 2 member 16, a 291-amino-acid, seven-transmembrane bitter-sensing G-protein-coupled receptor consistent with UniProt Q9NYV7, InterPro TAS2R, and Pfam TAS2R. No conflicting same-symbol protein was found. Direct human-receptor studies identify TAS2R16 primarily as a β-glycoside chemoreceptor, rather than an enzyme or transporter. Its best-established endogenous role is detection of bitter β-glycosides by taste-receptor cells; salicin is the standard experimental agonist. Heterologous expression establishes cell-surface trafficking and ligand-evoked signaling, while TAS2R-family evidence supports the native gustducin–PLCβ2–IP3/Ca²⁺–TRPM5 taste pathway. (thomas2017thebittertaste pages 11-12, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 1-2, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 2-3, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 12-13, andresbarquin2007molecularbasisof pages 6-8)

TAS2R16 is also detected in human sinonasal epithelium and gingival fibroblasts. In these compartments, activation has been linked respectively to nitric-oxide-dependent epithelial defense and suppression of LPS-induced inflammatory signaling. These extraoral findings are biologically credible but remain preclinical; there is currently no validated TAS2R16-directed therapy or biomarker in routine care. (tuzim2021anupdateon pages 5-6, dragos2022phytochemicalsandinflammation pages 11-13)

2. Primary molecular function and substrate/ligand specificity

TAS2R16 is a signaling receptor, not a catalytic protein. Its primary molecular action is to bind extracellular bitter molecules and convert ligand occupancy into intracellular G-protein signaling. Its pharmacological specialization is unusually clear: it preferentially recognizes compounds containing a β-linked carbohydrate, especially β-glucopyranosides, while tolerating substantial variation in the aglycone.

Reported agonists include salicin, arbutin, amygdalin and numerous synthetic aromatic, aliphatic, charged, thioglycosidic, and disaccharide derivatives. Glucose alone does not activate the receptor, showing that both glycone presentation and the attached aglycone contribute to productive binding. TAS2R16 can accept at least one β-mannoside but generally does not respond to β-galactosides, demonstrating stereochemical discrimination rather than nonspecific sugar sensing. (thomas2017thebittertaste pages 10-11, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 1-2, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 3-6)

In a controlled human-TAS2R16 calcium-flux assay, salicin had an EC50 of approximately 1.2 mM. Three structurally diverse agonists ranged from approximately 0.65–2.5 mM, and the broader tested set generally fell within 0.65–4.8 mM. These millimolar potencies are typical of sensory receptors exposed to dietary chemicals and are assay-dependent, particularly because many studies use engineered G proteins. (thomas2017thebittertaste pages 10-11, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 3-6)

3. Structural basis of ligand recognition and activation

The most detailed TAS2R16 structure–function study constructed 573 receptor mutants, covering substitutions at all 291 positions, while controlling separately for translation and plasma-membrane trafficking. It identified 13 residues with ligand-selective effects and 38 residues whose mutation disrupted signaling across all tested ligands. Most salicin-critical positions were in transmembrane helices, especially TM3 and TM5, rather than extracellular loops. (thomas2017thebittertaste pages 12-13, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 11-12, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 2-3)

The resulting model is a broad, “two-faced” binding cavity formed principally by TM3 and TM7. One face recognizes common carbohydrate features, whereas the other accommodates chemically diverse aglycones. Particularly important positions include W257/L258, W261 and E262 in TM7; V77, L81, T82 and I90 around TM3/ECL1; and L59 and A184, which help define pocket dimensions. W261 substitutions can reverse ligand preference—abolishing or weakening salicin responses while enhancing responses to some nitrophenyl glycosides—whereas E262A abolishes activation and E262D selectively changes recognition of carbohydrate C2 substitutions. (thomas2017thebittertaste pages 10-11, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 11-12)

This evidence is strong at the residue-function level but was interpreted through homology models rather than an experimentally solved TAS2R16 structure. Recent cryo-EM structures of other TAS2Rs improve family-wide mechanistic understanding, but atomic contacts inferred for TAS2R16 should remain classified as model-supported until a TAS2R16 experimental structure is obtained.

4. Cellular localization and signaling pathway

Gustatory localization

The established physiological setting is the plasma membrane of bitter-responsive taste-receptor cells in taste buds, where the receptor encounters dissolved compounds at the apical sensory surface. Direct heterologous experiments confirm that TAS2R16 can traffic to the cell surface, although the retrieved evidence did not provide receptor-specific endogenous apical imaging in human taste buds. TAS2R-family expression in gustducin-positive taste cells and direct human TAS2R16 pharmacology together make this localization/function assignment strong. (thomas2017thebittertaste pages 1-2, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 2-3, andresbarquin2007molecularbasisof pages 6-8)

Canonical pathway

The accepted gustatory sequence is:

  1. β-Glycoside binds TAS2R16.
  2. The activated receptor couples to a heterotrimeric G protein containing α-gustducin/GNAT3.
  3. Gβγ activates PLCβ2.
  4. PLCβ2 generates IP3, causing IP3-receptor-dependent release of Ca²⁺ from intracellular stores.
  5. Increased cytosolic Ca²⁺ activates TRPM5, depolarizing the taste cell and promoting transmitter output to gustatory afferents.

TAS2R16 directly produces ligand-dependent calcium responses and can signal through a Gα16 construct carrying the gustducin C-terminal tail. However, the complete GNAT3→PLCβ2→IP3→TRPM5 sequence has not been independently demonstrated for endogenous human TAS2R16 in the retrieved receptor-specific experiments; part of this annotation is therefore a well-supported TAS2R-family inference. The engineered-G-protein assays force calcium coupling and may not fully reproduce native efficacy or bias. (thomas2017thebittertaste pages 8-10, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 12-13, andresbarquin2007molecularbasisof pages 6-8)

5. Biological processes and tissue-specific functions

Bitter perception and dietary surveillance

TAS2R16 detects plant β-glycosides, a chemical class that includes potentially toxic cyanogenic glycosides as well as harmless or pharmacologically active compounds. Its gustatory output contributes to bitterness perception, rejection behavior, food choice, and exposure control. The receptor is therefore best understood as a dietary chemical-surveillance receptor, not as a universal toxin detector: many bitter compounds are harmless, and many toxins are not bitter. (thomas2017thebittertaste pages 1-2, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 3-6)

Sinonasal epithelium and innate defense

Human sinonasal epithelial cells express TAS2R16. Published human primary-cell work places TAS2R16 with other bitter receptors in epithelial cilia and reports dose-dependent nitric-oxide production after stimulation with salicin-related agonists through canonical taste-signaling machinery. Nitric oxide can increase ciliary beating and exert direct antimicrobial effects, providing a mechanistic connection between luminal chemical sensing and mucociliary defense. (tuzim2021anupdateon pages 5-6)

The interpretation must remain bounded: several airway studies activate multiple T2R isoforms, and downstream PLCβ2/TRPM5/Ca²⁺/NO evidence is often strongest at the receptor-family level. No TAS2R16 agonist has yet demonstrated clinical efficacy for chronic rhinosinusitis or respiratory infection.

Human gingival fibroblasts

Human gingival fibroblasts express TAS2R16 together with α-gustducin and PLCβ2. Salicin evokes a cytosolic Ca²⁺ response and suppresses LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine release, at least partly by limiting cAMP elevation and NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation. This is direct human-cell evidence for an anti-inflammatory signaling role and provides a rationale for local periodontal targeting. (dragos2022phytochemicalsandinflammation pages 11-13)

The evidence remains in vitro. A 2024 mouse periodontitis study implicated the murine receptor Tas2r143, considered functionally related to human TAS2R16, but murine receptor repertoires are not one-to-one orthologous with human TAS2Rs; this result should not be reported as direct human TAS2R16 validation.

Neural and other proposed functions

In SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells, salicin promoted neurite outgrowth through a pathway reported to involve TAS2R16 and ERK. This suggests that TAS2R16 can engage tissue-specific MAPK outputs outside taste cells. Nevertheless, confirmation in primary human neurons, receptor-null controls, and in vivo systems is needed before assigning a normal neurological function. (kriauciunas2024generalisedperiodontitisexamining pages 17-18)

Reports of TAS2R16 in gastrointestinal, skin, malignant, and other tissues should likewise be separated into expression evidence versus demonstrated receptor-specific function. Low TAS2R transcript abundance, uncertain antibody specificity, intracellular retention, overlapping ligand repertoires, and agonist off-target effects are recurring limitations in extraoral-TAS2R research.

6. Human variation

The best functionally supported common coding difference is at residue 172. The N172 form displays approximately twofold greater cell-surface expression and β-glycoside sensitivity than K172, supporting altered trafficking as at least part of the mechanism. N96T can reduce EC50 values for salicin and a β-mannoside by about fivefold, apparently by changing receptor-state stability rather than directly contacting ligand. (thomas2017thebittertaste pages 8-10, thomas2017thebittertaste pages 2-3)

Numerous TAS2R16 variants—including rs846672, rs978739, rs1525489, rs846664 and rs1308724—have been discussed in relation to taste, alcohol-related phenotypes, longevity, or disease. These associations are heterogeneous and generally lack sufficient replication or mechanistic validation for clinical use. (thomas2017thebittertaste pages 1-2, mao2023clinicalassociationsof pages 2-4)

7. Recent developments, 2023–2024

Natural receptor inhibitors

A 2023 study identified lignans from Schisandra chinensis that inhibit salicin-activated human TAS2R16 in cell assays. Together with the established allosteric antagonist probenecid, such compounds are useful probes and potential starting points for bitterness-masking agents. Their present status is in-vitro pharmacology rather than approved food or drug technology.

Human association studies

A 2024 generalized-periodontitis study enrolled 590 participants—280 cases and 310 controls—and measured rs860170, rs978739, rs1357949 and circulating immunoreactive TAS2R16. Overall serum levels were reported as 0.112 versus 0.075 ng/mL in cases and controls in the study summary; sex-stratified comparisons were 0.110 versus 0.084 ng/mL in women and 0.149 versus 0.101 ng/mL in men, neither reaching conventional significance in the cited analysis. Some genotype-stratified findings failed Bonferroni correction. Haplotype associations included T-T-A, OR 0.53 (95% CI 0.34–0.81), C-T-A, OR 0.18 (0.07–0.49), and C-C-A, OR 0.16 (0.05–0.56). These are exploratory associations, not evidence that circulating full-length membrane receptor is a validated disease biomarker. (kriauciunas2024generalisedperiodontitisexamining pages 13-15)

Other 2024 reports associated TAS2R16 variants or assay-measured serum levels with macular degeneration and multiple sclerosis. Such studies are hypothesis-generating because they require independent replication, correction for population structure and multiple testing, biochemical validation of the circulating analyte, and reconciliation with TAS2R16's membrane-receptor biology.

Broader structural progress

Cryo-EM structures published in 2024 for other TAS2R family members established experimentally that bitter receptors can possess unusual orthosteric and intracellular ligand sites and couple to gustducin. This is an important family-wide advance, but it does not replace a TAS2R16 structure or prove that TAS2R16 shares every observed binding mode.

8. Applications and real-world implementation

Potential applications are:

  • Food and pharmaceutical bitterness control: receptor antagonists could mask salicin-like or glycosidic bitterness, improving flavor and medication adherence.
  • Cell-based biosensors: TAS2R16's glycoside selectivity can be used to detect or profile bitter compounds.
  • Sinonasal therapeutics: topical agonists might augment Ca²⁺/NO-dependent mucociliary defense.
  • Periodontal therapy: locally delivered agonists could potentially dampen LPS/NF-κB-driven gingival inflammation.
  • Precision sensory nutrition: functional variants may help explain individual responses to β-glycoside-containing foods.

None is currently an established TAS2R16-selective clinical implementation. Probenecid is an approved drug for other indications, not an approved bitter blocker; natural lignans are research compounds; and genetic or serum assays have not reached validated diagnostic status.

9. Evidence assessment

The central functional annotation is much more secure than the disease literature: TAS2R16 is conclusively a human cell-surface GPCR that recognizes β-glycosides and initiates intracellular signaling. Ligand specificity and mutational determinants are supported by controlled receptor-expression and calcium-response experiments. Native oral pathway details are strongly supported by TAS2R-family biology, whereas extraoral roles range from persuasive human-cell evidence in sinonasal epithelium and gingival fibroblasts to preliminary associations in disease cohorts.

Conclusion

The most defensible annotation of Q9NYV7 is: a plasma-membrane, seven-transmembrane bitter GPCR specialized for β-glycoside recognition, operating primarily in gustatory chemical surveillance through Ca²⁺-mobilizing taste signaling. A broad TM3/TM7 pocket explains recognition of a conserved sugar moiety with diverse aglycones. Human cell studies support additional roles in sinonasal nitric-oxide defense and gingival inflammatory resolution, but these functions and recent disease associations have not yet produced a validated TAS2R16-selective clinical application.

  Taste receptor type 2 member 16 (T2R16) is a human member of the TAS2R
  family of class-A-like, single-exon (intronless), seven-transmembrane
  G protein-coupled receptors that detect bitter-tasting compounds. It
  functions as a plasma-membrane chemoreceptor specialized for
  beta-D-glycopyranosides: it is activated by salicin and structurally
  related aryl and alkyl beta-glucosides, beta-mannosides, and plant
  cyanogenic/defense glycosides such as amygdalin and arbutin, while
  glucose alone is inactive. Ligand recognition occurs in a broad
  "two-faced" binding pocket formed mainly by transmembrane helices 3
  and 7, which accommodates a conserved sugar moiety together with a
  chemically diverse aglycone. TAS2R16 is expressed in a subset of
  gustducin-positive taste receptor cells of the tongue (circumvallate
  and foliate papillae) and also in testis; upon agonist binding it
  couples to the heterotrimeric G protein alpha-subunit gustducin
  (GNAT3) and, through the TAS2R-family signaling cascade, activates
  phospholipase C-beta2 (PLCB2), generating IP3-dependent release of
  intracellular calcium that opens the cation channel TRPM5 and
  depolarizes the taste cell, triggering bitter-taste perception and
  behavioral rejection responses. Efficient trafficking of TAS2R16 to
  the plasma membrane is facilitated by the receptor-transporting
  proteins RTP3 and RTP4, with which it physically interacts; ectopic
  receptor accumulates transiently in the ER/Golgi compartment en route
  to the cell surface. A common coding polymorphism at residue 172
  (Asn172/Lys172) alters cell-surface expression and beta-glycopyranoside
  sensitivity, and has been studied for its relationship to individual
  differences in bitter-taste perception and, in some population
  studies, to alcohol-dependence risk. Extra-oral expression of TAS2R16
  in tissues such as sinonasal epithelium and gingiva has also been
  reported, with proposed but not yet clinically validated roles in
  innate mucosal defense and local anti-inflammatory signaling.
