Gene Description

MOTS-c (mitochondrial open reading frame of the 12S rRNA-c) is a 16-amino-acid mitochondrial-derived peptide encoded by a 51-bp alternative ORF inside the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene (MT-RNR1); because the mitochondrial genetic code reads the ORF as tandem start and stop codons, the peptide must be translated in the cytosol from an exported mitochondrial transcript, and nuclear NUMT copies of the locus do not encode the exact peptide. The peptide is amphipathic and cationic, with a hydrophobic core (Tyr8-Tyr11) and a basic C-terminal tail (Arg13-Arg16) that together govern both its nuclear entry and its interaction with nucleic acids and bacterial membranes. It is detected in mitochondria, in the nucleus and in extracellular fluid, circulating in human and rodent plasma; muscle and plasma levels rise with exercise and change with age. In the nucleus, following glucose restriction or oxidative stress, it occupies antioxidant response element (ARE)-containing promoters and associates with the ARE-regulating transcription factors NFE2L2/NRF2, NFE2L1/NRF1 and ATF1, thereby modulating RNA polymerase II-dependent stress-response gene expression - a case of a mitochondrially encoded factor acting on the nuclear genome. Applied as synthetic peptide it inhibits the folate-methionine cycle and de novo purine biosynthesis, causing AICAR to accumulate and AMPK to be activated, with downstream effects on glucose utilisation, insulin sensitivity, adiposity and muscle wasting signalling in rodents. As a cationic amphipathic peptide it also aggregates and damages Escherichia coli and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus directly, is induced in human monocytes by interferon gamma and lipopolysaccharide, and protects mice from MRSA challenge, properties shared with classical host defence peptides. The common Asian mtDNA variant m.1382A>C substitutes Lys14 with Gln and reduces the peptide's insulin-sensitising activity.

Core Functions

In the nucleus, MOTS-c binds DNA of antioxidant response element (ARE)-containing promoters and associates with the ARE-regulating transcription factors NFE2L2/NRF2, ATF1 and NFE2L1/NRF1, occupying the HO-1 and NQO1 promoters in a stress-dependent manner and shifting expression of several hundred nuclear genes, many of them bona fide NRF2 targets. Nuclear entry requires the hydrophobic core Tyr8-Tyr11 and the cationic tail Arg13-Arg16, and occurs after glucose restriction or oxidative stress in an AMPK-dependent manner. This makes the peptide a mitochondrially encoded contributor to nuclear stress-response transcription; it has no catalytic activity, and it is not itself a sequence-specific transcription factor, since the specificity controls in the binding assays cannot separate motif recognition from the charge-driven DNA affinity of a small cationic peptide.

MOTS-c binds the ARE-regulating stress-responsive transcription factors NFE2L2/NRF2, ATF1 and NFE2L1/NRF1 in the nucleus. The NRF2 association is stress-inducible and reciprocally confirmed with tagged constructs, and the two proteins reach the nucleus independently of each other, so the complex forms on chromatin rather than being a nuclear-import cargo relationship. Functionally this is how a mitochondrially encoded peptide is coupled to an existing nuclear transcription factor network, rather than acting as a transcription factor in its own right.

As a secreted, plasma-borne amphipathic cationic peptide, MOTS-c acts directly on bacteria - it aggregates and damages Escherichia coli and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus through its hydrophobic core and cationic tail, inhibits their growth, and neutralises MRSA infectivity in mice. Its expression is induced in human monocytes by interferon gamma, lipopolysaccharide and differentiation signals, and exogenous peptide reprogrammes differentiating monocytes into macrophages with greater bacterial killing capacity. These are the chemistry and the behaviour of a host defence peptide, and this is the function for which the peptide itself performs the step rather than sitting upstream of a metabolite cascade. No molecular-function term is asserted because membrane permeabilisation was shown as aggregation and surface damage, not as transmembrane pore formation.

Suggested Questions for Experts

Q: How is a peptide whose ORF lies inside the 12S rRNA actually made? The mitochondrial genetic code reads the ORF as tandem start and stop codons, so translation must occur in the cytosol from an exported mitochondrial transcript, by a route the original paper describes as poorly understood. Until an export mechanism and a cytosolic initiation event are demonstrated, every annotation on this entry rests on an unestablished biosynthetic premise.

Suggested experts: Changhan David Lee, Pinchas Cohen

Q: Has endogenous MOTS-c ever been identified at the peptide level by mass spectrometry, independently of the in-house antibody and ELISA? All localisation and abundance claims (mitochondrial, nuclear, plasma, exercise- and age-dependent changes) depend on those two reagents, and the peptide is only 16 residues long.

Suggested experts: Pinchas Cohen, Troy L Merry

Q: Is the DNA binding of MOTS-c sequence-specific? The EMSA specificity controls substitute the hydrophobic core and the cationic tail, which are also the residues that would drive charge-based binding of any short cationic peptide to DNA, so motif recognition has not been separated from non-specific affinity. The answer decides whether a cis-regulatory-region or sequence-specific DNA binding term is ever justified.

Suggested experts: Kyung Hwa Kim, Changhan David Lee

Q: What is the direct molecular target of MOTS-c in the folate-methionine cycle? The whole metabolic arm of the field - AICAR accumulation, AMPK activation, insulin sensitisation - is attributed to inhibition of this cycle, but no binding partner or inhibited enzyme has been identified, which is why the metabolic annotations have to stay at the regulation level.

Suggested experts: Changhan David Lee, Pinchas Cohen

Q: Do the reported effects occur at physiological peptide concentrations? Cellular work uses 0.5-10 micromolar synthetic peptide (100 micromolar for the antibacterial assays) and animal work 0.5-5 mg/kg/day by injection, while circulating endogenous levels are in the nanogram-per-millilitre range. Which of the phenotypes survive at endogenous concentrations is unresolved and determines which process terms are defensible.

Suggested experts: Troy L Merry, Hiroshi Kumagai

Suggested Experiments

Experiment: Targeted mass spectrometry (parallel reaction monitoring) for the MOTS-c peptide and its predicted proteolytic fragments in human plasma, skeletal muscle and isolated nuclei, with a heavy-isotope-labelled synthetic standard for quantification, in paired samples before and after exercise. A positive result would place the entry's existence beyond the antibody, and would give the absolute concentrations needed to judge which published effects are physiological.

Hypothesis: Endogenous MOTS-c exists as a discrete 16-residue peptide and can be detected without antibody reagents.

Experiment: Express the MOTS-c ORF from a nuclear, codon-optimised construct lacking any 12S rRNA context, with and without the YIFY and RKLR substitutions, in cells depleted of mitochondrial transcripts by actinonin, and test ARE promoter occupancy by ChIP-qPCR and stress-gene induction. Separating peptide from transcript settles whether the reported phenotypes belong on this protein entry at all.

Hypothesis: The nuclear and transcriptional functions require the peptide itself rather than the 12S rRNA transcript or a NUMT-derived product.

Experiment: Competition EMSA and a quantitative binding assay (fluorescence anisotropy or microscale thermophoresis) comparing ARE-containing probes against scrambled probes of identical length and GC content, and against poly-dIdC, at matched ionic strength, using charge-conservative mutants (for example Arg-to-Lys) as controls instead of alanine substitutions. This is the experiment that would license or exclude a sequence-specific DNA binding annotation.

Hypothesis: MOTS-c binding to ARE-containing DNA is charge-driven rather than motif-specific.

Experiment: Determine minimum inhibitory and minimum bactericidal concentrations for MOTS-c against E. coli and MRSA in physiological salt and serum, alongside calcein-leakage and membrane-potential assays on liposomes and on bacteria, and compare with LL-37 as a benchmark. Dose-response data in physiological media would show whether the host-defence role is plausible at endogenous levels, and leakage kinetics would settle whether a pore-forming molecular function term is warranted.

Hypothesis: The antibacterial activity operates at concentrations reachable in vivo and proceeds by membrane permeabilisation.

Experiment: Use a MOTS-c-neutralising antibody or an ORF-targeted antisense approach to lower endogenous peptide levels without ablating all mitochondrial gene expression (the confound in actinonin and rho-zero systems), then measure folate-cycle metabolites, AICAR, AMPK phosphorylation and glucose flux. Loss-of-function at endogenous levels is the missing half of the evidence for every metabolic annotation on this entry.

Hypothesis: The metabolic phenotypes depend on endogenous MOTS-c rather than on pharmacological peptide exposure.

1. Origin of the peptide, and the one unusual thing about it

The ORF is 51 bp inside the mitochondrial 12S rRNA
PMID:25738459.
Critically, it cannot be made by the mitochondrial ribosome:
PMID:25738459.
The authors therefore posit export of a polyadenylated mitochondrial transcript
to the cytosol, a route they concede is poorly understood. Nuclear NUMT copies
were excluded as the source of the exact peptide
PMID:25738459,
and mtDNA/mtRNA depletion (rho-0 HeLa, actinonin) removed the MOTS-c signal.

So: endogenous existence rests on antibody signal (immunofluorescence,
immunoblot of nuclear extracts), an in-house ELISA, and loss of signal on
mtDNA/mtRNA depletion. No peptide-level mass-spectrometric identification of
endogenous MOTS-c appears in the cached literature. The required cytosolic
translation of a mitochondrially transcribed rRNA-internal ORF remains the
unresolved mechanistic premise of the whole field. Curation consequence:
localisation and molecular-interaction annotations are retained where the assay
was done, but the physiological/metabolic phenotype terms that rest entirely on
injecting or adding synthetic peptide are flagged rather than treated as core.

3. Cross-cutting caveats recorded for the review

  • Dose and route. Essentially every functional result uses synthetic peptide
    at 0.5–10 µM in culture (100 µM in the antibacterial assays) or 0.5–5 mg/kg/day
    i.p. in mice. Endogenous-level manipulations do not exist, because mtDNA cannot
    be knocked out gene-specifically; actinonin/rho-0 depletion removes all
    mitochondrial gene expression at once.
