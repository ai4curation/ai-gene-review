| Identity versus NDE1 | **NDEL1/NUDEL is human UniProt Q9GZM8**, a NudE-family dynein regulator distinct from paralog **NDE1**. The paralogs are approximately 56% identical and 69% similar but differ in expression and some cellular roles. | Sequence comparison, expression studies, genetics, interaction mapping, and paralog-specific depletion. | **Identity verified; strong evidence.** Results for NDE1 must not automatically be assigned to NDEL1. | Garrott et al., 2022, [doi:10.3389/fcell.2022.871935](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcell.2022.871935) (pqac-00000001, pqac-00000008) |
| Architecture and dynein binding | NDEL1 has an N-terminal coiled-coil/NUDE region and a comparatively disordered regulatory C terminus. Its N-terminal approximately 200 residues bind the dynein intermediate-chain N terminus; **NDEL1 E48A** disrupts this interaction, whose affinity is in the low-micromolar range. | Mutagenesis, truncation mapping, binding assays, and structural modeling. | **Strong biochemical evidence**, although multiple NDEL1 surfaces may contact the dynein complex. | Garrott et al., 2022, [doi:10.3389/fcell.2022.871935](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcell.2022.871935) (pqac-00000001) |
| LIS1-binding interface | The NDEL1 coiled-coil segment spanning approximately residues **85–169** binds LIS1; **E116A** or **R127A** abolishes binding. The interaction has high-nanomolar affinity. | Targeted mutagenesis and quantitative protein-binding studies. | **Strong direct evidence** for a defined NDEL1–LIS1 interface. | Garrott et al., 2022, [doi:10.3389/fcell.2022.871935](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcell.2022.871935) (pqac-00000001) |
| Arg105 structural and disease relevance | **Arg105** lies within the coiled-coil region involved in self-association and LIS1 binding. The patient variant **p.Arg105Pro** disrupts LIS1 association and fails to support normal neuronal migration. | Patient sequencing, co-immunoprecipitation, cell imaging, and in-utero electroporation. | **Strong variant-to-mechanism evidence**, but based on only two unrelated patients. | Tsai et al., 2024, [doi:10.1007/s00401-023-02665-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00401-023-02665-y) (pqac-00000009, pqac-00000011) |
| Current dynein–LIS1 handoff model | NDEL1 recruits LIS1 to dynein through the intermediate-chain N terminus, but NDEL1-bound LIS1 and motor-domain-bound LIS1 are not a stable simultaneous complex. NDEL1 and the dynein motor compete for an overlapping LIS1 surface, implying temporally separated recruitment and **handoff** before dynein–dynactin–adaptor activation. | Purified human and yeast proteins, competition assays, mutagenesis, AlphaFold-based structural models, and evolutionary comparison. | **Leading peer-reviewed 2023 model.** It revises simple stable-tripartite-scaffold models; cellular timing remains incompletely resolved. | Okada et al., 2023, [doi:10.1038/s41467-023-41466-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-41466-5) (pqac-00000015) |
| Older in-vitro motility model | LIS1 suppressed dynein-driven microtubule gliding; NDEL1’s C terminus released dynein from microtubules, whereas its N terminus reversed LIS1-mediated suppression through LIS1 binding. | Recombinant fragments and microtubule-gliding assays. | **Direct but assay-dependent evidence.** These observations remain informative but should be interpreted alongside newer dynein-activation and handoff models. | Torisawa et al., 2011, [doi:10.1074/jbc.M110.169847](https://doi.org/10.1074/jbc.M110.169847) (pqac-00000003) |
| Alternative inhibition model | Purified NDEL1 inhibited dynein–dynactin–adaptor assembly and could sequester LIS1; C-terminal phosphomimetic substitutions increased inhibition. The authors proposed localized scaffolding of inactive dynein/LIS1 followed by regulated NDEL1 release. | Quantitative binding, protein biochemistry, and single-molecule imaging. | **Preprint finding—preliminary.** It highlights context dependence and has not superseded peer-reviewed cellular evidence that NDEL1 promotes dynein function. | Garrott et al., 2023 bioRxiv, [doi:10.1101/2023.01.25.525437](https://doi.org/10.1101/2023.01.25.525437) (pqac-00000004) |
| Centrosomes and neuronal nucleokinesis | NDEL1, LIS1, and dynein concentrate at centrosomes and microtubules in migrating neurons. Ndel1 depletion uncouples the centrosome from the nucleus, impairs nuclear translocation, and disrupts cortical neuronal positioning; LIS1 overexpression partially rescues Ndel1 depletion. | Developing-neocortex RNA interference, localization, centrosome–nucleus measurements, and genetic rescue. | **Strong pathway evidence** supporting a structural/regulatory role in dynein-generated nucleokinetic force. | Shu et al., 2004, [doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2004.09.030](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2004.09.030) (pqac-00000006) |
| Kinetochores and spindle poles | NDEL1/NDE1–LIS1–dynein complexes are recruited to kinetochores through CENP-F and contribute to spindle organization and checkpoint-protein transport toward spindle poles. | Immunolocalization, depletion, interaction mapping, and mitotic phenotyping. | **Strong cellular evidence**, although NDE1 can contribute more strongly than NDEL1 to kinetochore dynein recruitment in some systems. | Vergnolle & Taylor, 2007, [doi:10.1016/j.cub.2007.05.077](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2007.05.077); Garrott et al., 2022, [doi:10.3389/fcell.2022.871935](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcell.2022.871935) (pqac-00000001) |
| Ser285 and spatial mitotic control | Pole-localized Aurora A phosphorylates NDEL1 at **Ser285**. Replacement with nonphosphorylatable **S285A** increases Mad1 and NuMA at spindle poles and weakens nocodazole-induced checkpoint arrest, suggesting that phosphorylation locally limits dynein transport or cargo retention near poles. | HeLa T-REx replacement experiments, Aurora inhibitors, recombinant kinase assays, live imaging, and Xenopus aster assays. | **Strong mechanistic evidence** for a phosphorylation-controlled spatial switch; whether phosphorylation releases cargo, NDEL1, or dynein from microtubules remains unresolved. | Janczyk et al., 2023, [doi:10.1091/mbc.e21-09-0438](https://doi.org/10.1091/mbc.e21-09-0438) (pqac-00000013, pqac-00000014, pqac-00000016) |
| Axon initial segment and axonal cargo | NDEL1 localizes at the axon initial segment, where LIS1-dependent dynein regulation supports polarized cargo sorting. In mature axons, disrupting NDEL1/LIS1 impairs retrograde mitochondrial transport and alters mitochondrial length, with evidence of cargo-specific requirements. | Cultured neurons, knockdown and mutant rescue, live cargo imaging, and adult peripheral-nerve models. | **Moderate-to-strong experimental evidence**; most work uses rodent neurons rather than human neurons in vivo. | Kuijpers et al., 2016, [doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2016.01.022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2016.01.022); Pandey et al., 2022, [doi:10.3389/fnmol.2022.841047](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnmol.2022.841047) (pqac-00000005) |
| 2024 mosaic cortical-malformation cases | Two unrelated patients with pachygyria, with or without subcortical band heterotopia, carried the same de novo postzygotic **c.314G>C, p.Arg105Pro** variant. Variant fractions were approximately **25–30%** and **15–20%**, respectively; the CADD score was **32**. | Exome/Sanger sequencing, patient phenotyping, transcriptomics, protein interaction assays, and neuronal-migration models. | **Important first human genotype–mechanism report**, but recurrence in only two mosaic cases limits penetrance and prevalence estimates. | Tsai et al., 2024, [doi:10.1007/s00401-023-02665-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00401-023-02665-y) (pqac-00000009, pqac-00000011) |
| 2024 autism cortical organoids | ASD organoids were reported as **39–41% larger** and grew nearly **threefold faster** than controls. NDEL1 activity correlated inversely with growth rate (Spearman **ρ = −0.757, p = 0.014**) and size (**r = −0.697, p = 0.025**); expression correlated inversely with size (**r = −0.932, p = 0.006**). | A total of 4,910 organoids derived from 10 toddlers with ASD and 6 controls, with an independent production batch and linked clinical measures. | **Preliminary biomarker/model evidence.** The subject-level sample is small, associations do not prove causation, and clinical diagnostic validity has not been established. | Courchesne et al., 2024, [doi:10.1186/s13229-024-00602-8](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13229-024-00602-8) (pqac-00000010, pqac-00000012) |
| 2024 schizophrenia sodium-nitroprusside study | Adjunctive sodium nitroprusside reduced measured NDEL1 oligopeptidase activity relative to placebo (**t = 7.756, df = 97, p < 0.0001; Cohen’s d = 1.44**) in a crossover study involving **15 patients**, alongside results in spontaneously hypertensive rats. | Double-blind randomized crossover infusions: 0.5 μg/kg/min for 4 hours, weekly for 4 weeks; biochemical activity assay and psychopathology scales. | **Preliminary biomarker/pharmacodynamic evidence.** The clinical cohort was very small, and neither diagnostic performance nor evidence that NDEL1 is the drug’s causal target was established. | Nani et al., 2024, [doi:10.47626/1516-4446-2023-3315](https://doi.org/10.47626/1516-4446-2023-3315) |
| Proposed oligopeptidase activity | NDEL1 has been reported to cleave unstructured peptides as a serine oligopeptidase, with DISC1 inhibiting this activity; its physiological substrates and relationship to dynein regulation remain uncertain. | In-vitro activity assays and disease-associated biomarker studies. | **Unresolved secondary function.** NDEL1’s best-established primary role is as a nonmotor dynein/LIS1 scaffold-regulator, not as a validated metabolic enzyme with a defined endogenous substrate. | Garrott et al., 2022, [doi:10.3389/fcell.2022.871935](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcell.2022.871935) (pqac-00000001) |
