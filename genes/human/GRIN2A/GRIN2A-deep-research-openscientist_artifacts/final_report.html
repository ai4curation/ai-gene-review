GRIN2A (GluN2A / NR2A) — Functional Annotation Report

Gene: GRIN2A (HGNC:4585) · Synonyms: NMDAR2A · Protein: Glutamate receptor ionotropic, NMDA 2A (GluN2A / NR2A / epsilon-1 subunit) · UniProt: Q12879 · Organism: Homo sapiens · Family: Glutamate-gated ion channel (TC 1.A.10.1)

Summary

GRIN2A encodes GluN2A (also called NR2A or the glutamate receptor ε1 subunit), the principal glutamate-binding regulatory subunit of the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) subtype of ionotropic glutamate receptor. GluN2A does not function alone: it co-assembles with the obligatory GluN1 subunit (which binds the co-agonist glycine/D-serine) to build a heterotetrameric, glutamate-gated cation channel. The primary molecular activity of the receptor is ligand-gated ion conduction — specifically, it forms a non-selective cation channel that is highly permeable to Ca²⁺ and is subject to a voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ block. Because the channel opens only when glutamate binds and the postsynaptic membrane is depolarized (relieving the Mg²⁺ block), the GluN2A-containing NMDA receptor acts as a molecular coincidence detector at excitatory synapses. GluN2A is a ligand-gated ion channel subunit and signaling molecule — not an enzyme or a solute transporter; the "substrate" it conducts is Ca²⁺/Na⁺/K⁺, gated by the agonist glutamate. The gene symbol, organism, protein family, and domain architecture (ANF_lig-bd_rcpt, Glu/Gly-bd, Ionotropic_Glu_rcpt) all match the UniProt Q12879 target precisely; literature identity is unambiguous.

The gene product carries out its function at the postsynaptic density (PSD) of mature excitatory (glutamatergic) synapses in the central nervous system, with the extracellular N-terminal half of the protein facing the synaptic cleft and the C-terminal tail projecting into the postsynaptic cytoplasm, where it organizes downstream signaling. During development, cortical and hippocampal synapses undergo an activity- and experience-driven switch from GluN2B- to GluN2A-containing receptors, making GluN2A the hallmark subunit of the mature synapse. Functionally, Ca²⁺ entering through GluN2A-NMDARs is the trigger for the CaMKII → CREB signaling cascade that drives AMPA-receptor trafficking, spine enlargement, and long-term potentiation (LTP) — the cellular substrate of learning and memory — and it biases neural circuits toward stability rather than plasticity.

Clinically, GRIN2A is the glutamate-receptor gene most frequently mutated in human neurologic disease and the leading monogenic cause of the epilepsy-aphasia spectrum. Its variants produce either gain- or loss-of-function of the NMDA receptor in a domain-dependent manner, which directly validates the structure-function map of the protein: mutations in the glutamate-binding and amino-terminal regulatory domains tend to reduce receptor function, while mutations in the channel-gating transmembrane/linker regions tend to increase it.

Identity verification

The gene symbol, protein name, organism, family, and domain set given by UniProt are fully consistent with the published literature. The InterPro domains map one-to-one onto the experimentally defined NMDA-receptor architecture: ANF_lig-bd_rcpt (IPR001828) = extracellular amino-terminal domain (ATD); Glu/Gly-bd (IPR019594) = ligand-binding/agonist-binding domain (LBD/ABD); Ionotropic_Glu_rcpt / Iono_Glu_rcpt_met (IPR015683, IPR001508) = transmembrane ion-channel module; Iontro_rcpt_C (IPR001320) = intracellular C-terminal domain. There is no ambiguity: GRIN2A/Q12879 unambiguously denotes the human GluN2A subunit of the NMDA-type glutamate receptor. (A distinct body of work on GRIN2A somatic mutations in melanoma refers to the same gene/protein, not a homonym.)

Key Findings

Finding 1 — GluN2A is the glutamate-binding subunit of the NMDA receptor

GRIN2A encodes GluN2A, the regulatory subunit that binds the principal agonist, glutamate, within the NMDA-type ionotropic glutamate receptor. The receptor is an obligate heteromer: GluN2A must assemble with the GluN1 subunit (typically 2×GluN1 + 2×GluN2), and in the assembled receptor the two subunits bind different ligands — GluN2A binds glutamate, while GluN1 binds the co-agonist glycine (or D-serine); both must be occupied for activation. The decisive experimental evidence localizing the glutamate site to GluN2A comes from disease-mutation electrophysiology: the D731N mutation, located in the GluN2A agonist-binding domain (ABD) glutamate pocket, decreases glutamate potency by more than 3,000-fold (PMID: 28182669). A single residue substitution in the GluN2A ABD selectively crippling glutamate sensitivity is direct proof that this subunit houses the glutamate-binding pocket. A foundational description confirms the identity: "GRIN2A encodes the regulatory GluN2A subunit of the glutamate-gated N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor (NMDAR)" (PMID: 24455489). This is fully consistent with the UniProt Q12879 domain set — ANF_lig-bd_rcpt (IPR001828) and the Glu/Gly-binding domain (IPR019594) — confirming the correct protein.

GluN2A is not an enzyme or transporter in the classical sense: it is a ligand-gated ion channel subunit. The assembled NMDAR is a non-selective cation channel that is highly permeable to Ca²⁺ (in addition to Na⁺/K⁺) and shows a hallmark voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ block relieved by depolarization.

Finding 2 — GRIN2A is a major monogenic locus for the epilepsy-aphasia spectrum, with domain-dependent gain- vs. loss-of-function

GRIN2A is the glutamate-receptor gene most often affected by neurologic-disease mutations, and seizure disorders constitute the largest category of affected patients (PMID: 25904555: "the GRIN2A gene encoding the GluN2A N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor subunit being most often affected"). In a cohort of 248 individuals, pathogenic GRIN2A variants produced a clinical spectrum ranging from mild epilepsy with speech delay to severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathy — strongly within the epilepsy-aphasia spectrum. Critically, the phenotype tracked with both the domain location of the mutation and its functional consequence: missense variants in the transmembrane and linker domains (misTMD+Linker) produced severe phenotypes and predominantly NMDAR gain-of-function, whereas missense variants in the amino-terminal and ligand-binding domains (misATD+LBD) and null variants caused less severe phenotypes and loss-of-function (discovery P = 1×10⁻⁶; validation P = 0.0003) (PMID: 30544257: "misTMD+Linker predominantly led to NMDAR gain-of-function, while misATD+LBD exclusively caused NMDAR loss-of-function").

This genotype–function–phenotype correlation is itself a powerful structure-function readout: it indicates that the transmembrane/linker region controls channel gating (so mutations there open the channel too easily), whereas the ATD and LBD regulate and bind ligand (so mutations there weaken the response). It also rationalizes divergent precision therapies — NMDAR antagonists (e.g., memantine) for gain-of-function and co-agonist supplementation (L-serine) for loss-of-function (PMID: 36516565).

Finding 3 — GluN2A-NMDARs reside in the postsynaptic density and predominate at mature synapses after a developmental GluN2B→GluN2A switch

GluN2A (NR2A)-containing NMDARs are concentrated at the postsynaptic density (PSD) of excitatory synapses — the electron-dense scaffold directly beneath the postsynaptic membrane. Their abundance there is actively maintained: the auxiliary CUB-domain protein Neto1 is required to keep NR2A-NMDARs at the PSD, and Neto1-null mice shift LTP subunit-dependency away from NR2A-NMDARs and show depressed hippocampal LTP, learning, and memory (PMID: 19243221: "a novel component of the NMDAR complex critical for maintaining the abundance of NR2A-containing NMDARs in the postsynaptic density").

GluN2A is the signature subunit of the mature synapse. During cortical and hippocampal development there is an activity- and sensory-experience-driven switch from NR2B- to NR2A-containing NMDARs (PMID: 21521618: "In cerebral cortex there is a developmental switch from NR2B- to NR2A-containing NMDA receptors (NMDARs) driven by activity and sensory experience."). This switch requires joint NMDAR + mGluR5 activation signaling through the PLC/IP3R-Ca²⁺-release/PKC pathway, and is deficient in mGluR5-knockout mice in vivo (in both CA1 and visual cortex). Independent biochemical evidence from in situ photoaffinity labeling shows the adult NR2A-type (175 kDa) NMDAR labeling pattern emerges around postnatal day 10, confirming NR2A as a structural marker of receptor maturation (PMID: 8378316).

Finding 4 — Ca²⁺ permeability and voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ block make GluN2A-NMDARs molecular coincidence detectors required for circuit refinement

The defining biophysical property of the NMDA receptor is that it is a non-selective cation channel that is highly permeable to Ca²⁺ and subject to a voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ block that is relieved by postsynaptic depolarization. Consequently, channel opening requires the simultaneous occurrence of two events: presynaptic glutamate release AND postsynaptic depolarization — the biophysical definition of coincidence detection. The causal importance of these properties was shown genetically: an NR1 N598R mutation that renders NMDARs Mg²⁺-insensitive and Ca²⁺-impermeable prevents whisker-related somatotopic ("barrelette") pattern formation in the mouse brainstem despite normal receptor expression (PMID: 12657691: "coincidence-dependent calcium signaling by NMDA receptors (NMDARs) under control of the voltage-dependent Mg2+ block might play a special role"; and "This leads to the expression of functional NMDARs that are Mg2+ insensitive and Ca2+ impermeable."). This demonstrates that coincidence-gated Ca²⁺ signaling by NMDARs is necessary for activity-dependent synaptic map refinement, not merely correlated with it.

Finding 5 — GluN2A-NMDAR Ca²⁺ influx triggers the CaMKII → AMPAR-trafficking cascade underlying LTP and memory

Ca²⁺ admitted through postsynaptic NMDARs is the trigger for the central plasticity cascade. At mature synapses this Ca²⁺ primarily activates Ca²⁺/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) and drives its autophosphorylation, which renders the kinase calcium-independent so that its signaling outlasts the brief NMDAR Ca²⁺ transient (PMID: 34051268: "the elevated calcium levels activate primarily calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase II (CaMKII) and cause its autophophorylation"). Sustained CaMKII activity then drives the trafficking of AMPA-type glutamate receptors into the postsynaptic density and structural enlargement of the spine (PMID: 34375719: "Activation of postsynaptic NMDA type glutamate receptor (NMDA-R) induces trafficking of AMPA type glutamate receptors (AMPA-R) and other proteins to the synapse in sequential fashion."). This is the molecular basis of long-term potentiation (LTP), the cellular correlate of learning and memory. The reverse manipulation confirms the direction of causality: NMDAR hypofunction downregulates the CaMKII → ERK1/2 → CREB cascade and impairs LTP and recognition memory (PMID: 36417808; PMID: 33882624).

Finding 6 — The GluN2A-specific intracellular C-terminal domain couples NMDARs to CaMKII/CREB signaling and promotes mature-circuit stability

GluN2A and GluN2B are most divergent in their long intracellular C-terminal tails, which recruit distinct sets of signaling proteins; this is the structural basis for their different downstream effects. NR1/NR2A receptors are mainly expressed in mature neurons and induce circuit stability, whereas NR1/NR2B receptors predominate in immature neurons and promote plasticity and dendritogenesis (PMID: 28160495: "NR1NR2B receptors are predominantly expressed in immature neurons and promote plasticity, NR1NR2A receptors are mainly expressed in mature neurons and induce circuit stability"; and "this is likely related to the presence of their distinct C-terminal sequences that couple different signaling proteins"). Mechanistically, CaMKII is recruited to the GluN2 C-terminal domain, and engineering a GluN2A construct with increased CaMKII interaction (NR2AΔIN) reactivates dendritogenesis in mature hippocampal neurons both in vitro and in vivo — demonstrating that the GluN2A C-tail's CaMKII coupling, acting through CREB phosphorylation and the histone mark H3K27ac, controls the transcriptional program that governs the plasticity-versus-stability transition. GluN2A-NMDARs also interact with the PSD-95/MAGUK scaffold and are regulated by calmodulin and α-actinin at the PSD.

Finding 7 — GluN1/GluN2A NMDARs are modular heterotetramers allosterically gated via the GluN2A amino-terminal domain (high-affinity Zn²⁺ / proton inhibition)

Cryo-EM and crystal structures of the GluN1/GluN2A NMDA receptor reveal a layered, modular heterotetramer with four structural tiers, each corresponding to UniProt-annotated domains:

  1. an extracellular amino-terminal domain (ATD) — the ANF_lig-bd_rcpt / IPR001828 region;
  2. a ligand-binding domain (LBD/ABD) — the Glu/Gly-bd / IPR019594 region, where GluN2A binds glutamate and GluN1 binds glycine;
  3. a transmembrane ion-channel domain forming the Ca²⁺-permeable pore and Mg²⁺-block site;
  4. an intracellular C-terminal domain.

A subtype-defining feature of GluN2A-containing receptors is allosteric inhibition by nanomolar zinc and by low pH. Zinc binds a high-affinity site in the cleft of the GluN2A ATD bi-lobe; this binding elicits structural changes transduced through the LBD that constrict the channel gate and lower open probability (PMID: 30500536: "zinc binding to the amino terminal domain elicits structural changes that are transduced though the ligand-binding domain and result in constriction of the ion channel gate"; PMID: 27916457: "their ion channel activity is allosterically inhibited by a nano-molar concentration of zinc that binds to an extracellular domain called an amino-terminal domain (ATD)"). This ATD-based allosteric mechanism is evolutionarily conserved — the conformational closure induced by zinc on GluN2A is structurally analogous to that induced by ifenprodil on GluN2B (PMID: 26170175).

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

The findings integrate into a single coherent picture of GluN2A as the glutamate-sensing, Ca²⁺-conducting, plasticity-gating subunit of the mature excitatory synapse.

   ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  ATD layer   [GluN2A ATD]  ← Zn2+ (nM), H+ (low pH)   ← allosteric brake
   │              [GluN1 ATD ]
   │  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   │  LBD layer   [GluN2A ABD] ← GLUTAMATE (agonist)
   │              [GluN1 ABD ] ← GLYCINE / D-SERINE (co-agonist)
   ├───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
   │  TMD / PORE  voltage-dependent Mg2+ block;  Ca2+-permeable
   └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   POSTSYNAPTIC CYTOPLASM
      [GluN2A C-terminal tail] → recruits CaMKII → CREB / H3K27ac

Gating requires a logical AND of three conditions: (1) glutamate bound at the GluN2A ABD, (2) glycine/D-serine bound at the GluN1 ABD, and (3) membrane depolarization to expel Mg²⁺ from the pore. Only then does the Ca²⁺-permeable channel open. The GluN2A ATD superimposes a tunable allosteric brake — nanomolar Zn²⁺ and protons close the ATD bi-lobe and dampen the channel — allowing the local chemical environment to set receptor sensitivity.

Developmental and functional role

The receptor's coincidence-detection property makes it the molecular arbiter of activity-dependent circuit refinement: only coincident, correlated pre- and post-synaptic activity opens the channel and admits the Ca²⁺ needed to strengthen a synapse ("cells that fire together, wire together"). Early in development GluN2B dominates, favoring plasticity; sensory experience and activity then drive a GluN2B→GluN2A switch, after which the GluN2A C-terminal tail reconfigures downstream signaling to stabilize mature circuits. This explains why GluN2A is the hallmark of the mature synapse and why its loss derails both learning and the normal maturation of language and cortical networks.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  • Literature-based conclusions. No primary dataset was provided; all conclusions rest on published experimental and structural evidence.
  • Species of evidence. Several mechanistic studies (barrelette coincidence-detection, Neto1, NR2B→NR2A switch, dendritogenesis) are rodent/in vitro. The core biochemistry (ligand binding, Ca²⁺ permeability, zinc allostery, CaMKII coupling) is highly conserved and corroborated by human structural and genetic data, but some fine details are inferred for human by homology.
  • Triheteromeric receptors. Many native adult NMDARs are triheteromeric (GluN1/GluN2A/GluN2B), with intermediate kinetics. This report treats the diheteromeric GluN1/GluN2A receptor as canonical; native mixed-receptor behavior in vivo is less completely characterized.
  • C-terminal interactome. The full PSD interactome of the GluN2A C-tail (PSD-95/MAGUK, phosphatases, additional kinases) and how specific disease mutations perturb it is not exhaustively mapped here.
  • Non-neuronal roles. Functional NMDARs outside the CNS (e.g., vascular/immune cells, melanoma) are emerging but mechanistically less precise and were treated as secondary to the core synaptic function.
  • Variant-level functional data. Although the domain-level gain/loss split is statistically robust, functional annotation of individual rare human variants remains incomplete, limiting precision-therapy decisions.
