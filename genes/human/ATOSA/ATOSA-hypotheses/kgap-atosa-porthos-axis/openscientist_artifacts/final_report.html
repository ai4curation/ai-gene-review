ATOSA (Q32MH5) and the Porthos axis: hypothesis evaluation for AI Gene Review

Hypothesis slug: kgap-atosa-porthos-axis. Source: genes/human/ATOSA/ATOSA-ai-review.yaml, selector free-text. Target: human ATOSA, UniProt Q32MH5, NCBITaxon:9606. Focus type: free_text.

Summary

The hypothesis splits cleanly into three claims with three different verdicts. The helicase identification is supported. Fly Porthos is pths/CG9253 (FBgn0032919, UniProt Q9VIF6), already named "ATP-dependent RNA helicase DDX47" in UniProt, and its single human ortholog is DDX47 by reciprocal best hit in both directions: DIOPT v9.1 returns 13 of 13 tools with best_score and best_score_rev both Yes and confidence high, against 2 of 13 for the runner-up DDX49, and an independent Smith-Waterman alignment gives 71.3% identity over a 449-residue local alignment with a 1.71-fold score margin forward and 2.29-fold reverse. No hedging is needed on this point.

Whether human ATOSA acts through DDX47 is unresolved. No DDX or DHX protein appears in any public interaction record for Q32MH5. IntAct returns 23 records, all yeast two-hybrid, 18 of them from one screen (PMID: 25416956). STRING v12 returns 14 partners for FAM214A, all below combined score 0.52 and driven by text mining, with no helicase at any score. GTEx co-expression with DDX47 (Pearson r = 0.713 on log2 median TPM across 54 tissues) ranks only 24th of 36 DEAD/DEAH-box genes, and DDX47 median TPM sits at the quantification floor (0.00 to 1.99), so the correlation carries no specific signal. The Human Protein Atlas places ATOSA in the nucleoplasm and DDX47 in nucleoli. BioGRID (HTTP 401 without a registered access key) and the BioPlex API (HTTP 404) could not be queried, so the two resources most likely to hold affinity-proteomics data remain unchecked. A detail that reframes the whole question: the fly paper does not report an Atossa-Porthos protein complex. It reports that nuclear Atossa increases porthos mRNA (PMID: 35319107). The precedent is regulatory, at transcript level, so a human ATOSA-DDX47 binding claim would exceed what fly supports even before the missing human data is considered.

Asserting DNA-binding or transcription-factor activity for ATOSA is over-annotation. The AlphaFold model AF-Q32MH5-F1-model_v6 has exactly one well-folded module with a recognisable fold: residues 1-230 (mean pLDDT 82.5), which is a HORMA domain. Foldseek TM-align gives TM 0.619 to 0.665 against ATG13, ATG101-bound ATG13, S. pombe Mad2 and human MAD2 at 7.8-10.7% sequence identity, with a disordered negative control (residues 300-530, mean pLDDT 39.9) returning zero hits and the equally well-folded DUF4210 module (870-1000, mean pLDDT 84.2) topping out at TM 0.447 against mutually inconsistent targets. HORMA domains capture short closure motifs beneath a refolding safety belt (PMID: 29186573), which is peptide binding. The 1076-residue sequence contains no C2H2 or CCHC zinc finger, no RING or PHD, no AT-hook, no HMG box, no homeodomain, no bZIP basic region and no run of four consecutive basic residues. ATOSA is absent from the Lambert human transcription factor census (PMID: 29425488). The UniProt FUNCTION line "Transcription regulator that syncronizes transcriptional and translational programs" carries ECO:0000250/UniProtKB:Q69ZK7 in the human entry, a similarity transfer from mouse that itself rests on the single fly publication, and GO holds no molecular function for Atossa in any species.

Executive judgment

Split verdict: supported for the ortholog assignment, unresolved for the human axis, over-annotated for any DNA-binding or transcription-factor activity.

The most important caveats are stated once each. Foldseek and TM-align assign a fold, not a function, so a HORMA domain at 8-10% identity could in principle have lost closure-motif binding. The interaction search is incomplete because BioGRID and BioPlex could not be reached. The GTEx analysis uses bulk median TPM and would miss a relationship confined to one cell type or one stimulus, which matters because the fly work was done in migrating embryonic macrophages.

Key findings

F001. Porthos maps to human DDX47 as a 1:1 reciprocal best hit

Porthos resolves to Drosophila melanogaster pths/CG9253 (FBgn0032919, UniProt Q9VIF6), which UniProt already annotates as "ATP-dependent RNA helicase DDX47". Two orthology methods agree without qualification.

DIOPT v9.1, integrating 13 prediction tools, returns human DDX47 (Entrez 51202) at score 13/13 with best_score = Yes and best_score_rev = Yes, confidence high. The next candidate, DDX49, scores 2/13 with low confidence. The reverse query from human DDX47 to fly returns pths at 13/13, best forward and reverse, with Dbp45A second at 2/13.

An independent Smith-Waterman alignment (BLOSUM62, gap open 11, extend 1, implemented directly because Biopython was unavailable in the executor) of Q9VIF6 against all 58 human DEAD/DEAH-box proteins puts DDX47 top at SW = 1643, 71.3% identity over a 449-residue local alignment. Second-best DDX49 scores 960 at 49.9% identity, a 1.71-fold margin. The reverse alignment of human DDX47 (Q9H0S4) against 26 fly DEAD/DEAH-box proteins returns pths top at SW = 1643 with Dbp45A second at 719, a 2.29-fold margin. The reciprocal best hit criterion is satisfied in both directions with margins large enough that no alternative assignment is plausible.

Functional orthology agrees with sequence orthology. Porthos in fly supports ribosome assembly and raises the translational efficiency of a subset of mRNAs (PMID: 35319107), and human DDX47 associates with ribosome biogenesis machinery: NOP132 "associated mainly with proteins involved in ribosome biogenesis and RNA metabolism, including the DEAD-box RNA helicase protein, DDX47, whose yeast homolog is Rrp3, which has roles in pre-rRNA processing" (PMID: 16963496).

F003 and F005. The only recognisable fold in ATOSA is a HORMA domain, and there is no DNA-binding module

AF-Q32MH5-F1-model_v6 covers 1076 residues at mean pLDDT 54.9 and median 38.8. Of those, 612 residues (56.9%) have pLDDT below 50 and only 35.2% reach pLDDT 70 or above. The ordered segments are 15-49, 59-100, 114-136, 141-231 (together mean pLDDT 82.4 over residues 1-222), then 881-925, 934-998 and 1017-1047. Residues 232-877 have mean pLDDT 36.5 and are predicted disordered.

A Foldseek 3Di-AA search of the full model against PDB100 returned more than 20 hits, and every top hit is a HORMA-domain protein aligning to query residues 1-222: ATG13 HORMA (5XV1 E = 2.8e-06, 5XUY, 5XV4), the ATG13:ATG101 HORMA dimer with ATG9 (8DO8 E = 2.3e-06), MAD2/MAD2L1 (2VFX E = 6.3e-06, 3GMH, 2QYF, 2V64, 1GO4, 1KLQ), MAD2L2/REV7 within the shieldin complex (6WWA, 6WW9), yeast Atg13 HORMA (4J2G) and a bacterial HORMA1:HORMA2 pair (6P8S). Sequence identity across these is 8-15%, so this is a fold-level assignment made on structure alone.

Because sequence-driven superposition is unusable at that identity, the assignment was validated with TM-align rather than with phenix.superpose_pdbs, which returned 9.5 Å RMSD over 27 atoms and was uninformative. Foldseek TM-align on the isolated fragment gives TM 0.665 against 5XUY, 0.663 against 8DO8, 0.662 against 5XV1, 0.661 against 4YK8 (S. pombe Mad2), 0.661 against 5XV4, 0.626 against 4AEZ, 0.625 against 2QYF and 0.619 against 3GMH, over alignment lengths of 223-242 residues at 7.8-10.7% identity. TM-score above 0.5 is the same-fold threshold, and the assignment holds across three independent HORMA subfamilies.

Two controls establish that the result is not an artifact of high pLDDT or of the method. Residues 300-530 (mean pLDDT 39.9, disordered) return zero hits. Residues 870-1000, which cover DUF4210/IPR025261 and are just as confidently folded (mean pLDDT 84.2), reach a best TM of only 0.447 (2N59), followed by 0.442, 0.438 and 0.429, all against unrelated and mutually inconsistent PDB entries at 1.5-7.2% identity. A well-folded module with no fold family in the PDB.

Motif scans of the full 1076-residue sequence find no C2H2 zinc finger, no CCHC knuckle, no RING or PHD, no AT-hook, no HMG box or homeodomain signature, and no SV40-type basic run. Cysteine content is 2.42% (26 residues) and net charge is about +5 (K+R 12.1%, D+E 11.6%). Only two weak monopartite NLS-like matches appear, KKNK at 532 and KRPK at 709, and both fall inside disordered regions. InterPro and Pfam assign nothing at all to residues 1-462; their only entries are IPR025261/DUF4210 (878-936), IPR033473 Atos-like C-terminal (1018-1075) and the PANTHER family PTHR13199 (463-1076). The Foldseek hits to DNA polymerase zeta (6V93, 7S0T) are through the REV7/MAD2L2 HORMA subunit, not through any DNA-contacting element.

The same module is present in mouse Atosa Q69ZK7 at residues 1-220 (8DO8, E = 1.5e-06) and in Drosophila Atossa Q7JXG9 at residues 313-517 (2VFX, E = 1.0e-04), so it is the conserved part of the protein and not a human-specific prediction.

F008. The HORMA module is closest to MAD2, is conserved across vertebrates, and is absent from the paralog ATOSB

Searching ATOSA 1-230 against AFDB/SwissProt with TM-align ranks the non-Atos hits as MAD2 S. cerevisiae P40958 at 0.618, Cap7 P. aeruginosa P0DTF6 at 0.602 (a bacterial HORMA), MAD2 C. albicans Q59VQ3 at 0.600, MAD2A D. discoideum Q556Y9 at 0.594, MAD2A mouse Q9Z1B5 at 0.585, MAD2A human Q13257 at 0.569, REV7/DNA polymerase zeta processivity subunit A. thaliana Q94FL5 at 0.567, human HORMAD2 Q8N7B1 at 0.534 and S. pombe ATG101 O13978 at 0.493. Every scored match in the top 25 is either an Atos protein or a HORMA protein. The closest named subfamily is MAD2.

The module is under strong purifying selection in vertebrates: mouse Q69ZK7 shows 90.8-93.0% identity over the 230-residue alignment, chicken Q5ZI58 89.6%, Xenopus tropicalis Q5FW46 82.6% and zebrafish Q1LV22 77.0-80.4%.

The paralog test matters for curation. Human ATOSB (Q7L5A3) residues 1-230 have mean pLDDT 36.0 and return zero Foldseek hits. The ordered part of ATOSB begins at residue 351 (160 residues at pLDDT 70 or above; segment 340-538 mean pLDDT 84.5) and matches ATOSA orthologs at TM 0.572-0.575 with only 13.1-14.0% identity, which is the shared C-terminal Atos module (the DUF4210/invasion region), not a HORMA. The HORMA domain therefore separates ATOSA from ATOSB rather than being a family-wide feature, and the two paralogs should not inherit each other's annotations.

F002 and F007. No DDX protein appears in any ATOSA interaction record, and co-expression gives no specific signal

IntAct (EBI REST, findInteractions/Q32MH5) returns 23 interaction records. The human protein partners are KRT40, MAGEA6, MID2 isoform Q9UJV3-2, SSX2IP, GOLGA2, TRIM27, CASS4 isoform Q9NQ75-2 and APC, plus two non-human bait screens (Coxiella burnetii Q83D09 and a flagellar protein A0A2U2GYT1), plus one CLASH RNA record pairing the ATOSA mRNA with hsa-miR-15b-5p (PMID: 23622248). Every human protein record comes from yeast two-hybrid: 18 of 23 from one large-scale dataset (PMID: 25416956, MI score 0.56), the rest from Y2H pooling screens at MI score 0.37. There is no affinity-purification mass spectrometry, no co-immunoprecipitation and no proximity-labelling evidence for any ATOSA partner. STRING v12 returns 14 partners for FAM214A, all with combined score below 0.52 and driven by text mining (tscore up to 0.506); only UBE2H and HNRNPH1 carry any experimental channel score (escore 0.457), and no DDX or DHX protein appears at any score.

The partner list also contains several proteins with a known yeast two-hybrid false-positive profile (a keratin, MAGEA6, TRIM27), so it provides little positive information about ATOSA's real complex membership in either direction.

GTEx v8 median TPM across 54 tissues was used to test co-expression, with a 140-gene background panel (36 human DEAD/DEAH-box genes plus roughly 100 housekeeping, lineage and metabolic genes) so that the ATOSA-DDX47 value could be read against a null. ATOSA versus DDX47 gives Pearson r = +0.713 on log2(TPM+1) (p = 1.4e-09, n = 54 tissues) and Spearman rho = +0.679. That sits at the 78th percentile of the full panel (panel median r = 0.489) but ranks only 24th of 36 DEAD/DEAH-box genes. DDX46 (r = 0.869), DHX38 (0.855), DDX42 (0.836), DHX36 (0.828), DDX23 (0.824) and nine others correlate more strongly with ATOSA than DDX47 does. For reference, ATOSA versus DDX49 gives 0.638, versus ACTB 0.393, and versus its own paralog ATOSB 0.165 (p = 0.23). A technical caveat sharpens the negative: DDX47 median TPM in GTEx v8 spans only 0.00 to 1.99 (mean 0.52), far below DDX49 (14.4 to 62.8) and ATOSA (2.8 to 24.9), so the DDX47 row sits near the quantification floor and its apparent tissue profile is unreliable.

Subcellular localisation adds a mismatch. The Human Protein Atlas assigns FAM214A/ATOSA to the nucleoplasm, FAM214B/ATOSB to nucleoplasm plus nucleoli fibrillar center, and DDX47 to nucleoli. This is antibody-based database evidence, not a direct interaction test, but ATOSA and the candidate helicase are placed in different subnuclear compartments.

F006. The human transcription-regulator claim is a two-step similarity transfer

Reading the UniProt evidence tags directly settles where the claim comes from. The FUNCTION line "Transcription regulator that syncronizes transcriptional and translational programs" carries ECO:0000269/PubMed:35319107 in fly Atossa (Q7JXG9), ECO:0000269/PubMed:35319107 in mouse Atosa (Q69ZK7), and ECO:0000250/UniProtKB:Q69ZK7 in human ATOSA (Q32MH5). The human statement is an ISS projection from mouse, which rests on the same single publication.

The same pattern holds for every region-level feature in the human entry: TAD1 at 24-32, TAD2 at 962-970 and the invasion region at 878-935 are all ECO:0000250/UniProtKB:Q69ZK7. The fly equivalents (174-182, 1150-1158, 1074-1132) carry ECO:0000269. The DOMAIN comment is ECO:0000305, curator inference, in both fly and mouse.

GO is thinner still. Human ATOSA Q32MH5 has six records of GO:0005515 protein binding (IPI, PMID: 25416956) and GO:0005634 nucleus (ISS from Q7JXG9, plus IEA), with no molecular function beyond protein binding and no biological process. Fly Atossa Q7JXG9 has GO:0005634 nucleus (EXP, PMID: 35319107) and nothing else: no molecular function, no transcription-related biological process. Mouse Atosa carries ND for molecular function and biological process in MGI.

The asymmetry with the helicase arm of the same paper is informative. Fly Porthos received rRNA binding (IDA, PMID: 35413237), nucleolus (IDA), ribosome biogenesis (IMP) and positive regulation of oxidative phosphorylation and of cytoplasmic translation (IMP, PMID: 35319107); human DDX47 carries ATP-dependent RNA helicase activity and rRNA processing (IMP, PMID: 16963496). The same publication gave curators enough to assign four experimental GO terms to the helicase and only a localisation term to Atossa.

Finally, the Lambert human transcription factor census (PMID: 29425488, 2765 rows, 1639 proteins called TFs) contains neither FAM214A/ATOSA nor FAM214B/ATOSB in either the TF or the non-TF rows of the curated DNA-binding-domain survey.

Mechanistic model and interpretation

The fly result and the human prediction describe two different kinds of relationship, and conflating them is what generates the over-annotation risk.

In fly, nuclear Atossa raises the transcript levels of porthos, LKR/SDH and GR/HPR, and Porthos then supports ribosome assembly and raises translational efficiency of mRNAs affecting OxPhos (PMID: 35319107). The measured step closest to Atossa itself is a change in transcript abundance. No promoter occupancy was shown, and no Atossa-Porthos binding assay was reported.

In human, the structural prediction points somewhere specific. ATOSA has one folded module with a known fold, and that fold is a HORMA domain whose entire described mechanism is capture of a short closure-motif peptide beneath a refolding safety belt (PMID: 29186573). That is a protein-protein interaction with a defined assay. The 646-residue disordered middle and the two transactivation-domain-like segments transferred by similarity are compatible with a co-regulator that is recruited to a complex, where the DNA contact is made by a partner.

Two readings of the data remain open, and they make different predictions.

Adaptor model Transcription co-regulator model
Core activity HORMA domain captures a closure motif in one partner Disordered regions plus TADs recruit the transcription machinery
What explains the fly transcript changes Indirect, through the captured partner Indirect, through a DNA-binding partner
Predicted ATOSA interactome One or a few stoichiometric partners with a short linear motif Many weak, transient contacts with coactivators
Discriminating observation A closure-motif peptide binds ATOSA 1-230 in vitro and the complex is a TRIP13 substrate ATOSA knockdown shifts a defined transcriptional program in human cells, with no chromatin occupancy
Status untested untested in human

Neither model requires ATOSA to bind DNA, and no evidence available supports that it does.

GO curation implications

All items below are leads requiring curator verification. Machine-readable copy: provenance/go_decision_table.csv.

Do not add any DNA-binding or transcription-factor term. GO:0003700 (DNA-binding transcription factor activity), GO:0003677 (DNA binding) and the GO:0000976 family all fail on four independent checks: no DNA-binding fold in the AlphaFold model, no DNA-binding motif in the 1076-residue sequence, no InterPro assignment anywhere in residues 1-462, and exclusion from the Lambert TF census. The UniProt free text that invites such a term is itself ECO:0000250 from mouse.

GO:0006355 regulation of transcription, DNA-templated. Not currently present for human ATOSA and should not be added for human. The underlying experiment is in fly. If the process is to be represented at all, the defensible placement is a fly annotation with IMP from PMID: 35319107, from which an ISS or IBA projection could later be considered. Even in fly the measured effect is downstream transcript abundance, which supports positive regulation of transcription at most and carries no implication of direct promoter contact.

GO:0005634 nucleus (CC), currently ISS from Q7JXG9 plus IEA. Retain. A lead to make it more specific to GO:0005654 nucleoplasm is supported by the HPA subcellular record for FAM214A, subject to curator judgement on antibody-based evidence. Do not propagate a nucleolar term; that belongs to DDX47.

GO:0005515 protein binding (MF), six IPI records from PMID: 25416956. The instruction to avoid "protein binding" as a final recommendation is apt here, but the replacement is not yet available. All six records come from one Y2H screen at MI score 0.56 with no orthogonal confirmation, and the partner list (KRT40, MAGEA6, GOLGA2, APC) has the profile of a sticky-bait screen. Treat as non-core. The structurally motivated successor is a HORMA-type closure-motif binding term, but no binding assay exists, so nothing more specific can be asserted today.

No helicase-binding or DDX47-binding term. Nothing in IntAct, STRING, GTEx or HPA supports one, and the fly precedent is regulatory.

Informative lead for a future MF. If the HORMA domain is confirmed biochemically, the appropriate terms sit in the protein-sequence-binding or adaptor branch, not in any nucleic-acid branch. Flagging residues 1-230 as an unannotated HORMA domain is a concrete, verifiable addition to the review, and worth passing to InterPro, which currently assigns nothing to that region.

Mechanistic scope

The immediate molecular activity under test is whether ATOSA contacts DNA or chromatin, or instead binds proteins. The structural evidence answers the second half: residues 1-230 form a HORMA domain, a module whose known mechanism is capture of a short closure-motif peptide. Residues 232-877 are predicted disordered (mean pLDDT 36.5). Residues 878-1047 form a second ordered region, the DUF4210 and Atos C-terminal modules, with no fold relative in the PDB (best TM 0.447), so no activity can be read from it.

Separated from that: the fly phenotype. Macrophage germband invasion, mitochondrial bioenergetics and OxPhos output are organismal and cellular outcomes several steps downstream, observed on loss of function. The transcript increases in porthos, LKR/SDH and GR/HPR are the closest measured readouts to Atossa itself, and even those are abundance changes rather than demonstrated promoter occupancy. Nothing in the chain has been measured in human cells. The mouse rescue of the fly mutant, which this analysis was instructed not to re-derive, establishes functional conservation of the protein, not the identity of its molecular activity.

Conflicts and alternatives

The HORMA assignment competes directly with the transcription-regulator framing. A nuclear HORMA-domain protein with long disordered regions fits a scaffold or adaptor that captures a partner peptide and is itself regulated by conformational change, possibly by TRIP13 (PMID: 28659378). Under that model the transcriptional effects seen in fly are indirect, through a DNA-binding partner that ATOSA recruits or sequesters.

Paralog separation argues against treating the Atos family as a unit. ATOSB (Q7L5A3) has a disordered N-terminus (mean pLDDT 36.0 over 1-230, zero Foldseek hits), and its only folded module, 340-538, corresponds to the shared C-terminal Atos region at 13-14% identity. ATOSB also carries RNA binding HDA from two mRNA-interactome capture studies that did not capture ATOSA. The two paralogs should not inherit each other's annotations.

Organism-specific difference. Fly Atossa carries its HORMA-matching region at residues 313-517, and the experiments were done in a migrating embryonic macrophage. Human ATOSA is broadly expressed (GTEx median TPM 2.8 to 24.9 across 54 tissues). A program described in one specialised fly cell type need not be the human protein's core function.

Database carry-over is the most likely source of error in the existing record. The chain runs: one fly paper, then mouse ECO:0000269 on that paper, then human ECO:0000250 from mouse. Each step is legitimate on its own, and the end result is a human entry whose central functional sentence has never been tested in a human cell.

Experimental artifact. Yeast two-hybrid is the only interaction method behind every ATOSA partner. Its known false-positive profile, and the presence of a keratin and a MAGE antigen in the list, warrant caution before any of those partners is used mechanistically.

Isoform caution. The Foldseek search returned AF-Q32MH5-2 and AF-Q32MH5-3 isoform models that both retain the 1-230 module at 97.8-100% identity, so the HORMA assignment is not isoform-specific.

Limitations and knowledge gaps

Fold is not function. TM-score 0.62-0.67 at 7.8-10.7% identity assigns ATOSA 1-230 to the HORMA fold with confidence, but a HORMA domain can in principle lose closure-motif binding. No biochemistry exists for this module. What would resolve it: recombinant ATOSA 1-230 with a peptide-binding assay.

The interaction search is incomplete. IntAct and STRING were queried and both returned only Y2H and text mining. BioGRID returned HTTP 401 without a registered access key, and the BioPlex API endpoint returned HTTP 404, so neither was queried. This matters because BioPlex is exactly the resource that would hold an AP-MS result for a nuclear protein. What would resolve it: a registered BioGRID query and a BioPlex lookup, then a new AP-MS or BioID experiment if both are empty.

No chromatin occupancy data exists. No ChIP-seq, CUT&RUN or DamID dataset for ATOSA or FAM214A was found; the ReMap API returned HTTP 401/404 and was not queried. This matters because a transcription BP term, even without a DNA-binding MF, would need occupancy or a direct transcriptional readout. What would resolve it: CUT&RUN in a cell line with endogenous tagging.

No human perturbation data. No ATOSA knockdown RNA-seq in a human cell line was located, so whether the fly transcriptional program transfers is unknown. What would resolve it: knockdown plus RNA-seq, reading out DDX47 specifically.

Bulk expression cannot see a cell-type-restricted relationship. The GTEx analysis uses median TPM over whole tissues, and DDX47 sits at the quantification floor there. A relationship confined to macrophages or to a stress state would be invisible. What would resolve it: re-testing the correlation in a single-cell monocyte or macrophage atlas.

Localisation evidence is antibody-based. The nucleoplasm versus nucleoli split between ATOSA and DDX47 comes from HPA immunofluorescence, which is suggestive and not definitive. What would resolve it: tagged-protein imaging with fibrillarin co-staining.

Proposed follow-up experiments and actions

  1. Closure-motif pull-down. Purify ATOSA 1-230, test binding against a peptide library, and ask whether TRIP13 with p31comet disassembles any complex formed; PMID: 28659378 supplies the assay and PMID: 37794593 shows that closure-motif consensus varies across the family, so a library rather than a single candidate is the right design. A positive result converts "protein binding" into a mechanistically specific MF. A negative result means the fold is vestigial and the structural argument weakens to fold assignment alone.
  2. AP-MS or BioID on endogenously tagged ATOSA, nuclear fraction, in a human cell line. This directly asks whether DDX47 or any DEAD-box protein is a partner, and it is the single experiment that settles the seed hypothesis.
  3. ATOSA knockdown plus RNA-seq in human cells. Read out DDX47 and the human orthologs of the fly metabolic targets, AASS (LKR/SDH) and GRHPR (GR/HPR). This is a directed test of whether the fly program transfers.
  4. CUT&RUN for tagged ATOSA. Distinguishes direct chromatin association from an indirect transcriptional effect. Given the absence of any DNA-binding fold, a negative result is expected and would close the over-annotation question.
  5. Separation-of-function rescue in fly. Test whether mouse Atosa lacking the HORMA module (the equivalent of residues 1-220) still rescues the fly mutant. This connects the conserved fold to the established complementation without re-deriving it.
  6. Single-cell or stimulus-resolved co-expression. Re-test the ATOSA-DDX47 correlation in a macrophage or monocyte single-cell atlas, which is the cell type the fly work points to and the one bulk GTEx cannot resolve.
