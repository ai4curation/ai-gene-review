ASNSD1: catalytic competence of the GATase and synthetase sites

Hypothesis slug: kgap-asnsd1-gat-and-synthetase-sites Source file: genes/human/ASNSD1/ASNSD1-ai-review.yaml (source selector: free-text, focus type: free_text) Target: human ASNSD1, UniProt Q9NWL6, Homo sapiens (NCBITaxon:9606) Verdict: Partially supported, with the specific catalytic-competence claim refuted.

Summary

Human ASNSD1 keeps almost all of the asparagine synthetase apparatus and loses exactly one residue, and that one residue is the one with direct experimental evidence of catalytic necessity. The seed hypothesis asked whether ASNSD1 "even retains both domains and an N-terminal Cys after initiator processing". It does. Q9NWL6 begins MCGICCSV, which places a cysteine at position 2 and therefore exposes a free N-terminal Cys1 after methionine aminopeptidase removes the initiator, the arrangement that defines a class-II (purF-type, Ntn-hydrolase) glutamine amidotransferase. The AlphaFold model AF-Q9NWL6-F1 superposes on the substrate-bound E. coli asparagine synthetase B structure 1CT9 at 1.74 Å RMSD over 110 Cα for the GATase domain and 1.38-1.56 Å over 90-225 Cα for the synthetase domain, with mean pLDDT of 93.0 and 86.3 for the two domains. The glutamine-positioning side chains named by Larsen et al. (PMID: 10587437) are all present as Arg57, Asn76, Glu78 and Asp91, each conserved at 99-100% across vertebrate orthologs, and the N-type ATP pyrophosphatase P-loop is intact as ILFSGGIDSMVI at residues 324-335.

The failure is at a single position. E. coli AS-B Glu348, which Meyer et al. showed by mutagenesis, isotope transfer and positional isotope exchange is required for β-aspartyl-AMP formation and therefore for asparagine production (PMID: 20853825), maps to Ile494 of human ASNSD1 by two independent routes: pairwise sequence alignment and Cα superposition with ligand transplantation. The local contexts are unmistakable, human ASNS 361-369 = IFSGEGSDE with Glu365, human ASNSD1 490-498 = VLTGIGADE with Ile494. Scoring this position across UniProt gives 0 acidic residues in 429 resolvable vertebrate ASNSD1 orthologs against 376 of 376 Glu in vertebrate ASNS, and on an all-taxa scan 0 of 1731 members of the ASNSD1 family (InterPro IPR051857) are acidic against 1817 of 1869 (97.2%) in the glutamine-hydrolysing asparagine synthetase family (IPR006426). The zero holds in eleven eukaryotic groups including fungi, plants, apicomplexans, nematodes and arthropods, so the substitution predates vertebrates and defines the family. A three-structure acidic-shell census rules out the obvious alternative that a neighbouring carboxylate took over the job: the acidic neighbourhood is identical, residue for residue and distance for distance, in ASNSD1, human ASNS and AS-B, so nothing new has appeared.

The third iteration changed what a curator should actually do. A QuickGO audit of Q9NWL6 shows no asparagine synthase annotation exists: the gene carries three ND root annotations (GO:0003674, GO:0005575, GO:0008150, BHF-UCL, GO_REF:0000015) plus GO:0005515 protein binding from IntAct. GO:0004066 is absent, and the PANTHER split (ASNSD1 in PTHR45937, ASNS in PTHR11772 subfamily PTN000206334) already blocks the IBA propagation route. The recommendation is therefore to withhold asparagine synthase terms with this evidence on file, not to remove them. Characterising the one informative annotation produced a second result: all 22 recorded IntAct partners are PDZ-domain proteins carrying 98 PDZ domains between them, and the human C-terminus LENLSIEKETKL is a canonical class I PDZ-binding motif that human ASNS (ending RTLTHYKSAVKA) does not have. That supports GO:0030165 PDZ domain binding as a specific replacement for "protein binding", with two qualifiers that cannot be dropped: 21 of 22 partners come from in vitro peptide assays, and the motif is primate-restricted, present in 12 of 437 vertebrate orthologs.

Executive judgment

Partially supported. The seed hypothesis is a compound statement and its parts resolve differently.

The most important caveat is what the analysis does not establish. Showing that ASNSD1 cannot make asparagine does not show that it is an inactive protein. Its glutaminase pocket and nucleotide P-loop are under strong purifying selection across the family, which is consistent with a retained but different molecular activity. No glutaminase assay, no nucleotide-binding measurement and no acceptor-substrate identification exists for ASNSD1 in the literature retrieved. A second caveat is the tunnel test, which is suggestive and not decisive, because an AlphaFold model has no bound substrate, tunnels in this family are conformationally gated, and the AS-B positive control itself closes at a 2.8 Å probe clearance.

Key findings

The domains and the N-terminal cysteine are both retained

Human ASNSD1 (643 aa) begins MCGICCSV. Human ASNS (P08243) begins MCGIWAL and E. coli AsnB (P22106) begins MCSIFGV. The Met-Cys start is the arrangement that exposes a Cys1 nucleophile after initiator-Met excision, because methionine aminopeptidase requires a small P1' residue and cysteine qualifies. Across 442 unique vertebrate species with a UniProt ASNSD1 entry, 438 (99.1%) begin Met-Cys, against 354 of 382 (92.7%) for ASNS. InterPro and CDD assign ASNSD1 a glutamine amidotransferase type-2 domain (IPR017932, PS51278) at residues 2-184 and a Rossmann-like asparagine-synthase C-terminal domain (cd01991) at 320-608. Domain-wise iterative Cα superposition of AF-Q9NWL6-F1 v6 onto 1CT9 chain A gives 1.74 Å RMSD over 110 Cα for the GATase domain and 1.38-1.56 Å over 90-225 Cα for the synthetase domain. Mean pLDDT is 85.7 overall, 93.0 for residues 1-190 and 86.3 for 320-608. UniProt independently annotates Q9NWL6 Cys2 as "Active site: For GATase activity".

Hughes et al. established that the catalytic feature in question is a free N-terminal cysteine: "In common with other members of the purF family of amidotransferases, the recombinant enzyme possesses an NH2-terminal cysteine residue" (PMID: 7907328). The crystallographic work on AS-B used a Cys1Ala mutant precisely because residue 1 of the mature enzyme is the nucleophile: "Protein employed for this study was that of a site-directed mutant protein, Cys1Ala" (PMID: 10587437). Any argument that ASNSD1 is a non-enzyme therefore cannot rest on gross domain loss or on the absence of the Ntn nucleophile.

The synthetase-site carboxylate is absent in every vertebrate ortholog tested

E. coli AS-B Glu348 (UniProt P22106 position 349, annotated by UniProt as "Site: Important for beta-aspartyl-AMP intermediate formation") maps to Ile494 of human ASNSD1. Two independent routes agree. Pairwise BLOSUM62 affine-gap Needleman-Wunsch alignment gives the same answer whether run directly from AsnB to ASNSD1 or chained through human ASNS. Iterative Cα superposition of AF-Q9NWL6-F1 onto 1CT9 chain A over the synthetase domain (1.38 Å RMSD over 90 core Cα) transplants the AMP ligand into contact with ASNSD1 L325, F326, S327, D331, L352, N353, V354, D405, L491, T492, G493, I494, D497, Y503, N533, K601 and M604.

Flank-anchored residue calling across UniProt vertebrate orthologs resolved this position in 429 ASNSD1 species and 376 ASNS species.

Positive controls scored in the same ASNS ortholog set behaved as expected: Arg49 300/300, Asn75 348/348, Gly76 87/87, Glu77 81/81, Leu256 367/368, Ser363 375/375, Gly364 375/375. The method is therefore not systematically miscalling positions.

The functional weight of this position comes from direct mutagenesis. Meyer et al. report: "We find that formation of the β-aspartyl-AMP intermediate, and therefore the eventual production of asparagine, is dependent on the presence of a carboxylate side chain at this position in the synthetase active site" (PMID: 20853825). The same paper notes a second consequence that bears on the tunnel result: "Glu-348 may also play a role in mediating the conformational changes needed to (i) coordinate, albeit weakly, the glutaminase and synthetase activities of the enzyme and (ii) establish the structural integrity of the intramolecular tunnel along which ammonia is translocated."

The glutaminase pocket and the ATP P-loop are intact, so the loss is specific

Transplanting the 1CT9 glutamine ligand into the ASNSD1 frame places 16 ASNSD1 residues within 4.5 Å: M1, C2, V53, L54, H55, L56, R57, Q63, W75, N76, G77, E78, N90, D91, T92 and E547. Larsen et al. name the glutamine-positioning side chains of AS-B: "Key side chains employed for positioning the glutamine substrate within the binding pocket include Arg 49, Asn 74, Glu 76, and Asp 98" (PMID: 10587437). All four chemistries are present in the ASNSD1 pocket as Arg57, Asn76, Glu78 and Asp91, shifted by a few sequence positions but within the pocket in three dimensions. Conservation across the resolved vertebrate orthologs is near-total: R57 99.0% of 99 species, Q63 100% of 85, W75 100% of 79, N76 100% of 82, G77 100% of 134, E78 100% of 76, D91 100% of 73.

The synthetase P-loop is equally intact. ASNSD1 324-335 is ILFSGGIDSMVI, the canonical N-type ATP pyrophosphatase SGGLDS motif with one conservative Leu to Ile change, against AsnB 231-242 GVLLSGGLDSSI and ASNS 254-265 GCLLSGGLDSSL. S327, G328, G329, D331 and S332 are each 100% conserved across 313-398 vertebrate species, and I330 is hydrophobic in 100% (Ile 87.6%, Leu and Val the remainder). The AMP anchors are retained or conservatively changed. Larsen et al. describe them: "The AMP moiety is anchored to the protein via hydrogen bonds with O(gamma) of Ser 346 and the backbone carbonyl and amide groups of Val 272, Leu 232, and Gly 347." AS-B Leu232 becomes ASNSD1 Leu325 (100% of 80 species), Ser346 becomes Thr492 (93.0% of 384, a conservative hydroxyl-retaining change), Gly347 becomes Gly493 (100% of 429). Besides Glu348 to Ile494, the only other changed position is Asp384 to Asn533, which is Asn in 100% of 439 species.

ASNSD1 is therefore not a generically decayed pseudoenzyme. Its glutaminase pocket and nucleotide P-loop are under strong purifying selection. What has been lost, cleanly and lineage-wide, is the one residue required to commit the bound nucleotide to acyl-adenylate chemistry on aspartate.

The ammonia tunnel is not recovered at the stringency that recovers it in AS-B

A grid-based buried-cavity connectivity test (0.6 Å grid, 26-ray burial criterion at 0.75, breadth-first search between the glutamine amide carbon and the AMP α-phosphate) was calibrated on 1CT9 chain A, where Larsen et al. report a tunnel: "As observed for other amidotransferases, the two active sites are connected by a tunnel lined primarily with backbone atoms and hydrophobic and nonpolar amino acid residues" (PMID: 10587437).

The control connects at 2.4, 2.0, 1.8 and 1.6 Å and closes at 2.8 Å, which is the behaviour expected of a narrow gated tunnel. The two-domain geometry of ASNSD1 is preserved (Cys SG to AMP centroid 24.6 Å, against 22.9 Å in AS-B), so the two sites are in the right relative positions, but no comparably direct buried channel is recovered. Treat this as consistent with loss of coupled glutamine-dependent amidation, not as proof of it.

The two families separated before the last eukaryotic common ancestor and ASNSD1 has never, in any sampled lineage, carried the residue required for β-aspartyl-AMP formation. An asparagine synthase annotation on ASNSD1 or on any of its orthologs would be a family-level error.

No compensating carboxylate has appeared

The main structural alternative is that ASNSD1 recruited a different acidic side chain to the same chemical job. A census of acidic side-chain oxygens (Asp OD1/OD2, Glu OE1/OE2) around the Cβ of the target residue, excluding the residue itself, rules this out.

The counts and the neighbour identities match position for position in all three structures. The neighbouring acidic residues are present in the active enzymes too, so they already have their own roles and are not available as substitutes. In the 1CT9 crystal the Glu348 carboxylate oxygen sits 4.94 Å from the AMP phosphorus, which is the geometry needed to activate the aspartate β-carboxylate. The deficit in ASNSD1 is confined to that one side chain, and nothing new has appeared anywhere in its shell.

Inspection of individual entries shows the absence is real and not a truncated gene model. Caenorhabditis elegans Q21555 begins MGIDYNRWWHLKDETLGEELKG, with no cysteine in the first 40 residues and no internal Met-Cys in the first 60. Necator americanus A0ABR1D8J3 begins MDDMNLRTIANGMNTEPDNLKG. Theileria equi L0AYT6 begins MTAPDAITLTIEYRQGLEDADA. One entry, Cylicostephanus goldi A0A3P6RN75, begins MSHFSDENVAIAFSGGVDSLLV, placing the PP-loop at the very N-terminus and lacking the amidotransferase domain entirely.

This qualifies the one remaining positive structural claim. The glutaminase nucleophile is under selection in the lineages that matter for the human annotation and is dispensable in others. A glutamine amidotransferase molecular function for human ASNSD1 should stay a flagged candidate pending assay, and must not be propagated across the family by orthology alone.

Loss-of-function phenotypes separate ASNSD1 from ASNS

Asnsd1-null mice show a muscle phenotype. Blake et al. report: "Mice with an inactivating mutation in the gene encoding asparagine synthetase domain containing 1 (ASNSD1) develop a progressive degenerative myopathy that results in severe sarcopenia and myosteatosis" (PMID: 32638637). Normalised body fat percentage was more than 2 SD above the mean of 3651 chow-fed knockout lines, histologic lesions were essentially limited to muscle, and the same paper states plainly that "ASNSD1 is conserved across many species, and whole body gene expression surveys show maximal expression levels of ASNSD1 in skeletal muscle. However, potential functions of this protein have not been previously reported." Muscle weakness in this line was also detected by the inverted screen test in a separate screening report (PMID: 34269122), and the high-body-fat phenotype was supported by additional internal cohorts in the Lexicon Genome5000 screen of 3758 knockout lines (PMID: 34483672).

Human ASNS loss of function produces an unrelated phenotype: "Asparagine synthetase deficiency (ASNSD) is a rare metabolic disease causing congenital microcephaly, severe developmental delay, and spastic quadriplegia. Although the central nervous system is severely affected, other organ systems appear unaffected by asparagine deficiency" (PMID: 42741518). A review of 29 ASNS-deficient cases reaches the same clinical picture of microcephaly and epileptic encephalopathy with high mortality (PMID: 30978478).

If ASNSD1 were a second glutamine-dependent asparagine synthetase, its loss would be expected to produce a phenotype overlapping asparagine depletion. The brain, which is the organ that decompensates when ASNS fails, is spared in Asnsd1-null mice, and muscle, which is where Asnsd1 loss causes disease, is not reported as affected in ASNS deficiency. Orthology resources agree with the split: ASNSD1 is in PANTHER PTHR45937 and InterPro IPR051857, ASNS and AsnB are in PTHR11772, IPR050795 and IPR006426, and CDD assigns the ASNSD1 N-terminal domain to cd03766 "Gn_AT_II_novel" rather than cd00712 "AsnB". UniProt Q9NWL6 carries no CATALYTIC ACTIVITY statement, while P08243 and P22106 each carry experimentally supported ones.

The three ND root annotations mean a curator has formally recorded that no function, process or location is known for ASNSD1. The structural result in this investigation does not correct an existing over-annotation. It supplies the evidence for keeping asparagine synthase terms off the gene when name-based or Pfam-based pipelines propose them, and it explains why the PANTHER-driven IBA route never carried GO:0004066 across. A recommendation phrased as "remove GO:0004066" would be inaccurate for Q9NWL6 as it stands.

The one informative annotation is a primate-restricted PDZ ligand

All 22 interaction partners behind the GO:0005515 lines on Q9NWL6 contain PDZ domains: GRID2IP (2 domains), MPDZ (13), DLG4 (3), APBA1 (2), TJP1 (3), DLG1 (3), SNTB1 (1), IL16 (4), SCRIB (4), PDZK1 (4), FRMPD2 (3), MAGI2 (6), NHERF4 (4), LNX2 (4), PATJ (10), LNX1 (4), DLG3 (3), MAGI1 (6), APBA2 (2), GRIP2 (7), WHRN (3), GRIP1 (7). That is 98 PDZ domains across 22 of 22 proteins. Human ASNSD1 ends LENLSIEKETKL, with Thr at position -2 and Leu at position 0, a canonical class I PDZ-binding motif. Human ASNS ends RTLTHYKSAVKA and has no such motif.

A conservation test across 437 full-length-like vertebrate ASNSD1 orthologs found only 12 (2.7%) ending in a class I motif and only 10 ending in ETKL. "Protein binding" has a specific chemical explanation here and can be made specific. Two limits hold it in place. The evidence is dominated by one in vitro assay on isolated C-terminal peptides, which measures motif-level binding capacity rather than a complex formed in a cell, with PDZK1 the single cell-based corroboration. And the motif is primate-restricted, so it cannot account for the mouse Asnsd1 knockout myopathy and must not be propagated to orthologs.

Mechanistic model

The reaction that ASNS and AS-B perform has two half-reactions joined by a tunnel. ASNSD1 keeps the first half and the nucleotide-binding apparatus of the second, and loses the single residue that commits the bound ATP to acyl-adenylate chemistry on aspartate.

In ASNSD1 the same diagram is interrupted at one arrow.

The chain of inference runs like this. The two-domain architecture and the Cys1 nucleophile are present, so ASNSD1 is a class-II amidotransferase by structure. The glutamine pocket and the ATP P-loop are under purifying selection at 99-100%, so the protein plausibly still binds glutamine and a nucleotide. The committed-step carboxylate is absent across 1731 family members in eleven eukaryotic groups, with no compensating acidic residue anywhere in its shell, so the aspartate-activating step cannot proceed. The tunnel is not recovered at a stringency that recovers the AS-B control, which matches the coupling loss Meyer et al. predicted for Glu348 substitutions. The phenotypes agree: Asnsd1-null mice get myopathy and myosteatosis, ASNS-deficient humans get microcephaly and encephalopathy, and the two sets of lesions do not overlap.

Separate from the catalysis question, the human protein ends in a class I PDZ-binding motif that human ASNS lacks, and every recorded interaction partner is a PDZ-domain protein. That motif arose in primates, so it is a property of the human protein and not of the family, and it is a distinct observation from the mouse muscle phenotype.

Mechanistic scope

The immediate molecular question is whether one polypeptide can run two coupled half-reactions: hydrolysis of glutamine at an N-terminal cysteine nucleophile, and ATP-dependent activation of the aspartate β-carboxylate followed by amidation. That is the direct gene-product activity under test, and it is tested here by residue-level structural and evolutionary evidence rather than by assay, because no assay of ASNSD1 exists in the retrieved literature.

Everything else sits further out. The Asnsd1-null myopathy and myosteatosis are an organism-level phenotype in mouse, several steps removed from any molecular activity, and the originating paper states no function had previously been reported for the protein. The high body fat is a correlate of the muscle-to-adipose transdifferentiation in the same animals. The association of ASNSD1 with glutamine-metabolism gene panels in Alzheimer's disease (PMID: 39875978), diabetic foot ulcer (PMID: 38287255), nonspecific orbital inflammation (PMID: 38233749) and hepatocellular carcinoma (PMID: 36388696) comes from expression-based biomarker modelling in which the gene was placed in the candidate panel by its name and family assignment. None of that is evidence of a molecular function, and those panels carry the same family-level assumption this analysis refutes. Two homozygous stop-gain variants in ASNSD1 in a severe-obesity cohort (PMID: 35061034) are human genetic observations consistent with the mouse body-fat phenotype, again without a molecular mechanism. The hemizygous 2q32 deletion that removes ASNSD1 along with 21 other genes (PMID: 20648054) assigns the clinical phenotype to COL3A1, COL5A2 and MSTN, not to ASNSD1.

The PDZ interactions are direct gene-product activity, measured on the protein's own C-terminal peptide. For 21 of 22 partners they are a binding capacity measured outside a cell, and they belong to the primate protein specifically.

Conflicts and alternatives

Compensation by a neighbouring carboxylate. Tested and rejected. The acidic shell around the target position is identical in ASNSD1, human ASNS and AS-B: one acidic residue within 6 Å, four within 8 Å, four within 10 Å, with matched neighbour identities (ASNSD1 E498/D497/D537/D331, ASNS E369/D368/D401/D262, AS-B E352/D351/D384/D238). Those neighbours exist in the working enzymes and already have roles there.

A different acceptor substrate. Open. The glutaminase pocket and the PP-loop are conserved at 99-100%, which is hard to explain if the protein does nothing. ASNSD1 could amidate something other than aspartate, in which case the aspartate-specific Glu would be expected to be lost exactly as observed. Nothing in this analysis identifies the acceptor.

Paralog confusion from the gene name. The symbol "asparagine synthetase domain containing 1" and the shared Pfam hit invite transfer of ASNS annotations. InterPro, PANTHER and CDD all separate the two: ASNSD1 is PTHR45937 / IPR051857 / cd03766 "Gn_AT_II_novel", ASNS and AsnB are PTHR11772 / IPR050795 / IPR006426 / cd00712 "AsnB". The family-level scan shows this separation is chemically real.

Database carry-over from ASDURF. The ASNSD1 transcript is bicistronic. ASDURF, the uORF product in the 5'UTR, is the 12th PAQosome subunit (PMID: 31738558), and the medulloblastoma CRISPR screen found that these uORFs "exhibited selective functionality independent of main coding sequences" (PMID: 38176414). A review covering the same arrangement in three mammalian bicistronic transcripts is available as orientation (PMID: 40058972). Chaperone and PAQosome annotations belong to ASDURF, and any that appear on Q9NWL6 should be checked for provenance. A hepatic fibrosis study reports that "ASDURF regulates the expression of asparagine synthetase domain-containing 1 (ASNSD1)" (PMID: 38016755), which is a regulatory relationship between the two products and not a shared function.

Organism-specific differences in the PDZ lead. The ETKL motif is present in human, chimpanzee and both macaques and absent from mouse (GRLQILP), rat, cow, chicken, zebrafish and Xenopus. The mouse myopathy therefore cannot be a consequence of PDZ binding through this motif. The two observations describe different species and different parts of the protein.

Lineage-specific loss of the Cys nucleophile. Nematodes (2/56 Met-Cys) and apicomplexans (0/23) have lost it outright, verified on individual sequences. The family persists without the glutaminase nucleophile, which limits how strongly a GATase-type function can be asserted even for human ASNSD1.

The tunnel result as an artifact. The negative tunnel finding rests on a static predicted structure with no ligand. The AS-B control itself closes at 2.8 Å clearance, so the method is sensitive to probe radius. For that reason the tunnel is reported as suggestive and is not used to carry the conclusion.

Limitations and knowledge gaps

No direct assay of ASNSD1 exists. Every statement about its catalytic competence is a structural and evolutionary prediction. The mouse knockout paper states plainly that "potential functions of this protein have not been previously reported". A glutaminase assay on recombinant ASNSD1 would settle the one remaining positive claim in either direction.

No experimental ASNSD1 structure. All structural work used AF-Q9NWL6-F1 v6. Per-domain pLDDT of 93.0 and 86.3 is high enough for backbone and side-chain placement in the domain cores, but loop conformations, tunnel geometry and any substrate-induced closure are not represented. This matters most for the tunnel result and, to a lesser degree, for the acidic-shell distances.

The acceptor substrate is unknown. The glutamine pocket and P-loop are conserved at 99-100% across the family, which argues against complete inactivity, yet nothing identifies what the protein acts on. This decides whether GO:0016810 or a more specific amidotransferase term becomes assignable, or whether the protein is a nucleotide-binding scaffold.

Family scans were capped. The UniProt searches returned 2000 entries per family, of which 1731 and 1869 were resolvable by the anchor. About 10% were unresolved because the motif matched zero or more than one site. These are large samples, not complete families, so "0 of 1731" is a sample statement.

The tunnel calibration file was not written. The clearance-sweep run exceeded the executor time limit before saving its output file. The numbers are reported from captured console output, and this is stated in the evidence matrix rather than papered over.

Two abstracts could not be retrieved. PubMed search did not return abstracts for 36115835 or 33961781 in this run. Their titles were confirmed through NCBI esummary. The claims attributed to them (holdup assay, BioPlex AP-MS) come from the IntAct annotation records, so the assay types are database-level support and not verified abstract quotes. A curator should read both papers before using GO:0030165.

Whether human ASNSD1 is processed by methionine aminopeptidase in vivo is untested. The Met-Cys start permits it and UniProt annotates Cys2 as an active site, but no N-terminomics data for ASNSD1 was retrieved. The GATase candidate term depends on a free α-amino group at Cys1, which PMID: 8663035 shows acts as the proton acceptor and donor in this enzyme class.

ASNSD1 subcellular localisation is unrecorded. GO:0005575 is an ND root annotation. Binding to membrane-associated PDZ scaffolds such as MPDZ, PATJ, SCRIB and TJP1 implies a localisation that no CC term currently captures.

Arg325, Thr322 and Thr323 of AS-B were not individually scored. PMID: 9139684 shows these are required for β-aspartyl-AMP formation. Scoring them in ASNSD1 would add independent confirmation or, if they are retained, would sharpen the claim that the loss is confined to one residue.

The GO annotation state is a snapshot. The QuickGO audit reflects the database at the time of the run. The withhold-rather-than-remove recommendation would change if an automated pipeline adds GO:0004066 later.

Proposed follow-up experiments and actions

Gain-of-function reversion. Make the ASNSD1 I494E substitution and test for asparagine synthetase activity, with the reciprocal ASNS E365I as the negative control. If I494E does not restore activity, other positions have also degraded; if it restores partial activity, the single-residue model is confirmed directly. This is the most efficient single experiment for the central claim.

Glutaminase assay on recombinant human ASNSD1. Express Q9NWL6 in a system that leaves the N-terminus free, confirm initiator-Met removal by intact-mass or N-terminal sequencing, and measure glutamate release from glutamine. A Cys2Ala mutant is the control. This distinguishes "retained GATase activity with an unknown acceptor" from "fully degenerate pseudoenzyme" and decides GO:0016810.

Nucleotide binding. Measure ATP or AMP binding by differential scanning fluorimetry or ITC, with a P-loop mutant (S327A or D331A) as control, to test whether the 100%-conserved PP-loop is functional.

Metabolite measurement in the mouse model. Measure asparagine, aspartate, glutamine and glutamate in Asnsd1-null muscle. If the myopathy involved asparagine synthesis, asparagine would be depleted locally. The prediction from this analysis is that it will not be.

N-terminomics of human skeletal muscle. Confirm whether ASNSD1 Cys1 is exposed in vivo, by COFRADIC or TAILS in the tissue of maximal expression.

Cell-based test of the PDZ interactions. Co-immunoprecipitate full-length ASNSD1 with MPDZ, DLG1 or PATJ from a primate cell line, and repeat with a C-terminal truncation that removes ETKL. Only PDZK1 has cell-based support at present, and this would move GO:0030165 from peptide-level to complex-level evidence.

Score the Arg325, Thr322 and Thr323 equivalents across the ASNSD1 family. A direct extension of the existing scan that tests whether the degradation is confined to one residue or extends across the aspartate-binding segment.

Interaction screen on the mouse protein. Mouse Asnsd1 ends GRLQILP with no PDZ motif. Identifying the mouse interactome would show what the protein does in the species where the knockout phenotype exists.
