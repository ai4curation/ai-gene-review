Summary

Executive verdict: PARTIALLY SUPPORTED. The curation-relevant core of the seed hypothesis is correct and strongly supported by primary evidence, but the specific mechanism the hypothesis proposes for the historical Xenopus oocyte observations is refuted (for the named XenU1 candidate) or unsupported (for a hypothetical "XenNR2B").

The curation-critical claim — that GluN1/NR1 (GRIN1, UniProt Q05586) does not bind glutamate and does not by itself form a functional glutamate-gated channel — is well established by three independent and complementary lines of primary evidence: (1) direct ligand binding, where glutamate binding to the isolated NR1 S1S2 ligand-binding core is "not detectable even at a 300 mM concentration" (PMID: 16641235); (2) two X-ray crystal structures that assign the glycine/D-serine site to NR1 (PMID: 12805203) and the glutamate site to NR2A (PMID: 16281028); and (3) clean mammalian (HEK293) heterologous expression, which lacks endogenous NMDAR subunits, where NR1 expressed alone is electrically silent unless an NR2 subunit is co-expressed (PMID: 16214956; PMID: 17105944; PMID: 11809857). This directly implies that the GO molecular-function annotation glutamate binding (GO:0016595) is an over-annotation for GluN1; the appropriate molecular function for GluN1 is glycine binding (GO:0016594).

The mechanistic sub-claim — that the small glutamate/NMDA-activated, glycine-dependent currents historically recorded from Xenopus laevis oocytes injected with NR1 cRNA alone (e.g., the human NR1 reports PMID: 7685113 and PMID: 7679115) arise from heteromers between exogenous NR1 and an endogenous Xenopus GluN2-type subunit (named in the seed as "XenU1 / XenNR2B") — does not survive scrutiny. The proposed partner XenU1 was experimentally excluded by two independent primary studies (PMID: 11809857; PMID: 16214956), and XenU1 is in any case a kainate-binding protein, not a GluN2-type subunit. A targeted literature search returned no primary identification of an endogenous Xenopus oocyte "XenNR2B" GluN2 subunit that pairs with exogenous NR1. Thus, although NR1-alone currents are almost certainly not from homomeric GluN1 channels (consistent with the direct and structural data above), the true origin of those oocyte currents remains unresolved, and the seed's named mechanism should not be adopted as fact.

For a curator, the bottom line is clean and actionable: remove / do not assign GO:0016595 (glutamate binding) to GluN1, assign/retain GO:0016594 (glycine binding), and treat the "endogenous Xenopus GluN2/XenNR2B partner" narrative as an unverified hypothesis rather than a citable mechanism.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — GluN1 (NR1) does not bind glutamate: direct biochemistry plus clean HEK293 functional assays

The most decisive evidence is direct and does not depend on the ambiguous oocyte system. Using a soluble, isolated NR1 S1S2 ligand-binding domain, glutamate binding to NR1 was "not detectable even at a 300 mM concentration" (PMID: 16641235), an extraordinarily high test concentration that effectively rules out any physiologically meaningful glutamate affinity. In the same body of work, glutamate binds the NR2 subunit and, weakly, the regulatory NR3A subunit — but not NR1.

This biochemical result is corroborated by functional reconstitution in HEK293 cells, a mammalian line that lacks endogenous NMDAR subunits and therefore provides a far cleaner test system than Xenopus oocytes. In HEK293, NR1 expressed alone does not produce a glutamate-gated response: "In HEK293 cells, we did not get receptor response unless an NR2 subunit was coexpressed" (PMID: 16214956). A second independent study states the point directly — "NR1 alone, which does not form functional channels" (PMID: 17105944). The field consensus architecture — a glycine-binding NR1 paired with a glutamate-binding NR2 — is reiterated across multiple recombinant studies (PMID: 17962328; PMID: 20407581).

Curation consequence: glutamate binding is a property of GluN2, not GluN1. GO:0016595 on GluN1 is unsupported by direct evidence.

Finding 2 — The specific XenU1 explanation for NR1-alone oocyte currents is refuted; the endogenous partner's identity is unresolved

The seed hypothesis names XenU1 (and a hypothetical "XenNR2B") as the endogenous Xenopus partner. Two independent primary studies directly tested, and rejected, the XenU1 hypothesis. Green et al. (2002) isolated XenU1 and XenU1a cDNAs and found that co-injection of XenU1 with NR1 did not increase oocyte currents and did not form functional channels in HEK293, with no pharmacological or biochemical evidence of association — concluding that "XenU1 does not associate with the NR1 subunit and that an alternative explanation must be sought for the channels observed when NR1 is expressed alone in oocytes" (PMID: 11809857). Schmidt, Werner & Hollmann (2006) revisited the postulated "unitary glutamate receptor," cloned Xenopus NR1 variants, and showed that oocyte glutamate/glycine responses "were independent of coexpressed XenU1" (PMID: 16214956).

Critically, XenU1 is a kainate-binding protein, not a GluN2-type subunit, so even its name in the seed hypothesis conflates two distinct molecular entities. A targeted search for a primary report of an endogenous Xenopus oocyte GluN2-type subunit ("XenNR2B") that pairs with exogenous NR1 returned no primary identification. The seed's mechanistic claim is therefore refuted in its specific (XenU1) form and merely asserted — without primary support — in its generalized ("XenNR2B") form.

Finding 3 — Crystal structure: the GluN1 ligand-binding core forms the glycine/D-serine site, not a glutamate site

Furukawa & Gouaux (2003) solved co-crystal structures of the NR1 S1S2 ligand-binding core bound to glycine, D-serine, the partial agonist D-cycloserine, and the glycine-site antagonist 5,7-dichlorokynurenic acid. The bilobed "clamshell" domain closes on glycine-site agonists and opens with the antagonist, and the authors state that "NMDA receptors require both glycine and glutamate for activation with NR1 and NR2 forming glycine and glutamate sites, respectively" (PMID: 12805203). This structurally assigns the glycine site to NR1 and complements the direct binding data (Finding 1).

Finding 4 — The glutamate-binding site is structurally localized to GluN2 (NR2A), not GluN1

Furukawa, Singh, Mancusso & Gouaux (2005) solved the crystal structure of the NR2A ligand-binding core bound to glutamate and the NR1–NR2A heterodimer with glutamate plus glycine. The NR2A–glutamate complex "defines the determinants of glutamate and NMDA recognition," confirming the NR1–NR2A heterodimer as the functional ligand-binding unit (PMID: 16281028). This directly localizes glutamate binding (GO:0016595) to GluN2, completing the structural case: the glutamate site is physically on NR2, not NR1.

Why did NR1-alone oocyte currents ever appear (as in PMID: 7685113 and PMID: 7679115)? The seed's instinct is partially right: these currents are almost certainly not from homomeric GluN1 channels, because (a) the isolated NR1 domain cannot bind glutamate at all, and (b) in a clean mammalian system without endogenous subunits, NR1 alone is silent. The remaining question — what the exogenous NR1 assembles with in the oocyte — was the explicit subject of two follow-up studies, and the leading candidate (XenU1) was excluded. So the model is:

Mechanistic Scope

The immediate molecular function under test is ligand (glutamate) binding by the GluN1 gene product itself. The evidence cleanly separates:

  • Direct gene-product activity (GluN1): glycine/D-serine co-agonist binding (the NR1 S1S2 clamshell) and an obligatory structural/gating role in the heterotetramer. GluN1 does not directly bind glutamate.
  • Complex-level / downstream properties: glutamate-gated cation flux, Ca²⁺ permeability, Mg²⁺ block, and channel gating are emergent properties of the assembled GluN1/GluN2 heterotetramer, not of GluN1 alone. The oocyte "NR1-alone" currents are a downstream, system-dependent artifact of expressing NR1 in a cell that may supply an unidentified endogenous partner — not evidence of a GluN1 intrinsic glutamate-binding activity.

Attributing glutamate binding to GluN1 conflates a complex-level phenotype (the channel opens to glutamate) with a subunit-level molecular activity (which subunit's pocket the glutamate occupies). GO MF annotations should capture the latter.

Conflicts and Alternatives

  1. Historical primary data vs. modern biochemistry. The 1993 oocyte papers (PMID: 7685113, PMID: 7679115) genuinely recorded glutamate/NMDA-activated, glycine-dependent currents from NR1 cRNA alone and interpreted them as homomeric NR1 receptors. This is the principal conflicting evidence, but the interpretation is superseded: the currents are real, their homomeric attribution is not.
  2. Paralog/subunit confusion. The seed's "glutamate binding" belongs to the paralogous GluN2 subunits, not GluN1 — a classic subunit-level over-annotation.
  3. Entity confusion in the seed itself. "XenU1" (a kainate-binding protein) and "XenNR2B" (a hypothetical GluN2) are different molecular classes; the seed treats them as interchangeable candidates. Only XenU1 has been tested (and excluded); "XenNR2B" has no primary report.
  4. Organism-specific expression system. Xenopus oocytes are notorious for endogenous channels/partners; HEK293 (lacking endogenous NMDAR subunits) is the appropriate counter-system and shows NR1 silence — isolating the oocyte result as system-specific.
  5. Isoform qualifier (NR3 context). NR1/NR3 receptors are glutamate-independent, glycine-gated "excitatory glycine receptors" (PMID: 20407581) — reinforcing that GluN1's intrinsic ligand site is for glycine, not glutamate.

Conclusion
