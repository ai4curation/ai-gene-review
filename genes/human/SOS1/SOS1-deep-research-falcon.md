# Human SOS1 (UniProt Q07889): Functional Annotation and Research Report

## Executive summary

The requested target is unambiguous: **human SOS1**, UniProt **Q07889**, encoded by **SOS1** (approved name: *SOS Ras/Rac guanine nucleotide exchange factor 1*), is the 1,333-amino-acid **Son of sevenless homolog 1** protein. It is not fungal Cdc25, plant salt-overly-sensitive 1, or the human paralog SOS2. Its architecture—tandem N-terminal histone folds, Dbl-homology (DH), pleckstrin-homology (PH), RAS-exchanger motif (REM), CDC25-homology catalytic domain, and C-terminal proline-rich tail—matches both the supplied InterPro annotations and the human SOS1 literature. SOS2 is a separate, highly homologous paralog. (bhadhadhara2024studyingearlystructural pages 1-2, kessler2021targetingsonof pages 1-2, murthy2024insulinsignalingand pages 16-17)

SOS1’s primary, best-established function is to act as a **guanine-nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) for membrane-associated RAS proteins**. It catalyzes release of tightly bound GDP from RAS, stabilizes nucleotide-free RAS transiently, and thereby permits abundant cellular GTP to bind. SOS1 does **not** phosphorylate RAS and does not chemically convert GDP into GTP. A second RAS molecule—preferentially RAS–GTP—binds an allosteric site in SOS1’s REM domain, reinforces membrane residence, and promotes further exchange, generating positive feedback. A context-dependent RAC1-GEF function has also been reported for the DH–PH module in EPS8–ABI1/SOS1 complexes, but this branch is mechanistically less secure than the extensive structural and biochemical evidence for RAS exchange. (bandaru2019theinterdependentactivation pages 8-9, bandaru2019theinterdependentactivation pages 4-6, kessler2021targetingsonof pages 1-2)

## 2. Primary biochemical function and catalytic mechanism

### 2.1 Reaction

The operational reaction is:

**RAS–GDP + GTP ⇌ RAS–GTP + GDP**, catalyzed through a transient **nucleotide-free RAS·SOS1 complex**.

The CDC25-domain helical hairpin inserts between RAS Switch I and Switch II, opens the nucleotide-binding site, disrupts interactions that retain GDP/Mg²⁺, and stabilizes nucleotide-free RAS. Once RAS dissociates or nucleotide enters, intracellular GTP abundance biases reloading toward RAS–GTP. GEF catalysis is reversible in principle; SOS1 accelerates nucleotide exchange rather than supplying energy or determining the incoming nucleotide chemically. (bandaru2019theinterdependentactivation pages 4-6)

### 2.2 Substrate specificity

The strongest evidence supports direct action on classical RAS-family substrates, particularly KRAS in current biochemical and drug-discovery studies. The available evidence does not justify a universal quantitative preference ranking among HRAS, NRAS, and KRAS because apparent activity depends on membrane composition, RAS density, construct boundaries, and allosteric-site occupancy. Oncogenic RAS can still allosterically stimulate SOS1 and promote activation of wild-type RAS molecules, an important mechanism of pathway amplification. (kessler2021targetingsonof pages 1-2, bannoura2024targetingguaninenucleotide pages 3-4)

The DH–PH region has been assigned RAC1-GEF activity in an EPS8–ABI1/E3B1–SOS1 complex. Adaptor choice appears to partition function: GRB2-associated SOS1 favors receptor-to-RAS signaling, whereas ABI1/EPS8 complexes localize SOS1 to membrane ruffles and actin-remodeling sites. However, authoritative reviews note that direct RAC catalysis in vivo is less comprehensively established than CDC25-mediated RAS exchange and may include important scaffolding contributions. It should therefore be annotated as a **supported but context-dependent secondary function**, not as equivalent in certainty to RAS-GEF activity. (pierre2011understandingsos(son pages 5-8, baltanas2021sos2comesto pages 7-8, bandaru2019theinterdependentactivation pages 4-6)

## 3. Regulation and cellular localization

### 3.1 Cytosolic inactive pool and receptor-driven recruitment

In unstimulated cells, SOS1 is largely cytosolic and conformationally restrained. Following receptor tyrosine-kinase activation, GRB2’s SH2 domain recognizes receptor-associated phosphotyrosine sites while its SH3 domains bind PXXP motifs in the SOS1 C-terminal tail. This raises SOS1’s effective concentration at the **cytoplasmic face of the plasma membrane**, where prenylated RAS resides. SOS1 is therefore a soluble peripheral signaling protein rather than an integral membrane protein. (由樹2016asosin pages 6-13, pierre2011understandingsos(son pages 5-8)

GRB2 is not the entire localization mechanism. The PH and histone-fold regions interact with acidic lipids, including phosphatidic acid and phosphoinositides, while both catalytic- and allosteric-site RAS contacts reinforce membrane binding. Single-molecule/reconstituted-membrane experiments indicate that these individually moderate interactions can retain SOS at the membrane for minutes. Receptor internalization and endocytosis then contribute to removal and signal termination. (christensen2016onewaymembranetrafficking pages 14-16, pierre2011understandingsos(son pages 5-8)

### 3.2 Autoinhibition

The N-terminal histone/DH/PH module constrains the catalytic core. The DH domain sterically restricts access of RAS to the REM allosteric site, while the histone folds interact with the DH–PH/REM linker and stabilize the closed state. SOS1 constructs retaining these modules are less active than the isolated REM–CDC25 catalytic core. Lipid and protein interactions at the membrane shift this conformational ensemble toward productive states rather than acting as a simple binary switch. (由樹2016asosin pages 6-13, bandaru2019theinterdependentactivation pages 8-9)

### 3.3 RAS–GTP allosteric feedback

A second RAS molecule binds the REM allosteric site. RAS–GTP binds more productively than RAS–GDP, favors opening of the catalytic helical hairpin, and enhances SOS1 recruitment/retention near membrane RAS. This creates positive feedback: initial RAS–GTP promotes SOS1 activity, which produces additional RAS–GTP. Membrane-vesicle assays found approximately **500-fold greater exchange activity than solution measurements**, and one allosterically activated, membrane-bound SOS molecule can processively activate hundreds of RAS molecules; some reconstituted systems report thousands before SOS removal. (christensen2016onewaymembranetrafficking pages 14-16, bandaru2019theinterdependentactivation pages 8-9)

These findings refine the simplistic claim that allosteric RAS merely increases a fixed catalytic rate. Single-molecule results indicate that RAS–GTP can instead alter membrane recruitment and the occupancy or duration of highly active SOS states. Thus, the exact measured effect depends on whether an experiment averages over recruitment, conformational-state occupancy, or turnover by already membrane-bound molecules. (christensen2016onewaymembranetrafficking pages 14-16)

### 3.4 Negative feedback

ERK- and RSK-dependent phosphorylation of the C-terminal tail reduces productive GRB2/SOS1 recruitment and creates 14-3-3 docking sites, opposing the positive-feedback loop. Reported sites include ERK-responsive Ser1132/1167/1178/1193 and RSK-responsive Ser1134/1161, although individual-site importance varies by model. Endocytosis provides another termination mechanism. Combined positive and delayed negative feedback can generate thresholded, pulsatile, or oscillatory RAS–ERK responses rather than a simple sustained ON state. (由樹2016asosin pages 6-13, kolch2023dynamicregulationof pages 6-7)

## 4. Pathway position and biological processes

The precise receptor-proximal pathway is:

**activated RTK/adaptor complex → GRB2 recruitment of SOS1 → RAS GDP release/GTP loading → RAS–GTP effectors**.

The principal downstream branch is **RAF–MEK–ERK**, which controls context-dependent proliferation, differentiation, survival, and transcription. RAS–GTP can also engage PI3K–AKT and other effectors, but these are downstream consequences of RAS activation—not reactions catalyzed by SOS1—and contributions from SOS2 or other GEFs vary by cell type. (pierre2011understandingsos(son pages 23-33, baltanas2021sos2comesto pages 7-8)

In the proposed RAC branch, SOS1 assembles with EPS8 and ABI1/E3B1 at actin-rich membrane ruffles. RAC1–GTP then controls lamellipodia, actin polymerization, migration, and associated JNK signaling. These cellular phenotypes are downstream of nucleotide exchange and should not be described as intrinsic catalytic activities of SOS1. (pierre2011understandingsos(son pages 5-8, pierre2011understandingsos(son pages 23-33)

Subcellularly, SOS1’s best-supported site of productive RAS exchange is the **inner plasma-membrane surface**. RAS signaling can also occur on Golgi and endomembrane compartments, but the retrieved evidence does not establish a single constitutive SOS1 organelle residence; localization is stimulus-, adaptor-, lipid-, and trafficking-dependent. (christensen2016onewaymembranetrafficking pages 14-16, kolch2023dynamicregulationof pages 6-7)

## 5. Human disease relevance

### 5.1 Noonan syndrome and RASopathies

Heterozygous germline gain-of-function SOS1 variants cause a recognized form of **Noonan syndrome**, generally by weakening autoinhibition or increasing membrane/catalytic competence and thereby elevating RAS–MAPK signaling. Open Targets maps human SOS1 (ENSG00000115904) strongly to Noonan syndrome, with an association score of approximately **0.87** in the retrieved record. A 2023 cardiovascular review estimated that SOS1 is the second most frequently altered Noonan-syndrome gene, accounting for **16.4%** of patients in the cited dataset; this percentage is cohort- and ascertainment-dependent rather than a universal prevalence. RASopathies collectively affect roughly **1 in 1,000–2,500 children**. (OpenTargets Search: Noonan syndrome,hereditary gingival fibromatosis,cancer-SOS1)

This genetic evidence is especially informative mechanistically because pathogenic variants often cluster in regulatory regions and demonstrate that physiological restraint of SOS1—not merely its catalytic core—is essential during development. Noonan syndrome is therefore best understood as dysregulated pathway gain, not SOS1 deficiency.

### 5.2 Hereditary gingival fibromatosis

A distinct SOS1-associated phenotype, hereditary gingival fibromatosis type 1, has historically been linked to truncating variants that alter the C-terminal region. This association is biologically plausible because the tail controls adaptor binding, phosphorylation, and trafficking. However, the retrieved recent evidence was substantially thinner than for Noonan syndrome, and SOS1 variants do not explain all hereditary gingival-fibromatosis families. Accordingly, it should be treated as an established but rare, allelic disease association rather than a general explanation for gingival fibromatosis.

### 5.3 Cancer

Somatic SOS1 activating mutations are uncommon relative to mutations in KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, or upstream receptors. A 2024 expert review estimated activating SOS1 alterations in approximately **1% of lung adenocarcinomas and uterine carcinomas** and characterized them as rare, potentially weak oncogenic drivers. The therapeutic importance of SOS1 is broader than mutation frequency, however, because many RAS-driven tumors remain dependent on nucleotide exchange, wild-type RAS activation, or feedback reactivation after pathway inhibition. (bannoura2024targetingguaninenucleotide pages 3-4)

A November 2024 study identified **SOS1 P481delinsLFFL** in the BH1406 NSCLC cell line after a limited clinical sequencing panel had missed a driver. Cells were sensitive to BAY-293, MRTX0902, and BI-3406; sensitivity to BI-3406 was greater in 3D than 2D culture, and SOS1 inhibitors blocked the elevated ERK1/2 program observed in 3D. These findings support expanded sequencing and functional drug screening for rare drivers, but they derive from one cell-line model and do not establish patient benefit. [Hamilton et al., published 28 November 2024; DOI: https://doi.org/10.21037/tlcr-24-570] (hamilton2024characterizationofthe pages 1-2)

## 6. Recent developments, 2023–2024

### 6.1 Mechanistic research

A 2023 synthesis emphasized that RAS is a dynamic information-processing system: SOS1 positive feedback, ERK/RSK negative feedback, membrane nanoclusters, and compartmentalization jointly determine signaling amplitude and duration. This current view replaces the older model of SOS1 as a passive, single-step GDP/GTP switch. [Kolch et al., January 2023; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1042/BCJ20220234] (kolch2023dynamicregulationof pages 6-7)

A January 2024 structural-simulation study analyzed early SOS1-mediated KRAS rearrangements, reinforcing the division between the REM allosteric site and CDC25 catalytic site and the importance of interdomain conformational communication. As a computational study, it is hypothesis-generating and complements—but does not supersede—crystallography, mutagenesis, and membrane reconstitution. [Bhadhadhara et al., January 2024; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.crstbi.2023.100115] (bhadhadhara2024studyingearlystructural pages 1-2)

### 6.2 Small-molecule discovery

Most SOS1 inhibitors occupy a pocket adjacent to the SOS1–KRAS interface and prevent productive nucleotide exchange. Because SOS1 maintains or restores the GDP-bound RAS pool, combinations with GDP-state KRAS inhibitors or MEK inhibitors are mechanistically attractive. Expert reviews nevertheless stress pathway redundancy, SOS2 compensation, mutant-specific exchange dependence, and normal-tissue RAS signaling as potential limitations. Most GEF-directed agents remain preclinical. [Bannoura et al., June 2024; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/17460441.2024.2368242] (bannoura2024targetingguaninenucleotide pages 3-4)

A 2024 machine-learning screen searched more than **1.4 million compounds**, identified nine new inhibitory scaffolds, and reported a best hit with greater than **50% inhibition** in a KRAS-G12C/SOS1 interaction assay and an IC50 near **20 μg/mL**. This demonstrates discovery utility but remains far less potent than a clinical-quality lead and provides no evidence of human efficacy. [Duo et al., March 2024; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1039/D4MD00063C] (duo2024discoveryofnovel pages 1-2)

A separate 2024 medicinal-chemistry program nominated HH0043, which produced **76% tumor-growth inhibition** in an NCI-H358 KRAS-G12C xenograft versus **49%** for BI-3406 at the same dose. These are mouse-model data, not a clinical comparison. [Li et al., June 2024; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1021/acsmedchemlett.4c00156]

SOS1-targeted degraders/PROTACs and dual SOS1/EGFR inhibitors were also reported in 2024, illustrating expansion beyond reversible interface blockers. Their translational advantages remain unproven, particularly regarding selectivity over SOS2, tissue exposure, and tolerability.

## 7. Clinical translation and real-world status

There is **no approved SOS1-directed therapy** in the evidence reviewed. Clinical work remains early-phase, and registry status must not be confused with demonstrated efficacy.

* **NCT04111458:** Phase I BI-1701963 monotherapy or BI-1701963 plus trametinib in advanced KRAS-mutant solid tumors; the retrieved registry listed **71 participants** and **active, not recruiting**. The trial is designed around safety, dose, pharmacokinetics, and preliminary activity; registry objectives are not proof of benefit. (NCT04111458 chunk 2, bannoura2024targetingguaninenucleotide pages 3-4)
* **NCT05578092:** Phase I MRTX0902 alone or with adagrasib; **64 participants**, **terminated** because “business objectives have changed.” No efficacy result should be inferred from that administrative reason. (NCT05578092 chunk 1)
* **NCT04835714:** Phase I BI-1701963 alone or with BI-3011441; **8 participants**, **terminated** by sponsor decision. (NCT04835714 chunk 1)
* **NCT04975256/KRYSTAL-14:** Phase I adagrasib plus BI-1701963; **7 participants**, **terminated**, primarily after a change in development strategy. The registry provides no response-rate evidence in the retrieved record. (NCT04975256 chunk 1)
* **NCT04627142:** BI-1701963 plus irinotecan in advanced KRAS-mutant bowel cancer; the retrieved search record listed Phase I, **15 participants**, and **terminated** status.

These small enrollments and multiple strategic terminations show that compelling pathway biology has not yet translated into validated clinical efficacy. The most plausible applications remain combination treatment of exchange-dependent KRAS tumors, suppression of adaptive wild-type RAS reactivation, and biomarker-selected treatment of rare activating SOS1-mutant cancers.

## 8. Expert assessment and annotation confidence

**High-confidence annotation:** SOS1 is a regulated, membrane-proximal RAS GEF. Its CDC25 domain catalyzes GDP release; REM binds allosteric RAS; N-terminal domains enforce autoinhibition and participate in lipid recognition; the proline-rich tail controls adaptor recruitment and feedback. Productive function occurs mainly at the cytoplasmic membrane surface after receptor stimulation. This conclusion is supported by structures, mutagenesis, biochemical reconstitution, single-molecule measurements, disease genetics, and pharmacologic perturbation. (christensen2016onewaymembranetrafficking pages 14-16, bandaru2019theinterdependentactivation pages 8-9, bandaru2019theinterdependentactivation pages 4-6)

**Moderate-confidence secondary annotation:** SOS1 can participate in RAC1 activation through its DH–PH module in EPS8–ABI1 complexes, especially at membrane ruffles. The direct catalytic contribution in intact cells is more context-dependent and less thoroughly resolved than RAS exchange. (pierre2011understandingsos(son pages 5-8, kessler2021targetingsonof pages 1-2)

**Important limitations:** pathway outputs such as proliferation, ERK phosphorylation, AKT signaling, migration, and tumor growth are not unique molecular functions of SOS1. They depend on receptor context, RAS genotype, SOS2 and other GEFs, feedback, membrane organization, and cell state. Likewise, strong preclinical inhibitor activity and sound combination rationale do not yet constitute clinical validation.

## Conclusion

Human SOS1/Q07889 is best functionally annotated as a **multidomain, allosterically regulated RAS guanine-nucleotide exchange factor that operates predominantly on the inner plasma membrane downstream of activated receptors**. Its core biochemical role is nucleotide release from RAS; membrane recruitment, lipid contacts, relief of autoinhibition, and RAS–GTP allosteric feedback determine when and where that reaction occurs. This positions SOS1 as a central amplifier and dynamic controller of RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK signaling and, in selected complexes, RAC-dependent cytoskeletal remodeling. Germline gain-of-function variants establish its developmental importance in Noonan syndrome, while current oncology applications focus on inhibiting SOS1 to suppress RAS activation and adaptive feedback. As of the reviewed 2024 evidence, this therapeutic strategy remains investigational rather than clinically established.
