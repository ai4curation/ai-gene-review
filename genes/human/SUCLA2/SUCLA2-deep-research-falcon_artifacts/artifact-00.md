| **Established identity and localization** | Human **SUCLA2** is the ADP-specific β-subunit; it pairs with the shared α-subunit **SUCLG1** to form ATP-producing A-SCS in the **mitochondrial matrix**. The alternative β-subunit **SUCLG2** forms GDP-specific/GTP-producing G-SCS. | Human subunit assignments, tissue-specific isoforms, matrix localization, and biochemical distinction are supported by structural, biochemical, and localization studies. SUCLA2 must not be conflated with SUCLG1 or SUCLG2. (pqac-00000016, pqac-00000018, pqac-00000023) | SUCLA2 supplies nucleotide specificity but is not catalytically sufficient without SUCLG1; findings specific to SUCLG1 or SUCLG2 cannot automatically be assigned to SUCLA2. |
| **Canonical primary function—forward direction** | **Succinyl-CoA + ADP + Pi ⇌ succinate + CoA-SH + ATP.** In the TCA-cycle direction, A-SCS performs mitochondrial substrate-level phosphorylation. | The reaction is reversible and proceeds through an α-subunit phosphohistidine intermediate, while the β-subunit ATP-grasp region binds the adenine nucleotide. Human genetic deficiency selectively reduces ADP-forming activity. (pqac-00000016, pqac-00000017, pqac-00000018, pqac-00000022) | “ATP-specific” describes the ATP-forming enzyme product; mechanistically, ADP is the nucleotide substrate in the forward TCA-cycle direction. |
| **Canonical reaction—reverse direction** | The same complex can consume **ATP, succinate, and CoA-SH** to form **succinyl-CoA, ADP, and Pi** in vitro because the reaction is near equilibrium. | Reversibility is biochemically established, but physiological direction depends on matrix substrate/product concentrations and metabolic state. (pqac-00000016, pqac-00000018) | In-vitro reversibility does not establish that reverse A-SCS flux is a major physiological source of succinyl-CoA in a given tissue. |
| **Alternative ligase activities—recent development** | Recombinant human ADP-forming SUCLG1–SUCLA2 can ligate CoA to **itaconate** and **malate** using ATP, yielding itaconyl-CoA and malyl-CoA as promiscuous side products. | The peer-reviewed 2025 study reports these activities; detailed 2024 preprint kinetics indicate that, relative to succinate, catalytic efficiency of the **ADP-forming SUCLG1–SUCLA2 complex** was approximately **30-fold lower for itaconate** and **340-fold lower for malate**. The corresponding GDP-forming SUCLG1–SUCLG2 values were different and must not be substituted. (pqac-00000008, pqac-00000011, pqac-00000045, pqac-00000059) | These are low-efficiency side activities. Cellular isotope tracing established malate as a precursor of malyl-CoA, but did **not** prove that SUCLG1–SUCLA2 alone is its source in human cells; α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase can also produce malyl-CoA. |
| **Human patient-cell mechanism** | SUCLA2 deficiency causes succinyl-CoA accumulation and broad protein hypersuccinylation. | Patient fibroblast proteomics identified **933 succinylated lysines across 366 proteins**; patient myotubes contained 194 sites across 91 proteins, with 86% also detected in fibroblasts. Affected pathways included the TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, β-oxidation, and extramitochondrial processes. (pqac-00000038, pqac-00000040) | Site abundance does not by itself demonstrate functional impairment; effects are protein- and site-dependent. |
| **In-vivo mechanistic evidence** | Postnatal forebrain-specific Sucla2 loss strongly reduces SCS activity, raises methylmalonic acid, and causes hypersuccinylation without bulk cortical mtDNA depletion. | Mutant cortex showed **68% lower Sucla2 transcript**, **80–90% loss of the SCS complex**, **58% lower total SCS activity**, and a **14-fold MMA increase**; mtDNA copy number was slightly elevated rather than depleted. Complex-I dysfunction and altered neuronal transcription accompanied hypersuccinylation. (pqac-00000027, pqac-00000029, pqac-00000043) | Bulk cortex could mask neuron-specific mtDNA depletion. Associations with hypersuccinylation do not establish that every molecular or neurological phenotype is caused by that modification. |
| **Heme-pathway interaction** | SUCLA2 can physically associate with erythroid aminolevulinate synthase **ALAS2**, suggesting metabolic organization near the first step of heme synthesis. | Although reverse SCS flux was proposed to supply ALAS2 with succinyl-CoA, isotope/flux experiments found that late erythroid heme synthesis instead uses glutamine-derived α-ketoglutarate converted by α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase; succinate was not an effective heme precursor. (pqac-00000044) | The interaction is supported, but its physiological role remains unresolved; it is **not evidence** that SUCLA2-generated succinyl-CoA is the principal erythroid heme substrate. |
