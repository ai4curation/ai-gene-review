| Primary Function | Human FGF21 is a stress-inducible, non-enzymatic endocrine hormone that communicates hepatic nutrient status to distant organs. It coordinates adaptation to fasting, protein restriction, carbohydrate excess, cold, exercise, and cellular stress by regulating fuel selection, energy expenditure, and feeding behavior. It has no catalytic substrate; its molecular targets are receptor complexes containing β-Klotho and an FGFR. | Authoritative reviews define FGF21 as an inter-organ metabolic messenger rather than a conventional local mitogen (pqac-00000001, pqac-00000002, pqac-00000003). |
| Tissue Source / Localization | FGF21 is synthesized as a secretory precursor, trafficked through the endoplasmic-reticulum/COPII secretory pathway, and released extracellularly. The liver is the predominant source of circulating FGF21 under basal and many nutritional-stress conditions. Adipose tissue, pancreas, skeletal muscle, and heart can produce it in stimulus-dependent contexts, often for local autocrine or paracrine action. Circulating native FGF21 has a reported half-life of approximately 0.5–1.5 hours. | Secretory trafficking, endocrine distribution, hepatic predominance, and context-dependent extrahepatic production are supported by physiological and tissue-source reviews (pqac-00000018, pqac-00000019, pqac-00000022, pqac-00000023). |
| Receptor Complex | The principal signaling complex is FGF21–β-Klotho (KLB)–FGFR1c at the plasma membrane; FGFR2c or FGFR3c can contribute in selected contexts. The FGF21 C terminus binds KLB, while its N terminus engages the FGFR, activating the receptor tyrosine kinase. FGF21 binds KLB much more strongly than FGFR1c directly; one 2023 study reported an approximately 20–40 nM Kd for KLB versus weak micromolar FGFR1c interaction. Proteolytic loss of the KLB-binding C terminus inactivates the hormone. | Biochemical evidence supports cooperative ternary-complex formation rather than effective activation of either receptor component alone (pqac-00000009, pqac-00000010, pqac-00000011, pqac-00000015). A 2023 PNAS study further found that heparin supports optimal signaling and β-Klotho stability (pqac-00000009). |
| Target Tissues | KLB expression, rather than broadly distributed FGFRs, largely determines responsiveness. Adipose tissue is the clearest direct peripheral target; discrete CNS circuits are important in rodents for nutrient preference, sympathetic output, and energy expenditure. Pancreas and heart are additional responsive tissues. The liver is primarily the hormone source, and some apparent hepatic drug effects may be indirect through adipose or CNS signaling. Human KLB expression is most consistently detected in adipose tissue, liver, and pancreas; human-brain expression is sparse and less certain than rodent data. | Tissue-distribution analyses emphasize adipose tissue, liver, and pancreas while documenting species-specific uncertainty in CNS KLB expression (pqac-00000032, pqac-00000033, pqac-00000036, pqac-00000037). |
| Downstream Signaling Pathways | FGFR kinase activation phosphorylates FRS2α and recruits GRB2/SOS to activate RAS–MAPK–ERK. FRS2α–GRB2 can also recruit GAB1–PI3K–AKT; PLCγ and STAT signaling are possible canonical FGFR branches. Context-dependent metabolic pathways include AMPK–SIRT1–PGC-1α, AMPK–ACC/CPT1, AKT–NRF2, and autophagy/mTOR regulation. ERK phosphorylation is a robust proximal pharmacodynamic marker, but experts caution that ERK or GLUT1 alone cannot explain FGF21’s diverse physiology. | Receptor-proximal and metabolic pathway evidence is summarized in mechanistic reviews, including a 2024 FGF/FGFR analysis (pqac-00000024, pqac-00000025, pqac-00000026, pqac-00000028, pqac-00000029). |
| Transcriptional Regulation | Fasting and fatty-acid flux activate hepatic PPARα; CREBH can reinforce fasting responses. Carbohydrate or fructose excess induces FGF21 through ChREBP, with PPARα cooperation. Protein/amino-acid restriction engages GCN2–eIF2α–ATF4. Mitochondrial or endoplasmic-reticulum stress activates ATF4/CHOP. CNOT6L can negatively regulate FGF21 post-transcriptionally by promoting mRNA decay. Human fasting responses may require longer exposure and differ from mouse responses. | Nutrient- and stress-sensing control is supported by physiological reviews and a 2023 cardiometabolic synthesis (pqac-00000040, pqac-00000041, pqac-00000042, pqac-00000043). |
| Metabolic Effects | In adipose tissue, FGF21 increases glucose uptake, insulin sensitivity, adiponectin secretion, thermogenic programs, and lipid handling. Systemically it lowers triglycerides by reducing lipogenesis and promoting lipid clearance, supports fatty-acid oxidation and ketogenesis during fasting, and influences simple-sugar, protein, and alcohol preference through neuroendocrine circuits. Pharmacological effects can exceed or differ from endogenous physiological effects and vary by species. | Multi-organ studies and reviews support coordinated regulation of glucose disposal, lipid flux, energy expenditure, and nutrient choice (pqac-00000002, pqac-00000003, pqac-00000006, pqac-00000024). |
| Recent 2023–2024 Developments | Recent work refined receptor biology by identifying a role for heparin in optimal FGF21 signaling and KLB stability, and used structural information to engineer more stable or potent analogues. Clinical translation has focused on long-acting agents such as efruxifermin and pegozafermin for MASH and hypertriglyceridemia, with improvements reported in liver-fat, lipid, and fibrosis-related outcomes. A 2023 meta-analysis of eight randomized trials found reductions in fasting insulin, body weight, and total cholesterol, but no significant aggregate effect on fasting glucose, HbA1c, HOMA, free fatty acids, or systolic blood pressure; evidence quality ranged from moderate to very low. | Mechanistic and structure-guided advances are reported in 2023–2024 studies (pqac-00000009, pqac-00000048). The clinical-effect summary and its limitations come from the 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis (pqac-00000051). |
