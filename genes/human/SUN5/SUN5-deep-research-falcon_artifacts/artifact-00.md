| Identity and domain | The target is human **SUN5** (*Sad1 and UNC84 domain containing 5*), also called **SPAG4L/TSARG4**. It is a testis-associated transmembrane SUN-family protein with a conserved C-terminal SUN domain, consistent with the supplied UniProt **Q8TC36** annotation. | Human molecular literature; database-supported nomenclature; family inference | **High** for identity and SUN-family membership. Detailed topology is supported mainly by family architecture rather than a high-resolution human SUN5 structure. | Shang et al., 2017, [https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199) (pqac-00000003); Open Targets (pqac-00000000) |
| Cellular localization | SUN5 appears at the spermatid nuclear envelope, subsequently concentrates at the developing head–tail coupling apparatus, and in mature sperm localizes to the implantation fossa/neck. Neck localization was observed in human, mouse, rat, bovine, and sheep sperm. | Mouse spermatids and sperm; human and other mammalian sperm | **High** for sperm-neck localization and developmental redistribution. Older reports of apical/acrosomal localization differ, probably reflecting developmental stage, isoform, or reagent differences. | Shang et al., 2017, [https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199) (pqac-00000002); Kmonickova et al., 2020, [https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21239058](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21239058) (pqac-00000007) |
| Primary molecular function | SUN5 is a **structural anchor**, not an enzyme or transporter: it secures the sperm head–tail coupling apparatus to the nuclear envelope/implantation fossa during spermiogenesis. The apparatus can assemble without SUN5 but fails to remain attached to the nucleus. | Mouse knockout, ultrastructure, localization; corroborating human mutant-sperm morphology | **High** for the anchoring role. The description of SUN5 as the innermost or “root” element is a mechanistic interpretation rather than a fully resolved atomic model. | Shang et al., 2017, [https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199) (pqac-00000001, pqac-00000002) |
| Nesprin3 and ODF1 mechanism | SUN5-mutant human sperm show altered Nesprin3 and ODF1 distribution. Co-immunoprecipitation in transfected HEK293T cells supported SUN5–Nesprin3, SUN5–ODF1, and Nesprin3–ODF1 interactions; a three-protein head–tail-junction “triplet” was proposed. | Human sperm immunostaining; cultured-cell interaction assays | **Moderate**. Pairwise association is experimentally supported, but the exact stoichiometry, directness, topology, and proposed triplet architecture remain incompletely established in native human sperm. | Zhang et al., 2021, [https://doi.org/10.1093/molehr/gaab028](https://doi.org/10.1093/molehr/gaab028) (pqac-00000009, pqac-00000010, pqac-00000015) |
| Loss-of-function phenotype | Male **Sun5-null mice are infertile**. The head–tail coupling apparatus detaches during spermatid elongation; heads are often retained in seminiferous epithelium, while epididymal samples contain headless/coiled tails, reduced motility, disordered mitochondria, and secondary flagellar abnormalities. Meiosis, acrosome biogenesis, and nuclear remodeling are comparatively preserved. | Mouse knockout and electron microscopy | **High** for causal necessity in male fertility and head–tail attachment. Secondary tail defects may follow from failed coupling rather than represent SUN5’s primary function. | Shang et al., 2017, [https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199) (pqac-00000001, pqac-00000004); Kmonickova et al., 2020, [https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21239058](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21239058) (pqac-00000007) |
| Human disease and inheritance | **Biallelic loss-of-function or damaging SUN5 variants cause autosomal-recessive acephalic spermatozoa syndrome**, characterized by predominantly headless sperm, tailless heads, “pin-headed” forms, or abnormal head–tail junctions. A homozygous exon-8 deletion segregated in three related affected men. | Human pedigrees, sequencing, sperm phenotyping, minigene splicing assay | **High** for a recessive gene–disease relationship, although individual missense variants require variant-specific functional and segregation assessment. | Elkhatib et al., 2017, [https://doi.org/10.1093/hmg/ddx200](https://doi.org/10.1093/hmg/ddx200) (pqac-00000014); Shang et al., 2017, [https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199) (pqac-00000004, pqac-00000011) |
| Proportion of cases attributable to SUN5 | One cited human survey found biallelic SUN5 variants in **47.06%** of the investigated acephalic-spermatozoa patients; the broader literature has described an approximate **33–47%** range in selected cohorts. | Small, phenotype-enriched human cohorts; review-level aggregation | **Moderate**. This is not population prevalence and should not be generalized across ancestries or unselected infertility clinics; ascertainment and founder effects can strongly influence estimates. | Shang et al., 2017, [https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199) (pqac-00000002); Zhang et al., 2021, [https://doi.org/10.3389/fcell.2021.684826](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcell.2021.684826) |
| ICSI and clinical application | Injection of selected tailless sperm heads rescued fertility in Sun5-null mice and produced healthy offspring in reported human SUN5 cases. A prior 31-cycle series, as quoted by a later study, reported **81.5% fertilization, 81.8% good-quality embryos, and 100% pregnancy**; however, a separate five-cycle series produced one healthy boy and four early miscarriages. | Mouse assisted reproduction; small human case series and secondary attribution of a 31-cycle dataset | **Moderate/low for precise rates**. The 31-cycle percentages are secondarily reported in the gathered source, definitions and denominators require checking in the original study, and outcomes vary across small series. ICSI bypasses attachment failure but can transmit a recessive allele. | Zhang et al., 2021, [https://doi.org/10.1093/molehr/gaab028](https://doi.org/10.1093/molehr/gaab028) (pqac-00000009, pqac-00000017); Shang et al., 2017, [https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.28199) (pqac-00000002); Moghaddam et al., 2021, [https://doi.org/10.1007/s10815-020-02008-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10815-020-02008-w) (pqac-00000013) |
| 2023 trafficking development | **ARRDC5-deficient mice** displayed bent-head sperm, reduced motility, defective capacitation, and infertility. Proteomics and localization experiments suggested ARRDC5 and SEC22A-dependent trafficking affects SUN5, NDC1, and other head–tail coupling proteins. | Mouse knockout, mass spectrometry, trafficking/localization experiments | **Moderate and indirect for SUN5**. The study places SUN5 in a broader assembly/trafficking pathway but does not show that human ARRDC5 disease or direct ARRDC5–SUN5 binding explains SUN5-associated syndrome. | Liu et al., 2023, [https://doi.org/10.1242/dev.201959](https://doi.org/10.1242/dev.201959) |
