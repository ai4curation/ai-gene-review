Gene Description

SUN5 (SPAG4L, TSARG4) is a testis-enriched SUN-domain protein of the nuclear envelope, a 379-residue single-pass protein with a nucleoplasmic N-terminus, a luminal coiled coil and a C-terminal SUN domain. In spermatids it is glycosylated during transit through the Golgi, reaches the nuclear envelope and then concentrates at the posterior pole of the elongating nucleus, the implantation fossa, where the sperm head-tail coupling apparatus (connecting piece, basal plate and proximal centriole) docks; in mature sperm it marks the neck. There SUN5 forms a LINC complex with the KASH protein Nesprin-3 (SYNE3), which it is required to position at the posterior nuclear membrane, and it binds lamin B1 on the nucleoplasmic side, anchoring the coupling apparatus and flagellum to the nucleus. Loss of SUN5 in mice leaves meiosis, acrosome formation and nuclear condensation intact but the coupling apparatus detaches from the nucleus during spermatid elongation, producing headless (acephalic) spermatozoa and male infertility. Biallelic SUN5 variants are the most frequent cause of human acephalic spermatozoa syndrome (spermatogenic failure 16), which can be bypassed by intracytoplasmic injection of sperm heads.

Human SUN5 (UniProt Q8TC36): Functional-annotation research report

Executive conclusion

The requested target is correctly identified as human SUN5, Sad1 and UNC84 domain-containing protein 5 (UniProt Q8TC36; aliases SPAG4L and TSARG4). The literature consistently concerns a testis-associated SUN-domain protein rather than a different similarly named gene. Open Targets maps the approved human symbol SUN5 to ENSG00000167098 and records associations with male infertility and globozoospermia/acephalic-sperm phenotypes (OpenTargets Search: male infertility-SUN5).

SUN5 is not an enzyme, transporter, or conventional soluble signaling molecule. Its best-supported primary function is architectural: during spermiogenesis it anchors the sperm head–tail coupling apparatus (HTCA) to the posterior nuclear envelope/implantation fossa. Loss of this mechanical linkage allows the developing flagellum and neck structures to separate from the nucleus, causing autosomal-recessive acephalic spermatozoa syndrome and male infertility. This conclusion is supported by mouse knockout causality, ultrastructure and localization, human biallelic variants with characteristic sperm morphology, and rescue of fertility by intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) (shang2017essentialrolefor pages 10-12, shang2017essentialrolefor pages 7-10, shang2017essentialrolefor pages 12-13).

  • Family/domain: a member of the SUN-domain nuclear-envelope protein family, matching the supplied InterPro/Pfam annotations SUN1–5 (IPR045119), SUN domain (IPR012919), and Sad1_UNC (PF07738).

SUN-family proteins are integral membrane components of nuclear-envelope bridges. The literature describes the family architecture as including a transmembrane segment and conserved C-terminal SUN domain; SUN3, SUN4, and SUN5 have testis-restricted or strongly testis-associated expression. This aligns with Q8TC36 rather than indicating an unrelated protein (shang2017essentialrolefor pages 1-2). No conflicting human gene with the same symbol was encountered.

A qualification is necessary: the general SUN/LINC topology is strongly supported, but some detailed topology statements for human SUN5 remain based on homology, cell-expression systems, and family architecture rather than a high-resolution native human structure.

2. Primary molecular and cellular function

2.1 Structural role

SUN5 functions chiefly as a load-bearing attachment factor at the sperm neck. During spermatid differentiation, it is first detected at the nuclear envelope and subsequently concentrates at the developing HTCA. In mature sperm it localizes at the implantation fossa/neck, where the flagellar connecting piece attaches to the nucleus. Comparable neck localization in human, mouse, rat, cattle, and sheep supports evolutionary conservation (shang2017essentialrolefor pages 7-10).

The decisive experiment is the Sun5-null mouse. The basal plate–capitulum–segmented-column apparatus can still assemble, but it does not remain attached to the nuclear envelope. During elongation and spermiation, the HTCA and flagellum detach; heads are often retained in the seminiferous epithelium while headless tails enter the epididymis. Thus SUN5 is required primarily for attachment of a formed coupling apparatus, rather than for assembling the apparatus itself (shang2017essentialrolefor pages 10-12, shang2017essentialrolefor pages 7-10).

This distinction also separates SUN5 from neighboring sperm-nuclear-envelope systems. SUN3/SUN4 complexes principally participate in manchette organization and head shaping, whereas SUN5 loss comparatively preserves meiosis, acrosome biogenesis, and nuclear condensation but destroys head–tail integrity. Some apparently round-headed mutant sperm are actually coiled headless tails lacking chromatin and acrosome—the “pseudo-globozoospermatozoa” distinction (shang2017essentialrolefor pages 12-13, pasch2015thelinccomplex pages 5-6).

2.2 Localization over developmental time

The best-supported localization sequence is:

  1. Round/elongating spermatid nuclear envelope;
  2. redistribution toward the posterior nuclear region and developing HTCA;
  3. concentration at the implantation fossa/head–tail junction;
  4. persistence at the neck of mature sperm (shang2017essentialrolefor pages 7-10, kmonickova2020theroleof pages 12-15).

An alternatively spliced human transcript, SPAG4Lβ, lacks exons 3–5, including the reported transmembrane region, and consequently shows a more perinuclear/cytoplasmic distribution than full-length SPAG4L, which concentrates at the nuclear rim in a germ-cell model. This supports the importance of membrane insertion for normal localization, although the physiological importance of the β isoform is less established than that of canonical SUN5 (li2019spag4lspag4linteractswith pages 7-8).

3. Molecular partners and pathway context

SUN5 acts within a specialized nuclear-envelope/neck linkage rather than a conventional biochemical signaling cascade.

Nesprin3 and ODF1

Human SUN5-variant sperm show altered localization of Nesprin3 and ODF1. Co-immunoprecipitation in transfected HEK293T cells supported SUN5–Nesprin3, SUN5–ODF1, and Nesprin3–ODF1 associations. Investigators therefore proposed a SUN5–Nesprin3–ODF1 “triplet” that strengthens the mature head–tail junction (zhang2021pathogenesisofacephalic pages 6-7, zhang2021pathogenesisofacephalic pages 7-7, zhang2021pathogenesisofacephalic pages 5-6).

The evidence should be interpreted hierarchically. Pairwise association and variant-dependent mislocalization are experimentally supported; an exact native trimer, direct binding interfaces, stoichiometry, and mechanical arrangement have not yet been resolved. Moreover, a mouse study found that SUN5 is necessary for positioning Nesprin3 at the posterior nuclear membrane: without SUN5, Nesprin3 is lost from the implantation-fossa region and the centrosome/flagellar origin becomes separated from the nucleus (kmonickova2020theroleof pages 12-15).

Other reported partners and assembly factors

  • DNAJB13 reportedly interacts with SUN5 during spermiogenesis, assists its folding, and later relocates to the tail. This suggests a chaperone-assisted assembly step but not an enzymatic activity of SUN5 itself (kmonickova2020theroleof pages 12-15).
  • A human cell/testis study reported SPAG4L interaction with Nesprin2, proposing an additional LINC-related role during meiotic spermatogenesis. This evidence is less directly tied to the mature head–tail defect than the knockout and human ASS evidence (li2019spag4lspag4linteractswith pages 7-8).
  • Recent work is broadening the model toward a multicomponent bridge involving nuclear-envelope, centrosomal, outer-dense-fiber, and septin structures. These interactions should presently be regarded as an evolving network rather than a fully defined linear pathway.

4. Experimental loss-of-function evidence

Sun5-null male mice are infertile and produce predominantly acephalic sperm. Ultrastructure shows progressive separation of the HTCA from the nucleus during spermatid elongation. Secondary consequences include cytoplasmic retention, disorganized mitochondrial sheaths, axonemal abnormalities, and reduced motility. Because coupling-apparatus formation initially occurs, these defects are best interpreted as downstream consequences of failed head–tail anchoring rather than evidence that SUN5 directly constructs the axoneme or mitochondria (shang2017essentialrolefor pages 10-12, kmonickova2020theroleof pages 12-15).

The mouse phenotype closely parallels human disease. Human mutant sperm include headless tails, tailless heads, “pin-headed” forms, absent or abnormal implantation fossae, disturbed sperm-axis alignment, and occasional residual head–tail attachment. The greater variability in patients probably reflects allelic heterogeneity and partial-function variants, whereas engineered null alleles produce a more uniform severe phenotype (shang2017essentialrolefor pages 10-12).

5. Human genetics and disease mechanism

Biallelic SUN5 variants cause autosomal-recessive acephalic spermatozoa syndrome, a rare severe teratozoospermia. Reported alleles include nonsense, frameshift, splice-altering, missense, and exon-deletion variants. A 2017 family study identified a homozygous exon-8 deletion segregating in three related affected men; the same report used a minigene assay to show reduced normal splicing for p.Gly114Arg-associated sequence variation (elkhatib2017homozygousdeletionof pages 15-18).

A 2021 ten-patient series identified SUN5 variants in four unrelated patients: homozygous c.381delA (p.Val128Serfs*7), homozygous c.675C>A (p.Tyr225*), and compound heterozygous c.88C>T (p.Arg30*)/c.381delA. SUN5 transcript and protein were extremely reduced or absent in affected sperm; nonsense-mediated decay was proposed, although decay itself was inferred rather than directly measured. SUN5 was correspondingly absent from the head–tail junction (zhang2021pathogenesisofacephalic pages 5-6).

One selected cohort found biallelic SUN5 variants in 47.06% of investigated acephalic-sperm patients, while reviews cite approximately 33–47% in selected series (shang2017essentialrolefor pages 7-10). These are proportions in small, phenotype-enriched cohorts—not prevalence in the general population or among all infertile men. Ancestry, founder variants, ascertainment, and diagnostic criteria can materially change the proportion.

Open Targets records SUN5–male-infertility evidence with an association score of approximately 0.743, based principally on literature-linked genetic evidence. Such database scores rank evidence but are not clinical penetrance estimates (OpenTargets Search: male infertility-SUN5).

6. Clinical application and real-world implementation

Diagnostic use

SUN5 is a high-priority gene when semen examination shows predominantly headless flagella, tailless heads, pin-head forms, or abnormal implantation fossae. A practical evaluation combines detailed morphology, electron microscopy where available, and sequencing/CNV analysis capable of detecting biallelic SNVs, splice variants, and exon deletions. PMFBP1 and other HTCA genes should be evaluated when SUN5 testing is negative because acephalic sperm syndrome is genetically heterogeneous.

The clinical value is etiologic diagnosis, recessive-risk counseling, and assistance with sperm selection for ICSI. Current reviews emphasize phenotype-directed genetic testing rather than universal SUN5 screening of all infertile men; many male-infertility genes still lack evidence sufficient for indiscriminate routine panels (moghaddam2021geneticbasisof pages 1-3).

ICSI

ICSI can bypass the physical head–tail attachment defect if a viable nucleus-containing sperm head is selected. Sun5-null mouse sperm heads produced healthy heterozygous offspring, and two reported human couples with SUN5-associated infertility achieved pregnancies and healthy heterozygous children (shang2017essentialrolefor pages 7-10, shang2017essentialrolefor pages 1-2).

A later paper quoted a 31-cycle series with 81.5% fertilization, 81.8% good-quality embryos, and 100% pregnancy. However, another five-cycle series involving four SUN5 cases and one PMFBP1 case yielded one healthy boy and four early miscarriages (zhang2021pathogenesisofacephalic pages 6-7). These apparently discordant outcomes emphasize small samples, differing genotypes, sperm-selection procedures, maternal factors, and secondary reporting of the 31-cycle statistics. ICSI is therefore a plausible and often effective implementation, not a guarantee of live birth. It can also transmit a pathogenic allele, making partner testing and genetic counseling appropriate.

7. Developments in 2023–2024

2023: assembly and trafficking context

Liu et al. reported that Arrdc5-deficient mice have bent-head sperm, reduced motility, defective capacitation, and infertility. Proteomics and localization experiments suggested that ARRDC5 and SEC22A-dependent vesicle trafficking influence delivery/localization of SUN5, NDC1, and other HTCA proteins. This places SUN5 within a regulated membrane-protein trafficking pathway upstream of head–tail attachment. It is indirect mouse evidence for SUN5 regulation, not proof that ARRDC5 directly binds SUN5 or causes the same human syndrome. Publication: December 2023, Development, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1242/dev.201959.

2023: regulatory-variant prioritization

A computational study prioritized three SUN5 3′-UTR variants—rs1485087675, rs762026146, and rs1478197315—as potentially altering miRNA-binding sites. None was located in a conserved region, and there was no patient segregation, expression assay, or sperm-level validation. These remain hypothesis-generating candidates, not established pathogenic variants. Publication: October 2023, DNA, DOI: https://doi.org/10.3390/dna3040013.

2024: expanded structural network

A 2024 review retained SUN5 as an established genetic cause of acephalic sperm morphology and highlighted its requirement for posterior Nesprin3 positioning. Publication: October 2024, Journal of Assisted Reproduction and Genetics, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10815-024-03274-8.

A 2024 mechanistic report described interactions of SUN5 with lamin B1 on the nuclear side and SEPTIN12 on the cytoskeletal/neck side, extending the model of SUN5 as a molecular bridge. Publication: June 2024, Molecular Human Reproduction, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/molehr/gaae022. This is directionally consistent with the established anchoring phenotype, although independent replication and native-complex structural resolution remain necessary.

8. Expert interpretation and confidence assessment

Established with high confidence:

  1. SUN5/Q8TC36 is the human SPAG4L/TSARG4 SUN-domain protein requested.
  2. Its core role is structural attachment of the sperm HTCA/neck to the nuclear envelope.
  3. Its decisive functional location is the posterior spermatid nuclear envelope and mature sperm implantation fossa/neck.
  4. Biallelic loss causes autosomal-recessive acephalic spermatozoa syndrome and male infertility.
  5. The phenotype can often be bypassed by ICSI when an intact sperm head is recoverable.

Supported but still being refined:

  • The precise composition and topology of a SUN5–Nesprin3–ODF1 complex.
  • Additional links to lamin B1, SEPTIN12, Nesprin2, DNAJB13, NDC1, ARRDC5, and SEC22A.
  • How variant class determines residual head–tail attachment and ICSI outcome.

Not established:

  • An enzymatic reaction, transported substrate, or canonical signal-transduction activity for SUN5.
  • Causality of computationally prioritized SUN5 UTR variants.
  • Population-wide penetrance or prevalence inferred from the reported 33–47% of highly selected ASS cohorts.
  • Universal routine SUN5 testing outside phenotype-directed male-infertility evaluation.

Overall functional annotation

SUN5 is a testis-associated integral nuclear-envelope/neck protein whose conserved SUN domain supports a specialized LINC-like mechanical linkage. During spermiogenesis, SUN5 concentrates at the implantation fossa and anchors the head–tail coupling apparatus to the sperm nucleus, probably through a multiprotein bridge connecting nuclear-envelope components to Nesprin-, outer-dense-fiber-, septin-, and centrosome-associated structures. Biallelic disruption weakens this junction, causing separation of sperm heads and tails and autosomal-recessive male infertility.
