# CLCN4 functional review

## Transport mechanism and core function

CLCN4 encodes ClC-4, an electrogenic chloride:proton exchanger. The historical channel labels reflect real CLC-family homology and positive expression-system currents. They are refined to GO:0062158 using later coupled-transport evidence, without deleting the observations or rewriting the original assertion objects. In the original Scheel study, voltage clamp combined with pH measurements distinguishes ClC-4 from channel and untransfected controls and demonstrates proton transport against its gradient. The reported coupling estimate was rough, approximately 1–5 chloride ions per proton, with a ClC-4 estimate of 1.6 ± 0.7; it is not a precise experimental determination of 2:1 stoichiometry. The complete institutional author PDF is available independently of the unchanged normal abstract cache ([PMID:16034422](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16034422/), journal pages 424–427; Figure 1 and Figure 2). The independent Picollo/Pusch abstract also reports proton export, but its full Methods were not recovered ([PMID:16034421](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16034421/)).

The original 1994 cloning/homology and 1999 human skeletal-muscle/CHO-current papers remain abstract-only at the inspected access scope. The latter reports acid-activated outward currents; this is not silently converted into an original proton-coupling experiment. A specific exchange refinement is supported by the later direct target work rather than a claim that these earlier sources were wrong or about another gene ([PMID:8069296](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8069296/); [PMID:10564087](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10564087/)). Existing broad transporter and antiporter assertions are refined to the same mechanistic term, so an additional NEW molecular-function assertion is unnecessary. Broad transport processes are made chloride-specific where appropriate, retaining every original qualifier, including the upstream-or-within relation.

## Compartments, partners and products

The long human construct can reside predominantly in the ER when expressed alone. Actual original Methods and Results from the 2006 paper support GFP/FLAG-tagged human ClC-4 ER localization in HEK293 cells, with a separate mouse-brain fractionation experiment. That study proposed an N-terminal retention determinant; later CBS-linker and partner-sorting results are not treated as the same experiment or an exclusive targeting mechanism ([PMID:17023393](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17023393/), indexed author text Methods 766–857, Results 963–1036 and 1154–1243, Discussion 1966–2038). Its normal abstract cache is preserved, while external body access is documented separately.

The 2017 study used human long ClC-4 and mouse ClC-3 variants. ClC-3a/b promoted late endosomal/lysosomal distribution, whereas ClC-3c promoted recycling-endosomal distribution; a mouse astrocyte host comparison further supported partner-dependent sorting. Native-gel and association experiments support heteromer formation but also have solubilization-dependent stability limits. The shorter human 687-residue construct was poorly expressed and had no measured currents under the tested conditions. Both genuine UniProt products are retained exactly, without adding source isoform tags or declaring the shorter product universally inactive ([PMID:28972156](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28972156/), Results and Methods). ER and plasma-membrane observations remain valid contextual annotations. Endosomal and lysosomal membranes organize the exchange core, with distinct early, late and recycling source assertions preserved.

Human disease-variant studies measured currents in Xenopus oocytes and localization in HeLa or COS-7 cells. The G544R loss of current, broader variant effects and WT-like Asp15Asn result are kept at their actual assay scopes. Mouse neuronal perturbation/rescue or knockout morphology is a separate evidence stream, and algal structural modeling is not presented as a human structure ([PMID:23647072](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23647072/); [PMID:25644381](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25644381/); [PMID:27550844](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27550844/)).

Recent studies add regulatory context. The TMEM9 work includes human ClC-4 association and targeted ClC-3 transport-dead/ClC-4 vacuolization experiments, while many of its current and mapping assays instead concern ClC-3 or ClC-5. Human ClC-4 with mouse ClC-3c also shows voltage- and pH-dependent variant behavior, cautioning against equating reduced homomer current with every native heteromer effect ([PMID:40169677](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40169677/); [PMID:41439993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41439993/)). Mouse hippocampal knockout recordings show region-dependent neuronal effects; the proposed downstream channel-trafficking model is not direct evidence for an additional human ClC-4 activity or process ([PMID:40605603](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40605603/)).
