Gene Description

Vezatin is a multi-pass integral membrane protein of adherens junctions that couples cadherin-catenin adhesion complexes to the actomyosin cytoskeleton. It has two closely spaced transmembrane helices with cytoplasmic N- and C-terminal regions; the cytoplasmic C-terminal region binds the C-terminal FERM domain of the unconventional motor myosin VIIA (MYO7A), and vezatin also binds the FERM domain of radixin in its open, actin-binding conformation. Vezatin is recruited to cell-cell contacts through the cytoplasmic tail of E-cadherin and the C-terminal region of alpha-catenin, arriving after E-cadherin during junction assembly, so it acts as a maturation and mechanical-stability factor rather than an initiator of adhesion. It is broadly expressed and is found at adherens junctions of most epithelia; junctional vezatin recruits myosin VIIA and thereby generates tension between the junctional membrane and cortical actin, strengthening cell-cell adhesion. In cochlear and vestibular hair cells vezatin occupies two membrane sites, the adherens junctions between hair cells and their supporting cells, and the base of developing stereocilia, where it is a component of the transient ankle-link complex together with ADGRV1/VLGR1, usherin (USH2A) and whirlin, binding the usherin cytoplasmic region directly. Additional vertebrate sites and roles include the acrosome of differentiating spermatids and sperm, the postsynaptic membrane of the neuromuscular junction where it associates with acetylcholine receptors and promotes postsynaptic maturation and receptor stability, and dendritic spines, where it is needed for spine morphogenesis and functional synapse maturation. Loss of vezatin in mouse is lethal around implantation and causes delocalization of E-cadherin and beta-catenin, while hair-cell-specific loss causes extreme susceptibility to acoustic trauma and progressive hair cell death, indicating that the protein is required for junction integrity under mechanical load. Vezatin proteins are also conserved regulators of dynein-dependent, cargo-selective retrograde transport of late and signalling endosomes in invertebrate and fish axons, and the fungal homolog VezA promotes dynactin assembly. Vezatin has no known catalytic activity; its molecular role is that of a transmembrane adaptor and stabilizer of specialized membrane-cytoskeleton assemblies.

Core Functions

Transmembrane myosin VIIA-binding adaptor activity at adherens junctions. Vezatin, anchored in the junctional plasma membrane by its two transmembrane helices, binds the C-terminal FERM domain of myosin VIIA through its cytoplasmic C-terminal region and thereby bridges the cadherin-catenin adhesion complex (to which it is recruited via the E-cadherin cytoplasmic tail and the C-terminal region of alpha-catenin) to the actomyosin cytoskeleton, with an additional link to cortical actin through the open, actin-binding conformation of radixin. This tension-generating coupling is dispensable for junction formation but required for the maintenance and mechanical stability of already-formed junctions - loss of vezatin delocalizes E-cadherin and beta-catenin from the plasma membrane, disrupts intercellular adhesion, and renders cochlear hair cells acutely vulnerable to acoustic overstimulation.

Executive summary

Identity was verified before interpretation. The requested target is human VEZT, encoding vezatin, UniProt Q9HBM0. The literature consistently uses VEZT/Vezt for vertebrate vezatin, a membrane-associated member of the vezatin family. Its experimentally supported two-pass topology, cytoplasmic termini, and binding to the FERM-containing myosin VIIA are consistent with the supplied Vezatin/PF12632 and myosin-binding domain annotations. Fungal VezA and Drosophila Vezl are distant homologs and are discussed only as comparative evidence—not as the human protein.

VEZT is not an enzyme or transporter. Its best-supported primary function is that of a non-catalytic membrane–cytoskeleton adaptor and stabilizer. It is recruited to mature adherens junctions and other specialized membrane assemblies, where it connects membrane complexes to myosin/actin machinery. Depending on cell type, it contributes to epithelial-junction stability, cochlear mechanical resilience, stabilization and maturation of postsynaptic acetylcholine-receptor arrays, and possibly cargo-selective retrograde endosomal transport. The strongest human disease connection is an endometriosis-associated regulatory locus near VEZT, but causality and clinical utility remain unproven.

1. Protein identity, architecture, and biochemical class

The evidence supports a protein containing two closely spaced membrane-spanning segments, with both N- and C-terminal regions on the cytoplasmic side. In MDCKII cells, antibodies against either terminus required permeabilization; extracellular biotinylation and protease accessibility indicated that vezatin lacks a substantial extracellular domain. Endogenous bands of approximately 90 and 70 kDa were observed, close to predicted 88- and 71-kDa isoforms (Bahloul et al., published May 2009; https://doi.org/10.1002/emmm.200900015). Human endometrial blots instead detected bands near 100 and 90 kDa, illustrating isoform-, antibody-, or modification-dependent apparent masses (bahloul2009vezatinanintegral pages 2-3, holdsworthcarson2016endometrialvezatinand pages 8-9).

The central membrane region and cytoplasmic tails fit an intramembrane scaffold/adaptor model rather than a receptor with a large extracellular ligand-binding domain. Comparative sequence analysis predicts a central α-helical transmembrane region and relatively disordered terminal regions. The annotated myosin-binding domain is functionally coherent with binding between vezatin’s cytoplasmic C-terminal region and the FERM domain of myosin VIIA (spinner2020aconservedrole pages 10-15, sousa2004unconventionalmyosinviia pages 5-7).

Accordingly, no catalytic reaction, substrate specificity, ion conductance, or transported solute is known. “Substrate” terminology is therefore inappropriate: VEZT’s relevant molecular inputs are protein partners and membrane/cytoskeletal complexes.

2. Primary molecular function: stabilization of membrane–cytoskeleton assemblies

2.1 Adherens-junction role

Vezatin was originally characterized as a membrane component linking myosin VIIA to the cadherin–catenin apparatus. In polarized epithelial cells, vezatin and myosin VIIA localize at adherens junctions. The vezatin C-terminal region binds myosin VIIA, while junctional recruitment depends on the distal actin-binding region of α-catenin. Importantly, a direct VEZT–α-catenin interaction was not detected by two-hybrid analysis, so the frequently depicted α-catenin–VEZT connection is probably indirect and may require another component (sousa2004unconventionalmyosinviia pages 5-7, sousa2004unconventionalmyosinviia pages 1-2).

Calcium-switch experiments showed that E-cadherin arrives at nascent contacts before vezatin; vezatin appears later and becomes concentrated at stabilized contacts. This argues that VEZT is not a core initiator of cadherin adhesion but a maturation and mechanical-stability factor (bahloul2009vezatinanintegral pages 8-9).

A mechanistic application of this machinery occurs during Listeria monocytogenes entry through bacterial InlA binding to E-cadherin. In human Caco-2 epithelial cells, a myosin-VIIA tail reporter was recruited around 72 ± 5% of InlA-coated beads versus 7 ± 2.4% of InlB-coated beads. Dominant-negative myosin-VIIA tails or vezatin C-terminal fragments reduced InlA-dependent bacterial/bead uptake approximately threefold. These experiments demonstrate functional coupling among E-cadherin-associated machinery, vezatin, myosin VIIA, and cortical actin, although pathogen entry is an experimental exploitation of the complex rather than VEZT’s normal physiological purpose (Sousa et al., published April 2004; https://doi.org/10.1242/jcs.01066) (sousa2004unconventionalmyosinviia pages 5-7, sousa2004unconventionalmyosinviia pages 4-5).

2.2 Radixin and cortical actin

Vezatin directly interacts with radixin in its actin-binding conformation and associates with actin filaments at cell–cell junctions. Together with myosin VIIA binding, this supports a broader role in attaching membrane domains to mechanically active cortical actin rather than a single linear cadherin pathway (Bahloul et al., May 2009; https://doi.org/10.1002/emmm.200900015) (bahloul2009vezatinanintegral pages 2-3).

3. Cellular and tissue localization

The principal site of action is the cytoplasmic face of specialized plasma-membrane domains:

  • Epithelial adherens junctions, where it is recruited relatively late during junction maturation.
  • Cochlear hair-cell/supporting-cell junctions, particularly mechanically stressed outer-hair-cell–Deiters-cell contacts (bahloul2009vezatinanintegral pages 2-3, bahloul2009vezatinanintegral pages 8-9).
  • Postsynaptic membrane of the neuromuscular junction, where mouse vezatin associates directly with acetylcholine receptors independently of rapsyn (koppel2019vezatinisrequired pages 1-6).
  • Dendritic spines and neuronal compartments, supported by vertebrate studies, although direct human-neuronal evidence remains limited.
  • Human endometrium and myometrium. Immunohistochemistry detected nuclear and cytoplasmic staining, with cell-compartment and menstrual-cycle dependence. Secretory-phase gland-associated protein increased significantly, whereas transcript abundance did not show the same cycle dependence (holdsworthcarson2016endometrialvezatinand pages 12-13, holdsworthcarson2016endometrialvezatinand pages 6-7).

The reported nuclear staining in human endometrium is less readily reconciled with an integral-membrane topology and should be treated cautiously until validated with isoform-specific reagents, orthogonal imaging, or subcellular fractionation.

4. Biological processes and pathways

4.1 Cochlear junction stability and resistance to mechanical stress

Conditional loss of Vezt in mouse inner-ear sensory cells caused exceptional susceptibility to acoustic trauma: one minute of 105-dB broadband sound produced irreversible hearing loss. Mutants also developed progressive hearing and vestibular dysfunction with hair-cell death. At 12 and 24 weeks, substantial outer-hair-cell loss was present; inner-hair-cell loss became prominent by 24 weeks. These findings support a role in maintaining junctional integrity under repetitive mechanical load rather than forming the junction initially (Bahloul et al., May 2009; https://doi.org/10.1002/emmm.200900015) (bahloul2009vezatinanintegral pages 8-9).

This phenotype does not establish VEZT as a monogenic human deafness gene. It instead identifies junctional VEZT abundance as a potential modifier of sensory-epithelium resilience.

4.2 Neuromuscular postsynaptic maturation

Mass spectrometry identified mouse vezatin as an acetylcholine-receptor-associated protein, and biochemical assays supported direct AChR binding. Muscle-specific loss did not prevent initial neuromuscular-junction formation but impaired later architectural maturation and receptor stability (Koppel et al., published September 2019; https://doi.org/10.1091/mbc.e19-06-0313) (koppel2019vezatinisrequired pages 1-6).

At postnatal day 60, 48.6% of mutant synapses retained an immature morphology versus 6.5% of controls; AChR density was 80.7% of control, postjunctional folding decreased 15%, and fold mouths opening into the synaptic cleft decreased 31%. After agrin withdrawal in cultured myotubes, AChR-cluster half-life fell from 12 hours to 5 hours. By day 180, mutants displayed premature dystrophic features, although overt denervation or motor impairment was not detected (koppel2019vezatinisrequired pages 11-15).

The most precise interpretation is that VEZT is a postsynaptic membrane-organizing and receptor-stabilizing protein, not a component required for agrin–LRP4–MuSK initiation itself.

4.3 Cargo-selective retrograde axonal transport

Drosophila and zebrafish genetics indicate a second, potentially conserved function in dynein-dependent retrograde transport. Zebrafish vezt disruption selectively impaired retrograde movement of Rab7-positive late endosomes, causing terminal accumulation, while Rab5-positive early and Rab11-positive recycling compartments were largely spared. Mutant sensory-axon terminals expanded to 638 ± 49 μm³, compared with 417 ± 63 μm³ in controls, and accumulated the dynactin component p150 (Spinner et al., published October 2020; https://doi.org/10.1534/genetics.120.303499) (spinner2020aconservedrole pages 19-24, spinner2020vezatinisrequired pages 6-8).

This is evidence for a cargo-selective transport regulator, not proof that human VEZT is a canonical dynein adaptor. Tagged human VEZT did not rescue the fly phenotype, and direct interaction with human dynein–dynactin has not been established. A 2024 study of fungal VezA proposed a role in dynactin assembly, but fungal VezA must not be conflated with human Q9HBM0.

5. Human disease evidence

5.1 Endometriosis: strongest human association

The strongest human evidence concerns rs10859871 at chromosome 12q22, approximately 17 kb upstream of VEZT. A meta-analysis associated its C allele with endometriosis at OR 1.19, P = 7.9 × 10⁻²⁰. In eutopic endometrium, the same allele was associated with increased VEZT RNA expression; the reported cis-eQTL effect estimate was 0.20, with P = 7.19 × 10⁻²⁶. Evidence that the variant affected neighboring transcripts was comparatively weak, making VEZT a plausible effector gene (Holdsworth-Carson et al., published May 2016; https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/dew047) (holdsworthcarson2016endometrialvezatinand pages 2-3, holdsworthcarson2016endometrialvezatinand pages 7-8, holdsworthcarson2016endometrialvezatinand pages 10-11).

The principal datasets included 122 eutopic-endometrial samples for eQTL analysis, 862 blood samples in one expression dataset, 56 endometrial western-blot samples, and 42 immunohistochemistry samples. VEZT protein increased in secretory-phase glands, but overall protein abundance did not differ significantly by rs10859871 genotype or endometriosis status; the disease comparison gave P = 0.1400. Thus, strong statistical colocalization between risk and RNA expression has not yet been converted into a demonstrated protein-level or cellular mechanism (holdsworthcarson2016endometrialvezatinand pages 8-9, holdsworthcarson2016endometrialvezatinand pages 12-13, holdsworthcarson2016endometrialvezatinand pages 1-2).

A biologically plausible hypothesis is that altered VEZT abundance changes epithelial cohesion, cytoskeletal remodeling, or survival/implantation behavior of endometrial cells. Nevertheless, the GWAS/eQTL signal proves neither that VEZT is the sole causal gene nor that increased VEZT directly causes lesions.

5.2 Cancer and other proposed associations

Small expression studies have reported altered VEZT in gastric and other cancers, often interpreting it as a junction-related tumor suppressor. Such findings are not yet consistent enough to redefine VEZT’s primary function or support clinical use. Loss of epithelial-junction stability provides a plausible connection to invasion, but expression correlation is not equivalent to tumor-suppressor causality.

No convincing evidence reviewed here establishes a pathogenic human VEZT coding variant as the cause of a Mendelian junctional, auditory, neuromuscular, or neurological syndrome.

6. Recent developments, 2023–2024

A targeted search found little direct 2023–2024 mechanistic work on human VEZT/Q9HBM0. Recent endometriosis reviews continue to list VEZT among GWAS-supported susceptibility loci, but they largely rely on the earlier rs10859871 and endometrial-eQTL studies rather than new causal experiments. A 2024 fungal VezA preprint proposed that the homolog helps assemble dynactin, offering an evolutionary hypothesis for the vertebrate transport phenotype; it does not demonstrate that human VEZT performs that function.

This scarcity is itself important for current annotation: the most defensible model remains grounded in the 2000–2020 mechanistic literature. Recent omics associations should not be allowed to displace the better-supported membrane–cytoskeleton stabilization function.

7. Current applications and translational status

Current applications are predominantly research implementations:

  1. Junction biology: VEZT localization and loss-of-function models are used to study maturation and mechanical stability of adherens junctions.
  2. Host–pathogen entry: InlA-dependent Listeria uptake provides a perturbable system for E-cadherin–VEZT–myosin VIIA coupling.
  3. Synapse biology: Conditional mouse models quantify AChR stabilization and postsynaptic architecture.
  4. Axonal transport: Zebrafish and fly models test cargo selectivity in retrograde endosomal trafficking.
  5. Human genetics: rs10859871 and VEZT expression are research biomarkers for investigating endometriosis pathogenesis.

There is no validated VEZT diagnostic assay, approved VEZT-directed therapy, or established clinical decision rule. Targeting VEZT could also be hazardous because animal studies indicate requirements in mechanically stressed epithelia and mature synapses.

8. Expert assessment and evidence gaps

The convergent expert interpretation is that VEZT functions as a context-dependent organizer of stable membrane domains. Three observations support this conclusion: it arrives after initial cadherin contact formation; it binds cytoskeletal or receptor-associated proteins; and its loss preferentially destabilizes mature structures under mechanical or temporal stress.

Major unresolved questions are:

  • Which human Q9HBM0 isoforms correspond to the differing 70–125-kDa species reported across tissues?
  • What protein bridges VEZT to α-catenin, if the association is indirect?
  • Is VEZT a direct human dynein/dynactin adaptor, a membrane cargo determinant, or neither?
  • Does rs10859871-mediated RNA elevation alter VEZT protein abundance in defined endometrial cell states?
  • Are junctional, synaptic, and endosomal functions manifestations of one conserved membrane–cytoskeleton mechanism?

Resolving these issues requires isoform-specific proteomics, endogenous tagging in human epithelial and neuronal cells, proximity labeling, cryo-electron or structural modeling of partner complexes, and allele-edited endometrial organoids.

Conclusion

Human VEZT/Q9HBM0 is correctly identified as vezatin, a vezatin-family, two-pass membrane protein with cytoplasmic interaction regions. Its primary function is best described as a non-enzymatic adaptor and stabilizer coupling specialized membrane complexes to actomyosin-associated machinery. It acts mainly at adherens junctions and specialized postsynaptic membranes; vertebrate genetics additionally suggests a cargo-selective late-endosome transport role. The human endometriosis locus near VEZT is statistically compelling but remains mechanistically and clinically unvalidated. Therefore, VEZT should presently be annotated principally as a membrane–cytoskeleton structural organizer, with disease and transport roles explicitly qualified by evidence level and species.

IPR026858 Vezatin, IPR026859 Myosin-bd). UniProt Q9HBM0 is 779 aa with two
predicted transmembrane helices at 139–159 and 162–182, a coiled coil at
430–462, and long disordered stretches in the C-terminal region (618–719,
757–779). Four alternative products are annotated (Q9HBM0-1/-2/-5/-6); isoforms
5 and 6 are flagged as likely NMD substrates.

Topology was settled experimentally in the mouse/canine system:
PMID:20049712.
Surface accessibility of the short extracytoplasmic loop was shown earlier:
PMID:11080149.

This topology is the main reason to treat the nuclear annotations sceptically
(see below) — there is no obvious route for a two-pass membrane protein with
both termini cytoplasmic to reach the nucleoplasm, although a non-membrane
splice product exists (the A34.2 ORF cloned in PMID:11080149 has no predicted
TM segment).

Molecular function — myosin VIIA binding is the anchor

Vezatin was discovered as a two-hybrid partner of the MYO7A tail:
PMID:11080149, with domain resolution:
PMID:11080149 (binds the FERM half, not the MyTH4 half; confirmed by
co-IP from co-transfected HEK293 cells and by pull-down of endogenous vezatin
from human Caco-2 extracts).

GO:0017022 myosin binding (IEA from InterPro:IPR026859) is therefore an
unusually well-grounded IEA — the domain mapping is validated by direct
experiment on the human protein. It is also the only MF annotation on VEZT.
Note there is no GO:0005515 protein binding line to worry about here.

Second cytoskeletal link: PMID:20049712 and PMID:20049712.
Vezatin binds the radixin FERM domain (aa 1–310) but not the closed
T564A form — i.e. it engages ERM proteins only in their open, actin-competent
state. This is a candidate for a future MF term but there is currently no GOA
line for it.

Caveat carried forward from the primary paper: a direct vezatin–alpha-catenin
interaction was never demonstrated ("it remains to be established whether or
not vezatin directly binds to α-catenin", Discussion of PMID:11080149). The
frequently drawn VEZT–alpha-catenin edge is probably indirect. Do not annotate
cadherin binding or catenin binding on this evidence.

Location — adherens junction is the core

PMID:11080149, with biochemical association to the cadherin–catenin
complex: PMID:11080149. Recruitment requires the E-cadherin cytoplasmic tail
(lost in ΔCyto and ΔCB but retained in ΔPR) and is restored by an
E-cadherin/alpha-catenin chimera. Vezatin is explicitly not at desmosomes or
focal adhesions.

Timing matters for choosing the right BP term:
PMID:20049712 and PMID:20049712.

Hair cells and the ankle-link complex (the Usher-adjacent part)

Two distinct hair-cell pools:
1. Adherens junctions between hair cells and supporting cells (this is the pool
whose loss causes the noise phenotype).
2. The base of developing stereocilia, facing the ankle links:
PMID:11080149, PMID:11080149, and
PMID:11080149.

Michalski et al. established the complex directly:
PMID:17567809;
PMID:17567809;
PMID:17567809.
Vezatin is delocalized from the stereocilia base in three independent mutants
(Vlgr1-null, shaker-1/Myo7a-null, whirler/Whrn-null), so the evidence is
genetic as well as biochemical.

Key curation judgment. GO:0002142 "stereocilia ankle link complex" is
defined as "A complex of proteins that connect growing stereocilia in
developing cochlear hair cells, composed of Vlgr1, usherin, vezatin, and
whirlin" — vezatin is named in the term definition, so the ISS part_of
annotation is exactly right. By contrast GO:1990696 "USH2 complex" is defined
as the four proteins USH2A, GPR98/ADGRV1, WHRN and PDZD7. Vezatin is not one of
them, and no VEZT coding mutation has been shown to cause Usher syndrome or any
Mendelian deafness. So: accept GO:0002142; do not add GO:1990696 on the
strength of the vezatin–usherin interaction alone. The two structures overlap
in space and share components but are not the same complex.

Functional consequence of losing hair-cell vezatin:
PMID:20049712 plus late-onset progressive hearing/vestibular loss with hair cell
death. This is a modifier of sensory-epithelium resilience phenotype, not a
monogenic deafness gene claim.

description: >-
  Vezatin is a multi-pass integral membrane protein of adherens junctions that couples
  cadherin-catenin adhesion complexes to the actomyosin cytoskeleton. It has two closely
  spaced transmembrane helices with cytoplasmic N- and C-terminal regions; the cytoplasmic
  C-terminal region binds the C-terminal FERM domain of the unconventional motor myosin
  VIIA (MYO7A), and vezatin also binds the FERM domain of radixin in its open, actin-binding
  conformation. Vezatin is recruited to cell-cell contacts through the cytoplasmic
  tail of E-cadherin and the C-terminal region of alpha-catenin, arriving after E-cadherin
  during junction assembly, so it acts as a maturation and mechanical-stability factor
  rather than an initiator of adhesion. It is broadly expressed and is found at adherens
  junctions of most epithelia; junctional vezatin recruits myosin VIIA and thereby
  generates tension between the junctional membrane and cortical actin, strengthening
  cell-cell adhesion. In cochlear and vestibular hair cells vezatin occupies two membrane
  sites, the adherens junctions between hair cells and their supporting cells, and
  the base of developing stereocilia, where it is a component of the transient ankle-link
  complex together with ADGRV1/VLGR1, usherin (USH2A) and whirlin, binding the usherin
  cytoplasmic region directly. Additional vertebrate sites and roles include the acrosome
  of differentiating spermatids and sperm, the postsynaptic membrane of the neuromuscular
  junction where it associates with acetylcholine receptors and promotes postsynaptic
  maturation and receptor stability, and dendritic spines, where it is needed for spine
  morphogenesis and functional synapse maturation. Loss of vezatin in mouse is lethal
  around implantation and causes delocalization of E-cadherin and beta-catenin, while
  hair-cell-specific loss causes extreme susceptibility to acoustic trauma and progressive
  hair cell death, indicating that the protein is required for junction integrity under
  mechanical load. Vezatin proteins are also conserved regulators of dynein-dependent,
  cargo-selective retrograde transport of late and signalling endosomes in invertebrate
  and fish axons, and the fungal homolog VezA promotes dynactin assembly. Vezatin has
  no known catalytic activity; its molecular role is that of a transmembrane adaptor
  and stabilizer of specialized membrane-cytoskeleton assemblies.
