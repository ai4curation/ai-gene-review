# LMNA notes

## Primary function

LMNA encodes A-type lamins, principally lamin A and lamin C. The strongest GO
claim is structural constituent of nuclear lamina: the products are intermediate
filament proteins that assemble into the nuclear lamina on the nucleoplasmic
side of the inner nuclear membrane. UniProt summarizes this conserved function:
"Lamins are intermediate filament proteins that assemble into a filamentous
meshwork providing a framework for the nuclear envelope, a network that is
required for nuclear integrity, nuclear positioning, shape and mechanics."

The experimental rows fit that structural interpretation. Ward and Kirschner
showed that bacterially expressed human lamin C assembled into filaments in
vitro and that MPF-dependent phosphorylation disassembled those filaments
[PMID:2188730, "Bacterially expressed human nuclear lamin C, assembled in vitro
into filaments, showed increased phosphorylation on specific sites in the
extract in response to MPF. Phosphorylation was accompanied by disassembly of
the lamin filaments."]. Heald and McKeon showed that phosphorylation sites in
lamin A control lamina disassembly in mitosis [PMID:2344612, "Mutations in
Ser-22 and Ser-392 that prevent phosphorylation at these sites block the
disassembly of the nuclear lamina during mitosis."]. Kochin et al. likewise
state the structural term directly [PMID:24741066, "Nuclear lamins form the
major structural elements that comprise the nuclear lamina."].

LMNA is a force-bearing component of the nuclear envelope, but it is not a
primary mechanosensor by the criteria in the mechanosensation guide. Its
mechanobiology contribution is nuclear stiffness, shape, and force buffering.
The LINC complex connects the cytoskeleton to the nuclear lamina through SUN and
KASH proteins; lamins A/C are a structural lamina partner adjacent to that
complex, not the channel or receptor that detects mechanical force.

## Secondary roles

Several annotations capture bona fide secondary lamina roles:

- Lamin A/C forms an interior nucleoskeleton with IFFO1 and restricts movement
  of double-strand DNA breaks [PMID:31548606, "IFFO1 interacts with lamin A/C,
  forming an interior nucleoskeleton."].
- DNA damage can remodel lamin A/C via ATR-dependent phosphorylation and lamina
  reorganization [PMID:37832547, "Upon DNA damage, lamin A/C is phosphorylated
  in an ATR-dependent manner, leading to changes in lamina assembly and,
  ultimately, NE rupture."].
- Nucleoplasmic lamin A/C pools are reported, but the nuclear lamina remains the
  core active site [PMID:23658700, "A-type lamins encoded by LMNA form a
  structural fibrillar meshwork within the mammalian nucleus."].

I kept these DNA-break and nuclear-interior locations as non-core rather than
making repair or hypoxia/senescence/migration readouts part of the core LMNA
function.
