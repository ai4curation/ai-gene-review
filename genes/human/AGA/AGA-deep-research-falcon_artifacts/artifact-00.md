| Feature | Experimental observation | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Reaction and native substrate | Human AGA (EC 3.5.1.26) hydrolyzes the amide linkage between asparagine and the innermost N-acetylglucosamine of glycoasparagines, producing L-aspartate and a glycan with a 1-amino-GlcNAc reducing end; the 1-amino group is subsequently lost nonenzymatically. GlcNAc-Asn and larger glycoasparagines are physiological substrates. (pqac-00000019, pqac-00000009) | AGA performs a late, obligatory step in lysosomal degradation of asparagine-linked glycoproteins rather than acting primarily as a general protein asparaginase. |
| Substrate specificity | The enzyme tolerates substantial variation in the attached glycan, but core α1,6-linked fucose on GlcNAc blocks cleavage and must first be removed by α-fucosidase. A free α-carboxyl group on asparagine is required. Older sources also state that a free α-amino group is required, but later substrate-analogue work reported that it is dispensable; the α-amino requirement should therefore not be treated as universal. (pqac-00000019, pqac-00000021, pqac-00000022) | Recognition is centered on the glycosylated asparagine linkage and its local chemical environment; upstream defucosylation can determine pathway order. |
| Ntn-hydrolase maturation | AGA is synthesized as an inactive 346-residue precursor. After signal-peptide removal, precursor dimerization and autocleavage between Asp205 and Thr206 generate α and β chains; Thr206 becomes the β-chain N-terminal catalytic nucleophile. The active enzyme is an (αβ)₂ heterotetramer. (pqac-00000018, pqac-00000019) | Autoproteolysis is both a maturation event and creation of the active site; variants that impair folding, dimerization, or cleavage can abolish activity without directly altering substrate binding. |
| Intracellular localization | Folding, dimerization, and autocatalytic activation occur in the ER. N-glycan phosphorylation and mannose-6-phosphate-receptor sorting through the Golgi/endosomal pathway deliver AGA to lysosomes, where subunit trimming occurs. Immunofluorescence and immunoelectron microscopy directly localized native AGA to lysosomes in control human fibroblasts. (pqac-00000018, pqac-00000021) | The physiologically relevant reaction occurs predominantly in the lysosomal lumen; AGA is a soluble lysosomal catabolic enzyme, not an established signaling protein. |
| Deficiency and diagnostic substrate | Loss of AGA activity causes aspartylglucosaminuria, with lysosomal and extracellular accumulation of GlcNAc-Asn and other undegraded glycoasparagines. A 2024 case with homozygous AGA c.698+1G>T showed experimentally confirmed abnormal splicing and urinary aspartylglucosamine of 208 mmol/mol creatinine, versus undetectable in the control urine. (pqac-00000039, pqac-00000040) | Substrate accumulation provides a direct biochemical readout of failed AGA-mediated glycoprotein catabolism and complements molecular diagnosis. |
| 2023 serum activity assay | A validated Asp-AMC fluorometric assay measured 20 AGU samples at a mean 0.11 mU/L (range 0.0123–0.251) versus 3.252 mU/L (2.503–3.897) in healthy donors; between-run testing included eight donors. Healthy activity was approximately 29-fold higher, although many patient values were near or below the 0.18 mU/L quantification limit. (pqac-00000034, pqac-00000035, pqac-00000042) | Serum AGA activity can distinguish this genetically homogeneous AGU cohort from controls and can serve as a pharmacodynamic biomarker, but very low patient activities should preferably be compared within the same analytical run. |
