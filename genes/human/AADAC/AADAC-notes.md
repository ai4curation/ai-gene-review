# AADAC review notes

## Biological synthesis

AADAC (P22760) is a membrane-anchored serine hydrolase/deacetylase of the endoplasmic reticulum, expressed most strongly in human liver and gastrointestinal tissues. The human protein hydrolyzes both N-acyl and O-acetyl bonds in structurally diverse xenobiotics. Native human liver microsomal enzyme was purified before the gene was cloned [PMID:2043131, "An acetylaminofluorene deacetylase was purified 90 fold from human liver microsomes by PEG-fractionation, anion exchange and hydrophobic interaction chromatography."], and the cloned cDNA was linked back to that protein by tryptic peptides [PMID:8063807, "The cDNA was confirmed to be that for DAC in tryptic peptides from the purified human liver protein."].

Direct human recombinant and microsomal experiments establish a broad drug-hydrolysis role. Recombinant AADAC, but not CES1 or CES2, hydrolyzed flutamide, and activity in 50 human liver microsomal samples correlated with AADAC abundance [PMID:19339378, "In human liver microsomal samples (n = 50), the flutamide hydrolase activities were significantly correlated with the expression levels of AADAC protein (r = 0.66, p < 0.001)."]. Recombinant human AADAC also deacetylated rifampicin, rifabutin, and rifapentine, with microsomal kinetics and inhibition patterns supporting the same assignment [PMID:21856291, "The involvement of AADAC in the deacetylation of rifamycins in HLM was verified by the similarities of the K(m) and K(i) values and the inhibitory characteristics between recombinant AADAC and HLM."]. Human AADAC expression and substrate preferences vary by tissue and species; notably, high rifampicin hydrolase activity was specific to recombinant human AADAC and human liver and jejunum microsomes [PMID:22207054, "High rifampicin hydrolase activity was detected only by recombinant human AADAC and human liver and jejunum microsomes."].

The biological consequence is substrate-dependent, so `xenobiotic metabolic process` is more accurate than a blanket detoxification role. Rifamycin deacetylation reduced cytotoxicity in the tested systems, whereas ketoconazole hydrolysis generated a downstream toxic pathway. AADAC overexpression made HepaRG cells sensitive to ketoconazole, and an AADAC inhibitor attenuated toxicity in primary hepatocytes [PMID:27422753, "Overexpression of AADAC in HepaRG cells with an adenovirus expression system elicited the cytotoxicity of KC. Cytotoxicity of KC in human primary hepatocytes was attenuated by diisopropylfluorophosphate, an AADAC inhibitor."].

AADAC is a type-II ER membrane protein with a short cytosolic N terminus and a large lumen-facing catalytic region. The strongest direct human evidence places activity in microsomes and the ER [PMID:19339378, "AADAC is specifically expressed in the endoplasmic reticulum."]. N-glycosylation at Asn-78 and Asn-282, and the folding defect of N282Q, further support maturation of the catalytic domain in the ER lumen [PMID:24125761, "This result indicated that AADAC was glycosylated at both N78 and N282."]. The cached topology abstracts use legacy E3/50-kDa esterase nomenclature and do not always resolve the assayed construct to human P22760. PMID:10318829 supports type-II ER topology but leaves that species mapping implicit [PMID:10318829, "Both are type II membrane proteins with the C terminus projecting into the lumen of the ER."]. PMID:15152005 maps N-terminal topology determinants, but its cached abstract does not establish the species of the E3 construct [PMID:15152005, "Previous studies revealed that the luminal orientation of 11beta-HSD1 and 50-kDa esterase/arylacetamide deacetylase (E3) is determined by their highly similar N-terminal transmembrane domains."]. PMID:9665742 likewise provides topology/structure evidence but does not expose a direct catalytic assay or unambiguous species assignment in the cached abstract [PMID:9665742, "The proposed model of the 50-kDa protein predicts one transmembrane segment at the N-terminus, flanked by positively charged residues on the cytosolic surface and negatively charged residues on the lumenal side of the hydrophobic domain, with most of the polypeptide projecting into the lumen of the ER."]. The PMID:15152005 localization and PMID:9665742 deacetylase IDA tuples are therefore left undecided rather than removed; direct human studies independently support both properties.

## Endogenous lipid evidence

An endogenous lipid role is credible but less directly established in human protein biochemistry than xenobiotic hydrolysis. Mouse Aada expressed in rat hepatoma cells increased turnover of endogenous diacylglycerol but did not increase in-vitro hydrolysis of membrane-associated triacylglycerol or cholesteryl ester [PMID:19654421, "Although AADA shares similarity with HSL and like HSL showed hydrolytic activity toward endogenously synthesized DG, we did not observe any in vitro activity of AADA toward membrane-associated TG or CE."]. This is the clearest mechanistic support for `diacylglycerol lipase activity` and `diacylglycerol catabolic process`, but transfer to human AADAC remains orthology-based.

Human hepatoma-cell perturbation links AADAC to neutral-lipid mobilization without directly identifying the hydrolyzed molecular species. AADAC re-expression restored cellular triacylglycerol lipolysis after HCV-associated down-regulation, while stable knockdown impaired triacylglycerol lipolysis and VLDL production [PMID:23542347, "The re-introduction of AADAC to infected cells restored cellular TG lipolysis, indicating a role for HCV-mediated downregulation of AADAC in this process."]. A newer human-cell study found that miR-222-3p suppression of AADAC increased lipid accumulation and that AADAC rescue reversed it [PMID:38797091, "Overexpression of miR-222-3p resulted in increased lipid accumulation in Huh-1 cells, which was reversed by AADAC overexpression."]. In contrast, approximately 50% AADAC knockdown in primary human hepatocytes produced no change in the tested metabolic assays [PMID:28099843, "AADAC knockdown had no effect on metabolic assays but decreased gluconeogenic gene expression (Figure 2)."]. These results support a qualified, secondary core lipid role and argue against a simple universal statement about VLDL direction or bulk triacylglycerol lipase activity.

Human AADAC also rescued cholesterol-acetate turnover in a yeast `say1` mutant [PMID:18034159, "The observation that expression of the human homologue of SAY1 , AADAC, in yeast rescues the sterol acetate accumulation phenotype of say1 Δ-mutant cells indicates that the human aryl acetamide deacetylase acts on cholesterol acetate and thus has overlapping substrate specificity with Say1 in vivo"]. This supports acetylesterase activity toward cholesteryl acetate, but not a general mammalian cholesteryl-ester hydrolase function or a demonstrated physiological sterol cycle in human tissues.

## Secondary substrates

AADAC can deacetylate pineal indoles in vitro. Recombinant human AADAC converted N-acetylserotonin to serotonin and also deacetylated melatonin and N-acetyltryptamine [PMID:37002641, "In addition to NAS, recombinant hAADAC can deacetylate melatonin (to form 5-methoxytryptamine) and N-acetyltryptamine (NAT) (to form tryptamine)."]. Human liver and brain extracts had eserine-sensitive activity, but endogenous human flux and AADAC-specific genetic dependence were not shown. This makes `arylalkyl acylamidase activity` a valid molecular activity but not sufficient evidence for a core physiological indole-metabolism role.

## Annotation decisions and provenance cautions

- Retain specific deacetylase, acetylesterase, arylalkyl acylamidase, carboxylic-ester hydrolase, and serine-hydrolase activities. Replace generic `catalytic activity` and `hydrolase activity` with informative child terms.
- Leave the PMID:15152005 human ER-membrane IDA tuple undecided because its abstract-only cache does not resolve the E3 construct's species; retain the independently supported human ER-membrane annotations.
- Leave the PMID:9665742 human deacetylase IDA tuple undecided because its abstract-only cache does not resolve species or expose a direct catalytic assay; retain the direct human deacetylase annotations from PMID:2043131 and PMID:8063807.
- Remove the PMID:17936933 AADAC serine-hydrolase IDA tuple because the cited source is a review and cannot support an IDA evidence code; retain the function through independent primary and propagated annotations.
- Retain the orthology-based diacylglycerol-lipase and diacylglycerol-catabolism annotations with explicit species and assay caveats; do not infer a direct human triacylglycerol-lipase reaction from cellular lipid phenotypes.
- Remove `detoxification` from PMID:27422753 because the cited AADAC reaction triggers ketoconazole hepatotoxicity. Retain `xenobiotic metabolic process`, which is neutral to activation versus inactivation.
- Keep the cholesterol-acetate/sterol result as non-core because it is a heterologous yeast rescue rather than endogenous mammalian sterol flux.
- Mark the APPBP2 `protein binding` row from the proteome-scale HuRI map as over-annotated; it supplies no AADAC-specific mechanism.
- A live QuickGO annotation search for UniProtKB:P22760 (accessed 2026-08-08) contains two extension-level chemistry errors that are absent from the fetched 16-column TSV: the PMID:34450168 acetylesterase row names abiraterone (CHEBI:68642) as input although the tested substrate was abiraterone acetate (CHEBI:68639), and the PMID:37002641 row places N-acetylserotonin (CHEBI:17697) as an output even though it is an input.
- Reactome:R-HSA-5689000 models phenacetin hydrolysis in the cytosol, whereas the catalytic bulk of AADAC faces the ER lumen; use the event for reaction support but record the compartment/topology mismatch.

## Open evidence gaps

1. Purified recombinant human AADAC should be tested against defined diacylglycerol regioisomers and membrane presentations, with catalytic-dead rescue controls, to establish whether the mouse-derived DAG reaction transfers quantitatively to P22760.
2. The route by which hydrophobic and soluble xenobiotic substrates reach the ER-lumen-facing catalytic domain remains incompletely resolved.
3. Human tissue models with strong endogenous AADAC expression are needed to distinguish direct lipid-substrate hydrolysis from downstream changes in lipid storage, fatty-acid oxidation, and VLDL assembly.
4. Endogenous AADAC dependence of N-acetylserotonin/melatonin and cholesteryl-acetate flux should be tested before assigning physiological indole or sterol pathways as core functions.
