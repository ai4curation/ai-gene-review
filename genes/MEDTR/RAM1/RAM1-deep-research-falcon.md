# Functional annotation of *Medicago truncatula* RAM1 (UniProt G7L166)

## Identity and principal function

**The target is MtRAM1, a plant GRAS-family transcriptional regulator, not the similarly named MtRAM2 enzyme.** The supplied UniProt record assigns G7L166 to *M. truncatula* RAM1, locus MTR_7g027190, with a GRAS domain (Pfam PF03514; InterPro IPR005202). Independently, a *Medicago* genetic study identifies **Medtr7g027190** as RAM1: a transposon insertion in that locus produces the *ram1-3* phenotype, and introducing a wild-type copy complements it. The publication does not itself print the UniProt accession, so the accession-to-locus mapping is taken from the supplied record. MtRAM2, in contrast, encodes a glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase downstream of RAM1. (park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 3-6, park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 6-8, gobbato2013ram1andram2functionandexpression pages 3-5)

**Primary functional annotation:** MtRAM1 coordinates the root-cell transcriptional program that permits arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi to develop *finely branched arbuscules* in the root cortex. These transient intracellular fungal structures are enclosed by a plant-derived periarbuscular membrane and provide the interface for reciprocal nutrient exchange. RAM1 is a regulator of this program—not an enzyme with a substrate, a phosphate transporter, or a lipid-export protein. Its GRAS-domain assignment and genetic behavior support a role in transcriptional protein complexes; direct DNA binding by MtRAM1 alone has **not** been established for the downstream promoters discussed below. (gobbato2013ram1andram2functionandexpression pages 1-3, park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 3-6, park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 11-13)

## Experimental basis: what happens without RAM1?

The strongest stage-resolved evidence comes from Park *et al.* (*Plant Physiology*, **October 2015**; [DOI: 10.1104/pp.15.01155](https://doi.org/10.1104/pp.15.01155)). At 2–6 days after inoculation, *ram1-3* roots had approximately normal numbers of successful initial penetration events and unpenetrated fungal hyphopodia compared with controls. Once fungi entered cortical cells, however, they generally formed a thick arbuscule trunk with **no branches or only one or two thick branches**; the fine branches of mature arbuscules were absent. The defect remained evident at eight days and at three and five weeks. Complementation and genetic co-segregation tied it to Medtr7g027190. Thus, under the conditions tested, MtRAM1 is indispensable chiefly for **arbuscule maturation and branching**, not for the first penetration of the root. (park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 6-8)

At three to five weeks, fungi colonized approximately **18–25%** of *ram1-3* root length, compared with approximately **70–80%** in wild-type controls. This substantial late reduction must not be mistaken for proof of an initial-entry block: impaired arbuscules can also prevent a productive infection from expanding. Earlier work had reported defective hyphopodium formation in *ram1-1*; Park *et al.* explicitly revisited that interpretation and found similar unbranched-arbuscule phenotypes for *ram1-1* and *ram1-3* in a side-by-side comparison. An independent study also observed predominantly small, incompletely developed arbuscules in *ram1-2*. These differing early- versus late-stage observations should be retained rather than collapsed into a single absolute statement about fungal entry. (park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 6-8, park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 11-13, gobbato2013ram1andram2functionandexpression pages 3-5)

## Pathway and molecular mechanism

**Upstream signals.** Fungal lipochitooligosaccharide signals engage the plant common symbiosis-signaling pathway, which includes nuclear calcium signaling and the calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase CCaMK. CYCLOPS/IPD3 and gibberellin-sensitive DELLA regulators act upstream of RAM1 transcription in the established pathway model. In *Medicago*, constitutively active DELLA1 increased RAM1 transcripts even without fungal inoculation; the accompanying induction of RAM2, RAD1 and EXO70I depended on RAM1. A CYCLOPS-bound RAM1-promoter element and CYCLOPS–DELLA interactions have also been studied, but important promoter, heterologous-cell and rescue experiments were conducted in **other species**, notably *Lotus japonicus* and *Nicotiana benthamiana*. They support the conserved model without demonstrating every molecular step directly in native *Medicago* root cells. (park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 11-13, pimprikar2018transcriptionalregulationofa pages 46-47, pimprikar2018transcriptionalregulationof pages 11-13, pimprikar2018transcriptionalregulationofa pages 45-46)

RAM1 also has a measurable **pre-contact transcriptional role**. Hohnjec *et al.* (*BMC Genomics*, **November 2015**; [DOI: 10.1186/s12864-015-2224-7](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-015-2224-7)) exposed five-day-old *Medicago* roots to fungal Myc-LCOs for six hours. Of **348** genes activated by Myc-LCOs but not Nod-LCOs under their thresholds, only **21** remained responsive in *ram1-1* and **28** in *nsp1-1*. Dependence on both GRAS regulators was therefore pronounced for this treatment-defined gene set; Nod-LCO responses were more preferentially NSP1-dependent. These expression data place RAM1 in the response to fungal signals but do not establish direct binding of RAM1 to all 348 genes. (hohnjec2015preannouncementofsymbiotic pages 5-7, hohnjec2015preannouncementofsymbiotic pages 7-9)

**Downstream arbuscule program.** In colonized *ram1-3* roots, induction of the phosphate-transporter gene **MtPT4** and the lipid-biosynthetic gene **MtRAM2** was undetectable in the tested assays; **STR**, **EXO70I**, VAPYRIN and several other arbuscule-associated transcripts remained near basal levels. Expressing RAM1 at roughly **100 times** vector-control levels in nonmycorrhizal roots caused small but significant increases in RAM2, STR, EXO70I and RAD1 transcripts. These combined loss- and gain-of-function results support **RAM1-dependent expression**, but diminished fungal colonization can contribute to changes measured in whole roots. MtRAM2 supplies a lipid-biosynthetic enzyme; STR/STR2 are membrane ABC transporters implicated in plant-to-fungus lipid transfer; MtPT4 is a plant phosphate transporter. None of those biochemical activities belongs to the RAM1 protein itself. (park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 8-11, park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 11-13, petersen2022functionalanalysisof pages 18-21)

A plausible molecular explanation is assembly of **GRAS transcriptional complexes**. MtRAM1 interacts with NSP2, RAD1 and TF80 in yeast interaction experiments; earlier work associates RAM1 with the RAM2 promoter through NSP2. Crucially, Park *et al.* could **not** demonstrate MtRAM1 binding to STR or EXO70I upstream sequences, nor activation of those promoters by a tested RAM1–RAD1 combination. Accordingly, describing STR and EXO70I as *RAM1-dependent genes* is justified; calling them *confirmed directly DNA-bound MtRAM1 targets* is not. (park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 8-11, park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 11-13)

RAM1 also participates in a transcription-factor cascade controlling exchange at the arbuscule. In *Medicago*, **WRI5a** is an AP2-domain regulator of lipid provision; WRI5a and RAM1 influence one another’s transcript abundance. Unlike the unproven direct MtRAM1–STR interaction, WRI5a binding to AW-box elements of the **STR** promoter was tested by promoter alteration, yeast one-hybrid and electrophoretic mobility-shift experiments. Increased WRI5a expression also increased RAM1 transcripts, but the authors noted no AW box in the examined 1-kb RAM1 promoter and considered this WRI5a-to-RAM1 effect potentially **indirect**. (jiang2018medicagoap2domaintranscription pages 1-2, jiang2018medicagoap2domaintranscription pages 4-6, jiang2018medicagoap2domaintranscription pages 9-10)

## Where does the protein act?

The relevant **tissue and cell type** is the *Medicago* root, particularly cortical cells participating in arbuscule development. RAM1-promoter reporters were active in colonized cortical regions; another experimental system detected promoter activation near approaching fungal hyphae **before physical contact**. Reporter activity establishes a site of *gene expression*, **not** the physical location of the RAM1 protein. As a transcriptional regulator working with symbiotic nuclear signaling factors, MtRAM1 is expected to act principally **in plant nuclei**, not at the fungal surface, in the periarbuscular membrane or outside the cell. Nuclear fluorescence from an MtRAM1–NSP2 bimolecular-fluorescence-complementation pair has been described in heterologous tobacco leaves, but the examined studies did **not** establish native MtRAM1 protein localization independently with an MtRAM1-only fusion in *Medicago* roots. Nuclear localization should therefore be recorded as a strong functional inference with limited protein-localization evidence, rather than as a demonstrated native localization. (gobbato2013ram1andram2functionandexpression pages 1-3, gobbato2013ram1andram2functionandexpression pages 3-5, park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 3-6, petersen2022functionalanalysisofa pages 212-216, park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 44-53)

## Developments in 2023–2024 and practical significance

A **2023** *Medicago* study by Zhang *et al.* (*Nature Communications*, **September 2023**; [DOI: 10.1038/s41467-023-41493-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-41493-2)) places the RAM1-associated program in a more finely regulated network. It shows that the downstream AP2/ERF factors **ERM1 and WRI5a** promote lipid-export genes and activate **ERF12**; ERF12 subsequently recruits a TOPLESS-related corepressor and opposes arbuscule-promoting transcription. The newly demonstrated promoter-binding and negative-feedback mechanisms concern ERM1, WRI5a and ERF12; they should **not** be attributed to direct MtRAM1 binding of those promoters. The authors also report that ERM1 overexpression increases RAM1 expression, consistent with network feedback rather than proof of direct ERM1–RAM1 promoter binding. (zhang2023controlofarbuscule pages 5-6, zhang2023controlofarbuscule pages 10-11, zhang2023controlofarbuscule pages 9-10, zhang2023controlofarbuscule pages 8-9)

The particularly informative **2024** RAM1 manipulation study is in **tomato, not the requested *Medicago* protein**. Ho-Plágaro *et al.* (*Journal of Experimental Botany*, **May 2024**; [DOI: 10.1093/jxb/erae210](https://doi.org/10.1093/jxb/erae210)) tested the tomato orthologue SlGRAS27/SlRAM1, gene **Solyc02g094340.1**. Silencing impaired colonization and arbuscules, but constitutive overexpression also reduced colonized root length—by **41% at 82 days**—and suppressed strigolactone production. A strigolactone analogue largely rescued overall colonization, not normal arbuscule development. This is a warning that RAM1 dosage and timing matter; it is **not** direct evidence that *Medicago* G7L166 represses strigolactone synthesis. (hoplagaro2024adualregulatory pages 4-6, hoplagaro2024adualregulatory pages 3-4, hoplagaro2024adualregulatory pages 11-12)

**Current implementation is primarily experimental.** RAM1 mutants, complemented roots, promoter reporters and targeted expression profiling are established tools for dissecting mycorrhizal development. In the model grass *Brachypodium*, constitutive expression of its own RAM1 orthologue yielded approximately **twice the arbuscule density** in the sampled root volume without a significant increase in total fungal colonization; it also caused stunted, bushy shoots and poor seed production. Such proof-of-concept work motivates more spatially controlled manipulation but does **not** demonstrate improved yield or field deployment of engineered MtRAM1. Likewise, a *Medicago* seaweed-extract experiment reported increased mycorrhization and leaf area, yet its RAM1/RAD1 expression response did not establish RAM1 as the cause of those benefits. (muller2020constitutiveoverexpressionof pages 6-8, muller2020constitutiveoverexpressionof pages 1-4, hines2021alkalineextractof pages 7-8, hines2021alkalineextractof pages 2-4, hines2021alkalineextractof pages 1-2)

**Annotation judgment:** assign G7L166 a high-confidence role as a **GRAS-family regulator of arbuscular-mycorrhizal transcription and cortical arbuscule branching**, with experimentally supported effects on symbiotic lipid-supply, membrane-development and nutrient-exchange gene programs. Assign a *probable nuclear site of action*, while flagging native protein localization and direct binding of most putative downstream genes as unresolved. (park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 6-8, park2015hyphalbranchingduring pages 11-13, petersen2022functionalanalysisofa pages 212-216)
