Functional Annotation Report: LolE (Q88KY3 / PP_2156) in Pseudomonas putida KT2440

Summary

LolE (UniProt Q88KY3; locus PP_2156) is one of the two integral inner‑membrane permease subunits of the LolCDE ABC transporter — the "lipoprotein‑releasing system" that performs the first committed, ATP‑dependent step of the Lol pathway that sorts lipoproteins to the outer membrane of Gram‑negative bacteria. It is not itself a lipoprotein; the UniProt "Lipoprotein" descriptor refers to its substrate. LolE is a polytopic membrane protein of 413 residues with four transmembrane segments and a large periplasmic loop that carries the conserved cargo‑binding cleft engaging the lipoprotein's acyl anchor. Together with its paralog LolC (PP_2154) and two copies of the nucleotide‑binding ATPase LolD (PP_2155), LolE extrudes mature, triacylated, outer‑membrane‑destined lipoproteins from the outer leaflet of the inner membrane and hands them to the periplasmic chaperone LolA for delivery to LolB at the outer membrane.

Mechanistically, LolCDE belongs to the MacB/FtsX ABC transporter superfamily, which operates by "mechanotransmission": the transmembrane domain contains no solute channel and instead relays ATP‑driven conformational changes from the cytoplasmic LolD ATPase across the membrane to the periplasmic domain, physically levering the lipoprotein out of the bilayer. Substrate specificity is governed by an avoidance signal — lipoproteins bearing aspartate at position +2 relative to the lipidated N‑terminal cysteine are not released and are retained in the inner membrane, whereas outer‑membrane‑destined lipoproteins are extruded. The transporter is essential for outer‑membrane biogenesis and bacterial viability, and it is a validated antibacterial target, with multiple chemical scaffolds mapping resistance mutations directly to the LolC/LolE subunits.

The functional assignment of P. putida Q88KY3 is anchored by strong orthology to the experimentally characterized Escherichia coli system (~39% identity to E. coli LolE), a complete syntenic lolC–lolD–lolE operon at PP_2154–PP_2156, conserved diagnostic domains (FtsX PF02687, MacB_PCD, ABC3_permease_C, LolCE_TM), and a bioinformatically confirmed topology matching the experimentally determined LolC/LolE architecture. Direct biochemical and structural characterization derives from E. coli and, for lipoprotein sorting fidelity, Pseudomonas aeruginosa; there is no P. putida‑specific functional study of Q88KY3. The annotation is nevertheless of high confidence given the convergent orthology, synteny, and domain evidence.

Key Findings

F001 — LolE is a permease subunit of the LolCDE lipoprotein‑release ABC transporter

Q88KY3 is annotated by UniProt as "Lipoprotein releasing system, permease protein," gene lolE, and assigned to the ABC‑4 integral membrane protein family. Its domain architecture — ABC3_permease_C (IPR003838), LolCE_TM (IPR011925), the MacB periplasmic core domain (IPR025857/MacB_PCD), and FtsX (PF02687) — is diagnostic of the LolC/LolE class of transporter permeases. In the experimentally characterized E. coli system, the LolCDE complex is composed of one copy each of the membrane subunits LolC and LolE and two copies of the nucleotide‑binding ATPase LolD (PMID: 19809197). The complex releases outer‑membrane‑specific lipoproteins from the inner membrane in an ATP‑dependent manner, forming a soluble complex between the lipoprotein and the periplasmic chaperone LolA (PMID: 18218629). As stated directly in the primary literature: "An ATP binding cassette transporter LolCDE complex releases lipoproteins from the inner membrane of Escherichia coli in an ATP-dependent manner, leading to the formation of a complex between a lipoprotein and a periplasmic chaperone, LolA" and "the LolCDE complex catalyzes the extrusion of lipoproteins anchored to the outer leaflet of the inner membrane" (PMID: 18218629). LolE is therefore a transporter subunit — specifically one of the two integral‑membrane permease components — of this machine.

F002 — The periplasmic domain of LolE carries the conserved substrate‑binding cleft

PhoA‑fusion topology mapping showed that both LolC and LolE possess four transmembrane segments and a large periplasmic loop, and that sulfhydryl‑accessibility differences and release‑inhibition data indicate LolC and LolE have distinct functions despite their similar topology (PMID: 19809197). Recent cryo‑EM structures of LolCDE captured with three different lipoprotein substrates (Lpp, Pal, and LolB) revealed that "the lipoproteins make a sequence-independent but structurally conserved interaction with a cleft on the surface of the periplasmic domain of LolE that promotes efficient transport" (PMID: 41557797). This directly assigns the conserved cargo‑binding cleft to LolE (rather than LolC), providing a structural basis for the functional division of labor between the two paralogous subunits. Full transport‑cycle cryo‑EM structures of E. coli LolCDE (apo, lipoprotein‑, LolA‑, ADP‑, and AMP‑PNP‑bound) further resolve how the transporter sorts and moves its cargo (PMID: 33782615).

F003 — Substrate specificity: OM‑destined lipoproteins are released; the Asp+2 "Lol avoidance signal" is excluded

The LolCDE complex mediates release of outer‑membrane‑specific lipoproteins from the inner membrane, whereas inner‑membrane‑specific lipoproteins that carry aspartate at position +2 are not released: "the inner membrane-specific lipoproteins possessing Asp at position 2 are not released by LolCDE because it functions as a LolCDE avoidance signal, causing the retention of these lipoproteins in the inner membrane" (PMID: 20419407). This "+2 rule" is the central determinant of lipoprotein sorting fidelity in the Lol system. Independent support comes from Gram‑positive systems, where Asp at position +2 tightens anchoring of lipoproteins to the cytoplasmic membrane (PMID: 28901671), and "Lpp possessing aspartate (Asp) at position +2 are not translocated because it functions as a LolCDE avoidance signal" (PMID: 28901671). The species that is released forms a water‑soluble lipoprotein–LolA complex that crosses the periplasm to LolB at the outer membrane.

F004 — Mechanism: MacB‑superfamily "mechanotransmission," not a solute channel

LolCDE belongs to the MacB/FtsX ABC transporter superfamily. Structural studies of the archetype MacB established that "The MacB transmembrane domain lacks a central cavity through which substrates could be passed, but instead conveys conformational changes from one side of the membrane to the other, a process we term mechanotransmission" (PMID: 29109272). Crucially, that work explicitly names LolCDE as a homolog sharing these features: "Homologs of MacB that do not form tripartite pumps, but share structural features underpinning mechanotransmission, include the LolCDE lipoprotein trafficking complex and FtsEX cell division signaling protein" (PMID: 29109272). The energizing cycle works because "reversible dimerization of the nucleotide binding domains drives opening and closing of the MacB periplasmic domains via concerted movements of the second transmembrane segment and major coupling helix" (PMID: 29109272). Applied to LolE, ATP binding and hydrolysis at the LolD ATPase dimer are transmitted through LolE's transmembrane helices to lever its periplasmic domain — and the bound lipoprotein — up and out of the membrane. Full transport‑cycle cryo‑EM of E. coli LolCDE corroborates this ATP‑coupled conformational cycling (PMID: 33782615). Consistent with this, Q88KY3 carries the diagnostic FtsX (PF02687) and MacB_PCD periplasmic‑core domains, and the operon's LolD (PP_2155; EC 7.6.2.–; 227 aa) supplies the ATPase.

F005 — Genomic context: Q88KY3 is the LolE of a complete syntenic lolCDE operon in P. putida KT2440

UniProt/KEGG records for P. putida KT2440 show three consecutive loci that constitute the transporter: lolC = PP_2154 (Q88KY5, 416 aa), lolD = PP_2155 (Q88KY4, 227 aa; "Lipoprotein‑releasing system ATP‑binding protein LolD," EC 7.6.2.–), and lolE = PP_2156 (Q88KY3, 413 aa, permease). Q88KY3 is 413 aa, assigned to the ABC‑4 integral membrane protein family (LolC/E subfamily), and localized to the inner membrane as a multi‑pass membrane protein (UniProt keywords: Cell membrane, Membrane, Transmembrane helix, Transport). Global pairwise alignment gives ~38.9% identity to E. coli LolE (P75958) and ~37.6% to E. coli LolC (P0ADC3), consistent with LolC and LolE being ancient paralogs arising from gene duplication. The syntenic operon order (lolC–lolD–lolE) mirrors E. coli and cleanly resolves the ortholog assignment of Q88KY3 as LolE rather than LolC.

F006 — LolCDE is essential and a validated antibacterial target

The outer membrane is an essential structural component of Gram‑negative bacteria, and "The LolCDE ABC transporter is the inner membrane component, which is essential for bacterial viability" (PMID: 29339384). Chemical‑genetic validation directly implicates LolE: pyridineimidazole inhibitors of lipoprotein trafficking select resistance mutations that "map to either the LolC or LolE protein of the LolCDE transporter complex" and block LolA‑dependent release of the lipoprotein Lpp (PMID: 25583975). An independent pyrrolopyrimidinedione inhibitor (G0507) also targets LolCDE (PMID: 29339384). Importantly for P. putida, the pathway's essentiality and druggability extend to pseudomonads: in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, "Lipoprotein transport from the inner to the outer membrane, carried out by the Lol machinery, is essential for the biogenesis of the Gram-negative cell envelope and, consequently, for bacterial viability" (PMID: 32989085), and a small‑molecule Lol inhibitor triggers a defined transcriptional stress response.

F007 — Upstream: LolE acts on fully mature, triacylated lipoproteins from the Sec/Lgt/LspA/Lnt maturation pathway

LolE engages a specific, fully processed substrate. Bacterial lipoproteins are synthesized as preprolipoproteins and exported across the inner membrane via the Sec (or Tat) translocon; on exit they are recognized by phosphatidylglycerol::prolipoprotein diacylglyceryl transferase (Lgt), which adds a diacylglyceryl group to the invariant lipobox cysteine: "Following synthesis, preprolipoproteins are transported through the cytoplasmic membrane via the Sec or Tat translocon. As they exit the transport machinery, they are recognized by a phosphatidylglycerol::prolipoprotein diacylglyceryl transferase (Lgt), which converts them to prolipoproteins by adding a diacylglyceryl group to the sulfhydryl side chain of the invariant Cys" (PMID: 32490790). Lipoprotein‑specific signal peptidase II (LspA) then cleaves the signal peptide, and in Gram‑negatives apolipoprotein N‑acyltransferase (Lnt) adds the third, N‑linked acyl chain to yield the triacylated lipoprotein (PMID: 15186407). This mature, triacylated species — anchored by its N‑terminal acyl moiety in the outer leaflet of the inner membrane — is exactly what LolCDE engages: LolA accepts "triacylated lipoproteins from the inner membrane transporter LolCDE" (PMID: 36037338).

F008 — Downstream and division of labor: LolE binds cargo, LolC primes LolA, LolA ferries to LolB

Within LolCDE the two membrane subunits are functionally distinct (PMID: 19809197). Cryo‑EM assigns the conserved cargo‑binding cleft to LolE's periplasmic domain (PMID: 41557797), whereas LolA is recruited and primed by LolC: "The structure highlights how LolA, initially primed to receive lipoprotein by interaction with LolC, further opens to accommodate the three ligand acyl chains in a precise conformation within its cavity" (PMID: 36037338). After release, a water‑soluble lipoprotein–LolA complex traverses the periplasm: "This complex traverses the hydrophilic periplasm to reach the outer membrane, where LolB accepts a lipoprotein from LolA and then catalyzes its incorporation into the inner leaflet of the outer membrane" (PMID: 20419407). LolA's ability to carry structurally diverse cargo arises because "LolA forms extensive interactions with the acyl chains but not with any residue of the cargo" (PMID: 36037338) — mirroring LolE's sequence‑independent recognition of the acyl anchor. LolB homologs are present in γ‑proteobacteria, the clade that includes P. putida (PMID: 39149330).

F009 — Bioinformatic topology confirms a polytopic inner‑membrane protein with a large periplasmic loop

Kyte–Doolittle hydropathy analysis (window 19, threshold 1.6) of the 413‑aa Q88KY3 sequence predicts approximately four hydrophobic transmembrane‑like segments (≈ residues 21–53, 267–299, 318–347, 378–401) separated by a very large hydrophilic stretch of ~214 residues (≈ residues 53–267). This matches the experimentally determined LolC/LolE topology of "four transmembrane segments with a large periplasmic loop exposed to the periplasm" (PMID: 19809197). UniProt independently annotates Q88KY3 as a multi‑pass inner‑membrane protein, and the large loop corresponds to the MacB_PCD/periplasmic domain (IPR025857) that hosts the conserved lipoprotein‑binding cleft (PMID: 41557797).

F010 — LolE is the transporter, not a lipoprotein: no lipobox is present

Sequence inspection of Q88KY3 shows the N‑terminus (MFRPLPIFIGARYTRAKRRNHF…) contains no lipobox motif ([LVIMFATG][ASTVILGF][GAS]C) within the first ~35 residues; the first cysteine occurs at position 384, deep within a C‑terminal transmembrane helix rather than at a processed N‑terminal signal‑peptide/lipobox junction. LolE is therefore not a lipidated lipoprotein cargo but a polytopic inner‑membrane transporter subunit (four TM segments + large periplasmic loop; PMID: 19809197) whose substrates are triacylated lipoproteins (PMID: 20419407, PMID: 36037338). The UniProt "Lipoprotein" keyword denotes its substrate/function, not modification of the protein itself — an important clarification that prevents misannotation.

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

The findings converge on a single, coherent picture: LolE is the cargo‑binding permease subunit of the LolCDE inner‑membrane ABC transporter that launches lipoproteins into the Lol sorting pathway. The full relay can be depicted as follows:

Localization. LolE performs its function at the inner (cytoplasmic) membrane and its periplasmic face. Its four transmembrane helices anchor it in the bilayer; its large periplasmic loop projects into the periplasm where the cargo‑binding cleft and the interfaces with LolA/LolC reside; and its ATPase partner LolD sits on the cytoplasmic face.

Catalytic / transport logic. LolE does not form a solute channel. Instead, ATP binding and hydrolysis at the LolD dimer drive reversible nucleotide‑binding‑domain dimerization, which is transmitted through LolE's transmembrane helix 2 and coupling helix to swing the periplasmic domain — the essence of MacB‑superfamily mechanotransmission. During this cycle the periplasmic cleft of LolE grips the triacyl anchor of the lipoprotein and physically extracts it from the outer leaflet of the inner membrane. The extracted lipoprotein is then transferred to LolA, which has been pre‑positioned by LolC.

Specificity logic. Recognition is sequence‑independent — LolE and LolA both engage the conserved acyl chains rather than the polypeptide, which is why a single transporter can sort structurally diverse lipoproteins. Fidelity is imposed negatively, by the Asp+2 avoidance signal: lipoproteins destined to remain in the inner membrane carry Asp at position +2, which prevents LolCDE from releasing them.

Division of labor within the complex. Although LolC and LolE are paralogs with near‑identical four‑TM/large‑periplasmic‑loop topology, they are functionally specialized: LolE binds the cargo (conserved periplasmic cleft), while LolC primes and recruits the LolA chaperone. This asymmetry, first inferred from sulfhydryl‑accessibility and inhibition data, is now confirmed by cryo‑EM.

For P. putida KT2440 specifically, all direct mechanistic evidence is inherited from E. coli (and, for sorting fidelity in pseudomonads, P. aeruginosa), but the inference is exceptionally strong: Q88KY3 sits in a complete syntenic lolC–lolD–lolE operon, shows ~39% identity to E. coli LolE, carries every diagnostic domain (FtsX, MacB_PCD, ABC3_permease_C, LolCE_TM), and has a bioinformatically confirmed topology matching the experimental LolC/LolE architecture.

The evidence base is internally consistent. The strongest, most direct evidence (cryo‑EM structures, topology mapping, chemical genetics) all derives from E. coli; the Pseudomonas‑specific papers (PMID: 32989085, PMID: 30992347) establish that the same machinery and sorting rules operate in pseudomonads, bridging the inference to P. putida.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. No P. putida‑specific functional study. All mechanistic, structural, and biochemical data derive from E. coli; sorting‑fidelity data extend to P. aeruginosa. The assignment for Q88KY3 rests on orthology, synteny, and domain conservation — strong but inferential. No published gene‑knockout, essentiality assay, or structure exists specifically for PP_2156.

  2. Substrate repertoire in P. putida is uncharacterized. The specific set of outer‑membrane lipoproteins that LolCDE sorts in P. putida KT2440, and whether the Asp+2 rule holds with identical stringency in this organism, have not been experimentally defined.

  3. LolC vs. LolE functional asymmetry is inferred, not measured, in P. putida. The cargo‑binding cleft on LolE and the LolA‑priming role of LolC are established in E. coli; conservation of the exact residues forming the cleft in Q88KY3 was not exhaustively verified at the residue level.

  4. LolB status in P. putida. LolB homologs are present broadly in γ‑proteobacteria, but a direct confirmation of a functional LolB ortholog and its locus in KT2440 was not established here.

  5. Quantitative kinetics unknown. ATP‑hydrolysis rates, transport turnover, and inhibitor sensitivity for the P. putida LolCDE have not been measured.
