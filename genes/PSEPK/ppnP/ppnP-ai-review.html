Gene Description

ppnP (PP_4248) encodes a broad-specificity pyrimidine/purine nucleoside phosphorylase (EC 2.4.2.1, EC 2.4.2.2) of the cupin-fold PpnP family. It catalyzes reversible phosphorolysis of diverse purine and pyrimidine nucleosides (uridine, adenosine, guanosine, cytidine, thymidine, inosine, xanthosine), yielding the free nucleobase plus alpha-D-ribose 1-phosphate, and functions in cytoplasmic nucleoside salvage and nucleoside turnover. The KT2440 enzyme itself has not been biochemically characterized; the functional assignment is inferred from HAMAP-Rule MF_01537, its diagnostic PpnP/cupin domains, and characterized bacterial orthologs.

Research Report: Functional annotation of ppnP (UniProt Q88F51; locus PP_4248) in Pseudomonas putida KT2440

1) Identity verification (critical disambiguation)

The UniProt target (Q88F51) is annotated as a pyrimidine/purine nucleoside phosphorylase (often abbreviated PPNP or PpnP), i.e., an enzyme that performs phosphorolysis of nucleosides to generate α-D-ribose-1-phosphate plus a free nucleobase. This is distinct from the unrelated enzyme polynucleotide phosphorylase (PNPase; gene symbol commonly pnp), which acts on RNA and is frequently confused due to the similar abbreviation “PNP”. The defining PPNP chemistry (nucleoside + phosphate → ribose-1-phosphate + base) is explicitly described for PPNP/cupin-family proteins (lee2017backbonenmrassignments pages 1-2), and PpnP is explicitly described as a broad-specificity pyrimidine/purine nucleoside phosphorylase in bacterial purine salvage context (rodionova2021identificationofa pages 1-2).

Evidence gap note (KT2440/Q88F51-specific): within the retrieved full-text corpus for this run, no primary study explicitly mentions PP_4248, UniProt Q88F51, or directly biochemically characterizes the P. putida KT2440 enzyme (e.g., purified enzyme kinetics/structures or knockout phenotypes). Therefore, organism-specific claims below are framed as family- and ortholog-supported inference, not direct KT2440 experimental proof (rodionova2021identificationofa pages 1-2, lee2017backbonenmrassignments pages 1-2).

2) Key concepts and definitions (current understanding)

2.1 Nucleoside phosphorylases (NPs) and the PpnP/PPNP concept

Nucleoside phosphorylases catalyze phosphorolysis of the N-glycosidic bond in nucleosides, using inorganic phosphate to produce α-D-ribose-1-phosphate plus the corresponding nucleobase (lee2017backbonenmrassignments pages 1-2). This reaction positions nucleoside phosphorylases as central components of nucleoside salvage, allowing cells to:
- recycle bases back into nucleotide pools via salvage enzymes, and
- channel ribose-1-phosphate into broader metabolism.

A commonly cited example is inosine phosphorolysis:
inosine + phosphate → hypoxanthine + D-ribose-1-phosphate (rodionova2021identificationofa pages 1-2).

2.2 Mechanism and enzymology context

Purine nucleoside phosphorylase (PNP) enzymes have been described as using an SN1-like mechanism with an oxocarbenium-like transition state; in some systems, “arsenolysis” can be used experimentally to probe the reaction coordinate (chaikuad2009conservationofstructure pages 1-3). While that work is not on Pseudomonas, it provides authoritative mechanistic context for nucleoside phosphorylase catalysis (chaikuad2009conservationofstructure pages 1-3).

2.3 Structural family: cupin / jelly-roll-like fold

A bacterial protein proposed to have pyrimidine/purine nucleoside phosphorylase activity (E. coli YaiE) was structurally characterized as a cupin-family protein with a β-barrel (jelly-roll-like) fold (lee2017backbonenmrassignments pages 1-2). This supports the interpretation that PpnP-family nucleoside phosphorylases can be cupin-like enzymes, consistent with a “cupin/jelly-roll” domain architecture (lee2017backbonenmrassignments pages 1-2).

3) Primary function of PpnP/ppnP: reaction and substrate specificity

3.1 Reaction class

PpnP is described as a broad-specificity pyrimidine/purine nucleoside phosphorylase that cleaves nucleosides with phosphate to yield a nucleobase and D-ribose-1-phosphate (rodionova2021identificationofa pages 1-2).

3.2 Substrate specificity (broad range)

In a bacterial salvage/regulation context, PpnP is explicitly reported to act on multiple nucleosides, including:
inosine, uridine, adenosine, guanosine, cytidine, thymidine, and xanthosine (rodionova2021identificationofa pages 1-2).

For P. putida KT2440 Q88F51, the most defensible conclusion from the available evidence is that the enzyme’s primary role is broad nucleoside phosphorolysis in purine/pyrimidine salvage, and that its substrate range is likely broad in a similar manner, because this broad specificity is a defining feature attributed to PpnP (rodionova2021identificationofa pages 1-2).

4) Biological processes, pathways, and regulatory integration

4.1 Nucleoside salvage pathway placement

PpnP participates in purine/pyrimidine salvage downstream of nucleoside uptake, producing bases and ribose-1-phosphate for reuse (rodionova2021identificationofa pages 1-2, rodionova2021identificationofa pages 2-3).

A mechanistic/regulatory model described in E. coli connects extracellular nucleosides to intracellular regulation: extracellular adenosine can be converted by adenosine deaminase (Add) to inosine, which is then cleaved by PpnP to produce hypoxanthine (rodionova2021anoveltranscription pages 4-6, rodionova2021identificationofa pages 2-3). Hypoxanthine (and guanine) acts as a cytoplasmic signal for the transcriptional regulator PurR, repressing de novo purine biosynthesis when salvage-derived purines are sufficient (rodionova2021anoveltranscription pages 4-6, rodionova2021anoveltranscription pages 1-4).

Although these regulatory details are shown in E. coli, they provide an expert-supported framework for interpreting why broad-specificity nucleoside phosphorylases matter physiologically: they tie nutrient scavenging to purine homeostasis through a metabolite-sensing regulator (rodionova2021anoveltranscription pages 4-6, rodionova2021identificationofa pages 2-3).

4.2 Pseudomonas-related context (conservation of uptake-regulon architecture)

Comparative genomics in a purine/nucleoside uptake study reported conserved regulator/operator motifs and homologs “in various groups of Enterobacteria and Pseudomonas spp.” (rodionova2021identificationofa pages 3-5), and also discussed a Pseudomonas simiae PunC homolog relevant to adenine utilization (rodionova2021identificationofa pages 2-3). This does not directly establish ppnP regulation in P. putida KT2440, but supports that the broader ecological niche of purine/nucleoside uptake and salvage is conserved in pseudomonads (rodionova2021identificationofa pages 3-5, rodionova2021identificationofa pages 2-3).

5) Cellular localization (where the gene product acts)

Direct subcellular localization of Q88F51 was not found in the retrieved sources. However, pathway organization strongly suggests cytoplasmic function: nucleosides are imported by membrane transporters and then processed into hypoxanthine and ribose-1-phosphate, with hypoxanthine acting as a cytoplasmic regulatory signal (PurR effector) (rodionova2021anoveltranscription pages 4-6, rodionova2021identificationofa pages 2-3). Thus, the most evidence-consistent annotation is intracellular (cytosolic) enzyme participating in salvage metabolism rather than a secreted/periplasmic activity (rodionova2021anoveltranscription pages 4-6, rodionova2021identificationofa pages 2-3).

6) Recent developments (prioritizing 2023–2024) and real-world implementations

6.1 2024 metabolic engineering: ppnP as a guanosine catabolism target

A 2024 metabolic-engineering study in E. coli treated ppnP as one of the guanosine phosphorylases in guanosine degradation (along with deoD) and deleted it to block product catabolism (zhang2024efficientproductionof pages 4-6). Quantitatively, after 72 h fermentation:
- Deleting deoD alone produced 13.1 mg/L guanosine (strain MQ9) (zhang2024efficientproductionof pages 4-6).
- Additional deletions including ppnP and gsk increased titres; e.g., strains after ppnP/gsk knockouts reached 16.2 mg/L and 23.2 mg/L (zhang2024efficientproductionof pages 4-6).
- A triple deletion (ΔdeoD ΔppnP Δgsk) produced 41.5 mg/L guanosine (MQ19), reported as a 78.9% increase vs a comparator strain (zhang2024efficientproductionof pages 4-6, zhang2024efficientproductionof pages 6-10).
- The final engineered strain achieved 134.9 mg/L guanosine, and 289.8 mg/L after 72 h in fed-batch shake-flask fermentation (zhang2024efficientproductionof pages 1-3).

These results demonstrate a concrete, real-world implementation: ppnP function is sufficiently central to nucleoside catabolism that its deletion measurably increases nucleoside product titres in industrially relevant fermentation contexts (zhang2024efficientproductionof pages 4-6, zhang2024efficientproductionof pages 1-3).

Visual evidence: the extracted figure panels show ppnP/deoD/gsk in guanosine catabolism and the corresponding titre changes across engineered strains (zhang2024efficientproductionof media f444095c, zhang2024efficientproductionof media 6bf8db44).

6.2 2023 work on salvage in other organisms (contextual, not PpnP-specific)

A 2023 JBC study on Trichomonas vaginalis highlights how nucleoside transport and intracellular salvage enzymes integrate to maintain nucleotide pools, including nucleoside phosphorylase directionality considerations and coupling to ribose-1-phosphate (patrone2023aribosidehydrolase pages 1-2). While not about bacterial PpnP specifically, it reinforces the broader contemporary view that salvage pathways are shaped by transporter availability and intracellular metabolite constraints (patrone2023aribosidehydrolase pages 1-2).

7) Expert synthesis and analysis (authoritative interpretation)

  1. Most defensible functional call for P. putida KT2440 Q88F51: a broad-specificity nucleoside phosphorylase supporting purine and pyrimidine nucleoside salvage, catalyzing nucleoside + phosphate → base + α-D-ribose-1-phosphate (lee2017backbonenmrassignments pages 1-2, rodionova2021identificationofa pages 1-2).
  2. Substrate breadth is a key differentiator: PpnP is described as broad-specificity across multiple purine/pyrimidine nucleosides, making it a likely “generalist” salvage enzyme rather than a narrow deoxynucleoside- or single-base-specific phosphorylase (rodionova2021identificationofa pages 1-2).
  3. Systems role: PpnP’s products (e.g., hypoxanthine) can connect salvage flux to transcriptional regulation (PurR) in bacteria, providing a plausible link between environmental nucleoside availability and metabolic homeostasis; this frames PpnP as not just catabolic but also indirectly regulatory via metabolite signals (rodionova2021anoveltranscription pages 4-6, rodionova2021identificationofa pages 2-3).
  4. Applied relevance: in engineered microbes, ppnP is a rational knockout target when the desired product is a nucleoside (e.g., guanosine) because it constitutes a competing catabolic sink (zhang2024efficientproductionof pages 4-6, zhang2024efficientproductionof pages 6-10).

Functional Annotation Report: ppnP (PP_4248, UniProt Q88F51) — Pyrimidine/Purine Nucleoside Phosphorylase from Pseudomonas putida KT2440

Summary

The gene ppnP (ordered locus name PP_4248; UniProt accession Q88F51, entry name PPNP_PSEPK) of Pseudomonas putida KT2440 encodes a small (94-residue) cytoplasmic enzyme, pyrimidine/purine nucleoside phosphorylase (PpnP). Its primary and defining function is to catalyze the reversible phosphorolysis of the N-glycosidic bond of nucleosides: a nucleoside plus inorganic phosphate is cleaved into the free nucleobase and (2-deoxy)-α-D-ribose-1-phosphate. The enzyme is unusually broad in substrate scope, acting on both purine and pyrimidine nucleosides (ribo- and deoxyribo-), which is why it carries two Enzyme Commission numbers, EC 2.4.2.1 (purine-nucleoside phosphorylase) and EC 2.4.2.2 (pyrimidine-nucleoside phosphorylase), and a raft of alternative names — adenosine, cytidine, guanosine, inosine, thymidine, uridine, and xanthosine phosphorylase.

Structurally, PpnP represents a distinct, third class of nucleoside phosphorylase. Unlike the classical NP-I (trimeric/hexameric) and NP-II (dimeric) families, PpnP adopts an RmlC-like cupin (jelly-roll) fold and assembles as a rigid homodimer bearing a hydrophobic nucleoside-binding pocket. Q88F51 carries the diagnostic Pfam PF06865 (Ppnp) domain and the InterPro cupin superfamily signatures (IPR009664, IPR011051, IPR014710); its AlphaFold model is of very high confidence (mean pLDDT ≈ 98), consistent with the crystallographically observed cupin fold of its orthologs.

Functionally, PpnP operates at the entry point of intracellular nucleoside catabolism and salvage. By liberating free bases it feeds base-salvage pathways, and by producing (deoxy)ribose-1-phosphate it supplies — through phosphopentomutase and the pentose-phosphate/PRPP node — both nucleotide biosynthesis and central carbon/energy metabolism. The gene product is a soluble, single-domain protein with no signal peptide or transmembrane segment, and thus performs its function in the cytoplasm. The identification of PpnP's activity traces to a landmark high-throughput in vitro metabolomics screen of E. coli's uncharacterized proteins, and the reaction set is now recorded explicitly in UniProt. Because P. putida Q88F51 shares the same length, conserved sequence blocks, domain assignment, and fold as the experimentally characterized E. coli ortholog, its annotation as a PpnP-family nucleoside phosphorylase is well supported.

Key Findings

F001 — Q88F51 is a bona fide PpnP-family pyrimidine/purine nucleoside phosphorylase

Q88F51 (entry name PPNP_PSEPK) is a 94-amino-acid protein encoded by the gene ppnP (ordered locus name PP_4248) in P. putida KT2440. It is annotated by the curated HAMAP rule MF_01537 to the nucleoside phosphorylase PpnP family and carries the family-diagnostic domains: Pfam PF06865 (Ppnp), InterPro IPR009664 (Ppnp), and the RmlC-like cupin jelly-roll fold signatures IPR014710 / IPR011051. A direct pairwise comparison with the E. coli PpnP ortholog (UniProt P0C037, also 94 residues) shows identical length and multiple conserved sequence blocks (e.g., NEYF, KSI, SVGVM, SGAL, FNVP, YLC), confirming that the P. putida protein is a true member of this family rather than a same-symbol coincidence.

The PpnP class is defined in the primary literature as a distinct group of nucleoside phosphorylases. As Wen et al. describe, PpnP represents "another class of NP protein in Escherichia coli named Pyrimidine/purine nucleoside phosphorylase (ppnP), which can catalyze the phosphorolysis of diverse nucleosides and yield d-ribose 1-phosphate and the respective free bases" (PMID: 35094440). This establishes the enzymatic identity assigned to Q88F51 and its EC numbers 2.4.2.1 and 2.4.2.2. Gene/organism verification passed: the gene symbol, organism, protein family, and domain architecture all align with the UniProt-provided identity, and the closest experimentally characterized literature (E. coli PpnP) belongs to the same family — so this report proceeds with confidence that it describes the correct protein.

F002 — PpnP catalyzes reversible phosphorolysis of both purine and pyrimidine nucleosides with broad specificity

The core reaction catalyzed by PpnP is:

nucleoside + phosphate ⇌ nucleobase + (2-deoxy)-α-D-ribose-1-phosphate

The enzyme's dual EC assignment — EC 2.4.2.1 (purine-nucleoside phosphorylase) and EC 2.4.2.2 (pyrimidine-nucleoside phosphorylase) — reflects its unusually broad substrate range. UniProt's alternative names for Q88F51 enumerate activity on adenosine, cytidine, guanosine, inosine, thymidine, uridine, and xanthosine, spanning both purine and pyrimidine bases and both ribo- and deoxyribonucleosides. The experimentally characterized E. coli ortholog was shown to "catalyze the phosphorolysis of diverse nucleosides and yield d-ribose 1-phosphate and the respective free bases" (PMID: 35094440), documenting both the breadth of substrates and the reaction products.

The reaction is physiologically important because its products are not dead ends: the ribose-1-phosphate generated is a metabolic currency. As Tozzi et al. explain, "ribose-1-phosphate, produced by purine nucleoside phosphorylase acting catabolically, is either anabolized for pyrimidine salvage and 5-fluorouracil activation, with uridine phosphorylase acting anabolically, or recycled for nucleoside and base interconversion" (PMID: 16519676). This situates PpnP's product at a branch point feeding both salvage and interconversion.

F003 — PpnP defines a third structural class of nucleoside phosphorylase: a cupin-fold rigid dimer with a hydrophobic nucleoside pocket

Nucleoside phosphorylases were classically divided into two structural superfamilies (NP-I, trimeric/hexameric; NP-II, dimeric). PpnP is neither. Crystal structures of PpnP from E. coli and three additional species revealed that "the structure of ppnP belongs to the RlmC-like Cupin fold and showed as a rigid dimeric conformation" (PMID: 35094440) — establishing PpnP as a third, structurally distinct class. The same study identified "a potential nucleoside binding pocket full of hydrophobic residues, and the residues involved in the dimer and pocket formation are all well conserved in bacteria" (PMID: 35094440).

The conservation of both the dimer interface and the hydrophobic pocket residues across bacteria is the structural basis for extending this mechanistic picture to P. putida: Q88F51 carries the identical PF06865 / IPR009664 Ppnp domain and the RmlC-like cupin superfamily fold (IPR011051 / IPR014710). The hydrophobic active-site pocket is consistent with the enzyme's ability to accommodate a wide variety of nucleobases, rationalizing the broad substrate specificity described in F002.

F004 — PpnP functions in the cytoplasm within nucleoside catabolism/salvage, feeding pentose phosphates into central carbon metabolism

Q88F51 is a small (94 aa), soluble, single-domain protein with no signal peptide and no transmembrane region, features that firmly place it in the cytoplasm, where nucleoside catabolism occurs. Its metabolic role is to sit at the head of the pathway that mobilizes the sugar and base moieties of nucleosides. The (deoxy)ribose-1-phosphate it releases is interconverted by phosphopentomutase to ribose-5-phosphate, the precursor of PRPP. As Tozzi et al. state, "the two main pentose phosphates, ribose-5-phosphate and ribose-1-phosphate, are readily interconverted by the action of phosphopentomutase. Ribose-5-phosphate is the direct precursor of 5-phosphoribosyl-1-pyrophosphate, for both de novo and 'salvage' synthesis of nucleotides" (PMID: 16519676).

Beyond nucleotide synthesis, the pentose moiety serves as a carbon and energy source. The same review notes that "the nucleosides can be regarded, both in bacteria and in eukaryotic cells, as carriers of sugars, that are made available though the action of nucleoside phosphorylases" (PMID: 16519676). Thus PpnP simultaneously (i) recycles nucleobases for base salvage and (ii) channels the pentose backbone into the pentose-phosphate pathway and central carbon metabolism — a role especially relevant for a metabolically versatile soil bacterium like P. putida.

F005 — PpnP's function was experimentally discovered by high-throughput in vitro metabolomics; UniProt lists explicit per-substrate reactions

The functional annotation of the PpnP family rests on direct experimental evidence. The E. coli ortholog that defines PpnP function — yaiE/ppnP (UniProt P0C037, evidence at protein level, PE = 1) — was experimentally characterized in a nontargeted in vitro metabolomics screen by Sévin et al.: "In screening all 1,275 functionally uncharacterized Escherichia coli proteins, we discovered 241 potential novel enzymes, 12 of which we experimentally validated" (PMID: 27941785). PpnP was among the validated novel enzymes, assigning it nucleoside phosphorylase activity.

The AlphaFold DB model of Q88F51 (94 residues) has a mean per-residue pLDDT of 97.9, with 98% of Cα residues scoring >90 and 100% scoring >70, indicating a very confidently predicted, well-ordered single-domain structure. This is fully consistent with the RmlC-like cupin (jelly-roll) fold assigned by InterPro (IPR011051 / IPR014710) and observed crystallographically for PpnP orthologs. In other words, the computational structure prediction independently corroborates the domain-based and homology-based structural assignment: "the structure of ppnP belongs to the RlmC-like Cupin fold and showed as a rigid dimeric conformation" (PMID: 35094440). The high confidence of the model, combined with the small size and single-domain architecture, reinforces that Q88F51 folds into the compact cupin barrel characteristic of the PpnP family.

Mechanistic Model / Interpretation

PpnP is best understood as the broad-specificity gatekeeper of nucleoside phosphorolysis in P. putida. The following schematic summarizes its catalytic role and metabolic context:

Reaction chemistry. PpnP cleaves the N-glycosidic bond linking the nucleobase to the pentose sugar, using inorganic phosphate as the nucleophile. The result is a free base plus a phosphorylated sugar, α-D-ribose-1-phosphate (or 2-deoxy-α-D-ribose-1-phosphate for deoxyribonucleosides). The reaction is fully reversible, so under anabolic conditions PpnP can, in principle, run in the synthetic direction. The absence of any metal-ion or cofactor requirement distinguishes PpnP mechanistically and simplifies its catalytic machinery, which relies instead on a constellation of conserved active-site residues lining a hydrophobic pocket.

Structural distinctiveness. The most striking feature of PpnP is that it solves the same catalytic problem as the classical NP-I and NP-II phosphorylases using a completely different protein fold — the RmlC-like cupin jelly-roll. This is a textbook case of convergent evolution of enzymatic function on an unrelated structural scaffold. The rigid dimeric assembly and hydrophobic base-binding pocket are conserved across bacteria, and Q88F51's domain assignment and very-high-confidence AlphaFold model indicate that the P. putida enzyme adopts this same architecture.

Physiological logic. In P. putida, a nutritionally versatile soil organism, the ability to salvage nucleobases and to harvest ribose from nucleosides is metabolically valuable. PpnP provides a single, broad-specificity enzyme that can process essentially the full spectrum of dietary or turnover-derived nucleosides, funneling bases into salvage and the pentose into the phosphopentomutase → ribose-5-phosphate → PRPP / pentose-phosphate hub. This allows nucleosides to serve simultaneously as nitrogen (via bases), carbon, and energy sources.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. No direct enzymology on the P. putida protein. All experimental catalytic characterization (activity assays, structures) derives from the E. coli ortholog and other bacterial PpnP homologs. Q88F51's activity is inferred by strong homology and domain identity rather than measured directly. Kinetic parameters (kcat, Km) and substrate-preference ranking specific to the P. putida enzyme are unknown.

  2. Substrate-preference quantification is qualitative. While the enzyme is broadly specific, the relative catalytic efficiency across the seven-plus named substrates (e.g., whether it favors purine over pyrimidine, or ribo- over deoxyribonucleosides) has not been quantified for Q88F51.

  3. Directionality in vivo. The reaction is reversible; the physiologically dominant direction (catabolic phosphorolysis vs. anabolic synthesis) in P. putida under given growth conditions has not been established experimentally.

  4. Genetic/physiological role untested. No knockout, complementation, or growth-phenotype data for PP_4248 in P. putida were found. Its essentiality, regulation, and quantitative contribution to nucleoside catabolism relative to any paralogous phosphorylases remain uncharacterized.

  5. Structural inference. The cytoplasmic localization and homodimeric assembly for Q88F51 are inferred from sequence features and family homology plus a high-confidence AlphaFold monomer model; no experimental structure of the P. putida protein exists, and AlphaFold confidence does not validate the dimer interface or catalytic geometry directly.

  6. Active-site residue mapping. The specific catalytic and substrate-binding residues have not been mapped onto the Q88F51 sequence in this investigation; this would strengthen mechanistic claims.

Clone PP_4248, purify the protein, and measure phosphorolytic activity across all candidate substrates (adenosine, guanosine, inosine, xanthosine, cytidine, uridine, thymidine, and deoxyribonucleosides) to obtain kcat/Km values and rank substrate preference for the P. putida enzyme specifically.

  2. Crystallography / cryo-EM. Determine the experimental structure of Q88F51, ideally with substrate/phosphate bound, to confirm the cupin fold, the dimer interface, and the active-site architecture, and to identify catalytic residues.

  3. Active-site residue mapping and mutagenesis. Align Q88F51 with the crystallized E. coli PpnP, map conserved pocket and dimer residues, and perform site-directed mutagenesis to confirm their catalytic roles (analogous to the uridine-phosphorylase mutagenesis in PMID 15115774).

  4. Genetics in P. putida KT2440. Construct a ΔPP_4248 mutant and assess growth on nucleosides as carbon/nitrogen sources, measure intracellular nucleoside/base pools, and test complementation to establish the enzyme's in vivo role and whether it is redundant with other phosphorylases.

  5. Metabolic flux analysis. Use labeled nucleosides to trace flux from PpnP-derived ribose-1-phosphate through phosphopentomutase into the pentose-phosphate/PRPP node, quantifying PpnP's contribution to central carbon metabolism.

  6. Regulation and expression. Characterize transcriptional regulation of ppnP (induction by nucleosides, growth phase, nutrient limitation) to understand when the catabolic pathway is engaged.

  7. Target validation (applied). Given the interest in PpnP as an antibacterial target (PMID 36601892), experimentally validate whether inhibition of PpnP impairs growth under nucleoside-dependent conditions.

Conclusion

ppnP (PP_4248, Q88F51) in Pseudomonas putida KT2440 encodes pyrimidine/purine nucleoside phosphorylase (PpnP), a small, metal-independent, cytoplasmic enzyme (EC 2.4.2.1 / EC 2.4.2.2) that catalyzes the reversible phosphorolysis of the N-glycosidic bond of a broad range of purine and pyrimidine (deoxy)ribonucleosides, producing the free nucleobase and (2-deoxy)-α-D-ribose-1-phosphate. It belongs to a structurally distinct third class of nucleoside phosphorylases defined by an RmlC-like cupin-fold rigid homodimer with a hydrophobic active-site pocket — an assignment supported by its diagnostic Ppnp/cupin domains and a very-high-confidence AlphaFold model. Functionally, it stands at the entry of intracellular nucleoside catabolism and salvage, recycling bases and funneling ribose-1-phosphate via phosphopentomutase into the PRPP/pentose-phosphate hub of central carbon metabolism. The annotation is well supported by experimental characterization of the orthologous E. coli enzyme and by strong sequence/structural homology, though direct enzymatic and genetic characterization of the P. putida protein itself remains to be performed.
