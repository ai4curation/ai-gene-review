1. Summary (answer to the research question)

EutC is the small (β) subunit of ethanolamine ammonia-lyase (EAL, EC 4.3.1.7), an adenosylcobalamin (coenzyme B12)-dependent enzyme. Working as an obligate heterodimer with the large subunit EutB, EAL catalyzes the deamination of ethanolamine to acetaldehyde + ammonia by radical chemistry. EutC itself does not contain the catalytic TIM barrel; rather it partners with EutB to complete the cobalamin-binding pocket at the subunit interface and is required for a functional enzyme. In P. putida KT2440 the enzyme lets the cell use ethanolamine as a carbon and nitrogen source (given exogenous/endogenous B12), operating as a soluble cytoplasmic enzyme within a minimal, microcompartment-independent ethanolamine-utilization gene cluster.

2. Primary function: the catalyzed reaction and substrate specificity

Reaction (EC 4.3.1.7, Rhea:15313):

ethanolamine → acetaldehyde + NH4⁺

EAL is the committed, first catabolic step of ethanolamine degradation. Comparative genomics states plainly that "ethanolamine-ammonia lyase, the enzyme that breaks ethanolamine into acetaldehyde and ammonia, is encoded by the gene tandem eutBC" (Tsoy et al., 2009, 19783625). The products feed central metabolism: the ammonia serves as a nitrogen source, and acetaldehyde is oxidized (by the neighboring aldehyde dehydrogenase, below) toward acetyl-CoA/acetate as a carbon and energy source.

Substrate specificity. The physiological substrate is ethanolamine (2-aminoethanol). The family is characterized as deaminating "various vicinal amino-alcohols to oxo compounds" (UniProt FUNCTION, MF_00601), i.e. it is a vicinal-amino-alcohol ammonia-lyase; ethanolamine is the canonical and best substrate. The enzyme also deaminates both enantiomers of 2-amino-1-propanol (2-aminopropanol), non-stereospecifically; enantiomer-bound crystal structures rationalize the lower affinity for the (R)-form via a Val-α326 side-chain conformational change and predict pro-S hydrogen abstraction at C1 (Shibata et al., 2011, 21142024), consistent with ¹⁵N-isotope-effect/EPR studies on ethanolamine and (R/S)-2-aminopropanol (Poyner et al., 2006, 16734439). The specificity is thus narrow — small 1,2-amino alcohols. Crystallographic work shows the substrate is held by six hydrogen bonds to active-site residues, with Arg(α160) contributing binding through the substrate O1 hydroxyl (Shibata et al., 2010, 20519496) — the spectator hydroxyl group is mechanistically essential (Wetmore et al., 2002, 12440904).

Note on division of labor. The catalytic and substrate/cobalamin-binding residues lie on the large subunit EutB. Site-directed mutagenesis shows Arg160 of EutB is critical for both oligomer assembly and radical catalysis (R160A is inactive; R160K lowers k꜀ₐₜ/K_M ~180-fold) (Sun et al., 2008, 18444665). This reinforces that EutC is the non-catalytic structural partner that completes the interfacial cobalamin site.

3. Mechanism and cofactor (radical, B12-dependent catalysis)

EAL is a class II (adenosylcobalamin-dependent) isomerase/eliminase. Its cofactor is adenosylcob(III)alamin (coenzyme B12; ChEBI:18408), which UniProt notes "binds between the large and small subunits."

The chemistry is radical-based, not hydrolytic: 1. Homolytic cleavage of the cobalt–carbon bond of AdoCbl generates a 5′-deoxyadenosyl radical (Frey, 2001, 11395404). 2. This radical abstracts a hydrogen atom from ethanolamine, producing a substrate radical. 3. The substrate radical undergoes a 1,2-amino-group shift to a carbinolamine radical, which collapses to acetaldehyde + ammonia (Wetmore et al., 2002, 12440904).

Experimental EPR of purified EAL confirms "the formation of substrate-derived radicals during catalysis and the binding of cobalamin in the base-on mode," i.e. with 5,6-dimethylbenzimidazole coordinating the cobalt (Akita et al., 2010, 19762342).

4. Structural role of EutC (the small subunit) and localization within the enzyme

Quaternary structure. The functional unit is a EutB·EutC heterodimer (α·β) that assembles into a heterododecamer (EutB)6(EutC)6 — "the enzyme existed as the heterododecamer α6β6" (Akita et al., 2010, 19762342). Structurally it is a "trimer of the (αβ)2 dimer," with "6 large subunits form[ing] a core ring with 6 small subunits projecting outwards" (UniProt SUBUNIT; Shibata et al., 2010).

What EutC does structurally. The catalytic machinery — the (β/α)8 TIM barrel that holds the active site — resides in the large subunit EutB. The small subunit EutC "covers the lower part of the cobalamin that is bound in the interface of the α- and β-subunits" (Shibata et al., 2010, 20519496). Thus EutC: - forms the obligate protein partner of EutB (both genes are required for activity), - helps build and cap the cobalamin-binding site at the subunit interface, - is present in 1:1 stoichiometry with EutB, consistent with the finding that binding one cobalamin-analog inhibitor per αβ unit completely inhibits the enzyme (Akita et al., 2010).

Domain architecture / evolution. EutC arose by recruitment of a Rossmann-fold domain fused to an N-terminal α-helical domain (EutB is the paired TIM-barrel + N-terminal helical subunit) (Tsoy et al., 2009, 19783625) — matching the InterPro EutC_N/EutC_C two-domain layout of Q88QF2. The N-terminal region is dispensable for catalysis: N-terminally truncated EAL retains catalytic properties, and "a short N-terminal sequence is sufficient to change the solubility and stability of the enzyme" (Akita et al., 2010).

5. Subcellular localization

EAL is a cytoplasmic (soluble) enzyme. In enterobacteria such as Salmonella and E. coli, EAL is encapsulated inside a bacterial microcompartment (metabolosome) — a proteinaceous organelle whose shell (e.g., EutL, 19451619) confines the volatile/toxic intermediate acetaldehyde (Sturms et al., 2015, 25962913). UniProt's HAMAP-inferred "Bacterial microcompartment" localization for Q88QF2 is transferred from these organisms.

However, a P. putida KT2440-specific caveat applies. Genomic-neighborhood analysis (this work) shows the KT2440 eut cluster is minimal — eutC (PP_0542), eutB (PP_0543), an ethanolamine transporter (PP_0544, EutH-like), an aldehyde dehydrogenase (PP_0545, aldB-I, EutE-like), and a σ54-dependent regulator (PP_0546) — and contains no microcompartment shell-protein genes. A genome-wide UniProt proteome search (this work) confirms this: Pfam PF00936 = 0 hits, Pfam PF03319 = 0 hits, keyword "carboxysome" = 0, and keyword "microcompartment" returns only eutC/eutB themselves. Therefore P. putida KT2440 builds no bacterial microcompartment, and EutBC functions as a free cytoplasmic enzyme rather than inside a BMC. The HAMAP-inferred microcompartment localization is an over-transfer from enterobacteria and should be regarded as inapplicable to this organism.

Cofactor supply is intrinsic. Although UniProt phrases activity as requiring "external vitamin B12," KT2440 encodes cobalamin biosynthesis/salvage genes (cobN PP_3507, cobS PP_1681, cobD PP_1675, cobE PP_3409) and — critically — the corrinoid adenosyltransferase PduO (PP_1349, EC 2.5.1.17) that generates adenosylcobalamin, the exact AdoCbl coenzyme EutBC requires, plus periplasmic cobalamin transport proteins (PP_2418; HutB PP_0524). The organism can therefore both import and adenosylate B12 to feed EAL.

6. Pathway context in P. putida KT2440

EutC functions in amine degradation / ethanolamine catabolism (UniProt PATHWAY). The reconstructed minimal pathway from the gene cluster:

  1. Uptake: ethanolamine enters via the EutH-like transporter (PP_0544).
  2. Deamination: cytoplasmic EutBC (EutC = PP_0542 small subunit) converts ethanolamine → acetaldehyde + NH3 using AdoCbl.
  3. Aldehyde oxidation: the EutE-like aldehyde dehydrogenase (PP_0545, EC 1.2.1.3) oxidizes acetaldehyde, channeling carbon toward acetyl-CoA/acetate and central metabolism.
  4. Nitrogen assimilation: released NH3 is assimilated as a nitrogen source.
  5. Regulation: a σ54-dependent transcriptional regulator (PP_0546) controls cluster expression, consistent with the σ54 (RpoN)/NtrC regulatory cascade shown to govern eut loci in other proteobacteria (Barnes et al., 2024, 38713723) and linking induction to nitrogen status.
  6. Cofactor dependency: activity requires the coenzyme adenosylcobalamin; KT2440 supplies it via cobalamin biosynthesis/salvage plus the PduO corrinoid adenosyltransferase (PP_1349), and can also import B12 (PP_2418, HutB).

Ethanolamine itself is a widespread nutrient derived from turnover of the membrane phospholipid phosphatidylethanolamine (Kaval & Garsin, 2018, 29463652); catabolizing it lets P. putida scavenge additional carbon and nitrogen from its (soil/rhizosphere and host-associated) environment.

This "stripped-down," microcompartment-free architecture is consistent with the variable eut gene content across bacteria, including loss/absence of auxiliary genes in many lineages (Tsoy et al., 2009, 19783625).
