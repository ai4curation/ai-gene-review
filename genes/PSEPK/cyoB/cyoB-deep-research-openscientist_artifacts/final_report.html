Functional Annotation Report: cyoB (PP_0813, UniProt Q88PN6)

Cytochrome bo₃ ubiquinol oxidase subunit I — Pseudomonas putida KT2440

1. Summary (Answer to the Research Question)

cyoB (locus PP_0813, UniProt Q88PN6) encodes subunit I of the cytochrome bo₃ ubiquinol oxidase (EC 7.1.1.3), a member of the heme–copper respiratory oxidase superfamily. This subunit is the catalytic core of a terminal oxidase of the aerobic respiratory chain. It binds all of the enzyme's redox-active metal centers — a low-spin heme b and the high-spin heme o₃–Cu_B binuclear center — and there it carries out the enzyme's primary chemistry: oxidation of ubiquinol (2 e⁻) coupled to the four-electron reduction of molecular O₂ to water, using the released free energy to pump protons across the cytoplasmic (inner) membrane and generate proton-motive force. In P. putida KT2440 the enzyme (Cyo) is the dominant terminal oxidase during aerobic exponential growth and additionally acts as a respiratory redox sensor that feeds into global metabolic gene regulation.

The target identity is unambiguous and fully consistent with the UniProt record: gene symbol, EC number, protein family, and InterPro domains all match the primary literature on cytochrome bo₃/cyo.

3. Primary Function: The Catalyzed Reaction and Substrate Specificity

3.1 Reaction

Cytochrome bo₃ catalyzes the two-electron oxidation of ubiquinol and the reduction of molecular oxygen to water [Welter et al. 1994, 7961841]:

2 ubiquinol + O₂ (+ 8 H⁺_in) → 2 ubiquinone + 2 H₂O (+ 4 H⁺_out)

i.e., four electrons drawn from two ubiquinol molecules fully reduce O₂ to two H₂O, while additional protons are vectorially translocated (see §4). Subunit I (CyoB) is where the O₂ chemistry occurs: "subunit I contains the binuclear center where oxygen is reduced to water" [Park et al. 2002, 12080058]. The center comprises heme B at the low-spin site and heme O at the high-spin site of the binuclear center [Uchida et al. 2000, 11132640].

3.2 Substrate specificity

The physiological electron-donating substrate is ubiquinol — the reduced form of the membrane isoprenoid quinone pool (ubiquinol-8 in E. coli; Pseudomonas species use ubiquinone-9 as their major quinone). The enzyme also harbors a single, tightly bound high-affinity ubiquinone-8 that is a genuine redox cofactor: because cytochrome bo₃ lacks the Cu_A center of cytochrome c oxidases, this bound quinone acts as the "fourth electron" donor (a Cu_A substitute) that rapidly re-reduces low-spin heme b and lets the O₂ reaction run to completion instead of stalling at the ferryl intermediate; reconstitution with ubiquinone-8 converts monophasic to multiphasic kinetics [Puustinen et al. 1996, 8643669]. Thus there are effectively two quinone-binding sites (high- and low-affinity) [Musser & Chan 1998, 9545462], and photoaffinity labelling with azido-ubiquinone localizes quinol binding to the subunit I/II region [Welter et al. 1994, 7961841; Abramson et al. 2000, 11017202].

3.3 Structural basis of the active site

The first structure of a ubiquinol oxidase — E. coli cytochrome bo₃ — revealed that the membrane-spanning region of subunit I contains a cluster of polar residues exposed to the lipid bilayer that forms a quinone-binding site, and mutagenesis supports specific roles for these residues in electron/proton transfer [Abramson et al. 2000, 11017202]. The metal centers all reside in subunit I, the common functional core shared with cytochrome c oxidases [Gohlke et al. 1997, 9135135].

4. Mechanism: Proton Pumping and Energy Conservation

Cytochrome bo₃ is an A-type heme–copper oxygen reductase. Proton transfer to the catalytic site uses two well-defined pathways, the D- and K-pathways [Lee et al. 2012, 22056517]. The D-pathway terminates at the strictly conserved Glu286 (helix VI of subunit I), which controls proton delivery both to the active site for O₂ chemistry and to a proton-loading site for pumping; mutation of E286 blocks proton flux and abolishes turnover, exclusively by inhibiting proton transfer in the bo₃ enzyme [Egawa et al. 2011, 21684251; Svensson-Ek et al. 1996, 8885847]. Time-resolved FTIR shows redox-linked perturbation of E286 during the catalytic cycle, implicating it directly in proton pumping [Bailey et al. 2002, 11851414].

The net result is an electrogenic, redox-driven proton pump: electrons flow from ubiquinol through heme b to the heme o₃–Cu_B center to reduce O₂, while protons are pumped from the cytoplasm to the periplasm, contributing to the proton-motive force that powers ATP synthesis, transport, and motility. A quinol (Q-loop) contribution from the two quinone sites adds further charge separation unique to quinol oxidases [Musser & Chan 1998, 9545462].

5. Holoenzyme Architecture and Cofactor Biosynthesis

CyoB is subunit I of a four-subunit complex. Partial sequencing of the purified E. coli enzyme identified subunits I, II and III as the cyoB, cyoA and cyoC gene products (~58, 33, 22 kDa), plus subunit IV (cyoD, ~17 kDa) [Minghetti et al. 1992, 1322173]. Metal analysis shows 2 equivalents of iron (heme b + heme o₃) but only 1 equivalent of copper (Cu_B); the Cu_A center is absent [Minghetti et al. 1992, 1322173]. Accessory-subunit studies show subunit IV (CyoD) is required for Cu_B insertion into subunit I, acting as a domain-specific assembly chaperone that contacts helices VII/VIII (the Cu_B-binding region) of subunit I [Saiki et al. 1997, 9169436].

Operon and cofactor supply (bioinformatic, P. putida-specific). A UniProt query of P. putida KT2440 resolves a syntenic cyoABCDE operon: PP_0812 cyoA (subunit II, Q88PN7) – PP_0813 cyoB (subunit I, Q88PN6) – PP_0814 cyoC (subunit III, Q88PN5) – PP_0815 cyoD (subunit IV, Q88PN4) – PP_0816 cyoE2 (protoheme IX farnesyltransferase / heme-O synthase, Q88PN3). CyoE converts protoheme IX to heme O, the cofactor inserted into subunit I's high-spin (o₃) site [Minghetti et al. 1992, 1322173]. The operon organization matches the E. coli prototype, corroborating that CyoB assembles and functions identically.

6. Localization

CyoB carries out its function embedded in the cytoplasmic (inner) membrane. UniProt predicts 14 helical transmembrane spans (res 16–604), consistent with a deeply membrane-embedded catalytic subunit. The cytochrome bo₃ complex is an inner-membrane protein assembly: its subunit II (CyoA) is integrated/translocated across the E. coli inner membrane via the YidC and SecYEG pathways [van Bloois et al. 2006, 16481320; Celebi et al. 2008, 18155041]. The hemes and Cu_B of subunit I lie within the membrane; ubiquinol (a lipid-soluble substrate) is oxidized at a membrane-buried Q-site, and protons are translocated vectorially across the same membrane (cytoplasm → periplasm).

7. Biological Process / Pathway Context in P. putida KT2440

P. putida KT2440 has a branched aerobic respiratory chain with multiple terminal oxidases — Cyo (bo₃), CIO (cyanide-insensitive), cbb₃-1, cbb₃-2 and aa₃ — whose relative use varies with O₂ and growth phase [Morales et al. 2006, 16958757; Ugidos et al. 2008, 18341582]. Functional assays show Cyo has a leading role during aerobic exponential growth, while cbb₃-1 dominates in stationary phase/low O₂ [Ugidos et al. 2008, 18341582]. Expression is oxygen-responsive and coordinated by the ANR global regulator, which binds the cyo promoter [Ugidos et al. 2008, 18341582].

Beyond bioenergetics, the Cyo oxidase acts as a respiratory signal transmitter: inactivation of cyo has little effect on growth rate but broadly remodels the transcriptome and relieves the global (catabolite) control that represses alkane-degradation genes, identifying cyo specifically as "the oxidase that transmits a regulatory signal related to the activity of the electron transport chain"; loss of cyo is compensated by up-regulation of the CIO and cbb₃-1 oxidases [Morales et al. 2006, 16958757]. This links CyoB's catalytic (electron-transport) activity to metabolic gene regulation in this environmentally and biotechnologically important soil bacterium.

9. Supported and Refuted Hypotheses

Supported - CyoB is the catalytic subunit I of cytochrome bo₃ ubiquinol oxidase (identity confirmed by annotation, 69.1 % identity to E. coli CyoB, and strict conservation of all six metal ligands + Glu286). - Primary function = ubiquinol:O₂ oxidoreductase that reduces O₂ to water and pumps protons. - Localizes to and functions within the inner membrane. - In P. putida, it is the principal aerobic-growth terminal oxidase and a respiratory regulatory sensor.

Refuted / excluded - CyoB is not a cytochrome c oxidase (no Cu_A; electrons enter from ubiquinol, not cytochrome c). - The gene is not ambiguous or mis-assigned; no competing gene with the same symbol confounds the annotation.

10. Limitations and Future Directions

  • Most mechanistic/structural data derive from the orthologous E. coli enzyme; no P. putida CyoB crystal/cryo-EM structure or direct enzymology was found. The very high conservation makes transfer of mechanism robust, but organism-specific kinetic parameters (e.g., quinol specificity for ubiquinol-9, O₂ affinity) remain to be measured directly.
  • The precise molecular link between Cyo electron-transport activity and the alkane-degradation regulatory signal in P. putida is phenomenological; the downstream signalling components are not fully defined.
  • Proton-pumping stoichiometry (H⁺/e⁻) was not directly quantified here; the A-type consensus (~1 pumped H⁺ per electron, plus scalar/chemical protons) is inferred from the superfamily.
