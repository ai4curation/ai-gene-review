Gene Description

Ste9 (also called Srw1) is the fission yeast member of the Cdh1/Hct1/Fizzy-related subfamily of WD40 beta-propeller coactivators of the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C), the multisubunit RING E3 ubiquitin ligase that clears mitotic regulators at the end of mitosis and through G1. Ste9 is not itself catalytic: in G1, when Cdc2-cyclin kinase activity is low, the dephosphorylated protein docks onto the APC/C core and switches the ligase on toward its substrates, and purified fission yeast APC/C supplemented with Ste9 ubiquitylates the mitotic cyclin Cdc13 and securin Cut2 in vitro. In vivo, APC/C-Ste9 drives the ubiquitin-dependent destruction of the mitotic B-type cyclins Cdc13 and Cig1 in G1; it is not needed for the wave of cyclin destruction at mitotic exit, which is carried by the Cdc20-type coactivator Slp1, and it does not degrade the S-phase cyclin Cig2, although it restrains Cdc2-Cig2 activity genetically. By keeping Cdc13 low, Ste9 (together with the CDK inhibitor Rum1, with which it is partly redundant) holds cells in a pre-Start G1 state: it lengthens G1 in small cells, is required for G1 arrest upon nitrogen starvation and thus for mating and sexual differentiation, and prevents mitosis from being launched from G1. Cells lacking ste9 are viable and cycle normally in rich medium but are sterile, fail to arrest in G1 when starved, and are synthetically lethal with wee1 mutations because mitotic cyclin control is compromised; overexpression lowers Cdc13-associated kinase activity, blocks mitosis and induces an extra round of DNA replication. Ste9 is switched off outside G1 by Cdc2-Cdc13 phosphorylation on multiple Cdk consensus sites (Ser62, Thr98, Thr177, Ser214), which both dissociates it from the APC/C and targets it for proteolysis; its reactivation in pre-Start G1 requires PP2A-B56 (Par1)-dependent dephosphorylation, and the replication checkpoint kinase Cds1 also phosphorylates and inhibits it during S-phase arrest, protecting the MBF activator Rep2 from APC/C-Ste9-mediated destruction. The protein is nuclear, where the APC/C acts on its substrates, and it is heavily phosphorylated and inactive through most of meiosis.

Core Functions

APC/C coactivator for G1: in pre-Start G1, when Cdc2-cyclin activity is low, dephosphorylated Ste9 docks onto the APC/C core and switches the ubiquitin ligase on toward its substrates, driving the polyubiquitylation and proteasomal destruction of the mitotic cyclins Cdc13 and Cig1 (and, at the end of meiosis II, Mes1); purified fission yeast APC/C ubiquitylates Cdc13 and Cut2 only when supplied with an E2 and a coactivator, and Ste9 is the strongest activator of the five. The activity is switched off outside G1 by Cdc2-Cdc13 phosphorylation, which dissociates Ste9 from the APC/C and targets it for proteolysis, and is not required for the Slp1-dependent wave of cyclin destruction at mitotic exit.

1. Identity and domain verification

The 1998 Ste9 study cloned the S. pombe locus by complementation and identified Ste9 as homologous to budding-yeast Hct1/Cdh1 and metazoan Fizzy-related proteins. The same study connects ste9 with srw1, while the 2000 study explicitly calls the product the “fission yeast Fizzy-related protein srw1p.” Cdh1/Fizzy-related proteins contain a conserved C-terminal WD-repeat region; this agrees with the supplied InterPro annotations for Cdc20/Fizzy and WD40-repeat domains. (kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 1-2, yamaguchi2000fissionyeastfizzy‐related pages 1-2, kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 2-3)

The WD40 architecture is functionally appropriate for an APC/C coactivator: it provides a protein-interaction platform rather than a catalytic active site. Accordingly, Srw1/Ste9 should be annotated as a cell-cycle regulatory adapter and APC/C substrate receptor/coactivator, not as an autonomous ubiquitin ligase, enzyme, transporter, or structural cytoskeletal protein. The catalytic ubiquitin-transfer machinery resides in the APC/C and its associated E2 enzymes; Ste9 confers activation and substrate-selection properties.

2. Primary molecular function

2.1 APC/C-dependent proteolysis

The APC/C is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that polyubiquitylates selected cell-cycle regulators for proteasomal destruction. Srw1/Ste9 is the fission-yeast Cdh1-like coactivator that is most active in late mitosis/G1 and during G1 arrest. Its WD-repeat domain is expected, by family conservation, to participate in recognition of degradation motifs in substrates and their presentation to APC/C. The precise atomic substrate-recognition mechanism has not been solved specifically for O13286 in the retrieved literature, so this structural description is partly homolog-based inference.

Early Ste9 experiments were genetic and cell biological rather than purified-component biochemical reconstitutions. Nevertheless, several convergent observations establish the proteolysis model: Cdc13 persists in G1-arrested ste9 mutants; Ste9 overexpression lowers Cdc13 abundance and Cdc2–Cdc13/H1-histone kinase activity; and excess Ste9 can permit an additional round of DNA replication. The original investigators therefore concluded that Ste9 controls mitotic cyclin abundance through proteolysis. (kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 1-2, kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 11-12)

2.2 Principal substrate: Cdc13/cyclin B

The best-established physiological substrate is Cdc13, the major mitotic B-type cyclin. Srw1/Ste9 is required for G1-specific Cdc13 destruction but is not required for the separate wave of Cdc13 degradation at mitotic exit. Thus, fission yeast employs at least two temporally distinguishable cyclin-B destruction regimes: Slp1/Cdc20-dependent activity around anaphase and Srw1/Ste9-dependent maintenance of low Cdc13 during G1. (yamaguchi2000fissionyeastfizzy‐related pages 1-2)

This distinction explains the primary phenotype. Without Ste9, residual or accumulating Cdc13 sustains Cdc2 kinase activity when cells should be in pre-Start G1. Conversely, Ste9 overproduction lowers mitotic CDK activity sufficiently to inhibit mitosis and support genome rereplication. The effect is regulatory rather than a direct catalytic reaction by Srw1 itself. (kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 1-2, kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 11-12)

Cig2, an S-phase cyclin, has also been implicated genetically because cig2 mutation partially suppresses ste9 sterility. This supports excessive cyclin-CDK activity as a cause of the phenotype, but the retrieved evidence is weaker for Cig2 as a direct APC/C–Ste9 substrate than it is for Cdc13. (kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 1-2, kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 11-12)

2.3 Rep2 and the replication-checkpoint pathway

Rep2, an activating component of the MBF G1/S transcription factor, is another experimentally supported APC/C–Ste9 target. Rep2 levels oscillate, peak in G1 with MBF activity, and are controlled through ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis requiring APC/C function. During hydroxyurea-induced replication arrest, the Rad3–Cds1 checkpoint protects Rep2 by inhibiting Ste9: Cds1 phosphorylated Ste9 in vitro, and Ste9 phosphorylation/inhibition during arrest depended on Cds1. Stabilized Rep2 sustains MBF-dependent expression of genes needed for DNA replication, nucleotide production, S-phase regulation, and recovery. (chu2009acds1mediatedcheckpoint pages 1-2)

This mechanism gives Ste9 a precise checkpoint role: under an unperturbed G1 program, APC/C–Ste9 limits Rep2; under replication stress, Cds1 restrains Ste9 so that Rep2 and the S-phase transcriptional response remain active. This is more specific than describing Ste9 simply as a pleiotropic cell-cycle inhibitor.

2.4 Other proposed substrates

Ams2 has been reported elsewhere as an APC/C-dependent G1 proteolysis target associated with coupling histone-gene expression to DNA replication, but the full primary text was not available in the retrieved evidence set. It should therefore be considered a literature-supported candidate rather than assigned the same evidence weight here as Cdc13 or Rep2.

Slp1/Cdc20 is an alternative APC/C coactivator and can itself undergo APC/C-associated turnover. However, the retrieved 2024 Slp1 studies investigated Pmk1-dependent phosphorylation and degradation of Slp1, not direct regulation of Srw1/Ste9. Those results must not be misattributed to O13286. (sun2024negativeregulationof pages 9-10, sun2024negativeregulationof pages 19-21, sun2024negativeregulationof pages 7-8)

3. Regulation of Srw1/Ste9 activity

3.1 CDK-dependent phosphorylation switch

Srw1 appeared as multiple electrophoretic species in proliferating cells; phosphatase treatment removed the slower-migrating forms, directly demonstrating phosphorylation. The functional model is a reciprocal switch: Cdc2–Cdc13 phosphorylation inhibits Srw1/Ste9 during proliferative cell-cycle stages, whereas dephosphorylation activates it in G1. Active APC/C–Ste9 then degrades Cdc13, further reducing CDK activity and reinforcing G1. (yamaguchi2000fissionyeastfizzy‐related pages 1-2)

This creates a bistable regulatory relationship:

  1. High Cdc2–Cdc13 activity → phosphorylated/inactive Ste9 → Cdc13 stabilization.
  2. Low CDK activity → dephosphorylated/active Ste9 → APC/C-dependent Cdc13 destruction.

Rum1, the G1 CDK inhibitor, acts alongside Ste9. Rum1 directly inhibits CDK complexes, while Ste9 removes mitotic cyclin. Their partially overlapping activities establish and maintain the low-CDK state needed before Start. ste9 rum1 double mutants remain viable, indicating redundancy and network robustness rather than absolute Ste9 essentiality. (kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 11-12)

3.2 PP2A-B56/Par1 during nutrient-dependent G1 entry

A 2020 study identified PP2A-B56^Par1 as necessary for robust Ste9 activation during pre-Start G1. In nitrogen-starvation time courses extending to 300 minutes, par1Δ impaired Ste9 dephosphorylation and Cdc13 degradation and compromised proper G1 arrest. Experiments with inducible phosphorylation-resistant Rum1 further separated Par1 effects on Rum1 stability from effects on Ste9 activation. (stonyte2020requirementofpp2ab56par1 pages 13-14)

The safest mechanistic interpretation is that PP2A-B56^Par1 is required for Ste9 dephosphorylation, not necessarily that Ste9 is a proven direct Par1 substrate. No canonical B56-binding motif was identified in Ste9; Par1 could act through a noncanonical interaction, another phosphatase component, or indirectly by lowering CDK activity. (stonyte2020requirementofpp2ab56par1 pages 13-14)

3.3 Replication-stress inhibition by Cds1

The Cds1 pathway reverses the normal G1 logic under stalled replication. Cds1-mediated Ste9 phosphorylation inhibits APC/C–Ste9 and prevents Rep2 destruction, maintaining the transcriptional program needed to survive and recover from replication arrest. Thus, distinct kinases regulate Ste9 in different contexts: cyclin-CDK activity restrains it during normal cycle progression, whereas Cds1 restrains it during replication stress to preserve Rep2. (chu2009acds1mediatedcheckpoint pages 1-2)

4. Biological processes and phenotypes

4.1 Pre-Start G1 arrest under nitrogen starvation

Wild-type fission yeast responds to nitrogen removal by undergoing approximately two divisions and arresting in G1. ste9/srw1-pathway mutants fail to establish this arrest correctly; they can execute an additional S phase and accumulate with G2 DNA content. The phenotype is shared with defects in Apc10 and Rum1, demonstrating that APC/C-dependent proteolysis and direct CDK inhibition cooperate to enforce nutrient-responsive G1 arrest. (kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 1-2, kominami1998apc10andste9srw1 pages 1-2)

The biochemical explanation is coherent: failure to eliminate Cdc13 leaves excessive CDK activity, causing cells to pass Start rather than remain in mating-competent pre-Start G1. The 2020 Par1 study linked this phenotype to defective Ste9 dephosphorylation and impaired Cdc13 destruction. (stonyte2020requirementofpp2ab56par1 pages 13-14)

4.2 Differentiation, mating, and sterility

ste9 mutants are sterile or strongly mating-defective under nitrogen starvation. This is principally an indirect consequence of failing to enter the appropriate pre-Start G1 differentiation state, rather than evidence that Ste9 is a dedicated transcription factor for mating genes. Partial suppression by cig2 mutation supports inappropriate cyclin-CDK activity as an important causal mechanism. (kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 1-2, kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 11-12, kominami1998apc10andste9srw1 pages 1-2)

Ste9 is therefore important for coupling environmental nutrient information to cell-cycle exit and sexual differentiation. It is not generally essential for vegetative growth, although it becomes critical in sensitized backgrounds; for example, genetic interactions with wee1 reveal that Ste9-mediated cyclin removal is especially important when inhibitory control of Cdc2 is compromised. (kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 1-2, kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 11-12)

4.3 Replication licensing and rereplication

Overexpressing Ste9 decreases Cdc13–Cdc2 activity and can induce an extra DNA-replication round, whereas ste9 mutation suppresses rereplication caused by Cdc2 inactivation. These findings place Ste9 upstream of replication competence through removal of mitotic CDK activity. Ste9 is not a replication enzyme; it resets the CDK state that permits replication origins to become competent. (kitamura1998fissionyeastste9 pages 11-12)

5. Cellular localization

The retrieved Ste9-specific primary literature does not provide sufficiently strong endogenous imaging evidence to assign O13286 an exclusive compartment. Its established interaction with APC/C and its effects on Cdc13, Rep2, and nuclear cell-cycle transitions imply an important intracellular and likely nuclear functional pool, but “nuclear localization” should be treated as a mechanistic inference rather than a directly established exclusive localization.

Accordingly, the conservative annotation is: intracellular APC/C-associated cell-cycle regulator, acting on substrates that control nuclear cell-cycle events; precise dynamic localization unresolved in the retrieved evidence. There is no basis here to call it secreted, membrane-bound, extracellular, or organelle-specific.

No retrieved 2023–2024 publication directly revises the molecular annotation of Srw1/Ste9. The most recent focused mechanistic paper recovered was the 2020 PP2A-B56^Par1 study. This scarcity should not be interpreted as uncertainty about the core function; rather, Ste9’s classification as the fission-yeast Cdh1-type APC/C coactivator is mature, and recent work has concentrated on broader APC/C regulation and systems-level resources.

7. Current applications and real-world relevance

Srw1/Ste9 is principally a basic-research and systems-biology tool, not a current clinical target. Its applications include:

  • dissecting how ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis establishes a low-CDK G1 state;
  • testing nutrient-dependent cell-cycle exit and differentiation;
  • separating APC/C–Cdc20/Slp1 and APC/C–Cdh1/Ste9 substrate windows;
  • studying checkpoint control of transcription-factor stability through the Cds1–Ste9–Rep2 axis;
  • validating mathematical models of the fission-yeast cell cycle, particularly feedback between cyclin-CDK activity, APC/C activation, and replication competence; and
  • providing an evolutionarily tractable model for the conserved APC/C–Cdh1 system relevant to genome stability and proliferative control in higher eukaryotes.

Translation to mammals must remain explicitly homolog-based: human CDH1/FZR1 is analogous, but observations in S. pombe Ste9 are not automatically evidence for a human disease mechanism or therapeutic intervention.

8. Evidence-weighted functional annotation

Recommended primary annotation:

ste9 (srw1) — curation notes

UniProt O13286 (SRW1_SCHPO), PomBase SPAC144.13c, PANTHER PTHR19918:SF1 (Fizzy-related
subfamily). 556 aa, seven C-terminal WD repeats (246-541), disordered N-terminus carrying
the Cdk sites S62, T98, T177, S214 (PMID:10921878) and a mass-spec phosphosite S187
(PMID:18257517). Cited as the fission yeast Cdh1 exemplar in
modules/metaphase_anaphase_transition_and_mitotic_exit.yaml; the yeast CDH1 and human
FZR1 reviews are the finished comparators.

Identity and history

  • Isolated twice: as srw1 (suppressor of rad1-1 wee1-50), a multicopy suppressor of
    hyperactivated Cdc2 PMID:9398669,
    and as the classical sterile mutant ste9 PMID:9571240. Yamaguchi et al. note the
    identity: PMID:9398669.

What APC/C-Ste9 does

  • G1-specific mitotic cyclin destruction. PMID:10921876 and PMID:10921876
  • Independent confirmation: PMID:10921878. Mitotic-exit cyclin destruction is
    attributed to Slp1.
  • Cig2 is NOT an APC/C-Ste9 substrate (contrary to the brief I was given, which said Ste9
    targets Cig2). Yamaguchi 1997: PMID:9398669 and Blanco 2000 as above. Ste9 restrains Cdc2-Cig2
    genetically (cig2 deletion suppresses ste9 sterility) but not by degrading Cig2. The
    description and core functions reflect Cdc13 + Cig1, not Cig2.
  • Direct biochemistry (Kimata 2011, the PomBase IDA source): purified Lid1-TAP APC/C plus
    in vitro translated coactivators. PMID:21389117 (Cut2 and Cdc13). Cell-cycle regulation is
    reproduced in extracts: PMID:21389117 and PMID:21389117. Ste9 also ubiquitylates Mes1 in vitro.
  • IMP arm of Kimata 2011: Ste9 repressed in meiosis (Puhp1-HA-ste9) — PMID:21389117. Ste9 is hyperphosphorylated (inactive) through most of
    meiosis and is not involved in the MI to MII transition.

Regulation

  • Cdc2-Cdc13 phosphorylation inhibits Ste9 two ways: PMID:10921876 and PMID:10921876
  • Four Cdk sites; unphosphorylatable mutant stabilised and hyperactive: PMID:10921878; PMID:10921878; the 4A mutant causes diploidisation, suppressed by extra Cdc13.
  • Phosphorylation depends mainly on Cdc2-Cdc13, not Cig1/Cig2 PMID:10921878
  • From the deep-research file (papers not in the cache, not used for any action):
    PP2A-B56/Par1 needed for Ste9 dephosphorylation and Cdc13 loss in pre-Start G1 (Stonyte
    2020, iScience 23:101063); Cds1 phosphorylates and inhibits Ste9 during HU arrest,
    protecting the MBF activator Rep2, an APC/C-Ste9 substrate (Chu 2009, MCB,
    doi:10.1128/MCB.00562-09). Both raised as suggested_questions rather than annotated.

Physiology (the G1 terms)

  • Nitrogen-starvation G1 arrest, sterility: PMID:9398669; PMID:9571240; PMID:9398669
  • Pre-Start G1 block to mitosis: PMID:9571240; synthetic lethality with wee1.
  • Gain of function: PMID:9571240
  • Size control framing (Blanco): PMID:10921876
  • Redundancy with Rum1: the ste9 rum1 double deletion is viable with no mitotic defect
    PMID:10921878.
  • Localisation: nucleus by ORFeome YFP screen (PMID:16823372, abstract-only cache; the
    Ste9 datum is in the supplementary data, cited by UniProt). No cell-cycle-resolved imaging
    of endogenous Ste9 in the dedicated literature; suggested as an experiment.

  G1; it is not needed for the wave of cyclin destruction at mitotic exit, which is carried by
  the Cdc20-type coactivator Slp1, and it does not degrade the S-phase cyclin Cig2, although
  it restrains Cdc2-Cig2 activity genetically. By keeping Cdc13 low, Ste9 (together with the
  CDK inhibitor Rum1, with which it is partly redundant) holds cells in a pre-Start G1 state:
  it lengthens G1 in small cells, is required for G1 arrest upon nitrogen starvation and thus
  for mating and sexual differentiation, and prevents mitosis from being launched from G1.
  Cells lacking ste9 are viable and cycle normally in rich medium but are sterile, fail to
  arrest in G1 when starved, and are synthetically lethal with wee1 mutations because mitotic
  cyclin control is compromised; overexpression lowers Cdc13-associated kinase activity,
  blocks mitosis and induces an extra round of DNA replication. Ste9 is switched off outside
  G1 by Cdc2-Cdc13 phosphorylation on multiple Cdk consensus sites (Ser62, Thr98, Thr177,
  Ser214), which both dissociates it from the APC/C and targets it for proteolysis; its

  arrest, protecting the MBF activator Rep2 from APC/C-Ste9-mediated destruction. The
  protein is nuclear, where the APC/C acts on its substrates, and it is heavily phosphorylated
  and inactive through most of meiosis.
