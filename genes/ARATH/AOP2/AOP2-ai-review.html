Gene Description

AOP2 (ALKENYL HYDROXALKYL PRODUCING 2; At4g03060) is an Fe(II)- and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase (2-ODD) that catalyzes a side-chain modification in methionine-derived (aliphatic) glucosinolate biosynthesis, converting methylsulfinylalkyl glucosinolates into alkenyl glucosinolates (e.g. 3-methylsulfinylpropyl to allyl/2-propenyl glucosinolate, and 4-methylsulfinylbutyl glucoraphanin to 3-butenyl glucosinolate). It underlies the GS-ALK locus. AOP2 arose by tandem duplication with AOP3, which instead forms hydroxyalkyl glucosinolates, and the two are expressed in a mutually exclusive manner across accessions. This entry is the functional allele from the Cape Verde Islands (Cvi) accession (432 aa, AOP2-1); the Columbia allele carries a 5-bp deletion causing a frameshift and a non-functional truncated protein, so Col-0 accumulates methylsulfinylalkyl glucosinolates. AOP2 is expressed most strongly in leaves and stems in a light-dependent manner, and its activity shapes the glucosinolate profile and thus the isothiocyanate breakdown products that mediate defense against herbivores.

Proposed New Ontology Terms

alkenyl glucosinolate synthase activity

Definition: Catalysis of the Fe(II)- and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent conversion of an omega-(methylsulfinyl)alkyl glucosinolate to the corresponding omega-alkenyl glucosinolate with loss of the methylsulfinyl group, e.g. 3-methylsulfinylpropyl glucosinolate to allyl (2-propenyl) glucosinolate, or 4-methylsulfinylbutyl glucosinolate (glucoraphanin) to 3-butenyl glucosinolate (gluconapin).

Justification: AOP2 (GS-ALK) has a biochemically demonstrated, substrate-defined activity that determines alkenyl glucosinolate accumulation across Arabidopsis accessions and Brassica crops (BoGSL-ALK), but GO only offers the generic class term GO:0016706. The paralog AOP3 forms hydroxyalkyl glucosinolates from the same substrates, so a specific term would distinguish these neofunctionalized duplicates. Should be placed under 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase activity (GO:0016706).

Comprehensive Research Report: AOP2 Gene (UniProt Q945B5) from Arabidopsis thaliana

Gene Identity Verification

The gene AOP2 (UniProt accession Q945B5) from Arabidopsis thaliana encodes a 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase involved in aliphatic glucosinolate biosynthesis (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2). This assignment matches the UniProt annotation describing the protein as belonging to the iron/ascorbate-dependent oxidoreductase family and containing the DIOX_N and 2OG-FeII_Oxy domains (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 4-7, chen2023identificationandin pages 4-6). The gene is also known as GS-ALK (alkenyl glucosinolate) in functional studies (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 10-12).

Primary Enzymatic Function and Substrate Specificity

Core Catalytic Activity

AOP2 functions as a 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase that catalyzes the conversion of methylsulfinylalkyl glucosinolates into alkenyl glucosinolates (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 10-12). This represents a critical side-chain modification step in the aliphatic glucosinolate biosynthetic pathway (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 4-7, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 3-4). The general transformation involves removal of the methylsulfinyl group and introduction of a terminal carbon-carbon double bond in the glucosinolate side chain (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 2-3).

Specific Substrate-Product Pairs

The enzyme displays defined substrate specificity for short-chain aliphatic methylsulfinylalkyl glucosinolates:

  1. C3 substrate-product pair: AOP2 converts 3-methylsulfinylpropyl glucosinolate (also called glucoiberin or 3MSP) into 2-propenyl glucosinolate (allyl glucosinolate, commonly called sinigrin) (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 10-12). This conversion has been demonstrated through in vitro enzyme assays using recombinant AOP2 proteins (chen2023identificationandin pages 6-7).

  2. C4 substrate-product pair: AOP2 catalyzes the conversion of 4-methylsulfinylbutyl glucosinolate (glucoraphanin or 4MSB) into 3-butenyl glucosinolate (gluconapin) (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 10-12). This reaction has been validated through heterologous expression studies and Arabidopsis complementation experiments (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8, chen2023identificationandin pages 1-2, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 4-7).

Cofactor Requirements

As a member of the 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase family, AOP2 is expected to require 2-oxoglutarate, molecular oxygen, and Fe(II) as cofactors, with the reaction producing succinate and CO₂ as coproducts (barreda2024specializedmetabolitemodifications pages 8-10, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 4-7). This cofactor requirement is inferred from the conserved DIOX-N and 2OG-FeII_Oxy domain architecture and is consistent with the general mechanism of non-heme Fe(II)/2-oxoglutarate oxygenases (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8).

Protein Structure and Catalytic Mechanism

Domain Architecture

AOP2 contains two highly conserved domains essential for its catalytic activity:

  1. DIOX-N domain: Located in the N-terminal region, this domain is characteristic of dioxygenase enzymes and contributes to the overall catalytic fold (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 4-7, chen2023identificationandin pages 4-6).

  2. 2OG-FeII_Oxy domain: Positioned in the C-terminal region, this domain houses the active site and is responsible for Fe(II) and 2-oxoglutarate binding (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 4-7).

The central region between these conserved domains shows greater sequence variability and may contribute to substrate recognition or functional divergence among AOP paralogs (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 4-7, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2).

Critical Catalytic Residues

Site-directed mutagenesis studies on closely related Brassica AOP2 homologs have identified residues essential for catalytic activity. The catalytic center is proposed to contain a mononuclear, non-heme iron site coordinated by two histidine residues and one carboxylate moiety (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8). Specific residues critical for function include:

  • His356: Substitution to leucine (H356L) abolished detectable catalytic activity, indicating this residue is essential, likely through Fe(II) coordination or maintenance of active-site geometry (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8).

  • Asp310: The D310A mutation eliminated enzymatic activity, suggesting this aspartate is essential for catalytic-site integrity and/or metal coordination (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2).

  • Arg376: The R376W substitution abolished activity, indicating this arginine is likely involved in substrate positioning or active-site organization (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2).

In contrast, His308, despite being a conserved residue, was not individually essential under assay conditions, as the H308L mutant retained detectable catalytic activity (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 2-3).

Subcellular Localization

AOP2 protein is predominantly localized in the cytoplasm, where it carries out its glucosinolate side-chain modification reactions (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 4-7). This cytoplasmic localization is consistent with the location of secondary aliphatic glucosinolate biosynthesis and has been confirmed through GFP-fusion localization studies in closely related Brassica species, with results reported as consistent with known Arabidopsis localization patterns (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 4-7, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8).

Biochemical Pathway and Biological Processes

Role in Aliphatic Glucosinolate Biosynthesis

AOP2 functions at a key branch point in the aliphatic glucosinolate biosynthetic pathway. Following core glucosinolate structure formation and oxidation of methylthioalkyl groups to methylsulfinylalkyl groups by flavin-monooxygenases, AOP2 catalyzes the secondary side-chain modification that determines final glucosinolate structure (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 2-3, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 4-7). This enzymatic step is critical for generating the structural diversity observed in aliphatic glucosinolates across different Arabidopsis accessions (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2).

Transcriptional Regulation and Feedback Mechanisms

AOP2 participates in complex regulatory networks that extend beyond its direct enzymatic function:

  1. Interaction with MYB transcription factors: AOP2 activity increases transcript levels of the key aliphatic glucosinolate transcription factors MYB28 and MYB29 (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 1-2, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 2-3). Reciprocally, MYB28 and MYB29 can bind the AOP2 promoter, forming a positive feedback loop (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 7-9). The ability of AOP2 to stimulate aliphatic glucosinolate accumulation depends principally on MYB28, with a smaller contribution from MYB29 (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 1-2, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 2-3).

  2. MYC2-associated regulation: AOP2 expression increases MYC2 transcript levels and integrates into a MYC2–MYB28–MYB29 regulatory network (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 4-6, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 7-9). This connection links AOP2-associated glucosinolate metabolism with jasmonate-responsive defense transcription.

  3. Effects on pathway flux: Expression of functional AOP2 can increase total aliphatic glucosinolate accumulation approximately twofold, affecting both short-chain and long-chain glucosinolate levels (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 2-3, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 1-2). This indicates that AOP2 influences not only product identity but also overall pathway flux through feedback regulation.

Jasmonic Acid Signaling

AOP2 plays a multifaceted role in jasmonic acid (JA) signaling, functioning as part of a feedback regulatory circuit:

  1. Effects on JA biosynthesis genes: AOP2 expression alters transcript levels of 12 out of 25 examined jasmonate biosynthesis and metabolism genes, with strong induction of LOX3, LOX4, AOC1, AOC3, OPR3, CYP94B3, and CYP94C1 (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 4-6). It also increases transcripts for eight JAZ proteins, which are central repressors of JA signaling (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 4-6).

  2. JA response phenotype: Plants expressing AOP2 show altered sensitivity to exogenous jasmonic acid, with shorter roots under control conditions but reduced JA sensitivity (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 6-7, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 7-9). Importantly, this effect persists independently of MYB28 and MYB29 and is not accompanied by significant changes in measured endogenous JA concentrations (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 6-7, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 7-9).

  3. MYB-independent regulation: In myb28 myb29 double-mutant backgrounds lacking detectable aliphatic glucosinolate substrates, AOP2 still upregulates several pathway genes (GGP1, CYP83B1, SOT16) and downregulates others (CYP81F4, GS-OX2, MBP1, NSP1, NSP3, NSP4) (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 6-7, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 7-9, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 3-4). This persistence suggests that some regulatory effects may not require the canonical AOP2 enzymatic reaction, potentially involving low-level metabolites or an unidentified function of the AOP2 RNA or protein.

Flowering Time and Circadian Regulation

AOP2 has been implicated in the regulation of flowering time and circadian rhythms, although these effects are highly dependent on genetic background. Introducing functional AOP2 into AOP-null backgrounds can alter circadian period and flowering time (jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 1-2). In the Col-0 accession, AOP2 expression significantly delayed flowering, whereas in the Cph-0 accession it showed a non-significant tendency to accelerate flowering (jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 1-2, jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 4-6). These genetic-background-dependent effects support context-specific cross-talk between defense metabolism and reproductive timing (jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 1-2, jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 4-6).

Relationship with AOP3

AOP2 and AOP3 are closely related paralogs that arose through gene duplication from an ancestral AOP gene (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 10-12). Despite their evolutionary relationship, the two enzymes have diverged in both catalytic specificity and expression patterns:

  1. Catalytic differences: While both enzymes act on methylsulfinylalkyl glucosinolates, AOP2 produces alkenyl glucosinolates (converting both 3- and 4-carbon substrates), whereas AOP3 preferentially converts the 3-carbon substrate (3-methylsulfinylpropyl glucosinolate) into 3-hydroxypropyl glucosinolate rather than the alkenyl form (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 2-3, jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 1-2). AOP2 generally displays broader substrate activity than AOP3 (jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 1-2, jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 4-6).

  2. Expression patterns: Natural Arabidopsis accessions commonly express either AOP2, AOP3, or neither, but rarely express both simultaneously (chen2023identificationandin pages 1-2, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2). This mutually exclusive expression pattern, combined with their distinct catalytic activities, makes the GS-AOP locus a major determinant of Arabidopsis glucosinolate chemotype variation.

  3. Differential regulatory effects: AOP2 generally has a stronger effect on total glucosinolate accumulation than AOP3, potentially through more efficient substrate utilization or stronger feedback effects on pathway regulation (jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 4-6).

Biological and Ecological Significance

Chemical Defense

AOP2-produced alkenyl glucosinolates are integral components of the glucosinolate-myrosinase defense system characteristic of Brassicaceae plants (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 1-2, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2). Following tissue damage by herbivores or pathogens, glucosinolates are hydrolyzed by myrosinase enzymes to generate bioactive breakdown products, including isothiocyanates, that contribute to defense against both insect herbivores and microbial pathogens (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2, chen2023identificationandin pages 1-2). AOP2 activity modifies the glucosinolate chemical profile, thereby altering the spectrum of defensive compounds available for release upon attack (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 2-3, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2).

Adaptation and Natural Variation

Natural variation at the GS-AOP locus, including presence or absence of functional AOP2, generates contrasting glucosinolate chemotypes across Arabidopsis populations (jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 1-2, jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 4-6). This variation is interpreted as a potential substrate for local adaptation, reflecting trade-offs between defense benefits and metabolic costs (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 1-2, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 2-3). However, the specific ecological outcomes depend on genetic background, environmental context, and the particular herbivore or pathogen community encountered (jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 1-2, jensen2015naturalvariationin pages 4-6).

Agricultural and Nutritional Implications

AOP2 function has important implications for crop breeding and human nutrition. Functional AOP2 redirects glucoraphanin, whose hydrolysis product sulforaphane has been associated with health benefits, toward alkenyl glucosinolates such as gluconapin (chen2023identificationandin pages 1-2, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 10-12). Loss of AOP2 function can therefore preserve glucoraphanin content, making AOP2 orthologs practical targets for metabolic engineering and breeding programs aimed at modifying the balance between health-promoting and antinutritional glucosinolates in Brassica crops (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 10-12, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 1-2).

Experimental Evidence Summary

The functional characterization of AOP2 is supported by multiple complementary experimental approaches:

  1. In vitro enzyme assays: Recombinant AOP2 proteins expressed in E. coli have been shown to catalyze the conversion of glucoraphanin to gluconapin and glucoiberin to sinigrin in vitro (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 4-7, chen2023identificationandin pages 6-7).

  2. Complementation studies: Introduction of functional AOP2 into Arabidopsis AOP-null mutants restores alkenyl glucosinolate production, confirming in vivo activity (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8).

  3. Site-directed mutagenesis: Systematic mutation of conserved active-site residues has identified His356, Asp310, and Arg376 as essential for catalytic activity (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8).

  4. Subcellular localization: GFP-fusion experiments have confirmed cytoplasmic localization (zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 4-7, zhang2015threegenesencoding pages 7-8).

  5. Transcriptome analysis: Expression profiling and genetic studies have revealed AOP2's roles in transcriptional feedback regulation and JA signaling (burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 4-6, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 6-7, burow2015theglucosinolatebiosynthetic pages 1-2).

Conclusions

AOP2 from Arabidopsis thaliana is a 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase that catalyzes a key side-chain modification in aliphatic glucosinolate biosynthesis, specifically converting methylsulfinylalkyl glucosinolates into alkenyl glucosinolates. The enzyme displays defined substrate specificity for 3- and 4-carbon methylsulfinylalkyl substrates, producing sinigrin and gluconapin, respectively. AOP2 functions in the cytoplasm and contains conserved DIOX-N and 2OG-FeII_Oxy domains with critical catalytic residues that have been validated through mutagenesis studies.

Beyond its direct enzymatic role, AOP2 participates in complex regulatory networks involving feedback regulation of jasmonic acid signaling, interactions with MYB28/MYB29 transcription factors, and context-dependent effects on flowering time. The enzyme's activity influences not only glucosinolate structure but also total pathway flux, and its products contribute to plant chemical defense against herbivores and pathogens. Natural variation in AOP2 function represents a major source of glucosinolate chemotype diversity in Arabidopsis and has practical implications for crop breeding programs aimed at optimizing glucosinolate profiles for nutritional and agricultural benefits.
