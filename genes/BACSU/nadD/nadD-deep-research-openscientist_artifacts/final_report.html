Functional Annotation Report: nadD (NaMN Adenylyltransferase) in Bacillus subtilis

Gene: nadD (synonyms: yqeJ; ordered locus BSU25640) Protein: Nicotinate-nucleotide adenylyltransferase (NaMN adenylyltransferase) UniProt: P54455 · EC: 2.7.7.18 Organism: Bacillus subtilis (strain 168)

Summary

The gene nadD of Bacillus subtilis strain 168 (synonym yqeJ; locus BSU25640; UniProt P54455) encodes nicotinate mononucleotide (NaMN) adenylyltransferase (NaMNAT; EC 2.7.7.18), a soluble cytoplasmic enzyme that catalyzes the penultimate, essential step of NAD⁺ biosynthesis. The enzyme transfers the adenylyl (AMP) moiety of ATP to nicotinic acid mononucleotide (NaMN), producing nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide (NaAD, also called deamido-NAD) and inorganic pyrophosphate in a reversible, Mg²⁺-dependent reaction. The product NaAD is then amidated by NAD synthetase (NadE) to yield the mature redox cofactor NAD⁺. This identity is firmly established: the B. subtilis gene was directly identified and its product biochemically and structurally characterized by Olland and colleagues (PMID: 11704676). There is no gene-symbol ambiguity — the UniProt description, EC number, protein family (NadD family), and domain architecture (Cyt_trans-like/CTP_transf_like, Rossmann-like α/β fold) all align exactly with the experimental literature.

Mechanistically, NadD occupies the metabolic convergence point of all NAD⁺ biosynthetic routes. NaMN is the common intermediate generated both by the de novo pathway (from aspartate via NadA/NadB/NadC) and by the nicotinate salvage pathway (via PncA/PncB). Because every route funnels through NaMN and then through NadD → NadE, NadD is one of only two indispensable downstream enzymes in bacterial NAD⁺ synthesis and cannot be bypassed. The enzyme is distinguished from the related nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferases (NMNATs) by its substrate specificity for the deamidated (nicotinate) form, NaMN, a preference whose structural basis — recognition of the nicotinate carboxylate by backbone amides — was resolved crystallographically.

Structurally, B. subtilis NadD adopts the Rossmann-like α/β fold of the nucleotidyltransferase/α-β-phosphodiesterase superfamily, and uses the conserved (T/H)XGH catalytic motif for ATP binding and adenylyl transfer. Direct inspection of the 189-residue P54455 sequence locates this motif as HNGH at residues 15–18, embedded in an N-terminal glycine-rich phosphate-binding loop — confirming the annotation at the primary-sequence level. Unusually for this enzyme family, the B. subtilis protein is a homodimer. Because NadD is essential and broadly conserved across bacteria yet structurally divergent from the human enzyme, it is a validated, druggable broad-spectrum antibacterial target, a conclusion supported by structure-based inhibitor campaigns against close relatives (Bacillus anthracis, E. coli) and by genetic depletion studies in M. tuberculosis.

Key Findings

Finding 1 — nadD (BSU25640/yqeJ) encodes the NaMN adenylyltransferase of NAD⁺ biosynthesis

The central identity of the gene is established by direct experimental work on B. subtilis. Olland et al. (2002) identified the B. subtilis nadD gene (synonym yqeJ) by sequence homology to other bacterial nadD genes and confirmed its function by biochemical characterization of the gene product. They state explicitly: "The nadD gene, encoding the enzyme nicotinic acid mononucleotide (NaMN) adenylyltransferase (AT), is essential for the synthesis of NAD and subsequent viability of the cell. The nadD gene in Bacillus subtilis (yqeJ) was identified by sequence homology with other bacterial nadD genes and by biochemical characterization of the gene product" (PMID: 11704676).

The reaction catalyzed is the adenylyl transfer:

ATP + nicotinate β-D-ribonucleotide (NaMN) ⇌ diphosphate + nicotinate adenine dinucleotide (NaAD / deamido-NAD)

This is a reversible, Mg²⁺-dependent nucleotidyl transfer that produces the immediate precursor to NAD⁺. The reaction is the penultimate step of NAD⁺ biosynthesis, immediately preceding the amidation of NaAD to NAD⁺ by NAD synthetase (NadE). This finding anchors the entire annotation: the gene symbol, EC number (2.7.7.18), and organism all match the UniProt target identity, and the function was determined by precise, low-throughput biochemistry rather than inference alone.

Finding 2 — Substrate specificity: NadD prefers the deamidated nicotinate substrate (NaMN over NMN)

A defining biochemical feature of the NadD family — and of the B. subtilis enzyme in particular — is its specificity for the nicotinate (deamidated) mononucleotide, NaMN, rather than the amidated nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN). Olland et al. found that the enzyme "catalyzes the reversible adenylation of both NaMN and the nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) but shows specificity for the nicotinate" (PMID: 11704676). While the enzyme is catalytically capable of acting on both substrates, its physiological and kinetic preference is clearly for NaMN.

The structural basis for this preference was resolved in the same study: "Interactions of the nicotinic acid with backbone atoms indicate the structural basis for specificity" (PMID: 11704676). The nicotinate carboxylate group is recognized through hydrogen bonds to main-chain amide atoms in the substrate pocket. This specificity is functionally important: it channels the deamidated route of NAD⁺ synthesis (where amidation occurs late, at the NadE step) and distinguishes bacterial NadD from the nicotinamide-preferring NMNATs that operate in the non-deamidating salvage routes of other organisms. Corroborating structural evidence from the related Staphylococcus aureus enzyme shows the same principle — "the nicotinate carboxylates of NaAD are recognized by interaction with the main-chain amides of Thr85 and Tyr117, a positive helix dipole and two bridged-water molecules" (PMID: 16784754).

Finding 3 — Quaternary structure and fold: a homodimer with a Rossmann-like α/β nucleotidyltransferase fold

Olland et al. determined crystal structures of B. subtilis NadD in both the apo state (2.1 Å) and a product-bound state (3.2 Å). The monomer adopts "a monomer fold common to members of the nucleotidyl-transferase alpha/beta phosphodiesterase superfamily" (PMID: 11704676) — i.e., a Rossmann-like dinucleotide-binding fold. This matches the domain annotations for P54455 (InterPro IPR004821 Cyt_trans-like; IPR005248 NadD/NMNAT; IPR014729 Rossmann-like α/β/α fold; Pfam PF01467 CTP_transf_like).

A notable and unusual feature is the oligomeric state: whereas most characterized NMN/NaMN adenylyltransferases are monomeric, "In contrast to other known NMN ATs, biophysical characterizations reveal it to be a dimer" (PMID: 11704676). A consistent "functional" dimer was observed across both crystal forms, suggesting the dimer is biologically meaningful rather than a crystallization artifact. Catalysis relies on the superfamily-conserved (T/H)XGH motif for ATP binding, a hallmark first defined in the founding NMNAT structural work: "A strictly conserved HXGH motif (in single-letter amino acid code) is involved in ATP binding and recognition" (PMID: 10986466).

Finding 4 — NadD is essential and a validated broad-spectrum antibacterial drug target

Because NadD catalyzes a non-redundant, essential step, loss of its activity is lethal to bacteria. This essentiality combined with broad conservation and structural divergence from human NMNATs makes NadD an attractive antibacterial target. Sorci et al. selected it explicitly for inhibitor development: "Nicotinate mononucleotide adenylyltransferase of the NadD family, an essential enzyme of NAD biosynthesis in most bacteria, was selected as a target for structure-based inhibitor development" (PMID: 19716475). Structure-based inhibitors against E. coli and Bacillus anthracis NadD (the latter a close genus-level relative of B. subtilis) selectively inhibited the bacterial enzymes without affecting the human counterparts and showed on-target antibacterial activity.

Genetic validation comes from Mycobacterium tuberculosis, where controlled depletion of NadD confirmed the metabolic consequence of blocking this step: "Induced degradation of either target enzyme showed a strong bactericidal effect which coincided with anticipated changes in relative levels of NaMN and NaAD intermediates (substrates of NadD and NadE, respectively) and ultimate depletion of the NAD(H) pool" (PMID: 24549842). The accumulation of NaMN (NadD's substrate) and depletion of NAD(H) upon NadD loss directly demonstrates that NadD is the committed enzyme converting NaMN to NaAD, and that this conversion is indispensable for viability.

Finding 5 — NadD acts at the convergence of de novo and salvage NAD routes; its upstream supply is NiaR-regulated in B. subtilis

NaMN is the universal node of bacterial NAD⁺ metabolism: it is produced by the de novo pathway (NadA/NadB/NadC, from aspartate via quinolinate) and by the nicotinate salvage pathway (PncA/PncB). From NaMN onward, there is a single two-enzyme funnel — NadD then NadE — through which all routes must pass. As Sorci et al. articulated for group A streptococci, "the redundancy of functional upstream salvage pathways in GAS species narrows the choice of potential drug targets to the two indispensable downstream enzymes of NAD synthesis, nicotinate adenylyltransferase (NadD family) and NAD synthetase (NadE family)" (PMID: 23204464). This places NadD squarely at the non-redundant bottleneck of the pathway.

In B. subtilis specifically, the enzymes that supply NadD's substrate are under coordinated transcriptional control by the niacin-responsive repressor NiaR (YrxA). Rodionov et al. showed that "In addition to transcriptional control of the nadABC genes, the NiaR regulon in some species extends to niacin salvage (the pncAB genes) and includes uncharacterized membrane proteins possibly involved in niacin transport" (PMID: 18276644). Thus NadD's inputs — both de novo synthesis and salvage uptake of niacin via the NiaP (YceI) transporter — are modulated by NiaR in response to niacin availability, while NadD itself sits downstream as the constitutive, essential committed step.

Finding 6 — Conserved, druggable active-site architecture across Bacillus species

Structural studies of Bacillus anthracis NadD — a close relative of B. subtilis within the same genus — reinforce both the central metabolic role and the druggability of the active site. Huang et al. solved crystal structures of B. anthracis NadD in complex with three distinct inhibitors, revealing a common inhibitor-binding site adjacent to the substrate pocket and showing that subtle ligand modifications alter the binding mode. They affirm: "Bacterial nicotinate mononucleotide adenylyltransferase encoded by the essential gene nadD plays a central role in the synthesis of the redox cofactor NAD(+)" (PMID: 20578699). The conservation of active-site chemistry across Bacillus species supports transferring mechanistic and inhibitor insights to the B. subtilis enzyme.

Finding 7 — Primary-sequence confirmation of the catalytic (H)XGH motif in P54455

Direct analysis of the 189-residue B. subtilis NadD sequence (UniProt P54455) locates the conserved catalytic motif HNGH at residues 15–18, embedded in the N-terminal glycine-rich phosphate-binding loop (…GIFGGTFDPPHNGHLL…, residues ~6–19). This matches the (T/H)XGH ATP-binding/adenylyl-transfer motif that is diagnostic of the nucleotidyltransferase α/β phosphodiesterase superfamily, as defined by D'Angelo et al.: "A strictly conserved HXGH motif (in single-letter amino acid code) is involved in ATP binding and recognition" (PMID: 10986466). The presence of this motif at the sequence level, together with the N-terminal Rossmann-like dinucleotide-binding fold annotated for P54455 (InterPro IPR014729, IPR005248; Pfam PF01467), confirms the enzyme's catalytic identity independently of the crystal structure.

Mechanistic Model and Interpretation

The reaction and its place in the pathway

NadD catalyzes the adenylyl transfer that converts NaMN into deamido-NAD:

            NadD (EC 2.7.7.18), Mg2+
  NaMN  +  ATP  ================================>  NaAD (deamido-NAD)  +  PPi
  (nicotinate mononucleotide)                       (nicotinate adenine dinucleotide)

The product NaAD is then amidated by NadE (NAD synthetase) using glutamine or ammonia to form the mature cofactor:

            NadE (NAD synthetase)
  NaAD  +  ATP + NH3/Gln  ===================>  NAD+  +  AMP + PPi (+ Glu)

Pathway convergence — why NadD is a bottleneck

All biosynthetic routes to NAD⁺ in B. subtilis funnel through NaMN, then through the obligatory NadD → NadE pair:

  DE NOVO (from aspartate)                 SALVAGE (from niacin/nicotinate)
  Asp --NadB--> ... --NadA--> quinolinate   nicotinamide --PncA--> nicotinate
       --NadC--> NaMN                        nicotinate   --PncB--> NaMN
                      \                              /
                       \                            /
                        v                          v
                 ====================  NaMN  ====================
                                        |
                                        |  NadD   (ESSENTIAL, non-redundant)
                                        v
                                       NaAD (deamido-NAD)
                                        |
                                        |  NadE   (ESSENTIAL, non-redundant)
                                        v
                                       NAD+  --> (NadK) --> NADP+

Everything upstream of NaMN is potentially redundant (de novo vs. salvage) and is regulated by NiaR (YrxA) in response to niacin availability. Everything from NaMN onward is a single, non-redundant funnel. This topology is precisely why NadD (and NadE) are the two indispensable "choke-point" enzymes and why they are favored antibacterial targets.

B. subtilis NadD prefers the nicotinate form, committing the cell to the deamidated route in which amidation is deferred to the final NadE step. The specificity is encoded by backbone-amide recognition of the nicotinate carboxylate in the substrate pocket.

Subcellular localization

NadD is a soluble, cytoplasmic enzyme. It carries no signal peptide, transmembrane segment, or lipidation signal, and its substrates (NaMN, ATP) and product (NaAD) are cytoplasmic metabolites of central metabolism. Its function — the reversible adenylyl transfer producing deamido-NAD — is therefore executed entirely in the cytosol, consistent with the general cytoplasmic localization of the NAD⁺ biosynthetic machinery. This is an inference from the protein's physicochemical properties and the location of its substrates/products, which is strongly supported but not the subject of a dedicated localization study.

Convergence of evidence. The annotation rests on an unusually strong and self-consistent evidence base: direct biochemistry and crystallography on the B. subtilis enzyme itself (PMID: 11704676), primary-sequence confirmation of the catalytic motif in P54455, independent structural studies on genus relatives (B. anthracis) and other bacteria (S. aureus, M. tuberculosis), genetic essentiality/depletion data, and comparative-genomics pathway reconstruction. No conflicting evidence was found; all lines converge on the same functional assignment.

Limitations and Knowledge Gaps

  1. Gene-symbol verification — resolved, no ambiguity. The mandatory identity check is fully satisfied: the symbol nadD (and synonym yqeJ/BSU25640), EC 2.7.7.18, the NadD protein family, and the Cyt_trans-like/Rossmann-fold domains all match the experimentally characterized B. subtilis enzyme. There is no competing gene with the same symbol confounding the literature.

  2. Localization is inferred, not directly measured. Cytoplasmic localization is inferred from the absence of targeting/membrane signals and from the cytosolic nature of substrates and products. No dedicated experimental localization study (e.g., fluorescent fusion, fractionation) for B. subtilis NadD was identified. The inference is strong but not experimentally proven for this specific protein.

  3. Detailed steady-state kinetics for the B. subtilis enzyme are incomplete. While substrate preference (NaMN over NMN) is established, quantitative kinetic constants (kcat, Km for NaMN, NMN, ATP) and the ordered binding mechanism have been characterized in detail primarily for orthologs (e.g., M. tuberculosis NadD, where ATP binds first; PMID: 25631047). Transfer of this mechanism to B. subtilis is plausible but not directly demonstrated.

  4. Functional significance of dimerization is unresolved. The B. subtilis enzyme is a dimer, unusual within the family. Whether dimerization is required for activity, regulation, or stability — versus being incidental — has not been functionally dissected.

  5. Regulation at the protein level is uncharacterized. Upstream substrate supply is NiaR-regulated transcriptionally, but post-translational regulation of NadD itself (allostery, feedback inhibition by NAD⁺/NADH, conformational gating under energy stress as seen in mycobacteria) has not been studied in B. subtilis.

  6. The analysis is literature/bioinformatics-based. No new wet-lab data were generated; conclusions rest on published primary studies, structural data, and direct sequence analysis of P54455.

Proposed Follow-up Experiments and Actions

  1. Quantitative enzymology of B. subtilis NadD. Determine steady-state kinetic parameters (kcat, Km for NaMN, NMN, ATP; Mg²⁺ dependence) and establish the kinetic mechanism (ordered vs. random), testing whether the ATP-first ordered mechanism reported for mycobacterial NadD holds here.

  2. Direct localization confirmation. Construct a functional NadD–fluorescent protein fusion or perform subcellular fractionation + immunoblotting in B. subtilis 168 to experimentally verify cytoplasmic localization.

  3. Dimer-interface mutagenesis. Identify and mutate dimer-interface residues to generate monomeric variants and test effects on activity, stability, and in vivo complementation of a conditional nadD knockdown — resolving whether dimerization is functionally required.

  4. Conditional essentiality and metabolomics in B. subtilis. Build a CRISPRi/degron-based conditional nadD strain and measure, by targeted metabolomics, the predicted NaMN accumulation and NAD(H) depletion upon depletion — directly reproducing in B. subtilis the causal chain demonstrated in M. tuberculosis (PMID: 24549842).

  5. Feedback/allostery testing. Assay NadD activity in the presence of NAD⁺, NADH, and NaAD to detect product/end-product inhibition and any energy-charge-dependent conformational regulation.

  6. Structure-guided inhibitor profiling. Test the selective NadD inhibitors developed against B. anthracis/E. coli (PMID: 19716475, PMID: 20578699) against purified B. subtilis NadD and whole cells to confirm on-target antibacterial activity in this species.

Conclusion

B. subtilis nadD (P54455; yqeJ; BSU25640) encodes nicotinate mononucleotide adenylyltransferase (EC 2.7.7.18), a soluble cytoplasmic homodimer that catalyzes the essential, reversible, Mg²⁺-dependent transfer of the adenylyl group from ATP to NaMN, producing deamido-NAD (NaAD) plus pyrophosphate — the penultimate step of NAD⁺ biosynthesis. It is specific for the deamidated nicotinate substrate (NaMN over NMN), uses a Rossmann-like α/β fold with the catalytic (H)XGH motif present at residues 15–18 of its sequence, and sits at the non-redundant convergence point of the de novo and salvage pathways immediately upstream of NadE. Because there is no metabolic bypass, NadD is essential for viability and a validated broad-spectrum antibacterial drug target. The annotation is supported by direct biochemistry and crystallography of the B. subtilis enzyme, corroborated by structural, genetic, and comparative-genomic studies across related bacteria, with no conflicting evidence.
