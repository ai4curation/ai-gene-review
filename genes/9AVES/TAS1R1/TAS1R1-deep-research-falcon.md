# Functional annotation report: *Tas1r1* in *Oreotrochilus melanogaster*

## Executive conclusion

The gene symbol **Tas1r1** is consistent with the supplied protein description: a taste receptor type-1 member-1 (T1R1/TS1R1), belonging to the class-C—or “GPCR family 3”—TAS1R receptor family. The organism is the black-breasted hillstar, ***Oreotrochilus melanogaster***, and the relevant record is **UniProt A0A7L3NBT4**, ORF **OREMEL_R04111**, linked to EMBL protein **NXU76429.1**. Its reported ANF/venus-flytrap ligand-binding, GPCR_3, nine-cysteine, and GPCR_3 C-terminal domains all agree with a TAS1R-family class-C GPCR rather than an unrelated protein.

However, **the gene symbol “Tas1r1” is literature-limited for this specific protein**. Exact searches for A0A7L3NBT4, OREMEL_R04111, NXU76429.1, and *O. melanogaster* Tas1r1 found no accession- or species-specific functional publication. None of the retrieved hummingbird receptor experiments tested *O. melanogaster* or A0A7L3NBT4. Moreover, UniProt labels the sequence a **fragment**, so it cannot be assumed to encode a complete, properly trafficking receptor.

The best-supported annotation is therefore: **a predicted subunit of a cell-surface T1R1–T1R3 chemosensory heterodimer, probably expressed in oral type-II taste-receptor cells and likely contributing to carbohydrate and selected amino-acid detection in this hummingbird.** This is a moderate-confidence inference from protein-family architecture and experimentally characterized hummingbird orthologs—not a demonstrated function of A0A7L3NBT4 itself.

## 1. Protein identity and structural interpretation

TAS1R proteins are class-C GPCRs with an unusually large extracellular **venus-flytrap (VFT)/ANF-receptor-like domain**, followed by a cysteine-rich coupling region and a seven-transmembrane GPCR domain. The VFT region supplies major ligand-recognition and receptor-subunit interaction surfaces, while conformational changes are transmitted through the cysteine-rich and transmembrane regions to intracellular G proteins (baldwin2015evolutionofsweet pages 66-71, cockburn2022themolecularbasis pages 12-15).

The domains supplied for A0A7L3NBT4 align accordingly:

- **ANF_lig-bd_rcpt (IPR001828):** extracellular VFT-like ligand-binding region;
- **GPCR_3_9-Cys_dom / superfamily (IPR011500/IPR038550):** cysteine-rich linker that couples extracellular recognition to receptor activation;
- **GPCR_3 and GPCR_3_C (IPR000337/IPR017978):** class-C seven-transmembrane signaling region and associated C-terminal portion.

This coherent architecture strongly supports the **TAS1R1 family assignment**. It does not, by itself, establish which ligands activate the *O. melanogaster* receptor. Sequence conservation alone can be misleading for taste receptors because small sets of extracellular substitutions can produce substantial changes in ligand preference.

## 2. Primary molecular function

### Receptor composition

T1R1 is not an enzyme or transporter; it is a **signaling-receptor subunit**. Across well-studied vertebrates, T1R1 pairs with T1R3 to form a heterodimeric amino-acid/umami receptor. In hummingbirds, this ancestral receptor pair was evolutionarily repurposed into a bifunctional carbohydrate and amino-acid receptor (baldwin2014evolutionofsweet pages 1-1, baldwin2015evolutionofsweet pages 94-99).

The requirement for both subunits is experimentally supported in other hummingbirds: neither hummingbird T1R1 nor T1R3 alone produced the sugar response. Substitution of chicken T1R1 into the hummingbird pair sharply reduced sucrose responsiveness while retaining a strong L-alanine response. Thus, although many mapped sugar-tuning changes occur in T1R3, hummingbird T1R1 remains a necessary component of the functional receptor complex (baldwin2015evolutionofsweet pages 76-82, baldwin2014evolutionofsweet pages 1-1).

For A0A7L3NBT4, the most precise proposed molecular-function annotation is therefore:

> **Predicted extracellular nutrient-sensing subunit of a T1R1–T1R3 class-C GPCR heterodimer; likely contributes to receptor assembly, ligand recognition/allostery, and G-protein activation in response to carbohydrates and selected amino acids.**

### Ligand specificity inferred from hummingbird orthologs

The foundational study, Baldwin *et al.* in *Science* (published August 2014), showed that Anna’s hummingbird T1R1–T1R3 responds concentration-dependently to **sucrose, fructose, glucose, and sucralose**, with weaker responses to amino acids and sugar alcohols. It did not respond to every compound humans perceive as sweet: cyclamate, aspartame, and acesulfame-K were inactive. In contrast, chicken and chimney-swift T1R1–T1R3 responded strongly to amino acids such as alanine and serine but not carbohydrates (baldwin2014evolutionofsweet pages 1-1, baldwin2015evolutionofsweet pages 71-76). Publication: Baldwin *et al.*, “Evolution of sweet taste perception in hummingbirds by transformation of the ancestral umami receptor,” *Science* 345:929–933; DOI URL: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1255097.

Those experiments expressed receptors in HEK/293T cells with a calcium-sensitive photoprotein and promiscuous Gα15. Stimuli were generally tested at 100 mM—15 mM for aspartame—with six independent experiments. Thus, they demonstrate receptor activity but do not by themselves give physiological detection thresholds for *O. melanogaster* (baldwin2015evolutionofsweet pages 71-76, baldwin2014evolutionofsweet pages 2-4).

Later comparative work found that hummingbird receptors retain biologically meaningful amino-acid sensitivity. Glycine and alanine enhanced sucrose responses **synergistically**, including at 10 mM concentrations that did not activate the receptor alone; methionine, serine, and lysine also enhanced sucrose responses, whereas proline did not. Enhancement also occurred with glucose and fructose and was observed in saw-billed hermit receptors, but not in the insectivorous chimney-swift receptor (cockburn2022themolecularbasis pages 107-113). These assays generally used 100 mM sugars and selected amino acids, 50 mM for other amino acids, and 25 mM leucine, with four to six replicates from two to three transfections (cockburn2022themolecularbasis pages 113-117).

Accordingly, it would be overly narrow to annotate hummingbird T1R1 simply as a “sweet receptor.” The stronger current model is a **bifunctional nutrient receptor whose sugar and amino-acid inputs may interact allosterically**. Nevertheless, the exact A0A7L3NBT4 ligand spectrum, potency, and sensitivity remain unknown.

## 3. Cellular localization and signaling pathway

### Probable site of action

As a class-C GPCR, the complete protein is expected to be an **integral plasma-membrane receptor**. Its VFT domain would face the oral cavity/extracellular environment, whereas intracellular loops and the cytoplasmic tail would couple to heterotrimeric G proteins. T1Rs are generally localized to **type-II taste-receptor cells in taste buds**; the comparative avian literature supports oral-tissue expression but provides no native localization data for A0A7L3NBT4 or *O. melanogaster* (baldwin2015evolutionofsweet pages 71-76, cockburn2022themolecularbasis pages 12-15).

Because the database sequence is fragmentary, it is important not to claim that A0A7L3NBT4 itself reaches the plasma membrane. Missing N-terminal signal/trafficking determinants, transmembrane helices, or the C-terminal region could prevent normal folding or signaling. The record may represent an incomplete assembly rather than a naturally truncated protein.

### Expected signal-transduction cascade

The comparative canonical pathway is:

1. Sugar or amino-acid binding stabilizes an active T1R1–T1R3 conformation.
2. The receptor activates a taste-cell G protein—commonly described as gustducin, transducin, or a Gi-family protein.
3. G-protein signaling activates **PLCβ2**.
4. PLCβ2 hydrolyses PIP2, generating IP3.
5. IP3 releases intracellular Ca²⁺.
6. Ca²⁺ activates **TRPM5**, depolarizing the taste cell.
7. Taste-cell output activates afferent gustatory pathways carried by cranial nerves VII, IX, or X (cockburn2022themolecularbasis pages 12-15).

This pathway is a biologically plausible annotation for *O. melanogaster* Tas1r1, but its individual components and coupling partners have not been tested in this species. Heterologous studies used Gα15 as an assay tool, not necessarily the native hummingbird coupling protein (baldwin2015evolutionofsweet pages 71-76, cockburn2022themolecularbasis pages 113-117).

## 4. Biological process and evolutionary role

Birds generally lack the canonical mammalian sweet-receptor subunit T1R2. Hummingbirds solved this sensory problem by changing the ligand specificity of the ancestral T1R1–T1R3 amino-acid receptor. Comparative functional assays, chimeric receptors, ancestral reconstruction, and behavioral experiments jointly support this conclusion (baldwin2014evolutionofsweet pages 1-1, cockburn2022themolecularbasis pages 96-101, cockburn2022themolecularbasis pages 15-18).

Receptor engineering mapped hummingbird sugar responsiveness principally to the extracellular T1R3 VFT domain: **19 nonconsecutive sites in three surface clusters** could restore sugar sensitivity when introduced into the chicken receptor background. One cluster is near a candidate ligand pocket; two others may change T1R1–T1R3 interactions. This underscores that T1R1 may influence sugar sensing through heterodimer assembly and allostery even when decisive substitutions lie in T1R3 (baldwin2015evolutionofsweet pages 76-82).

Ancestral reconstructions sampled eight representatives of major hummingbird clades. The reconstructed hummingbird ancestor responded strongly to sugars, while the reconstructed hummingbird–swift ancestor did not, placing acquisition of sugar sensitivity after divergence from swifts and at least **approximately 22 million years ago** (cockburn2022themolecularbasis pages 96-101). Recent broad research has further revised the simple view that vertebrates possess only three ancestral TAS1R genes: Nishihara *et al.* reported five additional family members, TAS1R4–TAS1R8, and inferred nine TAS1Rs in the bony-vertebrate ancestor. Their functional work showed extensive amino-acid ligand diversity in non-teleost fishes, illustrating the wider evolutionary plasticity of this receptor family. This 2024 study provides important family-level context but no direct evidence for A0A7L3NBT4: Nishihara *et al.*, *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 8:111–120, published January 2024; DOI URL: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-023-02258-8.

The likely biological process for *O. melanogaster* Tas1r1 is therefore **gustatory perception of nectar nutrients**, linking extracellular sugar/amino-acid composition to food selection and ingestion. The evolutionary interpretation is especially plausible because *O. melanogaster* is a hummingbird, but species-level ecological tuning remains possible and cannot be inferred precisely from Anna’s hummingbird or other sampled taxa.

## 5. Behavioral and real-world evidence

Receptor-level findings have been connected to behavior in other hummingbirds. Ruby-throated hummingbirds preferred sucrose to water, consumed sucrose and the receptor agonist erythritol comparably in one assay, and strongly preferred sucrose to receptor-inactive aspartame. Decisions occurred after roughly three to four licks, about **250 ms**, consistent with rapid gustatory processing (baldwin2014evolutionofsweet pages 2-4).

Wild black-chinned and Anna’s hummingbirds preferred sucrose over fructose and glucose. At high—but not low—concentrations, receptor-active alanine, serine, and glycine promoted long drinking bouts, whereas receptor-inactive proline was not preferred over water (cockburn2022themolecularbasis pages 96-101). These experiments support ecological relevance rather than merely demonstrating activity in cultured cells. They still cannot establish the preferences or receptor pharmacology of the black-breasted hillstar.

Potential applications are primarily research-oriented:

- understanding sensory adaptations that support nectar feeding and hummingbird diversification;
- predicting how nectar sugar/amino-acid composition influences pollinator choice;
- reconstructing receptor evolution and co-adaptation between flowering plants and pollinators;
- designing comparative GPCR assays or engineered nutrient sensors.

There is no identified clinical, agricultural, or commercial implementation specifically involving A0A7L3NBT4.

## 6. Evidence limitations and recommended validation

The annotation should remain **provisional** for four reasons:

1. **No direct publication:** no retrieved study mentions A0A7L3NBT4, OREMEL_R04111, NXU76429.1, or experimentally tests *O. melanogaster* Tas1r1.
2. **Fragmentary sequence:** completeness, membrane topology, and receptor competence are unresolved.
3. **Obligate partner dependence:** function requires a compatible T1R3 and appropriate cellular trafficking machinery.
4. **Species-specific tuning:** hummingbird receptors share carbohydrate sensitivity, but amino-acid responsiveness, synergy, and thresholds vary among taxa; receptor sequence alone cannot prove phenotype (cockburn2022themolecularbasis pages 107-113).

The decisive experiments would be: obtain a full-length, transcript-supported *Tas1r1* coding sequence; confirm conserved synteny and phylogenetic orthology; demonstrate oral taste-bud expression by spatial transcriptomics or in situ hybridization; co-localize T1R1 with T1R3 and type-II-cell signaling markers; express both full-length subunits in a calcium or IP1 assay; measure concentration–response curves for nectar sugars, sugar alcohols, amino acids, and mixtures; test receptor-surface trafficking; and relate receptor agonism to brief-access feeding behavior in *O. melanogaster*.

## Final annotation

**A0A7L3NBT4 is best annotated as a fragmentary, computationally identified *O. melanogaster* TAS1R1 class-C GPCR subunit. Its expected native role is to partner with T1R3 at the apical membrane of oral type-II taste cells and initiate PLCβ2–IP3–Ca²⁺–TRPM5 gustatory signaling. Based on experimentally characterized hummingbird orthologs, the heterodimer probably detects nectar carbohydrates and selected amino acids, potentially with sugar–amino-acid synergism. Confidence is high for family identity, moderate for receptor pairing, pathway, and taste-cell localization, and low-to-moderate for this accession’s exact ligand profile because no species-specific functional evidence exists.**
