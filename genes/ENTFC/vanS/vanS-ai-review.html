Gene Description

VanS is the membrane sensor kinase of the two-component system that switches on glycopeptide resistance in enterococci. Two transmembrane helices flank a short extracytoplasmic region, and the cytoplasmic part carries a canonical histidine kinase domain with the autophosphorylation site at His164 and an ATP-binding region that prefers ATP to GTP. Three catalytic activities have been measured on the protein: ATP-dependent autophosphorylation on the histidine, transfer of that phosphoryl group to an aspartate of the response regulator VanR, and dephosphorylation of phospho-VanR. The phosphotransfer is strikingly selective for its cognate partner, with a ten-thousand-fold kinetic preference for VanR over the heterologous regulator PhoB and no detectable transfer to OmpR, ArcB or CreB. The two opposite arms make VanS a switch rather than a simple kinase: in the absence of the inducing signal the phosphatase arm keeps VanR dephosphorylated and the resistance operon silent, and a mutation expected to lock the protein on reduces the phosphatase activity as predicted. When the switch flips, phospho-VanR binds the resistance promoter and vanH, vanA and vanX are transcribed, so that the cell rebuilds its peptidoglycan precursors to end in D-Ala-D-Lac. What VanS actually senses remains unsettled: one study reports that vancomycin and teicoplanin bind the protein and raise its activity, while another finds the activities of full-length VanS completely insensitive to vancomycin and argues that activation needs additional factors or no direct drug contact at all.

Core Functions

Bifunctional sensor of the VanS-VanR two-component system, anchored in the plasma membrane by two transmembrane helices. Its kinase arm autophosphorylates His164 using ATP and transfers the phosphoryl group to an aspartate of VanR, with a ten-thousand-fold kinetic preference for VanR over a heterologous response regulator and no detectable transfer to three others; phospho-VanR then activates the resistance promoter. Its phosphatase arm dephosphorylates phospho-VanR, which holds the operon off in the absence of the inducing signal, so the resting state is actively enforced rather than merely unstimulated. A mutation expected to lock the switch on reduces the phosphatase activity, as the model predicts. What signal flips the switch is unresolved and no activator is asserted here.

Suggested Experiments

Experiment: Settle the activation question in a membrane rather than in detergent. Insert purified full-length VanS into nanodiscs or proteoliposomes of defined lipid composition with VanR in the lumen, and measure phospho-VanR levels with and without vancomycin and teicoplanin. Comparing the same preparation with and without lipid II or a peptidoglycan fragment would test the indirect-sensing model alongside the direct one.

Experiment: Separate the two arms in vivo. Build VanS variants that selectively impair the kinase or the phosphatase activity, starting from the T168K mutant that reduces the phosphatase arm, and measure resistance-gene induction and vancomycin minimum inhibitory concentration in each. If induction works by suppressing the phosphatase, a phosphatase-dead strain should be constitutively resistant and drug-insensitive.

Knowledge Gaps

What is not known — curated, literature-grounded statements of the open unknowns (the inverse of core functions).

Gap: What VanS senses is unknown, and the two direct tests disagree. One study reports that vancomycin and teicoplanin bind full-length VanS, shifting its tryptophan and tyrosine spectra, and raise its activity, while peptidoglycan components lower it; another finds the autophosphorylation, phosphotransfer and phosphatase activities of full-length VanS completely insensitive to vancomycin at many times the minimum inhibitory concentration, and argues that either additional components are needed for a functional signalling complex or that activation requires no contact with the drug at all. A third possibility, raised by the first study itself, is that the drug acts by suppressing the phosphatase arm rather than by stimulating the kinase arm, which neither activity assay directly resolves.

BIOLOGY MF_DARK

What is known: Firmly established: the three catalytic activities and their rates, the histidine autophosphorylation site, the ten-thousand-fold selectivity of phosphotransfer for VanR, the reduction in phosphatase activity in a constitutively-active-type mutant, the membrane topology, and the in vivo requirement of VanS-VanR for induction of vanH, vanA and vanX.

Significance: The identity of the signal decides whether glycopeptide resistance can be blocked at the sensor, and it is also what determines whether vancomycin should appear as a small-molecule activator in pathway models of this operon; three GO-CAM-adjacent representations currently differ on exactly that point.
