Gene Description

Cell-surface Toll-like receptor that forms a distinct subfamily of the Toll-like receptors, with no mammalian orthologue, and the only known member of the family that is switched on by proteolysis rather than by binding a microbial molecule. It is best known from birds and reptiles, but orthologues also occur in lungfish and in holocephalan cartilaginous fish, so the gene is an ancient jawed-vertebrate lineage that has been lost or pseudogenised in many groups, including all mammals, rather than an avian invention. It has the usual TLR architecture of a leucine-rich-repeat ectodomain, a transmembrane segment and a cytoplasmic TIR domain, and is displayed as a glycoprotein at the cell surface. Secreted proteases from fungi and bacteria cleave its ectodomain, and this cleavage activates the receptor and drives NF-kappa-B-dependent transcription; a truncated form lacking the whole ectodomain is constitutively active, indicating that the leucine-rich repeats hold the receptor in an inhibited state that proteolysis relieves. Consistent with this mechanism, yeast lysates activate the receptor in a manner abolished by heat or by serine-protease inhibition, while purified ligands for the other Toll-like receptors, including acylated lipopeptides, lipopolysaccharide, flagellin, zymosan and CpG DNA, do not. Its transcript is induced in chicken tissues after Salmonella infection. Phylogenetically it sits near the TLR1/TLR2/TLR6/TLR10 group without belonging to it, and its ectodomain has a single-domain architecture rather than the three-domain architecture shared by that group; its activation mechanism resembles that of the protease-activated receptors more than that of a pattern recognition receptor. The cytoplasmic TIR domain has been crystallised as a dimer whose interface differs from those of the TLR1-subfamily TIR domains, and comparison of that structure with adaptor TIR domains led its authors to propose MyD88 as the adaptor.

Suggested Questions for Experts

Q: Is the Eimeria tenella evidence sufficient for a defence-response process annotation on this receptor? Overexpression and knockdown in a fibroblast line change NF-kappa-B and NLRP3/IL-1-beta activation by sporozoites, a parasite aspartyl protease was recovered as a candidate ligand, and cecal expression correlates negatively with oocyst output, but no loss-of-function animal has been tested and the ligand paper leaves other pattern recognition receptors open, so no process term is proposed here.

Q: Does GO need a molecular function for a receptor activated by proteolysis of its own ectodomain? This receptor is left on the generic GO:0038023 because GO:0038187 pattern recognition receptor activity would assert pattern binding that does not occur; the protease-activated receptors face the same modelling problem.

Q: Is any co-receptor required for TLR15 signalling? Candidate chicken surface TLRs were co-expressed without effect, and the GO:0043235 row inherited from the TLR2/TLR1 heterodimer is kept only as a generic claim; identifying the actual dimer would let it be replaced by something specific.

Q: Do the reported responses to bacterial infection and to yeast lysates both reduce to protease sensing, or is there a second, protease-independent activation route? Yeast-lysate activity was abolished by serine-protease inhibition and zymosan did not activate, which suggests one mechanism, but this has not been tested with protease-deficient pathogens.

Q: Which host proteases, if any, can activate this receptor? Non-microbial proteases were reported to activate it in vitro, which raises the question of whether the receptor also responds to host-derived proteolysis during inflammation, as the protease-activated receptors do.

Comprehensive Research Report: TLR15 (Toll-like receptor 15, UniProt: A0A8V0Z0H8) in Gallus gallus (Chicken)

1. Gene/Protein Identity Validation

Extensive recent literature confirms that the gene product identified as TLR15 (UniProt: A0A8V0Z0H8) is correctly annotated as a chicken (Gallus gallus) Toll-like receptor, member of the TLR family, with characteristic domains: extracellular leucine-rich repeats (LRR), a single transmembrane region, and an intracellular Toll/IL-1 receptor (TIR) domain. The protein product's structure and biological function correspond with database annotation and all recent research collected, and there is no conflict with other proteins sharing the same symbol (neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 9-11, neves2022uncoveringa500 pages 2-4).

2. Key Concepts & Definitions

TLR15 is an avian- and reptile-lineage-specific pattern recognition receptor (PRR) that is most robustly present in birds, including chicken, but absent from mammals. It is localized primarily at the cell surface/plasma membrane of immune (macrophages, dendritic cells) and non-immune (epithelial) cells, where it surveils for pathogen intrusion (neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 9-11). Unlike most TLRs that directly recognize defined molecular patterns, TLR15 is activated by proteolytic cleavage—most notably by proteases secreted by pathogenic bacteria, fungi, and some parasites. This mechanism is unique among TLRs studied in vertebrates and highlights a dedicated role for detection of extracellular protease activity as a danger signal (neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 3-6, neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 9-11).

3. Recent Developments (2023-2025)

a. Structure and Mechanism

Recently resolved high-resolution crystal structures (PDB: 7YLF, 7YLG) revealed the atomic details of TLR15’s intracellular TIR domain, including unique features (extended BB and DD loops, C2 helix) critical for signaling dimer formation. Structural studies confirm the pathway proceeds via MyD88-dependent activation (ko2023structuralanalysisof pages 3-4, ko2023structuralanalysisof pages 1-2).

b. Pathogen-Derived Ligands

In 2025, the aspartyl protease EteASP from the coccidial parasite Eimeria tenella was identified as a direct candidate TLR15 ligand. Recombinant EteASP was shown to activate chicken macrophages and dendritic cells, stimulating NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades, the NLRP3 inflammasome, and driving production of cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), nitric oxide, and reactive oxygen species (chen2025eimeriatenellaaspartyl pages 1-2, chen2025eimeriatenellaaspartyl pages 8-11). Induction of the same signaling axis has since been corroborated in sporozoite-challenged chicken fibroblasts and in vivo infection models (li2021activationofchtlr15chnfκbchnlrp3chil1β pages 1-2, li2021activationofchtlr15chnfκbchnlrp3chil1β pages 7-9).

Other recognized stimuli include microbial proteases from Salmonella, Enterococcus, Mycoplasma synoviae (diacylated lipopeptide), and possibly yeast-derived lipoproteins, though the latter are less directly established (mehrzad2024avianinnateand pages 6-8, neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 9-11, rehman2021thepotentialof pages 2-3).

c. Signaling Pathways

Upon receptor activation at the plasma membrane, TLR15 initiates a cascade involving the MyD88 adaptor, leading to strong NF-κB and MAPK activation, subsequently stimulating the NLRP3 inflammasome/caspase-1 axis and release of IL-1β and IL-18. This results in enhanced macrophage/dendritic cell activation, phagocytosis, and pro-inflammatory mediator and nitric oxide production (neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 9-11, chen2025eimeriatenellaaspartyl pages 1-2, li2021activationofchtlr15chnfκbchnlrp3chil1β pages 1-2).

d. Tissue Distribution and Expression

TLR15 is expressed in several immune tissues (spleen, bursa of Fabricius, bone marrow, cecum, ileum) and non-immune tissues, and its expression is inducible upon pathogen challenge and during development. Upregulation is observed in the cecum post-Eimeria and Salmonella infection, ileum and spleen during necrotic enteritis, and in response to Mycoplasma and various stresses (mehrzad2024avianinnateand pages 6-8, neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 9-11, cader2023activationoftolllike pages 41-46, rehman2021thepotentialof pages 5-6).

4. Applications and Real-World Relevance

a. Disease Control

  • Eimeria tenella (coccidiosis): TLR15 is rapidly upregulated in the cecum during infection (negative correlation with oocyst output, suggesting a protective association), and is now a central biomarker and mechanistic node in understanding avian resistance to coccidiosis (zhu2026tolllikereceptormediatedinnate pages 1-2, chen2025eimeriatenellaaspartyl pages 1-2, chen2025eimeriatenellaaspartyl pages 8-11, li2021activationofchtlr15chnfκbchnlrp3chil1β pages 1-2).
  • Salmonella enterica: TLR15 is upregulated in the cecum and fibroblasts following infection, reinforcing its status as a sensor of bacterial invasiveness and virulence-factor activity (neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 9-11).
  • Clostridium perfringens (necrotic enteritis): Induction in ileum/spleen; a candidate biomarker for enteric disease and a putative target for nutritional or immunologic modulation (rehman2021thepotentialof pages 5-6).
  • Mycoplasma synoviae: Induced by infection and its lipopeptide in macrophages/chondrocytes, with proven receptor dependence via RNAi, supporting a key role in synovitis/inflammatory joint disease (neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 9-11).

b. Vaccine and Gut Health Strategies

Given its protease-driven recognition mechanism, TLR15 is investigated as a model for adjuvants and feed additives that could augment or mimic protease stimulus, potentially improving disease resistance in commercial poultry (rehman2021thepotentialof pages 2-3).

5. Expert Opinions and Analysis

  • Evolution: TLR15 is not truly avian-specific but is an ancient vertebrate lineage, present in jawed vertebrates, lungfish, and cartilaginous fish, but lost in mammals. Retention in birds and reptiles appears to reflect a specialized evolutionary adaptation for extracellular protease surveillance (neves2022uncoveringa500 pages 1-2, neves2022uncoveringa500 pages 4-6, neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 9-11, neves2022uncoveringa500 pages 2-4).
  • Structural Biology: The TIR domain dimer structure is uniquely suited to MyD88-dependent signaling, and loss-of-function/mutational studies identify important residues for dimerization and signal propagation (ko2023structuralanalysisof pages 7-9, ko2023structuralanalysisof pages 4-7).
  • Open Questions: While TLR15's role in protease detection is clear, the full spectrum of natural activating ligands, receptor oligomerization patterns after cleavage, and potential direct involvement in viral sensing or heterodimerization with other TLRs remain under study (neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 3-6, neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 9-11, ko2023structuralanalysisof pages 9-10).

6. Statistics and Data from Recent Studies

  • TLR15, MyD88, NF-κB, NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18 expression are significantly increased (p<0.01) in cecal tissue within 4–24 hours of Eimeria tenella infection (li2021activationofchtlr15chnfκbchnlrp3chil1β pages 1-2, li2021activationofchtlr15chnfκbchnlrp3chil1β pages 7-9).
  • Recombinant Eimeria tenella aspartyl protease (EteASP) upregulates IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, nitric oxide, and increases expression of antigen presentation molecules on dendritic cells (chen2025eimeriatenellaaspartyl pages 1-2, chen2025eimeriatenellaaspartyl pages 8-11).
  • TLR15 upregulation is negatively correlated with parasite oocyst output in Eimeria infection (zhu2026tolllikereceptormediatedinnate pages 1-2).

(zhu2026tolllikereceptormediatedinnate pages 1-2, li2021activationofchtlr15chnfκbchnlrp3chil1β pages 1-2) Mechanistic evidence supports inflammatory involvement; expression correlations do not by themselves establish protection or causation.
Bacterial-pathogen responses TLR15 is induced in the cecum during Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium infection and in fibroblasts exposed to heat-killed bacteria. It is also induced by M. synoviae or its diacylated lipopeptide and is upregulated in ileum and spleen during Clostridium perfringens-associated necrotic enteritis. (neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 9-11, rehman2021thepotentialof pages 5-6) These findings establish responsiveness, not necessarily direct recognition of a pathogen-specific molecule in every case. Salmonella and C. perfringens should not automatically be treated as proven direct TLR15 ligands.
Viral associations TLR15 expression changes have been reported during some avian viral infections, including splenic upregulation with fowl adenovirus 8b; however, TLR15 is not established as a direct viral nucleic-acid sensor. (zhu2026tolllikereceptormediatedinnate pages 1-2) Primarily expression-level association; direct viral ligand recognition and antiviral mechanism remain uncertain.
Evolutionary distribution TLR15 is absent from mammals but retained broadly in birds and some reptiles. Comparative genomic work extends its origin to the common ancestor of jawed vertebrates more than 450 million years ago, with orthologs in lungfish and holocephalan cartilaginous fishes and extensive lineage-specific loss or pseudogenization elsewhere. (neves2022uncoveringa500 pages 1-2, neves2022uncoveringa500 pages 4-6, neves2022uncoveringa500 pages 2-4) The older description “avian-specific” is operationally useful in poultry immunology but phylogenetically incomplete; current evidence supports an ancient, patchily retained vertebrate lineage.
Functional interpretation The most defensible annotation is: cell-surface innate immune receptor activated by pathogen-associated extracellular proteolysis, signaling mainly through TIR–MyD88–NF-κB and, in relevant infection contexts, MAPK and NLRP3-inflammasome pathways. (neerukonda2020avianpatternrecognition pages 9-11, li2021activationofchtlr15chnfκbchnlrp3chil1β pages 1-2) High-confidence core annotation; exact protease specificity, cleavage site, co-receptors and stimulus-dependent pathway branching remain research gaps.

9. Conclusion

Chicken TLR15 is a uniquely positioned, cell-surface pattern-recognition receptor specializing in the detection of extracellular microbial proteolytic activity—bridging innate sensing to inflammation through MyD88–NF-κB–MAPK–NLRP3 axes. It provides a critical defense against protozoan, bacterial, and some viral poultry pathogens, with functional relevance that has been greatly clarified in the last 3–5 years. TLR15 serves not only as a biomarker for poultry health research, but as a model for understanding ancient and non-mammalian immune strategies, and as a potential target for immunomodulation in agriculture.

Entry identity (TrEMBL, unreviewed)

A0A8V0Z0H8_CHICK is an unreviewed Ensembl-derived entry, 924 aa, named
"Toll-like receptor 15" with gene name TLR15, cross-referenced to
Ensembl ENSGALG00010016958, RefSeq NP_001385167 and GeneID 421219. Ensembl gives
that gene as "TLR15 toll like receptor 15 [Source:NCBI gene; Acc:421219]" on
chromosome 3, so this entry is the right gene. This matters because the project's
candidate table listed a different accession, Q2XQ10, whose submitter name is
"Toll-like receptor 2"; the entry fetched here carries the matching name and the
NCBI/Ensembl cross-references.

Architecture is canonical for a TLR: leucine-rich repeats (Pfam LRR_1, LRR_8), two
predicted transmembrane segments (59..80, 709..732) and a TIR domain (761..902),
PANTHER family PTHR24365 (subfamily SF17, named after TLR2 — consistent with the
family placement below rather than with orthology).

Established biology

  • TLR15 is restricted to birds and reptiles and is not the orthologue of any
    mammalian TLR: PMID:23066147 and
    reptilian genomes, which places its origin after the bird/reptile–mammal split.
    Phylogenetically it falls near, but not inside, the TLR1/2/6/10 group:
    PMID:16495540.
  • It is a cell-surface receptor:
    PMID:21383168.
  • Its activation mechanism is unique among TLRs: it is not activated by binding a
    microbial molecular pattern but by proteolytic cleavage of its own ectodomain by
    secreted microbial proteases —
    PMID:21383168
    and stimulation of NF-kappa-B-dependent transcription; removing the whole
    ectodomain makes the receptor constitutively active. The paper's own controls
    exclude the classical ligands: a panel including
    PMID:21383168
    with and without candidate chicken heterodimerisation partners, and
    PMID:21383168.
  • An independent study found activation by yeast lysates:
    PMID:23066147

Annotation decisions (summary)

The interesting rows are the two IBA annotations transferred from human TLR2
(via PANTHER:PTN002808136).

  • GO:0042497 triacyl lipopeptide binding (IBA from UniProtKB:O60603/TLR2) —
    REMOVE. This is exactly the ligand-specificity transfer the project set out to
    check. TLR15 is not a TLR2 orthologue but a lineage-specific receptor, and the
    cleanest experimental test available reports that di- and triacylated lipopeptides
    do not activate it, with or without candidate chicken co-receptors.
  • GO:0043235 signaling receptor complex (IBA, part_of, same donor) — kept as
    non-core. The donor basis is the TLR2/TLR1 heterodimer, which TLR15 does not form
    as far as anyone has shown, but TLRs signal as dimers and the truncated receptor's
    autoactivation was interpreted as self-dimerisation, so complex membership is
    defensible at this generic level while the identity of the complex is unknown.
  • GO:0002224 (IBA) modified to GO:0035681 toll-like receptor 15 signaling pathway.
    The receptor-specific term exists, it names this receptor, and two independent
    studies show NF-kappa-B activation initiated at this receptor.
  • GO:0038023 signaling receptor activity (IBA) — accepted deliberately, and not
    upgraded to GO:0038187 pattern recognition receptor activity    . GO:0038187 requires
    combining with a pathogen-associated molecular pattern; TLR15 is activated by
    cleavage of its ectodomain, and the proteases that cleave it are enzymes rather
    than patterns bound by the receptor. The generic receptor term is the accurate one
    here, which is worth recording explicitly because every other reviewed TLR in this
    batch takes the GO:0038187 upgrade.
  • GO:0005886 plasma membrane (IBA) accepted: unusually for an IBA location call in
    this family, it is confirmed experimentally for this receptor by surface
    biotinylation and confocal microscopy.
  • GO:0007165 and GO:0016020 kept as non-core.

description: Cell-surface Toll-like receptor that forms a distinct subfamily of the Toll-like
  receptors, with no mammalian orthologue, and the only known member of the family that is switched
  on by proteolysis rather than by binding a microbial molecule. It is best known from birds and
  reptiles, but orthologues also occur in lungfish and in holocephalan cartilaginous fish, so the
  gene is an ancient jawed-vertebrate lineage that has been lost or pseudogenised in many groups,
  including all mammals, rather than an avian invention. It has the usual TLR architecture of a leucine-rich-repeat
  ectodomain, a transmembrane segment and a cytoplasmic TIR domain, and is displayed as a glycoprotein
  at the cell surface. Secreted proteases from fungi and bacteria cleave its ectodomain, and this
  cleavage activates the receptor and drives NF-kappa-B-dependent transcription; a truncated form
  lacking the whole ectodomain is constitutively active, indicating that the leucine-rich repeats
  hold the receptor in an inhibited state that proteolysis relieves. Consistent with this mechanism,
  yeast lysates activate the receptor in a manner abolished by heat or by serine-protease inhibition,
  while purified ligands for the other Toll-like receptors, including acylated lipopeptides, lipopolysaccharide,
  flagellin, zymosan and CpG DNA, do not. Its transcript is induced in chicken tissues after Salmonella
  infection. Phylogenetically it sits near the TLR1/TLR2/TLR6/TLR10 group without belonging to
  it, and its ectodomain has a single-domain architecture rather than the three-domain architecture
  shared by that group; its activation mechanism resembles that of the protease-activated receptors
  more than that of a pattern recognition receptor. The cytoplasmic TIR domain has been crystallised
  as a dimer whose interface differs from those of the TLR1-subfamily TIR domains, and comparison
  of that structure with adaptor TIR domains led its authors to propose MyD88 as the adaptor.
