L-threonine biosynthesis from L-aspartate

L-threonine synthesis from L-aspartate through five successive transformations, represented by bacterial, fungal and plant enzymes and by two experimentally characterized archaeal steps. Aspartate kinase forms 4-phospho-L-aspartate; NADPH-dependent aspartate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase produces L-aspartate 4-semialdehyde; homoserine dehydrogenase produces L-homoserine; homoserine phosphorylation supplies O-phospho-L-homoserine; and PLP-dependent threonine synthase forms L-threonine. Homoserine phosphorylation can use ATP through a GHMP kinase or phosphoserine through the distinct Pseudomonas ThrH phosphotransferase, which releases serine. In the represented yeasts and E. coli, homoserine is shared with methionine synthesis; in plants the shared intermediate is O-phosphohomoserine, so homoserine kinase does not commit flux exclusively to threonine. Aspartate kinase and homoserine dehydrogenase may be separate proteins or domains of an AK-HSDH fusion. Cofactor preference, feedback sensitivity and oligomeric state are enzyme-specific. Arabidopsis TS1 is activated by S-adenosylmethionine, an allosteric effector rather than a consumed substrate. The aspartate-semialdehyde branch can supply diaminopimelate-pathway lysine synthesis in plants and many prokaryotes; the represented fungi instead use the alpha-aminoadipate lysine pathway. This is a reusable reaction module, not a claim that every member of any named taxon has a complete pathway.

Boundary: aspartate supply, phosphoserine supply for ThrH, NADPH/ATP/PLP production, methionine and lysine branches, isoleucine formation, and threonine catabolism are outside the five transformations modeled here. This boundary is not a reason to reject broader GO involvement by an upstream enzyme. All five transformations are required; architecture, donor and synthase variants are alternatives. One fusion protein can satisfy steps 1 and 3, but never bypasses step 2. Representatives orient known implementations and do not prove complete routes in every represented species. No common compartment or universal feedback inhibitor is asserted. The represented yeasts use the alpha-aminoadipate lysine route; modern fungal DHDPS characterization supports this boundary without extrapolating a historical lower-fungi DAP claim. PANTHER membership is checked independently of readable labels: PTHR43070:SF5 mixes mono- and bifunctional architectures, and PTHR10314 has the surprising official name CYSTATHIONINE BETA-SYNTHASE. Independent activity and architecture restrictions therefore accompany these selectors. Six GO-CAM sources were normally refreshed; current source assessments above supersede the old absent-edge/reversed-edge claims. The retained activity links are verified against current enabled-by/MF records. Broad source process labels and AAT1 localization are assessed on biological evidence rather than this module boundary. Inferred product-to-substrate order does not assert substrate channeling. Nuclear silencing/repair-associated findings for yeast pathway proteins are distinct contexts reviewed at gene level and are not steps of this biosynthetic reaction module. Additional architectures discussed in the comparative review, including a phosphoglycerate-mutase-like homoserine-phosphorylation alternative, are not equated with HAD-like ThrH and are not assigned guessed accessions. The present panel documents the two donor implementations supported by its reviewed representatives.

Arabidopsis TS1 uses PLP for the terminal reaction and is activated by noncovalently bound SAM. SAM is neither a methyl donor nor a consumed cofactor in this reaction. This regulation is not generalized to bacterial or fungal threonine synthases (PMID:9748328, PMID:10429206).
