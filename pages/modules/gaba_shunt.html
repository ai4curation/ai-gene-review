GABA shunt (glutamate to GABA to succinate)

Three-step pathway, found in animals, plants and fungi, that converts L-glutamate to succinate through gamma-aminobutyrate (GABA) and succinate semialdehyde. It bypasses the 2-oxoglutarate-to-succinate segment of the TCA cycle. A pyridoxal-phosphate glutamate decarboxylase first converts L-glutamate to GABA and CO2 in the cytosol. A pyridoxal-phosphate GABA transaminase then transfers the amino group of GABA to an acceptor 2-oxo acid, giving succinate semialdehyde. Finally, succinate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase oxidizes succinate semialdehyde to succinate with NAD+ or NADP+. The chemistry is conserved, but the implementations differ between lineages. Animal glutamate decarboxylases form a family separate from the calmodulin-binding decarboxylases of plants and fungi. Animal and fungal GABA transaminases use 2-oxoglutarate and so regenerate glutamate, whereas the main plant enzyme uses pyruvate or glyoxylate. GABA catabolism takes place in the mitochondrial matrix of animals and plants but in the cytosol of budding yeast. In animals the shunt makes and clears the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA. In plants it responds to stress and makes the GABA gradient that guides pollen tubes. In yeasts it lets GABA serve as a nitrogen source and helps cells tolerate oxidative stress. Organisms that lack glutamate decarboxylase, such as fission yeast, can still degrade GABA taken up from outside.

GENERALIZED (YeastPathways crosswalk, 2026-10) from the earlier mammalian module to cover fungi and plants. The mammalian realization is kept as the animal lineage variant: human GAD1/GAD2 (PTHR45677), ABAT (P80404) and ALDH5A1 (P51649), grounded in the human Reactome reactions and the completed human gene reviews. In mammals GAD is cytosolic, while ABAT and ALDH5A1 are in the mitochondrial matrix, so GABA must be imported. GAD1 deficiency causes spastic paraplegia, cerebral palsy or developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, ABAT deficiency causes GABA-transaminase deficiency, and ALDH5A1 deficiency causes SSADH deficiency (4-hydroxybutyric aciduria). ABAT also transaminates beta-amino acids, which links it to thymine catabolism. In yeasts that function belongs to a separate paralog in the S. kluyveri lineage (PYD4; PMID:17355287). Fungal grounding: S. cerevisiae GAD1, UGA1 and UGA2 (YeastCyc GLUDEG-I-PWY-1 and YEAST-4AMINOBUTMETAB-PWY; all three gene assignments retained). S. pombe uga1, ssd1 and ssd2 come from the PomBase GABA-catabolism model. YeastCyc corrections: none to gene content. The GLUDEG-I-PWY-1 comment notes that GDH2 glutamate dehydrogenase, not this shunt, is the main route of glutamate degradation in S. cerevisiae. Compartments differ by lineage. S. cerevisiae UGA1 and UGA2 are cytosolic (UniProt, PMID:11914276, PMID:14562095), whereas the PomBase model places S. pombe uga1, ssd1 and ssd2 in the mitochondrial matrix. Because of this, no location is asserted on the catabolic annotons. GO-CAM disagreements: the PomBase model types ssd1/ssd2 as NAD+-specific (GO:0004777), while UniProt names them NADP+-dependent. The SGD models have no causal links. The PomBase model's plasma-membrane and mitochondrial GABA import activities are enabled by unidentified proteins and are outside the module. S. pombe has no glutamate decarboxylase, so the GAD part is optional. The uptake permeases (S. cerevisiae UGA4, PUT4, GAP1) and the UGA3/DAL81 transcriptional control are excluded. Scope: eukaryotic GABA shunt; the bacterial GabT/GabD pathway (for example in P. putida) and its transport and regulation belong in a separate bacterial module.
