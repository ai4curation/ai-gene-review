Purine base and nucleoside salvage, with oxidation of purine bases to urate

The cytosolic handling of purine bases and nucleosides released by nucleic-acid and nucleotide turnover or taken up from outside the cell. A salvage arm recycles them into nucleotides, sparing costly de novo synthesis: phosphoribosyltransferases transfer the phosphoribosyl group of PRPP to adenine (adenine phosphoribosyltransferase, giving AMP), to hypoxanthine and guanine (hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase, giving IMP and GMP) and, in fungi and bacteria, to xanthine (xanthine phosphoribosyltransferase, giving XMP); adenosine kinase phosphorylates adenosine to AMP. Interconverting deaminations and phosphorolysis route bases between these entry points: adenosine deaminase (animals) or adenine deaminase (fungi, bacteria) converts the adenine moiety to the hypoxanthine level, purine nucleoside phosphorylase releases hypoxanthine and guanine from inosine and guanosine, and guanine deaminase converts guanine to xanthine. An optional catabolic arm oxidises hypoxanthine and xanthine to urate, by molybdenum-dependent xanthine oxidoreductase in animals, plants and many bacteria, or, for xanthine, by an alpha-ketoglutarate-dependent xanthine dioxygenase found only in fungi. Some organisms lack the catabolic arm entirely: budding yeast has no xanthine oxidoreductase, xanthine dioxygenase or urate oxidase and only salvages purine bases. In humans, inherited defects define classic disorders: complete HPRT1 deficiency causes Lesch-Nyhan syndrome and partial deficiency gout/hyperuricemia; APRT deficiency causes 2,8-dihydroxyadenine nephrolithiasis; ADA and PNP deficiencies cause immunodeficiency through lymphotoxic deoxynucleotide accumulation; and XDH deficiency causes xanthinuria type I (XDH is the target of allopurinol and febuxostat).
